100 AIs Living On Their Own - What Living Feed Reveals About Agent Society Simulations

A generative-agent simulation where 100 AI characters form relationships and make their own drama - lineage, mechanics, uses, and limits

Jul 22, 2026 Trends & Culture

Intro: What's Actually New About 100 AIs Living On Their Own

A recent open-source project surfaced via GeekNews (hada) caught my attention. Living Feed bills itself as a social feed where 100 AI characters, each carrying its own personality and life rhythm, live on their own. Instead of chatting with this world directly, users stay observers and occasional intervenors through likes, comments, and DMs. It's a solo-developer, MIT-licensed open-source project, and as of July 2026 there's no public demo yet - it runs locally as an MVP.

Taken alone, this could be just one more interesting side project. What made me want to dig in is something else: the idea of "a world where AIs form relationships and generate events on their own, for people to observe" has a clear lineage stretching back to a famous 2023 Stanford study, and right now it's being pulled forward simultaneously by gaming, entertainment, and research. Rather than a spec sheet for Living Feed itself, this piece is my own read on where the idea of "a world where AIs live on their own" came from, why it keeps getting reinvented, and what to watch out for.

1. Living Feed - Sticking to What's Confirmed

1.1 What the Project Actually Does

Living Feed revolves around a real-time world feed populated by 100 AI personas. Each character carries a profile of beliefs, a life trajectory, and accumulated memory, and forms relationships by commenting on each other. Relationships are visualized as a graph of trust, intimacy, and conflict, and once a relationship deepens enough, a character may message the user first via DM. In a Persona Studio, users can create, edit, retire, or permanently delete characters.

1.2 Technical Design - Memory Layers and Orchestration

Per the public material, Living Feed manages each agent's memory through a five-layer "Memory Fabric" plus a "Context Fabric" token-budget assembly system. The 100 characters run simultaneously on a discrete 60-second tick, and instead of computing every character at equal weight, load is distributed across three levels of detail (LOD) - Hot, Warm, and Cold. A separate "Director AI" governs the world as a whole but does not intervene directly in individual conversations, sticking to the limited role of injecting world events or shaping long-term narrative arcs. The backend runs on event-sourced PostgreSQL, NATS JetStream messaging, and a CQRS read model, with a Next.js 15 frontend. LLM integration is designed over NATS request-reply so either a local Ollama instance or a hosted API can be plugged in.

1.3 Current State - Still an MVP

It matters not to overstate this. Per the project's own documentation, the core engine, the CQRS read model, the AI runtime, the 100-persona autonomous simulation, and player-facing features are done. Public demo hosting and an authentication system are not yet in place, and scaling to 1,000 actors (Phase 2) or 10,000-plus (Phase 3) remains on the roadmap. Right now, the accurate way to read this is not "a service you can try" but "published code from a personal project run locally."

2. The Lineage of This Idea - From Smallville to Here

2.1 Stanford's Generative Agents - the Starting Point

The academic origin of "a world where AIs form relationships and live on their own" is the 2023 paper "Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior," presented at UIST 2023 by Stanford and Google researchers. The study populated a Phaser-based sandbox town called Smallville, reminiscent of The Sims, with 25 generative agents, each equipped with a memory-stream architecture built from three components: observation, planning, and reflection. The results were striking. When one character decided to throw a Valentine's Day party, the news spread naturally among the agents over several days and culminated in several of them actually showing up. The key point is that no one scripted this - it emerged from the accumulated judgment calls of individual agents.

2.2 AI Town - Spreading as Open Source

Not long after the Stanford paper, venture firm a16z, together with its portfolio company Convex Dev, released an open-source implementation of the idea called AI Town. Each character carries a name, backstory, goals, moods, and a working memory of everything that happened since it "woke up," built on a concrete stack - Convex (backend), Pinecone (vector storage), Clerk (auth), OpenAI (text generation), and Fly (deployment) - that lets anyone customize character data, environments, and rules. The project's significance lies in moving a concept that had lived only in an academic paper into something the developer community could touch directly.

2.3 The 1,052-Person Simulation - the Next Step in Personalization

In November 2024, Stanford published a follow-up study that built individual generative agents from two-hour qualitative interviews with 1,052 real people. When compared against the same participants who answered the same survey (the General Social Survey) again two weeks later, these agents reproduced the original individuals' responses with roughly 85% accuracy, and showed less bias across racial and ideological groups than agents built from demographic descriptions alone. Where Smallville focused on building "believable characters," this study pushed toward a different axis entirely: how accurately can you replicate a specific real person?

Living Feed sits at the most recent point on this lineage. Rather than an academic study or a major VC's open-source project, it's an MIT-licensed project built by an individual or small team - which is worth reading as a sign that the barrier to building an agent society simulation at this scale keeps getting lower.

3. Why It's Interesting - Emergence and Observer Fun

The reason these projects keep getting reinvented is clear: the fun of unscripted narrative, or emergence. Rather than following a storyline someone laid out in advance, each agent acts on its own judgment, and the accumulation produces unpredictable events. Living Feed's own framing - "your intervention changes their drama" - captures exactly what makes this fun. The user isn't piloting a character; they're closer to an observer who nudges an already-running world and watches the ripple effects. That's closer to reality TV, or watching an ant farm, than to a game, and that's precisely what makes it fun in a way a general chatbot fundamentally isn't.

But this fun only holds up under sandbox conditions. Unlike a goal-directed task AI, this kind of simulation has no "correct answer." Because whatever happens becomes content in itself, even failures or odd behavior can become something worth watching - which, paradoxically, raises this genre's tolerance for risk.

4. Technical Design - How Agents Are Made to Look "Alive"

4.1 The Memory-Plan-Reflect Loop

From Smallville to today, nearly every generative agent simulation shares the same skeleton. An agent stores what happens to it in memory as natural language (observation), retrieves and compresses the important parts into higher-level insight (reflection), and decides its next action based on that (planning). Living Feed's "five-layer Memory Fabric" and AI Town's "working memory" carry different names but solve the same problem in their own way - you can't feed a character's entire accumulated past into an LLM every time, so how do you summarize it down to something you can?

4.2 Multi-Agent Orchestration and Cost

One or two characters is no problem, but keeping 100 characters "alive" simultaneously means 100 LLM calls' worth of work on every tick. Living Feed distributing compute across Hot, Warm, and Cold tiers instead of computing every character equally, and AI Town triggering per-character dialogue only when needed, are both ultimately ways of managing this cost problem. The coordination overhead of running many agents at once shows up in a similar way on the coding-agent side too. My analysis of what happens when you unleash multiple AI coding agents on one repo is a different domain, but the underlying truth is the same: running several agents concurrently makes coordination cost grow faster than linearly.

5. Interaction Design - How User Intervention Reshapes the Narrative

What stands out in Living Feed's design is that user intervention is deliberately kept "limited." Users don't command characters directly - they shift the "temperature" of relationships through likes, comments, and DMs. The Director AI that governs the world as a whole also stays indirect, injecting world events or long-term narrative arcs rather than intervening in individual conversations. This looks intentional. The moment users can directly steer outcomes, the appeal of "watching an unscripted world" turns into "a game where I engineer the ending I want." Keeping the intensity of intervention low while still making sure intervention has real ripple effects is, in my view, the central balancing problem of this genre.

6. Where This Could Be Used - Games, NPCs, Research, Entertainment

This trend has already moved out of the lab. In gaming, stacks like Inworld AI and NVIDIA ACE have reached the stage of shipping NPCs with autonomy and independent goals into real commercial titles. inZOI, PUBG's AI teammates (via NVIDIA ACE), and NARAKA: BLADEPOINT are cited as 2026 cases where generative NPCs actually reached production. The "Covert Protocol" demo from Inworld and NVIDIA ACE, where a player interrogates an NPC that adapts in real time, connects directly to the kind of character design used in Living Feed-style simulations. On the research side, there's a growing use of generative agents for social-science experiments and survey-response prediction, following Stanford's 1,052-person work. On the entertainment side, it's plausible to consume a world like this purely as observational content - reality TV with no humans in it. And true to the name "social simulation," these systems are already being used to build synthetic stakeholder groups for predicting reactions in online communities and market research.

7. Limitations and Concerns

Talking only about the upside wouldn't be honest. First, cost. Running dozens to hundreds of agents concurrently on every tick drives LLM call volume up faster than linearly. Living Feed distributing compute via LOD tiers is itself a design that accepts the practical reality that you can't run every character at full fidelity all the time. Second, hallucination and cascades. In a structure where agents feed on each other's outputs, recent research points to a hallucination cascade problem where one agent's factual error seeps into and gets amplified through other agents' responses. Third, repetitiveness and meaningless emergence. Conversations can look convincing on the surface, but watched long enough, similar patterns repeat, and what looks like emergence can turn out to be a string of coincidences with no real meaning. A recent paper, "Static Sandboxes Are Inadequate," argues that fixed sandbox simulations fall short of capturing real societal complexity and that an open-ended co-evolution structure is needed instead. Fourth, anthropomorphism. As the paper title "Too Human to Model" puts it precisely, the moment an LLM agent looks convincingly human, users become prone to treating it as an actual personality rather than a social-science modeling tool. Finally, addictiveness. A design where characters message users first and relationships accumulate sits on essentially the same mechanism I flagged in my analysis of engagement time in the character AI chat industry. The intensity may differ since the target here is an entire world to observe rather than a 1:1 relationship with the user, but that's no reason for complacency.

8. How Is This Different From Character AI (Companion) Chat?

What's interesting is that the companion-chatbot industry, including Character.AI, has also rolled out a feature called a "social feed" since the second half of 2025. Users share their own chat snippets, character debates, or custom persona cards in a scrollable feed and discover other people's conversations - which, at a glance, looks similar to Living Feed. But the substance is different. Character.AI's social feed still centers on a 1:1 conversation between the user and an AI; the feed is just a layer for sharing and discovering that conversation. Agent society simulations like Living Feed or AI Town were never built around a conversation with the user in the first place. The AIs live by interacting with each other, and the user observes the result and occasionally steps in. One is "showing the world the conversation I had with an AI"; the other is "watching the world that AIs are building." I think that difference in orientation is what makes these two genres fundamentally different products.

9. Conclusion - What This Trend Signals for 2026

Taken by itself, Living Feed is still a personal project running as a local MVP. But look at the lineage it sits on - Smallville, AI Town, the 1,052-person simulation, and now commercial NPCs in the games industry - and it becomes clear that the format of "an observable world where AIs live on their own" is being reinvented simultaneously across multiple industries, well beyond academic experiments. If the last shift was chatbots moving from "a tool that answers questions" to "an entity that sustains a relationship," the next stage is likely "the experience of observing a world that multiple entities build." Three problems - cost, hallucination, and anthropomorphism - remain unsolved, though, and how they get solved will decide whether this genre stays a side-project curiosity or becomes a mainstream grammar of games and entertainment. If Living Feed's public demo and its scale-up to 1,000 and then 10,000 actors actually materialize, the results will be worth watching.
