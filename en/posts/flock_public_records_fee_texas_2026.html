The Trigger — $2.3 Million on a Single Invoice

On October 5, 2026, the Texas Tribune reported that in North Richland Hills, a suburb in the Fort Worth area, getting answers about how police use their Flock Safety cameras could cost $2.3 million. The byline is Ayden Runnels. On the same day, Ashley Belanger of Ars Technica wrote an article based on that report, and the story was introduced on GeekNews on October 6.

The numbers should be sorted out first. The Tribune headline reads “Texas city charges $2 million for Flock records as agencies make it harder to see how cameras are used,” and the Ars headline reads “Texas city demands $2M for public records on Flock usage.” Both articles use $2 million in the headline and $2.3 million in the body. The $2.3 million in the body is the amount on the invoice, and the $2 million in the headlines reads as a shortened form of that number (an interpretation).

What deserves attention in this case is its shape. The agency did not answer that it would not hand over the records. It answered that the labor cost of handing them over had to be paid first. According to the Tribune, the city required the entire amount to be paid up-front. The issue here is not whether the records are open but what price is attached to opening them.

The scope of this post is fees and nothing else. What Flock cameras record and what controversies have followed is covered in The Vigilante Movement Against Flock Cameras, and the form in which data subject to mandatory disclosure is published is covered in When Data Published for Oversight Becomes a Map. A case in which the very question of whether material falls within the reach of a records request was disputed is discussed in What the Rodin Museum 3D Scan Ruling Decided and What It Did Not.

The Formula Behind the Estimate — The Numbers on the Invoice

The contents of the invoice as relayed by the Tribune are as follows. It is the invoice the city sent after the requester filed a public records request.

  • What is to be searched. About a terabyte of communications about errors, misuse and effectiveness of the Flock system. The original wording is “about a terabyte worth of communications about errors, misuse and effectiveness of the Flock system.”
  • Rate. Labor costs were set at $15 an hour.
  • Duration. The Tribune describes this as equivalent to more than 14 years of work. Ars carries the same point as a claim by officials that the search would take approximately 14 years of labor.
  • Payment terms. The city required the full amount to be paid up-front.
  • Total. $2.3 million.

The following table sets out whose figure each number is.

Labor rate$15 an hourThe invoice (as reported by the Tribune)

The last row is a calculation that does not appear in the sources. Dividing $2.3 million by $15 an hour gives about 153,000 hours (a calculation). The basis for converting those hours into more than 14 years of work, that is, how many people working how many hours a day were assumed, is not given in either article. The formula as a whole belongs to the invoice issued by the city, and the articles do not include any third-party review of whether its assumptions are reasonable.

The City’s Explanation — The Spokesperson, the Police Post, and the Police Chief

The Tribune article carries the city’s side in three strands: the answer from a city spokesperson, a post the police department published on social media on Monday night, and a statement from the police chief included in that post.

  • Confirmation of the invoice. A North Richland Hills city spokesperson confirmed the public records invoice to the Texas Tribune.
  • Questions about camera use. The spokesperson referred questions about the city’s Flock camera use to a city website with information about the cameras.
  • Cost after narrowing. The requester has since narrowed the request. When the Tribune asked whether that would lower the cost, the city did not respond.
  • What the police post said. The police department’s post, however, said the modified request’s cost is “expected to be significantly less.”
  • The explanation of the amount. The city’s post said the $2.3 million cost estimate was “understandably shocking,” but that it was calculated based on the amount of communications legally required to be reviewed within the almost terabyte worth of data.
  • The police chief’s statement. Police Chief Jeff Garner said the city’s use of Flock cameras reflected the “community’s expectations.” He continued: “As far as this latest record request, we always strive for transparency,” and “Our use of [license plate readers] adheres to these community principles and honors our commitment to pursue justice for victims of crime.”

Reduced to its essentials, the city’s explanation has three parts. The amount on the invoice is real. The amount is large because of the volume of communications that must legally be reviewed. If the scope is narrowed, the cost goes down. The Tribune discloses that it has a similar pending request for records to the same city.

One point is worth knowing when the two articles are read side by side. The Ars article carries the formula as a claim by officials, but it does not include the explanation in the police post or the police chief’s statement. The city’s side has to be read in the Tribune article.

The Requester — Who Asked for What

The two articles say the following about the party that filed the request.

  • Who filed. A person using the name Phil Mynona filed the records request in late August on behalf of a group called the Texas Privacy Coalition. The Tribune notes that the name is a pseudonym he has often used to speak publicly about Flock.
  • Scope of his requests. According to the Tribune, he has filed nearly identical public records requests with almost 200 law enforcement agencies. Ars describes this as more than 200 agencies across the United States, so the wording of the two articles differs.
  • What he said about the amount. The Tribune reports that the invoice struck him as “ludicrous” and as a way of stifling his search for records. That is his assessment.
  • The comparison he offered. “This is a crazy outlier, I think the highest that we received in some amount was [$5,000] from Victoria, Texas, and at the time, I thought that was ridiculous,” he said.
  • What he did next. He narrowed the request.

The Tribune writes that he has received troves of information from agencies, some at no cost and others with varying price tags for labor costs. Ars relays his remark that it was impossible to predict how cities assessed fees for Flock records.

The Tribune puts it this way: information provided for free by some law enforcement agencies has been held behind invoices costing thousands by others. Why the amounts differ, whether because the volume of records differs or because the method of calculation differs, cannot be determined from the articles.

When Records Are Withheld Without a Price — The State Agencies

Apart from fees, the Tribune also describes cases in which requests were not granted. One example is the course of the Tribune’s own requests for Flock audit data to the Texas Department of Public Safety (DPS).

  • July. DPS gave the Tribune a complete list of the department’s searches and those of thousands of other agencies using the agency’s Flock network, with officer and license plate numbers redacted.
  • Early August. In response to an identical request for the next few weeks of data, DPS provided only records of its own searches and not those of outside agencies.
  • The third request. When asked for a third set of search data shortly after, DPS withheld the records completely and asked the Attorney General’s Office to allow it to withhold the entirety of the search data it had previously provided. DPS did not respond to the Tribune’s request for comment about the change in its response.
  • Camera locations. The Texas Department of Transportation, which approves permits for license plate readers placed along state roads and highways, declined a request to share the number of permits it approved and the locations of approved cameras, citing public safety concerns in a challenge sent to the Attorney General’s Office. DPS and the Texas Department of Criminal Justice have also challenged record requests that would identify camera locations.
  • Examples in the other direction. Police in Live Oak, northeast of San Antonio, provide an interactive map of their Flock camera locations, and Corinth in North Texas lists the street locations of all of its cameras online.

According to the Tribune, DPS operates more than 900 of the estimated 13,000 Flock cameras statewide. Seen against the Attorney General’s guidance, this route is different from the fee route. The guidance says that if a governmental body determines that information is excepted from disclosure, both the request and the information at issue must be reviewed by the Open Records Division (ORD), and the ORD decides whether the body may withhold the information or must release it. Withholding passes through a decision by the Attorney General’s office, while fees follow the path described in the next section.

The Process Described by the Texas Attorney General — Estimates, Inspection or Copies, and Overcharge Complaints

The website of the Office of the Attorney General of Texas introduces the Public Information Act (PIA) as a mechanism for citizens to inspect or copy government records. Arranging the sentences of that guidance that relate to cost by stage gives the following table.

StageWhat the guidance saysGuidance page
RequestA request must be in writing and must be for information already in existence. A governmental body is not required to answer questions or create new informationHow to Request Public Information
EstimateA governmental body “must send you an estimate of charges in advance”Requestor’s Rights
Choice of methodThe requester can choose whether to inspect the information, receive copies, or bothRequestor’s Rights
Cost rulesA governmental body must follow the cost rules written by the attorney general. The rules are found in the Texas Administrative Code and are mandatory for all governmental bodies, except to the extent that other laws establish charges for specific kinds of informationHow to Report a Violation of the Public Information Act
Overcharge complaintA requester who believes they are being overcharged for a copy or inspection may file a written complaint. It must be received no later than 10 business days after the requester knows of the alleged overcharge, and it must include a copy of the original request and any correspondence stating the chargesHow to Report a Violation of the Public Information Act
Responsible divisionThe ORD determines charges and methods for charging for copies, handles complaints about overcharges, and handles requests from governmental bodies for cost rule exemptions. The page cites Government Code Chapter 552, sections 552.009, 552.205, and 552.261 through 552.274Charges for Public Information

Other sentences in the same guidance also bear on this case.

  • Equal treatment. Governmental bodies are required to treat all requestors equally.
  • Prompt production. A governmental body must “promptly” produce information. The guidance explains this as meaning that a body may take a reasonable amount of time but may not delay, and that if it will take longer than ten business days, the body must notify the requester when the information will be released.
  • When a body wants to withhold. If a governmental body withholds any of the requested information, in most cases it must request a ruling from the Attorney General’s office, and it must notify the requester that it has done so.
  • Where to ask. The guidance tells requesters who believe a body has not properly responded to contact the Open Government Hotline.
  • Detailed document. The site offers the Public Information Act Handbook as a PDF.

What can be read from this guidance is where a dispute would take place. The size of an estimate does not settle anything by itself. What can be contested is whether the amount was calculated in line with the cost rules, and the body that looks at that question is the ORD. The other route that appears in the reporting is narrowing the scope. The requester did narrow his request, and the police said the cost in that case is expected to be significantly less.

Transparency Portals — Flock’s Statement and the Tribune’s Count

The Tribune writes that Flock Safety is an Atlanta-based company that releases few details about its cameras, leaving disclosure and transparency decisions to state and local governments. According to the Tribune, camera locations, the amount of data retained, and how often police search for vehicles are unknown in most cities across Texas unless departments explicitly share them, which places even more importance on public records.

  • What is collected. According to the Tribune, Flock cameras collect license plate numbers as well as other identifying information, including make, model, bumper stickers and dents, and officers can search thousands of cameras owned by hundreds of police agencies in a single query.
  • Audit data. The Tribune writes that agencies’ audit data of searches is exportable through the click of a few buttons on Flock’s platform.
  • Transparency portals. The company gives agencies the option to host transparency portals. A portal shows how many cameras a department has, how many searches it conducts, and how many vehicles are scanned, and it can even allow search data to be downloaded directly from the site. Whether and how the feature is used is up to each agency.
  • The company’s statement. Flock spokesperson Andrew Smith said in a statement: “The portals give agencies a public-facing way to publish information about their [license plate reader] programs, including policies, usage and access rules to help communities better understand how the technology is being used.”
  • The Tribune’s count. Out of the hundreds of agencies that use Flock cameras in Texas, 60 make use of the transparency portals, representing more than 2,600 of the estimated 13,000 cameras operating in the state. Of those, 16 share their search records. Ars gives the number of agencies using portals as 59, which differs from the Tribune’s 60.

The Tribune writes that information unearthed through public records requests has led to investigations, policy changes, and departments’ first audits. It also reports that Governor Greg Abbott blocked state funding for the cameras and paused certain installations in August and September. When the voluntary means of disclosure are used only to the extent shown by the numbers above, the remaining way to obtain records is a public records request, and the price attached to that route is the subject of this reporting.

What Could Not Be Verified

  • The invoice itself. The invoice is not among the source material and is known only through the Tribune’s description. Whether “more than 14 years of work” is wording from the invoice or a conversion made by the reporter cannot be determined from that sentence alone.
  • The basis for converting hours into years. The assumptions about staffing and working hours under which about 153,000 hours (a calculation) become more than 14 years are not given in either article.
  • How $15 an hour relates to the cost rules. The Attorney General’s guidance pages do not state a labor rate. The text of the rules in the Texas Administrative Code and the Handbook offered as a PDF were not included in the source material. For the same reason, the conditions under which advance payment may be required, the threshold for sending an estimate, and any fee waiver provisions are not used in this post.
  • The amount for the narrowed request. The articles do not give a new estimate from the city for the narrowed request. The police post’s phrase that it is expected to be significantly less is all there is.
  • Whether a complaint was filed and how it was decided. The articles do not say whether the requester filed an overcharge complaint with the Attorney General’s office, or whether the ORD has made any determination on this estimate.
  • The decision on the DPS request to withhold. The articles do not report how the Attorney General’s office ruled on the DPS request.
  • The original text of the other reports and posts cited. The city police post, the city website, and the reporting by KPRC and Current Revolt are used only to the extent the Tribune relays them.
  • Comparison with the Korean system. A comparison with Korea’s fee system for information disclosure is not attempted here, because the statutory text was not checked.
