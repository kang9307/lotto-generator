How Ethereum Staking Actually Works and What You Can Lose

It is payment for work, not interest. Lock-up time, penalties, and counterparty risk arrive together

2026-08-01 Finance

Stated up front. This article is informational. It describes the structure and risks of Ethereum staking and does not recommend buying any asset or using any service. Crypto assets can lose principal, and the penalties and withdrawal delays described here can themselves be a cause of loss. All decisions and their outcomes are your own responsibility. The rules and tax section describes South Korea specifically and your jurisdiction will differ, so confirm tax matters directly with a tax professional and your tax authority.

Introduction: the costly assumption is that it grows while you do nothing

Most explanations of staking open with a percentage. The moment that number comes first, staking becomes a deposit account that pays interest in the reader's mind. Once that picture settles, everything else lands wrong, because staking has three things a deposit does not: time locked up, penalties, and the risk of whoever holds the assets for you.

This article does not start with a number. It starts with where the reward comes from. Knowing that one thing connects everything else into a single explanation: why rewards can shrink, why principal can be cut, and why exiting takes time. Without it, no percentage tells you what the payment is actually for.

Every fact here was checked at a primary source. Protocol behaviour comes from the official Ethereum documentation, and Korean rules and taxation come from the Financial Services Commission and the National Tax Service. Anything not confirmed is marked as needing verification, and the reward rate is described only as something that varies with no figure given. No service or product is named. What this article covers is not a product but a structure.

If crypto assets are new to you, the beginner guide to cryptocurrency investing is the better starting point, and wallet custody and inheritance are handled separately in if you decided to hold long term. This article does not repeat that material and stays on staking alone.

1. Where the reward comes from - payment for work, not interest

The source of deposit interest is unambiguous. A bank borrowed your money and pays you for it, and on the bank's books that is a debt. Interest accrues whether or not you do anything, because the only thing you did was hand the money over.

Staking differs precisely here. The Ethereum documentation describes rewards as given for actions that help the network reach consensus. That is payment for work. Three activities carry rewards.

  • Attestation. Checking the work of other validators and voting on time. Votes on the source checkpoint, the target checkpoint and the head block are each assessed
  • Block proposal. Batching transactions into a block in your assigned slot. Alongside protocol rewards this also produces a share arising from fees
  • Sync committee participation. Being assigned to a specific role for a period and performing it

Two consequences follow. First, the reward is not fixed. It moves with how many validators are participating, how often your proposal turn comes up, and the fee environment at the time. That is why no figure appears here. A fixed annual yield quoted anywhere is an observation from one moment, not a promise.

Second, and more importantly, having no counterparty who owes you also means having no counterparty who guarantees you. A deposit keeps its principal unless the bank fails, and even then a statutory scheme absorbs part of it. Staking has nobody in that position. The protocol both increases the balance and reduces it.

2. What a validator actually does - staying online is not the whole job

If the reward is payment for work, you have to know the work to know what happens when it is not done. What a validator owes is staying online continuously and casting correct votes on time. Simple in outline, with conditions attached.

  • Votes must go out on time. Late votes lose their value
  • Votes must point at the correct target. Pointing somewhere other than what the majority recognises means the vote is not counted
  • When a proposal turn arrives you must propose in that slot. Miss the moment and the turn is gone
  • All of this must run without interruption. Power cuts, link failures and restarts during upgrades all land here

The failure case is the crux. The documentation states that the penalties for missing the target and source votes are equal to the rewards the attestor would have received had they submitted them. Read that carefully. This is a deduction, not merely a forgone gain. A day offline does not cost you a day of earnings, it reduces your balance by a day's worth.

Not every failure is penalised, though. The same document notes there is no penalty for missing the head vote, and no penalty associated with inclusion delay. The penalty structure is graded.

On top of that sits a mechanism for when the whole network is struggling. The documentation explains that if the consensus layer has gone more than four epochs without finalizing, an emergency protocol called the inactivity leak is activated. In that state the stake of inactive validators is gradually reduced, the purpose being to create the conditions for the chain to recover finality.

Worth pausing here. The inactivity leak operates in a situation you did not cause. If the network is in trouble and you happen to be offline during that window, the reduction is yours. That is a category of risk hard to imagine in a deposit, and one nobody anticipates while thinking of staking as interest.

3. Slashing - the three ways assets get cut

If the penalties above are for not doing the work, slashing is for doing contradictory work. It differs in both scale and character. The Ethereum documentation enumerates exactly three slashable offences.

  1. Proposing and signing two different blocks for the same slot. Two answers offered for one position
  2. Attesting to a block that surrounds another one. The documentation describes this as effectively changing history
  3. Double voting by attesting to two candidates for the same block. Two votes cast on one question

What the three share is plain: each leaves mutually contradictory signatures on the network. And here comes the most practically important fact. These conditions are defined by outcome and do not ask about intent. Having meant no harm is not part of the test.

That is why running the same signing key in two places at once is dangerous. An old instance survives a migration, a spare is left running as a backup, or a previous process is not stopped during recovery. If both instances sign faithfully, the result matches the conditions above exactly. The paradox is that an attempt to improve uptime becomes the path to slashing.

3.1 The penalty arrives in two stages

Slashing losses are layered. Per the documentation, verified 2026-08-01, the structure is as follows.

  • Initial penalty. Applied immediately on detection. The document states that 0.0078125 ETH is immediately burned for a 32 ETH validator, scaled linearly with active balance
  • Correlation penalty. The document explains that at the mid-point, day 18, an additional penalty is applied whose magnitude scales with the total staked ether of all slashed validators in the 36 days prior
  • Ejection. Separately from the fines, a slashed validator is removed from the network

Reading the design intent of the correlation penalty reveals the shape of the risk. It deliberately treats an isolated mistake differently from many validators failing at once. Inverted, that means: concentration on the same software, the same operator and the same infrastructure raises the chance of simultaneous failure, and that is exactly when the fine grows. From an individual participant's seat this is an uncontrollable variable.

Which is why the final size of a slashing loss cannot be known in advance. The initial penalty is calculable; the correlation penalty is settled only by looking back over 36 days. That is why this article does not reduce slashing loss to a single percentage.

4. Minimum amount and entry methods - three routes, three risk profiles

The documentation states that you need 32 ETH to activate your own validator while adding that it is possible to stake less. That fork is where routes appear, and each route changes the type of risk. The critical point is that risk is not reduced, it is exchanged for a different kind.

4.1 Solo operation

  • Run your own validator with 32 ETH. You hold the signing key
  • Uptime, security, software updates and backups are entirely yours
  • Slashing risk arises from your own mistakes, which conversely means it is risk you can control
  • The documentation states that the gold standard for staking should always be individuals running validators on their own hardware whenever possible. That is the document's wording, not a recommendation from this article

4.2 Delegated service

  • The documentation describes this as depositing 32 ETH and uploading your signing keys to the operator
  • Hardware and uptime burden disappears
  • In exchange, your balance becomes tied to their operating quality. If they mishandle the key, the outcome is paid from your balance
  • The capital threshold remains unchanged

4.3 Pools and liquid tokens

  • The documentation calls this a collaborative approach to allow many with smaller amounts of ETH to obtain the 32 ETH required. Small amounts can participate
  • In many cases you receive a token that represents a claim on your staked ETH and the rewards it generates, in the documentation's words
  • That token can trade on markets and is sometimes used as collateral elsewhere
  • You do not hold the signing key. Funds are managed by smart contracts or mediated off-chain
MethodMinimumSigning keyRisk that growsBurden that shrinks

Delegated32 ETHUploaded to operatorBalance tied to their operating qualityHardware and uptime
Pool and liquid tokenSmall amountsYou do not hold itSmart contract · counterparty · price divergenceCapital threshold and operations

Read the table vertically and the rule appears. The less burden you carry, the more of the arrangement sits outside your control. Which is safer is not settled by this table alone. Solo operation is riskier for someone without the operational skill, and delegation is riskier for someone unable to evaluate a counterparty. The question to answer for yourself is which risk you can actually bear.

5. The time it takes to get in and out - not an instantly liquid asset

This is the sharpest divergence from a deposit. The documentation states that validator accounts are required to provide a withdrawal address before they can access and withdraw accrued network rewards, or process a full withdrawal. Then the decisive sentence: once an address is chosen and submitted to the consensus layer, this cannot be undone or changed again.

That single line carries the most weight in practice. An irreversible setting means one typo, one wrongly pasted address, is permanent. Discussions of staking risk usually reach for slashing and price first, but the simplest route to actually losing assets is a wrong address. This step happens only once, so spending time on that one occasion has the best cost-to-benefit ratio in the whole risk exercise.

5.1 There are two kinds of withdrawal

  • Partial withdrawal. Balance above a threshold is sent automatically and regularly to the withdrawal address. It proceeds without a separate request
  • Full withdrawal. Exiting staking entirely. The documentation describes submitting a transaction to withdraw, waiting for any withdrawal queue timeline based on network demand, and unlocking the full validator balance
  • The documentation also describes validator types where rewards compound into the effective balance up to 2048 ETH, with automatic sweeps applying only above that threshold

5.2 Capacity and your waiting time are different questions

The documentation gives concrete throughput figures: a maximum of 16 withdrawals can be processed in a single block, and at that rate 115,200 validator withdrawals can be processed per day. Its example table shows roughly 3.5 days to process 400,000 withdrawals and around 7 days for 800,000.

Do not misread those numbers. They are the ceiling of what the network can process, not the period you will wait. Your actual wait depends on how many people are standing ahead of you when you file, and the length of that line is produced by other participants' decisions. The moments when everyone wants out at once are generally the moments markets move fastest. In other words, the line is longest exactly when you most want to sell.

  • Staked assets do not have the character of money you may need on short notice
  • In a sharp decline, cutting losses is simply not an available option at that moment
  • Prices keep moving during the wait. Quantity may be preserved while value is not
  • The queue length right now is something to check yourself before starting. This article does not supply that value

6. The risk of handing it over - the stretch where it is yours but not yours

Because small amounts can participate, pooling is what most people actually meet. Yet what the documentation records about this route weighs more heavily on risk than on convenience. Transcribed directly:

  • Counterparty risk. The document says these solutions come with additional risk by delegating all node operations to a third-party
  • Concentration. It explains that such tokens tend to create cartel-like behaviors where a large amount of staked ETH ends up under the control of a few centralized organizations
  • Censorship. It follows that this concentration creates conditions for censorship or value extraction
  • Smart contract risk. The document notes the solutions involve either smart contracts managing funds or off-chain mediation. A defect in code becomes a defect in your position
  • Single point of failure. On centralised exchanges the document warns they create a large centralized target and point of failure, making the network more vulnerable to attack or bugs

6.1 Price divergence in liquid tokens

Get the nature of a liquid staking token exactly right. The documentation defines it as a token that represents a claim on your staked ETH and the rewards it generates. The word claim carries everything.

  • A claim is not the underlying asset itself. A promise of one-to-one redemption and one-to-one market trading are different statements
  • Selling the token on a market is not withdrawal, it is transferring the claim to someone else. You gain immediacy and receive whatever the market prices it at then
  • When everyone tries to leave at once, the queue lengthening and the claim price falling happen together. Both exits narrow for the same reason
  • If the token is posted as collateral elsewhere, another layer stacks on. Price divergence feeds straight into collateral valuation

This is the stretch where it is yours but not yours. On paper your share plainly exists. Yet you hold neither the signing key, nor execution rights, nor the power to choose when to exit. What you hold is a claim, and its value depends on whether the counterparty can keep its promise and on how the market currently views that.

8. Checklists for before, during and after

This turns the above into something usable. The three-stage split exists because staking risk wears a different face at entry, during operation, and on exit.

8.1 Check before starting

  • Does life still work if these funds sit locked in a queue
  • Have you understood that the reward rate varies rather than being fixed
  • Can you tell an offline penalty apart from slashing
  • Do you know the withdrawal address is irreversible, and have you reviewed it again
  • For delegated methods, is who holds the signing key written down
  • Where a smart contract is involved, have you read what that contract can do
  • Have you understood that a liquid token can trade away from the underlying asset
  • If a loss occurs, have you confirmed who you could ask for what
  • Have you checked the scope of local rules directly at the primary source
  • Did you size the amount assuming principal can be lost

8.2 Record while running

Records get used in two places later: filing and disputes. Assembling them at that point is too late.

  • Deposit date and quantity
  • Market price at deposit and the source used for it
  • Withdrawal address and registration date
  • Reward receipts, with date, quantity and price at the time
  • If you changed method, the date and the before and after
  • Service notices of terms changes and their content
  • Offline intervals and their causes
  • Fees and management deductions

8.3 Check when stopping

  • Did you record the exit request date and the queue state at that moment
  • Did you confirm that the final amount received can differ from the amount deposited
  • If there was a shortfall, did you record its cause by type
  • Did you confirm the funds actually arrived at the withdrawal address
  • Did you organise annual gain and loss data in a form usable for filing
  • Did you check whether any claim tokens remain
  • Did you close the service account and revoke access

9. Five common misreadings

  1. It is like deposit interest — no. Interest pays for lending money; a staking reward pays for work performed. Failing at the work does not merely reduce income, it cuts the balance
  2. The principal is safe — no. Principal can shrink from three directions: protocol penalties, price decline, and an incident at whoever holds it. These are independent, so they can happen at once
  3. You can withdraw any time — no. A withdrawal address must be registered first, and full withdrawal must pass through a queue whose length you do not set
  4. A liquid token means it is not locked — imprecise. Selling the token is not withdrawal but transfer of a claim. You gain immediacy and take the market price at that moment
  5. Small amounts mean small risk — the amount is smaller but the number of risk types grows. Small participation is usually a delegated structure, which adds counterparty and smart contract risk that solo operation does not have

Closing: the one thing this article says

Staking is payment for work, not interest. Everything else follows from that sentence. Failing at the work can cost you, the assets must stay in place while the work is done, and handing the work to someone else makes their risk your risk.

So the order of judgement has to flip too. Not how much can I receive, but what can I lose, comes first. If something remains after counting everything that can be lost, that is when a number becomes worth looking at. It is also why this article never quoted a reward rate.

# Ethereum Staking — Structure and Risk Checklist

## 0. One-line summary

Staking rewards are not interest. They are payment for the work of validating a network.
Fail at the work and rewards shrink; sign contradictory messages and the balance is cut; exiting takes time.

## 1. How it differs from a bank deposit

| Item | Deposit interest | Staking reward |
|---|---|---|
| Payer | A bank (a debt owed to you) | The protocol (not a debt) |
| Reason paid | You lent money | You contributed work to consensus |

| Early exit | Lose interest, principal is instant | Must pass through a queue |

## 2. Four things that reduce your balance

| Cause | When | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| Offline penalty | Missing source and target votes | Equal in size to the reward you would have earned |
| Inactivity leak | More than four epochs without finality | Inactive stake is gradually reduced |

| Slashing correlation penalty | Day 18 after detection | Scales with total slashed stake over the prior 36 days. Not predictable in advance |

Note: There is no penalty for missing the head vote, and none for inclusion delay itself.

## 3. The three slashing conditions (ethereum.org)

1. Proposing and signing two different blocks for the same slot
2. Attesting to a block that surrounds another one, effectively changing history
3. Double voting by attesting to two candidates for the same block

All three are defined by outcome. Intent is not part of the test.
Running the same signing key in two places at once can satisfy these conditions directly.

## 5. Withdrawal mechanics

- A withdrawal address must be registered before rewards or principal can be reached
- Once chosen and submitted to the consensus layer, that address cannot be undone or changed
- Partial withdrawal: excess balance is swept automatically to the withdrawal address
- Full withdrawal: exit transaction → queue → balance unlocked
- Throughput cap is 16 withdrawals per block, 115,200 validator withdrawals per day
- Your actual wait depends on the queue length at the moment you exit → check it yourself before starting
