From Recording to Searchable Text

Transcription is the start - the value comes from finding the passage you need in three seconds

Jul 30, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: the transcript exists, but nobody opens it

Record a two-hour meeting, feed it to a transcription tool, and you get one text file. The problem starts right there. Nobody has opened it. There are no paragraphs, no way to tell who spoke, and no sense of where you are as you scroll. So when someone asks next week what happened with the budget, people replay the recording from the beginning instead of searching the text. Where did the time spent transcribing go?

The starting point for this article is a tool post published on 29 July 2026 on GeekNews, a Korean developer community. The tool is called ToText and lives at totext.app. Opening the primary source directly showed a web service that takes uploaded audio and video files, produces an editable transcript with timestamps, recognises speakers, and generates summaries, chapters and action items. The supported formats listed are MP3, MP4, M4A, MOV, WAV and WebM; exports cover TXT, DOCX, PDF, SRT and VTT; three transcriptions are free without a credit card, and free accounts can evaluate the first 20 minutes of a file. On speaker labels the service itself states that quality depends on language, microphone placement, overlapping speech and recording clarity, that labels may differ across sections of a long file, and that you can edit them in the transcript.

The most important sentence, though, was not on the product page but in the single comment on the community post. It asked whether both the video and the recording are sent to the server. As of checking, no reply had been posted. That question is the subject of this article. The first thing people ask about a transcription tool is accuracy, but what actually governs the choice is where the file goes.

Two principles up front. This article does not recommend or rank any product. Product facts are drawn only from official pages and official repositories checked on 30 July 2026, and accuracy figures whose measurement conditions were not published were left out entirely. The service page did display an accuracy figure, but it did not publish what data was used or how it was measured. That is closer to an industry-wide habit than something specific to one product, which is why a section below covers how to measure with your own audio instead.

1. Transcription is the start - the value is in becoming searchable

When people discuss the performance of a transcription tool they mostly look at how many characters are correct. But the moment meeting notes or lecture notes actually become useful lies elsewhere: the moment the cost of returning to the passage you need drops.

Compare the same two-hour recording in two states. The first is twenty thousand characters of text with nothing but line breaks. The second is a document where every sentence carries a time, speakers are separated, and five lines of decisions sit at the top. Even assuming identical character accuracy, the two documents are not comparable in value. In the first you can search, but you cannot see the context around the hit, and confirming it means digging through two hours again. In the second you click the time on the line you found, replay eight minutes of the original, and you are done.

So the output of transcription is not text but a route of access. A block of text is closer to raw material with no route attached.

StatePossible actionCost to verifyWhat happens in practice
Recording onlySequential playbackVery highNobody listens again
Transcript with line breaks onlyString searchHighYou find it but cannot trust it, so you listen again
Transcript with timestampsSearch, then play that segmentLowThirty seconds of checking on the doubtful line
Speakers addedTrack by speakerLowYou can confirm who committed to what
Structured summary addedRead decisions, owners, deadlinesVery lowSomeone absent gets up to speed in three minutes
Index across filesSearch a whole periodVery lowYou can answer when a topic first came up

What grows as you move down the table is not character accuracy but the range of possible actions. Judging which row a tool takes you to is far faster than reading its marketing copy.

2. The four components of a searchable transcript

Moving down that table takes four components. They are independent, so each one you have helps by exactly that much, and each one missing costs exactly that much.

2.1 Timestamps - the route back

  • Segment level — a start and stop time on each sentence or utterance block. Sufficient for most purposes.
  • Word level — needed for subtitling and precise editing. This is where quality differences show when exporting to the SRT or VTT subtitle formats.
  • Linkage to the original — the real question is whether clicking a time actually plays that segment. A time printed as a bare number with no playback is only half the feature.
  • Why this matters most — the premise is that transcription will be wrong. Whether you can get back to the original when it is wrong determines how much the document can be trusted.

2.2 Speaker labels - who said it

  • In meeting notes, who made a decision is often more important than the decision itself.
  • Labels first arrive as anonymous numbers such as speaker 1 and speaker 2, and a human has to map them to real names.
  • In long files it is common for the same person to get different numbers in different sections. That is why editing matters.
  • Accuracy in this area is genuinely low, so section 5 treats it separately.

2.3 Structured summary - a form that gets read

  • Prose summaries get used less than you would expect. What actually gets used has fixed slots: decisions, action items, owners, deadlines and parked topics.
  • Always keep the summary together with the source. Keeping only the summary destroys the verification route.
  • Attaching the time of the supporting utterance to each summary item makes verification as cheap as a string search.
  • For the judgement criteria when handing summarisation to a language model, see how to actually use an AI assistant at work, which frames it as verification cost; it is not repeated here.

2.4 Index - search beyond a single file

  • The real demand is searching six months of meetings, not one meeting.
  • If the tool has no unified search, exporting transcripts as text into an internal wiki or document search is the better move.
  • That requires open export formats, which is why avoiding lock-in appears in the checklist later.
  • Simply fixing a filename convention around date and topic raises search success rates considerably.

A transcript with all four components looks roughly like the following. Whatever tool you use, judge it by whether the final artefact comes out in this shape.

[Meeting] 2026-07-30 Product weekly / 4 attendees / Consent: recorded in opening remarks

## Decisions
- Defer Q3 budget proposal | Owner: team lead | Due: 08-05 | Source: 00:41:12
- Split the test environment | Owner: dev group | Due: 08-14 | Source: 01:12:38

## Parked
- External audit schedule | Reason: owner absent | Source: 01:35:02

## Transcript
00:00:03 Speaker1(lead)  We are recording today. Purpose is the minutes, kept on the
                        internal wiki for three months and then deleted. Is that alright?
00:00:14 Speaker2(plan)  Yes, I agree.
00:00:16 Speaker3(dev)   Agreed.
00:41:12 Speaker1(lead)  Let us not submit the budget proposal this quarter.
                        NOTE: a figure was mentioned but transcription uncertain
                        -> replay original audio
01:12:38 Speaker3(dev)   Splitting the test environment is the right call.

Two things stand out. First, the recording consent is preserved as a record at the top. Second, a risky item such as an amount carries an uncertainty note and an instruction to re-listen. Those two lines are what prevent problems later.

3. Privacy governs the choice of tool

This is the centre of the article. The reason becomes obvious once you think about what a meeting recording actually contains.

  • Personnel matters — evaluations, transfers and resignation discussions about specific people, with names attached.
  • Contracts and pricing — counterparty names, amounts and terms that may qualify as trade secrets.
  • Personal circumstances — family situations, health and reasons for leave, slipping in as small talk.
  • Third-party speech — lecture and interview recordings contain the voices of people who did not decide to record.
  • The voice itself — even converted to text, if the original audio is retained alongside it, that audio is identifiable information in its own right.

Set against those contents, it becomes natural that where this file may be sent has to be settled before which tool is most accurate. And that fork is local processing versus cloud upload.

3.1 The local option genuinely exists

Checking the official repository of Whisper, the speech recognition model released by OpenAI, shows explicitly that the code and model weights are released under the MIT License. Running it requires Python, PyTorch and the command-line tool ffmpeg. In other words, a route to finish transcription on your own machine without uploading anything is open. The paper is titled Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision, and its abstract states that the models were trained on 680,000 hours of multilingual and multitask supervision and are often competitive with prior fully supervised results in a zero-shot setting without fine-tuning. It says that compared with humans the models approach their accuracy and robustness. Approach is worth reading carefully: it does not mean reach.

3.2 That does not make local always right

Recommending local processing unconditionally would be unbalanced. There are real costs.

DimensionLocal processingCloud service

In short: sensitivity starts the decision, operational capacity closes it. Uploading sensitive recordings to the cloud is risky, but so is an organisation with no capacity to maintain a local setup declaring local and ending up with everyone quietly using whatever free tool they found. The latter is more common.

3.3 There are two layers to check

  • The service's data handling — processing location, retention period, deletion method, whether files train models, access rights, sub-processors.
  • Your organisation's policy — the service may permit what the organisation forbids, and the organisation is usually stricter.
  • The legal basis for processing personal data — if the recording contains third-party information, handling it needs a basis. Make that call together with whoever owns data protection in your organisation.
  • Neither layer substitutes for the other — checking only one and moving on is the most common mistake.

For reference, the product page checked here explained that it uses encrypted connections and limits access to your account or an authorised share link, but processing location and retention period were not verifiable at the level of the introduction page. Not verifiable does not mean bad; it means this is an item to ask about before adoption. Whatever service you use, this check remains homework on the customer side.

4. The premise behind recording - tell people and get agreement

Discussions of transcription tools frequently skip the upstream question of whether recording is acceptable at all. Better tools mean more recording, so the order flips easily.

The legal conclusion varies with jurisdiction and individual circumstances, so this article does not assert one. The nature of the conversation, whether you are a participant, the intended use and the country all change the answer. If the situation could touch a dispute, disciplinary action or an investigation, confirm with a lawyer before recording. What this article can offer is not a legal judgement but practical principles that never cost you anything.

  • Announce before starting — state out loud that you are recording, the purpose, the retention period and who will have access.
  • Obtain agreement — ask every participant and wait for an answer. This differs from a formal notification.
  • Keep the consent inside the recording — placing the notice and the responses as the first utterances after recording begins writes them straight into the top of the transcript. That is where later evidence comes from.
  • If anyone objects, do not record — switch to written notes. The meeting running slightly slower is a cost worth accepting.
  • Do not use it for another purpose — consent given for minutes does not cover building performance-review material.
  • Fix retention and deletion in advance — an unspecified recording is effectively kept forever. Set separate deletion points for the original audio and the transcript.
  • Narrow access rights — check whether the share link is open to the whole organisation. Wide defaults are common.
  • This article does not cover recording someone without their knowledge — that direction is not explained here.

For settings with many participants and designated speakers, such as lectures and seminars, check the organiser's or speaker's policy first. Sharing a transcript from a lecture that prohibits distributing materials is a separate matter from recording consent.

5. Speaker diarization is a different problem from transcription

When a product page lists transcription and speaker recognition on the same line, it reads as though they work equally well. In reality they differ in nature and in difficulty.

Transcription turns what was said into characters. Diarization divides who spoke when into time segments. The official OpenAI Whisper repository does not mention diarization at all, which means you must attach a different tool to get it.

Checking the repository of pyannote.audio, the representative open-source toolkit in this area, shows a pipeline built from stacked neural components such as voice activity detection, speaker change detection, overlapped speech detection and speaker embedding. It is MIT licensed, and using the community pipeline requires accepting the model page conditions and obtaining an access token. Output arrives as a speaker number attached to a start and stop time. A graphics processor is optional but helps.

The difficulty shows in the metric the repository publishes. Diarization error rate is a lower-is-better measure, and the figures below are taken from that table, with pipeline generations running left to right.

Three things to read from the table. First, a double-digit error rate remains even in meeting conditions, including AMI with close-talking microphones. Second, it degrades sharply when the recording environment worsens: the distant microphone condition is always worse than the close one, and Ego4D, recorded on everyday wearable cameras, sits in another dimension entirely. Third, successive generations improve but the problem does not disappear.

The practical conclusions are therefore simple.

  • Speaker labels are a draft, not established fact — a human should pass over them before they reach the minutes.
  • Whether labels can be edited is the real criterion — a tool that will not let you fix labels is hard to use on long files.
  • Reducing overlapping speech is the most effective improvement available. Changing how the meeting is run is cheaper than changing the algorithm.
  • Microphones dominate performance — the gap between a laptop's built-in microphone in the middle of a room and individual microphones can exceed two rows of that table.
  • Tell the tool the number of participants if you can — removing the need to estimate speaker count removes that much room for error.

6. How to manage accuracy - transcription gets things wrong

Managing accuracy starts with designing the document on the assumption that it will be wrong. Not a method for never being wrong, but a method for making errors visible and reversible.

6.1 Model size is a trade-off

The model table in the official Whisper repository makes that trade explicit. The values below come from that table.

SizeParametersEnglish-only modelRequired VRAMRelative speed
tiny39 Mtiny.en~1 GB~10x
base74 Mbase.en~1 GB~7x
small244 Msmall.en~2 GB~4x
medium769 Mmedium.en~5 GB~2x
large1550 MNot available~10 GB1x
turbo809 MNot available~6 GB~8x

The repository notes that the English-only models tend to perform better for English work, and that the difference is especially large at the tiny and base sizes. For work in other languages the English-only variants do not apply, so you use the multilingual models and should account for losing more at the smaller sizes. Running it looks like this.

# Basic usage (local run, the file never leaves the device)
whisper <input-file> --model medium --language Korean

# For subtitles: export SRT carrying segment times
whisper meeting.m4a --model medium --language Korean --output_format srt

# Fix spelling by telling the model the proper nouns in advance
whisper meeting.m4a --model medium --language Korean \
  --initial_prompt "Meeting minutes. Terms: BrainDetox, data lake, on-premises"

# Prerequisites: Python / PyTorch / ffmpeg  (per the official repository)

6.2 Proper-noun dictionary and post-editing

  • Build the dictionary first — collect the correct spelling of company names, product names, internal abbreviations and attendee names in one file.
  • Tell the model in advance — feeding the terms through an initial prompt, as above, tends to stabilise spelling.
  • Then apply bulk replacement — record frequent misspellings as pairs in the dictionary and swap them in one pass after transcription.
  • Log the replacement rules reversibly — if you do not record what was changed into what, later verification against the source becomes impossible.
  • The dictionary grows with every meeting — adding new terms on the spot is the cheapest way to maintain it.

6.3 Do not trust the transcript for risky items

  • Amounts, quantities, percentages — the most important information in a meeting is the most error-prone. Make replaying the original and cross-checking documents a rule.
  • Dates and deadlines — numbers blur easily in connected speech. Always re-confirm at the moment you move them into a calendar.
  • Names and titles — these connect to responsibility, so errors are expensive.
  • Negations — a flip between will and will not is rare but fatal. Attach the source utterance to every decision.
  • Set a notation rule — simply marking low-confidence stretches as uncertain changes how much the document can be trusted.

6.4 Measuring it yourself is the fastest route

An accuracy figure on a product page is not a basis for comparison unless the measurement conditions and dataset are published with it. The same number can describe a single speaker in a quiet studio or many speakers in a meeting room, which are completely different tasks. That is why this article cites no unverified accuracy figures. Instead, here is a self-measurement procedure that takes thirty minutes.

  • Pick three of your own recordings — one good condition, one ordinary, one bad. Three minutes each is enough.
  • Write the ground truth by hand — transcribe just those three minutes yourself. Without this baseline no comparison holds.
  • Feed the same files to each candidate — the trick is not changing the conditions.
  • Decide what you count — proper nouns and numbers matter more in practice than overall character accuracy. Counting only those two is enough to decide.
  • Count speaker labels separately — good transcription with tangled labels does not reduce the time spent writing minutes.
  • Record the results — tools change quietly. Re-measuring the same files in six months shows you the drift.

7. Where transcription actually struggles

The hard spots in transcribing a language such as Korean are fairly fixed. What follows concerns where errors appear rather than any numbers. No published language-specific benchmark figure was confirmed while writing this, so no number is given.

  • Proper nouns — company and product names have several plausible spellings. When one sound maps to two or three written forms, the tool simply picks one.
  • Numbers and units — large-number words, percentages versus points, and date expressions mix and produce errors. Precisely the most important part of a meeting.
  • Mixed English — technical meetings drop English terms mid-sentence. If automatic language detection wavers sentence by sentence, spelling loses consistency.
  • Jargon and internal abbreviations — words absent from training data turn into similar-sounding ordinary words. This is where a dictionary earns its keep.
  • Sentence endings and boundaries — in languages where politeness levels change the verb ending, mixed registers and trailing-off speech blur sentence boundaries, which drags summary quality down with them.
  • Speech rate and overlap — fast speech and simultaneous talking make transcription and diarization hard at the same time.
  • Recording environment — air-conditioning noise, laptop fans and room reverberation affect the result more than model choice does.

The response lies on the process side, not the algorithm side. Put microphones near speakers, reduce overlapping speech, and after saying an important number out loud, write it once more on screen or in chat. Those three habits visibly improve results with any tool.

9. Questions to ask before choosing a tool

Instead of endorsing a product, here is a list of questions. If you can fill in answers, you can judge any tool; where an answer will not come, that is the tool's risk point.

9.1 Data handling

  • Where is uploaded audio and video processed? On the device or on a server?
  • How long are the original file and the transcript each retained? The two can differ.
  • If you request deletion, what is erased and by when? Does it include backups?
  • Are your files used to train models? Is that setting on or off by default?
  • Are there sub-processors besides the provider? Is the list published?
  • In which region is data stored?
  • What is the default visibility of a share link? Can anyone with the link see everything?
  • If you delete the account, are the uploaded files deleted too?

9.2 Output and verification

  • Are timestamps segment level or word level?
  • Can you click a time in the transcript and hear that segment immediately?
  • Can a human correct speaker labels? Does one correction propagate through the file?
  • Do the export formats include something machine-readable? Subtitle formats and structured formats let you move to another tool.
  • If an accuracy figure is advertised, are the measurement conditions published with it?
  • Can you test with your own audio first? What are the limits of the trial?
  • Can you fix proper nouns through a term dictionary or an initial prompt?
  • If there is a summary feature, does it give the time of the supporting utterance?

9.3 Operations and cost

  • Is billing per minute, per file or by subscription?
  • What are the file length and size limits? Will a three-hour seminar fit?
  • Is your language listed explicitly among supported languages, rather than relying on automatic detection alone?
  • When several people use it, is there role separation?
  • To use it in an organisation, what approval process applies, and are the documents for it available?
  • If you switch tools, can you carry existing transcripts across?

Conclusion: choose a pipeline, not a tool

Comparing transcription tools usually starts with accuracy and ends with accuracy. Yet the reason meeting notes never accumulate is not that characters are wrong. It is that you cannot do anything with the text that was produced. So what needs checking is not whether it transcribes, but whether timestamps take you back to the original, whether speakers can be corrected, whether the summary separates decisions from owners, and whether many files can be searched at once.

Two things come before all of that. One is whether this is a setting where recording is acceptable; the other is where this file may be sent. The first begins with telling participants and obtaining agreement. The second begins with checking two layers, the service's data handling and your organisation's policy. Comparing accuracy before those two are settled is doing things out of order.

The technology itself is already good enough to be useful. A model trained on 680,000 hours is published under the MIT License, so the option of finishing transcription on your own device exists, and diarization can be bolted on from separate open source. But as the diarization error rate table shows, this is not the stage at which human checking can be removed. So rather than aiming at automation without people, aim at reducing the points a human must check and making those points visible. That is what it means to say a transcript with uncertainty markers beats a smooth one.

One last thing. Spending thirty minutes measuring three of your own recordings almost always gives a more accurate answer than spending days choosing a tool. One good condition, one ordinary, one bad. Count only proper nouns and numbers. That result will tell you more about your situation than any table in this article.
