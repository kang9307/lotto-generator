Our Workplace's LLM Collective Delusion - The Paradox of Rising AI Budgets and Shrinking Core Operations

When everyone funds AI and no one asks if it works

Jun 13, 2026 Tech

Introduction: The Strange Scene in a Budget Sheet

When I first saw our company's budget this year, I stared at the screen for a long while. The AI-related line items were up almost everywhere. New LLM platform licenses, AI consulting, an "AI transformation task force," data pipeline modernization. Yet lower in the same sheet, the core operations that had quietly held up our service for years were getting struck through one by one. Monitoring renewals deferred, infrastructure hiring frozen, aging hardware replacement postponed. No one said it out loud, but the message was clear: "There is money for AI, but no money to keep operations running."

This is not an attack on any particular company. What I experienced is just one instance of a pattern playing out simultaneously across many organizations in 2026. I call it the "LLM collective delusion": a state in which a group of smart, rational people somehow, collectively, overvalues AI's impact without verification while devaluing the proven worth of the core operations they are cutting. In this article I will calmly dissect what the delusion really is, why the paradox happens, what the actual data says, and how both organizations and practitioners can distinguish healthy adoption from performative adoption.

1. What the "LLM Collective Delusion" Actually Is

Let me be precise about the term. The "LLM collective delusion" points not to individual irrationality but to a distortion at the organizational level. Here is the core. Ask each member privately and most are cautious: "Honestly, I am not sure AI delivers as much as we say." Yet gather them in a meeting room, and no one voices that doubt.

Why? Because the moment you voice AI skepticism, you risk being filed under "behind the times" or "resistant to change." Push AI aggressively, on the other hand, and you earn a reputation as "forward-looking" and "innovative." So every private doubt is buried in silence, and only enthusiasm is amplified in public. That is the mechanism by which the delusion self-replicates at the group level.

A collective delusion is not built from lies. When telling the truth is costly and riding the enthusiasm is rewarded, the mere accumulation of silence is enough to build it.

Economics has a concept called the "information cascade": you observe the choices of those ahead of you, suspend your own judgment, and follow. The same thing happens in AI adoption. A competitor announces an "AI strategy" and now we feel we must too; the team next door adopts an LLM tool and ours cannot be the only one left out. Each actor believes they are being rational, but the basis of that rationality is merely "everyone else is doing it," not verified impact.

2. The Paradox in the Flesh: Rising Budgets, Shrinking Operations

The most concrete symptom of the delusion is the budget paradox. On one side AI spending explodes; on the other, core operations are cut. The two flows are not coincidental but causally linked, because budgets are finite, and funding new AI means cutting something somewhere.

Industry analyses suggest enterprise AI spending grows by double digits in 2026 while overall IT budget growth lags far behind. In other words, much of the money going into AI is not "new money" but money moved over from existing line items. So what gets cut first? The answer is brutally obvious: whatever no one notices when it works, namely core operations.

A typical budget reallocation pattern (structural illustration)

[Rising side]                         [Cut side]
 LLM platform licenses   <-- funds --   infrastructure hiring
 AI consulting / PoC     <-- funds --   monitoring / observability
 "AI transformation" team <-- funds --  aging hardware replacement
 generative AI marketing <-- funds --   tech-debt paydown / docs
        |                                    |
        v                                    v
   high visibility                      low visibility
   (signals to stock & reputation)     (invisible when it works)

The key word here is "visibility." AI investment shines in quarterly earnings calls, investor meetings, and press releases. A stable database, well-built monitoring, and adequate operations staff are, by contrast, invisible right up until an incident hits. Invisible value always loses budget negotiations. So the most proven asset gets cut first. This paradox arises from the asymmetry of visibility alone, independent of whether AI's impact is real.

3. Why It Happens (1) Signaling and Stock Price

So why would a rational leadership cut proven operations to fund unproven AI? The first reason is signaling. AI spending is not merely a cost; it is a signal sent to the market.

In practice, when public companies announce an "AI strategy," the stock often reacts. For executives, AI investment is the easiest way to tell investors "we are a company preparing for the future." The message "we invest in stable operations," by contrast, is not glamorous. No one expects it to lift the share price.

A cruel asymmetry compounds this. Invest boldly in AI and fail, and you can plead "we were trying to get ahead of the curve"; skip AI and fall behind, and you cannot escape the charge "why were you sitting on your hands?" To borrow the phrasing of one big-tech executive, the market is in a "FOMO arms race" where the cost of underinvesting is framed as larger than the cost of overinvesting. In this setup, the rational executive choice is to fund AI regardless of whether its impact has been verified.

4. Why It Happens (2) FOMO and Fashion

The second reason is more human: fear of missing out, and fashion. Since 2023, AI has become not just a technology but a social trend. Conferences, LinkedIn, and industry newsletters fill up with stories of "what we transformed with AI."

In that environment, a decision-maker faces two pressures at once. From above, the board and investors ask "what is our AI strategy?" From below, employees ask "why are we the only ones not getting the latest tools?" The fastest way to quiet both pressures is to spend budget and launch a visible AI project. Whether that project actually creates value is a question for much later.

Fashion changes the question. Not "do we need this?" but "why are we not doing it yet?" In front of the second question, prudence looks like laziness.

The problem is the time pressure fashion creates. Because AI is wrapped in the language of "experimentation" and "innovation," judgments about value are quietly delegated to technical teams. Success is defined in technical terms, and no senior executive wants to be the one who says "let us stop this project." The result is predictable: AI projects launch without a clear theory of value, without explicit trade-offs, and without credible kill criteria. For how the narrative of AI replacing employees becomes an alibi for management failure, I went deeper in the bad management behind "AI is replacing employees".

5. What the Data Says (1) MIT's 95% Pilot Failure

Now let us look at data rather than vibes. MIT's 2025 State of AI in Business report (often called the "GenAI Divide" report) pours cold water on the delusion. Drawing on 52 executive interviews, surveys of 153 leaders, and analysis of 300 public AI deployments, it reaches a striking conclusion.

  • About 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots delivered no measurable P&L impact - despite some $30-40 billion in investment.
  • Only about 5% of pilots achieved rapid revenue acceleration; the vast majority stalled or had negligible effect.
  • The core barrier was not model quality but the organizational learning gap. The problem, in other words, was the organization, not the technology.

A particularly notable point is the misalignment of investment. According to the report, AI budgets skewed overwhelmingly toward sales and marketing rather than the higher-ROI operations and finance functions. Money went where things look flashiest, while the back-office automation that actually produces returns was neglected. This is the data-level evidence of the very "visibility bias" we saw earlier.

6. What the Data Says (2) The Inability to Measure ROI

Another core symptom of the delusion is the absence of ROI verification. Multiple surveys point the same way.

  • In one survey, only about 29% of executives said they could confidently measure AI ROI. So seven in ten cannot properly measure the impact of the money they spent.
  • Another CEO-focused study found only about 25% of AI initiatives delivered the expected ROI, and just 16% had scaled enterprise-wide.
  • Deloitte's analysis calls this the "paradox of AI ROI": investment keeps rising while returns stay elusive.

The message of these numbers is clear. Most organizations spend on AI without knowing whether that money creates value. And yet budgets keep climbing. Because it is not measured, failure is not visible; because failure is not visible, there is no reason to stop. The delusion feeds on the absence of verification.

Investment that is not measured is not verified. Investment that is not verified is never recorded as failure even when it fails. So only the operating cost beside it, the one that is measured, gets cut.

7. What the Data Says (3) The Shadow AI Paradox

The most ironic data concerns shadow AI. It lays bare the disconnect between official adoption and actual use.

Surveys show that workers at more than 90% of companies use personal AI tools like ChatGPT or Claude for daily work, while only about 40% of companies have an official LLM subscription. In one IDC survey, 56% of employees used unauthorized AI tools, while only 23% used the tools their organization provides and governs.

The point where this disconnect meets the delusion is biting. At the top, large sums go to expensive enterprise AI platforms, while the real value is quietly produced at the bottom by employees using cheap personal tools. Much of the "AI transformation success" an organization boasts about likely came not from the official budget but from shadow AI. The delusion mistakes expensive official adoption for results, while leaving the genuinely value-creating informal usage saddled with only the risks of security and governance. This problem of cost control and visibility also connects to the case of Uber's $1,500 monthly AI tool cap.

8. The Real Danger: Erosion of Core Operations

Now the most important question. Why is this delusion dangerous? Not merely because it wastes money, but because it erodes proven core operations.

Core operations get cut precisely because of their defining trait: they are invisible when they work. But the bill for erosion is delayed. Cut monitoring and nothing happens now, but detection lags at the next incident. Freeze infrastructure hiring and you cope now, but accumulated tech debt erupts all at once someday. Postpone hardware replacement and you save now, but a sudden hardware failure returns as a larger cost.

  • Weakened detection and recovery: Cutting observability creates systems that are "fine in normal times but collapse in a crisis."
  • Leakage of operational knowledge: Cutting core operations staff lets undocumented tacit knowledge walk out the door with them.
  • Compounding tech debt: Deferring debt paydown slows every future bit of development. Ironically, the debt eats the very productivity gains AI was supposed to deliver.
  • Damaged trust: The absence of automation erodes confidence in AI too, reinforcing a vicious cycle of deployment dysfunction.

The most painful paradox is this. To maximize AI's impact you need solid data infrastructure, stable operations, and adequate observability. Yet the delusion cuts exactly that foundation to fund AI. It is a self-destructive structure in which enthusiasm for AI tears down the very base required to use AI well.

9. Healthy Adoption vs. Performative Adoption

That said, not every AI investment is delusion. The key is to separate healthy adoption from performative adoption. The two diverge not in outcome but in process and posture.

DimensionHealthy adoptionPerformative adoption
Starting pointA concrete problem or bottleneckThe imperative "we must do AI"
Definition of successMeasurable business metricsA technical demo / the fact of adoption
Kill criteriaDefined in advanceNone (no one to call a halt)
Budget sourceNew funds or explicit trade-offQuietly cut from core operations
ROI verificationSpend tied to and tracked against outputUnverified, "feels better"
Target areaHigh-ROI back-office firstHigh-visibility showcase first

Healthy adoption starts small, measures, and stops if there is no effect. Performative adoption starts big, announces, and never asks about effect. Map this table onto your own organization and you can diagnose fairly quickly which side your current AI projects fall on.

10. How Organizations Can Tell the Difference

So how should an organization break the delusion? The key is not "blocking enthusiasm" but "mandating verification."

  1. Force a theory of value: Require every AI project to document, up front, "which metric will this improve, and by how much?" Do not approve projects without a hypothesis.
  2. Set kill criteria in advance: Before starting, nail down "under these conditions we stop." A project you cannot stop should not be started.
  3. Ring-fence core operations budget: Set a defense line so AI investment cannot invade operations, infrastructure, and observability budgets.
  4. Bring shadow AI into the light: Understand which cheap tools employees actually create value with, and acknowledge that reality before buying an expensive platform.
  5. Protect the skeptics: Build the psychological safety so that asking "do we really need this AI project?" carries no penalty. The delusion feeds on silence, so encouraging the question is itself the most powerful antidote.

In short, a healthy organization does not reject AI. It simply holds AI to the same standard of verification as every other investment. Ending the special treatment is the first step out of the delusion.

11. How Practitioners Can Tell the Difference

What if you are a practitioner, not an executive? When the delusion sweeps the company, there are still things an individual can do.

  • Translate into the language of value: Frame AI skepticism as "opposition" and you get cast as a Luddite. Instead, speak in value and trade-off terms: "with the budget for this AI we could keep monitoring and cut the cost of the next incident."
  • Collect small evidence: A concrete data point - "I measured this task and the effect was this much" - breaks the delusion far better than the blanket claim "AI does not work."
  • Surface shadow AI: If you create real value with a cheap tool, make that visible and help shatter the illusion that "only expensive adoption is a result."
  • Make core operations visible: Turn the value of operations that are invisible because they work into metrics. Calculate "the economic value of zero downtime last quarter," and operations stops losing budget negotiations.

At the individual level, the ability to genuinely use AI tools well is itself an asset. What matters is proving that ability through "verified productivity" rather than "showmanship." 12. Conclusion + 3 Things You Can Apply Today

The LLM collective delusion is not a claim that AI is bad. Quite the opposite. Precisely because AI can deliver real value, a delusion that blindly trusts that value without verification is dangerous. The paradox of rising AI budgets and shrinking core operations is not a problem of technology but of organizational verification failure. MIT's 95% pilot failure, the 70% who cannot measure ROI, and the shadow AI disconnect all point to the same truth: we spend on AI without asking whether it works.

The answer is not "stop using AI" but "use AI while verifying it." And the first target of that verification should not be AI, but the true value of the core operations we are cutting. Let me close with three things you can apply today.

  1. Distrust any AI project without a theory of value. Ask your current AI projects "which metric will you improve, and by how much?" If the answer is vague, that vagueness is the signal of delusion.
  2. Draw a defense line around core operations budget. Separate AI investment so it cannot invade monitoring, infrastructure, and operations staff. AI built by cutting the foundation collapses with the foundation.
  3. Protect the people who ask questions. The delusion feeds on silence. Only organizations where asking "do we really need this?" is safe ever escape it.
