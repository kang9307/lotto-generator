Can We Design a Perfect World? Pinker, VanderWeele, and the Debate Over Human Flourishing

A dormant MIT conversation resurfaces in 2026 - on the reality of progress, the trap of utopian design, and how to define human flourishing in the age of AI

2026-07-03 Trends & Culture

Introduction: Why a 2023 MIT Conversation Resurfaced in 2026

In July 2026, a video link began circulating again on GeekNews (news.hada.io). The title: "Can We Engineer a Perfect World?" It's a conversation between Harvard cognitive psychologist Steven Pinker and Harvard epidemiologist Tyler VanderWeele, director of Harvard's Human Flourishing Program. The talk actually took place on March 2, 2023, hosted by the Veritas Forum at MIT's Wong Auditorium and moderated by MIT Media Lab's Rosalind Picard. Why is a nearly three-year-old recording being passed around again? The reason is straightforward: in 2026, with AI woven deep into daily life, the question of what we should even call "progress" has become more urgent than it was back then.

This piece is not a recap of that conversation. It lays out each thinker's position, grounded in their own published work, and then builds on top of it to reflect on a question of my own - is progress real while designing a perfect world is a form of hubris? Rather than declaring a winner, the focus here is on how the two perspectives complement each other.

1. What Each Side Actually Argues

1.1 Pinker: Progress Is Data

Pinker's argument is consistent across Enlightenment Now and its follow-up, Rationality. He defines progress as "improvement in human flourishing" and tries to demonstrate that improvement through long-run data across eight strands: life, health, prosperity, peace, freedom, safety, knowledge, and happiness. His core claim is that when people actually deploy reason, science, and humanism to solve problems, measurable gains in wellbeing follow. He insists this is an observation, not an act of optimism.

1.2 VanderWeele: Flourishing Is Multidimensional

VanderWeele starts from a different place. In a 2017 paper published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS), he defines human flourishing as "a state in which all aspects of a person's life are good," and proposes a Flourishing Measure built on five domains: happiness and life satisfaction, physical and mental health, meaning and purpose, character and virtue, and close social relationships. These five were selected because each is nearly universally desired and can constitute an end in itself. In VanderWeele's framework, a single metric like GDP or life expectancy captures only a slice of flourishing, never the whole.

2. Progress Is Real - But It Is Not the Same as Perfect Design

2.1 The Line Pinker Draws Himself

What's interesting is that Pinker himself draws a line between progress and perfection. He warns against the common mistake of treating Enlightenment reason as a claim that humans are fully rational beings, arguing instead that progress is not a matter of reaching perfection but of accumulating increments. In other words, Pinker's own answer to "can we design a perfect world?" is closer to "no, and that was never the point."

2.2 What the Data Doesn't Tell You

That doesn't mean Pinker's progress narrative is beyond dispute. Historian David Bell has argued his portrayal of the Enlightenment is overly monolithic, while historian David Wootton has pointed out that a graph trending upward doesn't by itself prove the Enlightenment caused that trend. Other critics raise a methodological concern that every graph in the book seems to point in the same convenient direction, along with a charge that Pinker is comparatively unconcerned about problems, such as inequality, that hide behind averages. My own read is that these critiques don't undermine the reality of progress itself, but they correctly identify that which progress, measured by whose standard, and attributed to which cause is not a question data alone can settle.

3. The Historical Danger of Engineering Utopia - Popper's Warning

3.1 Holistic Engineering vs. Piecemeal Engineering

The philosopher who confronted the idea of "designing a perfect world" most directly was Karl Popper. In his 1945 book The Open Society and Its Enemies, he split social reform into two categories: utopian, or holistic, engineering, which tries to overhaul an entire society to fit a single ideal blueprint, and piecemeal engineering, which improves existing institutions through small, reversible experiments.

3.2 Where "Perfect" Slides Into Violence

Popper's core insight is this: as long as there is no rational way to agree on what the perfect ideal actually is, disagreements among would-be utopian engineers can only be resolved by power rather than reason. Read alongside the lessons of the 20th century's various totalitarian experiments, this warning stops sounding like abstract philosophy and starts reading as a practical principle - the scale of a design is the scale of its risk. The more total and irreversible a design is, the more irreversible the cost of its failure becomes.

4. How Do You Measure Flourishing - The Tension Between Happiness, Meaning, and Freedom

4.1 There Is No Single Metric

Look again at VanderWeele's five domains and it becomes clear they don't line up neatly with each other. Momentary happiness and long-term meaning often demand different choices. Commitment to a close relationship can conflict with individual freedom or personal achievement. It's more accurate to read VanderWeele's measure not as a "finished answer" but as a tool for surfacing trade-offs between different, genuine goods.

4.2 Why the Single-Metric Temptation Is Dangerous

This is exactly where the fundamental problem with "designing a perfect world" shows up. Design requires a single objective function to optimize, but happiness, meaning, freedom, relationships, and virtue behave more like vectors with different optimal points. Maximizing one inevitably sacrifices another. The very act of treating flourishing as multidimensional already implies the conclusion that perfect design cannot hold together.

5. Why the AI Era Forces Us to Redefine Flourishing

5.1 The Gap Between Convenience and Flourishing

As of 2026, AI has worked its way into information-seeking, decision-making, and even emotional conversation. It's no accident that institutions such as Harvard's Human Flourishing Program have recently launched dedicated research initiatives linking AI and flourishing. The core problem is that AI reliably improves narrow metrics like convenience and productivity, but that improvement doesn't automatically translate into gains across all five of VanderWeele's domains.

5.2 Three Stress Tests: Relationships, Meaning, Agency

Three specific tensions stand out. First, when conversations with AI substitute for relationships with other people, one axis of flourishing - close social relationships - risks eroding. Second, the more AI takes over judgment and creative work, the more the sense of achievement and meaning people derive from their own effort may shrink. Third, the more recommendation and automation systems optimize decisions on our behalf, the more quietly personal agency, or freedom, can be thinned out. All three sit precisely in the blind spot of the measurable improvements Pinker emphasizes. Convenience is easy to measure; the depth of a relationship or the meaning of a life is not.

6. My Own Read - The Balance Point Is a Condition, Not a Design

Placing the two positions side by side, my conclusion is that this looks less like a genuine clash and more like two lenses trained on the same problem. Pinker defends a factual claim - that the progress visible in long-run data is real - while VanderWeele refines a normative claim - what that progress should actually be measured against. Both can be right at once. What matters is that when it comes to the question the conversation's title actually asks - can we design a perfect world - both thinkers land close to "no," for different reasons. Pinker talks about increments, not perfection; VanderWeele argues flourishing can't be reduced to a single objective function.

Add Popper's warning on top of that, and a practical conclusion emerges. What should be designed is not a perfect final state, but the conditions for running reversible experiments repeatedly. That applies equally to policy, product design, and AI system deployment. Resisting the temptation to optimize everything at once in favor of small, measured, reversible steps fits the multidimensional nature of human flourishing far better.

Conclusion: Stop Designing Perfection, Cultivate the Conditions for Flourishing

As of July 2026, part of why this conversation is being passed around again is the question AI keeps forcing to the surface. The smarter the systems we design, the harder it becomes to dodge the question of what we're actually optimizing for. This piece's conclusion is straightforward. Progress is a real phenomenon with no good reason to deny it, but the moment it gets stretched into a claim about "designing a perfect world," it falls into two traps: flattening multidimensional flourishing into a single metric, and, as Popper warned, imposing an unagreed-upon ideal through power rather than reason. Whether you're an individual, an organization, or a team shipping AI systems, the practical message this old conversation leaves for us in 2026 is to keep repeating small, reversible improvements and keep re-asking the balance among happiness, health, meaning, relationships, and freedom - rather than chasing a perfect blueprint.
