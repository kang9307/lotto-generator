Frozen Shoulder (Adhesive Capsulitis) Complete Guide — Symptoms, Stages, Treatment & Exercises

Everything you need to know: causes, risk factors, 3 stages, self-assessment, treatment options, and home recovery exercises

2026-06-28 Health

Introduction: When Your Shoulder Literally Freezes

One morning you reach back to fasten a seatbelt and a sharp pain stops you cold. A few weeks later, raising your arm above shoulder height feels impossible, and sleeping on the affected side wakes you at 3 a.m. This experience is far more common than most people realize: adhesive capsulitis — popularly known as frozen shoulder — affects roughly 2 to 5 percent of adults at some point in their lives.

The name "frozen shoulder" describes the condition precisely: the joint capsule surrounding the shoulder gradually thickens, tightens, and adheres to surrounding tissue, restricting movement in every direction. As of June 2026, this guide draws on established guidance from the NHS, Mayo Clinic, the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS), and peer-reviewed research to give you an accurate, evidence-informed overview of the condition.

Important: This is general health information, not a substitute for professional medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. If you have persistent shoulder pain or stiffness, please consult an orthopedic specialist.

1. What Is Frozen Shoulder?

Frozen shoulder is a condition in which the joint capsule — the fibrous tissue envelope surrounding the ball-and-socket shoulder joint — becomes chronically inflamed, thickened, and scarred. As the capsule tightens, it forms adhesions (bands of scar tissue) that bind the shoulder joint and dramatically reduce its range of motion.

In a healthy shoulder, the joint capsule is loose and pliable, allowing the arm to move freely in all directions. In frozen shoulder, the capsule becomes so contracted that it acts like a vice, limiting forward flexion, abduction, and rotation — sometimes to a fraction of normal range.

Primary (idiopathic) frozen shoulder develops without any obvious preceding injury or cause. Secondary frozen shoulder develops after a specific event — shoulder surgery, fracture, stroke, or prolonged arm immobilization — that limits shoulder movement for an extended period.

2. Causes and Risk Factors

The exact cause of frozen shoulder remains incompletely understood, but several risk factors are well established.

2.1 Diabetes — the Strongest Known Risk Factor

Diabetes (particularly type 2) is by far the most strongly linked systemic condition. Studies show that approximately 10–20% of people with diabetes develop frozen shoulder, compared to 2–5% of the general population — a 3 to 5-fold higher risk. People with diabetes tend to experience more severe symptoms, longer duration, and slower response to treatment. Well-controlled blood sugar is consistently associated with faster recovery.

2.2 Thyroid Disorders

Both hypothyroidism (underactive thyroid) and hyperthyroidism (overactive thyroid) are associated with increased risk of frozen shoulder. Thyroid hormones play a role in connective tissue metabolism, which may explain this link.

2.3 Age and Sex

Frozen shoulder most commonly occurs in adults between 40 and 60 years of age, and women are affected slightly more often than men. Hormonal changes around perimenopause may increase susceptibility of the joint capsule to inflammation.

2.4 Prolonged Immobilization

Any condition that keeps the shoulder still for an extended period can trigger secondary frozen shoulder. This includes recovery from rotator cuff surgery, shoulder fractures, strokes that cause arm weakness, or even prolonged use of a sling after wrist or forearm injury.

2.5 Other Associated Factors

  • Parkinson's disease
  • Cardiovascular disease history
  • Previous frozen shoulder (the opposite shoulder can be affected in up to 17% of cases)
  • Prior shoulder injury or surgery
  • Prolonged sedentary lifestyle

3. The 3 Stages of Frozen Shoulder

Frozen shoulder typically progresses through three overlapping stages. The total duration varies considerably — commonly 1 to 3 years — but some people recover faster and others more slowly.

StageNameKey FeaturesTypical Duration
1FreezingProgressive pain; range of motion begins to decrease6 weeks – 9 months
2FrozenPain may ease slightly; stiffness is at its worst4 – 12 months
3ThawingRange of motion gradually returns; pain continues to improve5 months – 2 years

3.1 Stage 1 — Freezing

Pain develops gradually and intensifies over weeks to months. It is often sharp and stabbing during specific movements (reaching behind, lifting overhead) and a dull, aching pain at rest. Night pain is particularly disruptive, making it hard to sleep on the affected side. Range of motion begins to decrease alongside the worsening pain. Forcing movement in this stage can aggravate inflammation; gentle range-of-motion exercises within a pain-free zone and targeted anti-inflammatory treatment are appropriate.

3.2 Stage 2 — Frozen

Pain may stabilize or decrease slightly compared to Stage 1, but the shoulder is at its stiffest. Movement is restricted in all directions — forward, sideways, and rotational — and even a helper cannot move the arm through normal range. Daily tasks such as dressing, reaching for a seatbelt, or placing items on a shelf become difficult or impossible. Consistent physical therapy is critical during this stage to prevent the capsule from becoming more densely adherent.

3.3 Stage 3 — Thawing

Range of motion gradually returns. The shoulder slowly loosens, and pain continues to diminish. Strengthening exercises become more important as range improves. The recovery in this stage can be slow, sometimes stretching over months to years. A subset of patients does not recover full range of motion even after several years without appropriate treatment.

4. Symptoms and Self-Assessment

The two hallmark features of frozen shoulder are pain and reduced range of motion in all directions of shoulder movement.

4.1 Typical Symptoms

  • Deep, aching pain in the shoulder and upper arm (often around the deltoid muscle)
  • Night pain — worsened when lying on the affected shoulder
  • Difficulty or inability to raise the arm forward, to the side, or behind the back
  • Trouble with everyday activities: fastening a bra, reaching into a back pocket, combing hair, putting on a jacket
  • Stiffness that restricts movement even when someone else tries to move your arm (passive range of motion is also limited)

4.2 Differentiating Frozen Shoulder from Rotator Cuff Injury

Shoulder pain has many causes, and frozen shoulder is frequently confused with rotator cuff tears or tendinopathy. The single most useful distinguishing feature is passive range of motion.

FeatureFrozen ShoulderRotator Cuff Tear
Passive range of motionRestricted — even with assistanceRelatively preserved — assistant can move arm more freely
Pain characterConstant ache, worsens at nightSharp during specific movements (lifting, reaching)
OnsetGradual, insidiousOften sudden or after an injury/overuse

Clicking / poppingUncommonMay be present

Accurate differentiation requires a physical examination and imaging (X-ray, ultrasound, or MRI). Do not self-diagnose — see an orthopedic specialist if shoulder pain persists for more than a few weeks.

5. Treatment Options — A Stage-Based Approach

Treatment of frozen shoulder is tailored to the stage and severity of the condition. Non-surgical management is the first-line approach, with surgery reserved for refractory cases.

5.1 Physical Therapy and Stretching

Shoulder stretching and range-of-motion exercises are the cornerstone of frozen shoulder management. The key principle is to stretch within the limits of tolerable discomfort — overly aggressive stretching can worsen inflammation, particularly in Stage 1. A physical therapist can design an individualized program and add modalities such as heat therapy, ultrasound, or manual therapy to improve tissue flexibility and reduce pain.

5.2 Medications (NSAIDs)

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen or naproxen can provide short-term pain relief and reduce inflammation. They are typically most helpful in the early freezing stage. Long-term NSAID use carries gastrointestinal and cardiovascular risks; follow your doctor's guidance on dosage and duration.

5.3 Corticosteroid Injections

Injecting corticosteroids directly into the shoulder joint (intra-articular injection) provides meaningful short-term pain relief and can facilitate participation in physical therapy. Evidence supports significant benefit in the freezing and early frozen stages. The number of injections and intervals are determined by your physician; repeated high-dose injections carry risks including tendon weakening and cartilage damage. Patients with diabetes should monitor blood glucose closely after injection, as temporary elevation is common.

5.4 Hydrodilatation

Also called distension arthrography, this procedure involves injecting saline (with or without corticosteroid) into the joint capsule under image guidance to physically expand the contracted capsule. It is a minimally invasive option that may help when standard injections and physiotherapy have not produced sufficient improvement.

5.5 Manipulation Under Anesthesia (MUA)

Under general or regional anesthesia, the clinician manually moves the shoulder through its full range of motion to break up adhesions. This technique has largely been replaced by arthroscopic capsular release but is still performed in some settings.

5.6 Arthroscopic Capsular Release (Surgery)

Surgery is considered when 6 to 12 months of intensive non-surgical treatment produces inadequate improvement. A small camera (arthroscope) is inserted into the shoulder, and the thickened, contracted portions of the joint capsule are surgically released. Post-operative physical therapy is essential to maintain and build on the improved range of motion achieved during surgery.

6. Home Recovery Exercises

The following exercises are commonly recommended for frozen shoulder recovery. Warm the shoulder with a heating pad for 10–15 minutes before exercising. Stop any exercise that causes significant pain, and confirm your specific exercise plan with a qualified physical therapist or your treating physician.

6.1 Pendulum Swings

Lean forward with your healthy arm resting on a table for support. Let the affected arm hang freely. Using gentle body momentum (not shoulder muscle effort), swing the arm in small circles, then forward and back, then side to side. This gravity-assisted stretch gently distracts the joint and can be started even in Stage 1. Perform 1–2 minutes, 2–3 times per day.

6.2 External Rotation Stretch

Stand facing a door frame. Bend the elbow to 90 degrees with the palm facing forward. Place the forearm gently against the door frame, then slowly rotate your body away from the door until you feel a gentle stretch across the front of the shoulder. Hold for 20–30 seconds, then relax. Repeat 3–5 times.

6.3 Towel Internal Rotation Stretch

Hold a towel behind your back with both hands — healthy hand above, affected hand below. Gently pull upward with the healthy hand to stretch the affected shoulder into internal rotation. Hold where you feel a comfortable stretch for 20–30 seconds. Repeat 3–5 times.

6.4 Finger Wall Walk

Stand facing a wall. Walk the fingers of the affected arm slowly up the wall as high as comfortable. Hold for 10–15 seconds at the point of mild stretch, then slowly lower. Repeat facing the wall (forward flexion) and sideways to the wall (abduction) to work different directions of movement.

6.5 Crossbody Stretch

Use your healthy arm to gently pull the affected arm across your chest, applying gentle pressure just above the elbow. Hold for 20–30 seconds, feeling a stretch in the back of the shoulder. Relax and repeat 3–5 times.

7. Prognosis and Recovery Timeline

The prognosis for frozen shoulder is generally good: the condition resolves in most patients, though the timeline is slow. Studies suggest that within 1 to 3 years, the majority of patients experience significant symptom improvement. However, some degree of residual stiffness may persist in a subset of patients even after several years, particularly when treatment was delayed.

Factors associated with faster recovery include:

  • Early diagnosis and prompt start of physical therapy
  • Well-controlled blood glucose (for patients with diabetes)
  • Consistent adherence to home exercise programs
  • Continuing to use the shoulder within a pain-free range (avoiding complete rest)
  • Appropriate medical management of corticosteroid injections when needed

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Can I speed up recovery by exercising aggressively?

Not necessarily. In Stage 1 (freezing), forced or aggressive stretching can increase inflammation and worsen pain. Gentle, pain-threshold stretching is more effective. In Stage 3 (thawing), more progressive strengthening becomes appropriate. Always work within the guidance of your physical therapist.

Q2. Heat or ice for frozen shoulder?

In the acute pain phase, ice packs (15–20 minutes) can help reduce inflammation. Before stretching or exercise, applying heat (heating pad, warm shower) for 10–15 minutes loosens the joint and may improve stretch effectiveness. Individual responses vary; discuss with your therapist what works best for you.

Q3. Will frozen shoulder come back?

The same shoulder rarely develops a second episode of frozen shoulder, but the opposite shoulder can be affected in up to 17% of patients. Managing underlying risk factors — especially diabetes and thyroid disorders — may help reduce the chance of recurrence in the other shoulder.

Q4. Can I continue to exercise and stay active?

Yes, with appropriate modifications. Overhead or impactful shoulder activities (racket sports, swimming butterfly stroke, overhead pressing) should be avoided during painful stages. Lower-body exercise, walking, and other cardio that does not stress the shoulder can typically continue.
