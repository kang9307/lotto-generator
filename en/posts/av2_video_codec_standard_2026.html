AV2 Video Codec Standard v1.0 Released 2026 - What the Next Open Codec Means

AV2 Is Here - How the 2026 Open Video Codec Improves on AV1 and Why It Matters

Jun 2, 2026 Tech

Introduction: In the Age of Video, Codecs Are the Hidden Infrastructure

Right now, more than half of all internet traffic is video. Every time you open Netflix, swipe through YouTube, join a Zoom call, or scroll Instagram Reels, one invisible technology sits underneath it all: the video codec. A codec compresses video down to a transmittable size and then decompresses it on the receiving end so it can be drawn on screen. If a codec is even 1% more efficient, the world's data centers and networks save enormous amounts of power and bandwidth cost. Codecs are invisible, but they are the real infrastructure of the video era.

In June 2026, a new generation of that infrastructure arrived. The Alliance for Open Media (AOMedia) officially released the AV2 video codec standard v1.0. AV2 is the successor to AV1 and a direct, royalty-free challenger to the paid MPEG-family codecs such as HEVC and VVC. Alongside it, the AV2 decoder project dav2d has emerged, quickly filling the gap where a standard exists but a usable implementation does not.

This article explains what AV2 actually is, how exactly it improves on AV1, why open codecs matter so much, and what all of this means for streaming, OTT, and the broader video ecosystem. Even if "codec" is an unfamiliar word, that is fine. By the end, you will understand what is happening behind the scenes the next time a 4K video plays more smoothly on a slower connection.

1. What Is AV2 - An Overview of AOMedia Video 2

AV2 stands for AOMedia Video 2, the successor to AV1, which was standardized in 2018. It is developed by AOMedia, a non-profit consortium that includes nearly every heavyweight in video, semiconductors, and platforms: Google, Amazon, Netflix, Meta, Microsoft, Apple, Samsung, Intel, ARM, and NVIDIA. That membership alone tells you AV2 is not an experimental side project but a strategic product aimed at becoming an industry standard.

AV2 has three core goals. First, higher compression efficiency than AV1 (same quality, less data). Second, the same royalty-free policy AV1 established. Third, broad coverage from high-quality content such as 4K, 8K, and HDR all the way to low-latency scenarios such as video conferencing and game streaming. In other words, AV2 aims to be "one codec for every video scenario, for free."

An easy misconception to clear up: AV2 does not instantly replace AV1. Even AV1 does not yet have hardware decoders on every device. AV2 is a long-term bet on the next five to ten years of video infrastructure, and the v1.0 release is the starting gun for that long journey.

2. Codec Basics - What Compression Actually Does

To appreciate AV2's improvements, you first need to understand what a codec does at all. Here are the essentials in plain language.

Raw video is enormous. One second of uncompressed 4K video can be around 1.5 GB. A two-hour film would exceed 10 TB without compression. Sending that over the internet as-is is impossible. A codec compresses this data to a tiny fraction of its size while making the loss nearly invisible to the human eye.

Compression works through three main mechanisms:

  • Spatial redundancy (intra): Within a single frame, areas of similar color or pattern are collapsed. A wide blue sky does not store every pixel individually.
  • Temporal redundancy (inter): Across consecutive frames, parts that barely change are not re-stored; they are simply marked as "same as before." This is where the biggest savings come from.
  • Perceptual lossy compression: Detail in areas the eye is insensitive to (fine texture in fast-moving regions, for example) is discarded aggressively. The art of a codec is throwing away only what the eye will not notice.

Each codec generation does these three things more cleverly: it splits frames into smaller, more flexible blocks, predicts motion more precisely, and tries more prediction modes. The price is that encoding computation explodes. That is exactly why AV2 compresses better than AV1, and also why AV2 encoding is heavier.

3. Five Ways AV2 Improves on AV1

AOMedia's stated improvements for AV2 fall into five buckets. Here are the essentials in terms readers can actually feel.

3.1 Compression - About 30% Less Data at the Same Quality

This is the headline. AV2 can transmit video at roughly 30% lower bitrate than AV1 for the same quality. That translates directly into 30% lower bandwidth costs for streamers, or higher quality at the same file size. For users watching on mobile data, it means lower data usage.

3.2 Quality - Finer Block Partitioning and Prediction

AV2 divides frames into more flexible units and adds more prediction modes, preserving more detail and color gradation at a given bitrate. In particular, banding in dark scenes and smearing in complex textures are reduced.

3.3 Encoding - Designed With Parallelism in Mind

AV1 was notorious for heavy encoding. AV2 pushes compression even further while designing its tools with parallel processing and hardware acceleration in mind from the start. The absolute compute cost is still high, but there is more room for chipsets and encoders to optimize.

3.4 HDR and Wide Color - Stronger High-Quality Support

As 4K and 8K HDR content grows, AV2 strengthens tools for handling wide color gamuts and high dynamic range efficiently. This touches directly on the quality race among premium OTT services.

3.5 Low Latency - Conferencing and Game Streaming

AV2 refines low-latency modes for time-sensitive scenarios. It targets video conferencing, cloud gaming, and live streaming, where compression must happen fast and with minimal delay.

4. AV1 vs HEVC vs VVC vs AV2 - The Comparison Table

To see the codec landscape at a glance, here is a single table. Compression is shown relative to H.264/AVC as the baseline (100%); actual figures vary by content and settings.

This table has two key axes. The visible axis is compression (better as you go down). The hidden axis is licensing. The MPEG family (HEVC, VVC) compresses well but is held back by patent and royalty burdens. The AOMedia family (AV1, AV2) compresses well and is free. AV2 sits at the strongest position of all: best compression and zero license cost. Its one remaining weakness is hardware availability.

5. Why Open Codecs Matter - Royalties and Ecosystem

More important than the technical numbers is the licensing structure, because the codec wars were decided less by compression and more by royalties.

The reason HEVC, despite excellent compression, never spread as fast as AV1 is clear. HEVC's patents are fragmented across multiple pools (MPEG LA, HEVC Advance, and others), so providers could not even tell whom to pay or how much. As streaming traffic grew, royalties snowballed. For large platforms, that was not just a cost but an unpredictable risk.

So Google, Netflix, and Amazon joined forces to create AOMedia and ship the royalty-free AV1, and AV2 is the second generation of that strategy. Here is what open codecs really mean:

  • Cost predictability: Even if traffic grows 100x, codec licensing cost stays at zero. The codec never becomes a brake on business expansion.
  • Open-source implementations: Decoders like dav1d and dav2d and encoders like SVT-AV1 are open source, so anyone can improve and port them.
  • Default browser support: With no royalties, Chrome, Firefox, and Edge can support these codecs by default. That is the decisive engine of web video adoption.
  • Freedom from standard lock-in: The industry is not held hostage by a specific group of patent holders.

In my view, AV2's real weapon is not the 30% compression but the zero royalty. Compression gaps close over time, and MPEG will catch up eventually. But the structural advantage of "free" cannot be bought.

6. AV2 Adoption Outlook - Who Uses It, and When

A released standard does not mean everyone uses AV2 tomorrow. Codec rollout is staged. Here is how I see the adoption path unfolding.

6.1 Stage 1 - Software Decoding by Large Streamers

The first movers are YouTube and Netflix. They have the strongest motivation to cut bandwidth costs and control their own encoding pipelines, so they do not wait for hardware. Early on, the likely pattern is applying AV2 via software decoding to a slice of traffic on high-end devices to save data. AV1 began on exactly this path.

6.2 Stage 2 - Browser and OS Support

Once Chrome, Firefox, and Edge add AV2 software decoding, web video follows quickly. With no royalties, the adoption barrier for the browser camp is low. The maturity of open-source decoders like dav2d sets the pace of this stage.

6.3 Stage 3 - Hardware Decoders in Chipsets

True mass adoption begins when hardware decoders land in chipsets. Once phone SoCs (Qualcomm, Samsung, Apple), TV SoCs, and GPUs (NVIDIA, AMD, Intel) include AV2 decoders, devices can play 4K AV2 without draining the battery. This stage typically takes two to four years after a standard ships. That puts 2028 to 2030 as the likely inflection point for AV2's mainstream arrival.

7. Impact on Developers and Content Creators

Codec standards look abstract, but for practitioners they translate into concrete decisions.

  • Streaming developers: There is no need to switch to AV2 right now. But if you abstract the codec in your encoding pipeline, adding AV2 later becomes a one-line config change. Multi-codec adaptive bitrate (ABR) architecture becomes more important.
  • Content creators: Keep masters in H.264/H.265 for now, but preserve the highest possible source (master) quality. When you re-encode to a future codec, the source quality sets the ceiling.
  • Product and service planners: The key variable is whether your target users' devices support AV2 hardware decoding. Services with many high-end devices benefit from early adoption; those with many older devices need a fallback codec strategy.

The core principle is designing without lock-in to a single codec. Codecs change every five to ten years, so a system that can swap codecs out is the mark of good video architecture.

8. The Decoder Ecosystem - dav2d and Beyond

A codec specification on its own plays no video. You need actual decoder and encoder software that implements the standard before a codec works. Here, the key piece of the AV2 ecosystem is dav2d.

dav2d is the successor to the AV1 decoder dav1d, a high-performance open-source AV2 decoder project led by the VideoLAN (makers of VLC) and FFmpeg communities. Why dav1d was decisive for AV1 explains why dav2d matters. dav1d used hand-optimized assembly to push software decoding speed high enough to make AV1 playback practical even on devices without hardware decoders. If dav2d does the same for AV2, AV2 can spread without waiting for hardware.

The encoder side is similar. Just as SVT-AV1, libaom, and rav1e competed to push AV1 encoding speed, reference and production encoders for AV2 will mature quickly. You can treat the maturity of the decoder/encoder ecosystem as the speed of AV2 adoption itself. The standard is just the start; the real race plays out in implementation.

9. Risks and Limitations - Hardware Lag and Encoding Cost

Viewing AV2 through rose-tinted glasses would be a mistake. Here are the limits and risks I see.

  • The hardware-decoding gap: This is the biggest practical barrier. Software-decoding 4K AV2 on devices without a hardware decoder taxes the battery, generates heat, and loads the CPU. Chipset rollout takes years.
  • Encoding cost: The price of higher compression is heavier encoding compute. Encoding large content libraries to AV2 requires significant compute spend, and live encoding is even trickier.
  • Transition-era complexity: A multi-codec era of running H.264, H.265, AV1, and AV2 at once will persist for a while, raising operational complexity and storage cost.
  • Patent-dispute risk: Royalty-free does not mean zero patent-dispute risk. Even AV1 saw some patent holders assert separate pools. Some legal uncertainty remains.

In short, AV2 is technically excellent, but adoption is a fight over time and hardware. The standard's release is a starting line, not a finish line.

10. Conclusion + Three Things to Know Right Now

The AV2 v1.0 release signals a generational change in video infrastructure. My one-line summary: AV2 is about 30% more efficient than AV1, still free, and therefore likely to become the standard eventually - but the timing will be decided by hardware.

Here are three things worth knowing now, whether you are a layperson or a practitioner:

  1. AV2 = 30% over AV1 + royalty-free. The point is not the compression but the structural advantage of being free. That is the decisive weapon HEVC and VVC lack.
  2. Mainstream arrival is 2028-2030. We are currently in the software-decoding stage for large streamers and browsers. The real start comes when hardware decoders ship in chipsets.
  3. Design for multi-codec. Do not lock into a single codec; build for swap-ability. Watch the maturity of the decoder ecosystem, including dav2d.

Codecs are invisible, yet they decide the quality, speed, and cost of every video we watch. AV2 has opened the next chapter of that invisible infrastructure. You do not need to change anything today, but if video is part of how you make a living, keep this shift at your back as you move forward.
