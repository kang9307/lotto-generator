Ulcerative Colitis Flares - What Shows Up First When Remission Starts to Break

Why stool frequency, blood and nocturnal bowel movements are the early signals, where the emergency line sits, and what a bowel diary is actually used for

September 1, 2026 Health & Wellness

People who have lived with ulcerative colitis for a few years learn something before they learn the name of the disease. It is the sense that a gut which was fine yesterday has started moving differently. This illness does not divide into cured and not cured. Quiet stretches and worse stretches take turns.

The National Health Information Portal run by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency describes the course as a chronic one of alternating remission and relapse, and gives cumulative relapse rates of 30.2 percent at one year, 72.0 percent at five years and 88.4 percent at ten years. Over a decade, not having another episode is the rarer outcome. So the useful knowledge is not in what the disease is. It is in what shows up first when a quiet stretch starts to wobble, and in which of those things can wait for the next appointment and which mean going to the emergency department today.

This article is general information. Diagnosis and treatment follow the judgement of the clinician who examines you. Nothing written here was built to serve as a basis for self-diagnosis or for grading your own disease severity. If your symptoms have changed, do not decide alone; get assessed.

The structure of swinging between remission and flare

Remission is the state where symptoms have settled and ordinary life has come back. A flare is that state breaking again. The fact that these two words alternate explains almost everything about the character of the disease. A flare is not an accident or a failure; it is an event contained within the normal course of this illness. Accepting that first shortens the time lost when one arrives.

Here is the scale in one country. The National Health Information Portal reports roughly 37,000 patients with ulcerative colitis in 2019 and a prevalence of 65.7 per 100,000 population, and, citing Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service statistics, about 59,000 patients in 2023. Onset is most common in the twenties. The same document also states that no difference in average life expectancy is known between people with ulcerative colitis and healthy people. That sentence is not consolation; it sets the goal of management. The goal is not survival but reducing how often and how deeply flares come.

There is a point where things regularly go wrong. Having no symptoms is not the same as having no inflammation. The 2025 update to the American College of Gastroenterology guideline does not stop at symptom resolution but looks through to endoscopic improvement. It separates the state where symptoms alone have gone from the state where the mucosa has also healed, and treats achieving both as the better outcome. Without that distinction, people put the tablets down the moment they feel comfortable, and that becomes the trigger that recurs throughout the sections below.

Where it parts from Crohn's disease - confined to the colon, and continuous

Both sit under inflammatory bowel disease, but ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease occupy different territory. The National Health Information Portal defines ulcerative colitis as a disease in which inflammation or ulceration arises in the bowel wall, confined to the colon, and notes that the pathological change begins in the rectum next to the anus and progresses inward. Then comes the decisive sentence: the pathological change is not scattered here and there but is all connected.

Crohn's disease is the opposite. It can involve anywhere from mouth to anus, and its lesions scatter, skipping healthy segments. This difference is not trivia; it is a management issue. Whether involvement stops at the rectum or covers the whole colon changes what gets examined during a flare, and it changes the start point and interval of the surveillance colonoscopy discussed further down. Knowing the extent of your own disease is among the most practical things a patient can bring to an appointment.

There is another condition that is easy to confuse. Judged only on the impression of severe abdominal pain and distension, it overlaps with bowel obstruction, but that is a physical blockage of the bowel while this is inflammation of the colonic mucosa. All they share is that either can be an emergency.

What shows up first when a flare begins

The three symptoms the National Health Information Portal names as most common in ulcerative colitis are rectal bleeding, diarrhoea and stool urgency. It adds that tenesmus, mucus in the stool, nocturnal diarrhoea and cramping lower abdominal pain can appear, and that in more severe cases loss of appetite, fever and weight loss come with them.

What a flare actually looks like at the start is not this whole list switching on together. It usually arrives as a small drift away from your own baseline. Someone who normally goes twice a day goes four times, mucus that was not there appears, and there is no longer any margin on the way to the bathroom. The gap from your own usual value is more useful than any absolute number compared with other people.

Why nocturnal bowel movements and blood carry more weight

Two items on that list get handled differently from the rest. There are reasons.

Nocturnal bowel movements - diarrhoea that wakes you

Bowel motility normally slows during sleep. So diarrhoea heavy enough to wake someone is hard to explain by functional causes alone. That is the background to nocturnal diarrhoea having long been treated as an alarm feature pointing towards an organic cause. The National Health Information Portal lists nocturnal diarrhoea separately as well. Daytime stool counts wobble with meals and schedules, but the night has fewer of those confounders, which raises its value as a signal.

Rectal bleeding - one of the two axes patients report themselves

In the tools that measure disease activity, the items patients report themselves have long compressed to two: stool frequency and rectal bleeding. The 2025 update to the American College of Gastroenterology guideline likewise places bleeding, bowel habits and urgency in its first domain, patient-reported outcomes. Blood is privileged not because it is always bad but because it is one of the rare items a patient can count themselves that also connects to the state of the mucosa.

That does not make blood proof of a flare. Haemorrhoids and fissures produce the same appearance, and there are flares that progress without visible blood. The right use of this item is not as a verdict on its own but as a reason to bring the appointment forward.

Situations that mean the emergency department without delay

If any of the following apply, do not wait for the next appointment. Go to the emergency department now.

  • Severe abdominal pain with visible distension of the abdomen — particularly when tenderness worsens and the abdomen becomes rigid
  • Fever above 38 degrees Celsius
  • A pulse above 120 beats per minute, or severe dizziness and fainting on standing up
  • Diarrhoea that stops while the person becomes systemically worse — easy to read as improvement, which makes it one of the most dangerous signals
  • Massive bleeding, bleeding that fills the toilet bowl red, going pale with cold sweats
  • Vomiting that will not stop, or clouding consciousness

What this combination points to is toxic megacolon and perforation. In both, time weighs heavily on the outcome, so let the judgement happen in the emergency department and travel first.

The background is worth a paragraph. The StatPearls entry on NCBI Bookshelf, run under the National Institutes of Health, gives the diagnostic criteria for toxic megacolon as colonic dilation beyond 6 cm on imaging together with systemic findings. The systemic findings are fever above 38 degrees Celsius, a pulse above 120 beats per minute, neutrophilic leukocytosis and anaemia, of which at least three must be present, with one of altered sensorium, hypotension, hypovolemia or electrolyte abnormality added on top. The same document gives mortality in inflammatory bowel disease as 0 to 2 percent and, when perforation is present, 40 percent or more. That gap is why this is handled as an emergency.

The same document's list of precipitants carries one practical warning. Antimotility agents, opiates and anticholinergics are on it. That means reaching for whatever antidiarrhoeal is at hand because the diarrhoea is bad can be dangerous. Hypokalaemia, Clostridioides difficile infection, and colonoscopy or bowel preparation in an inflamed colon are also listed. What to take and what to postpone during a flare is safer not decided alone.

What severity is actually graded on

The frame guidelines use to grade severity is simpler than expected. There are three axes: stool frequency, rectal bleeding and systemic state. The first two are counted by the patient; the last is measured. The classic Truelove and Witts criteria classify disease as acute severe when there are six or more bloody stools a day together with at least one systemic finding. Those systemic findings include fever, tachycardia, anaemia and raised inflammatory markers.

AxisWhat goes in itWho establishes it
Stool frequencyHow many times a day, and whether blood is mixed inThe patient can count it, which is why a record becomes data
Degree of bleedingMixed into the stool, or blood passed aloneThe patient can observe it, but the source is separated in clinic

Inflammatory burdenEndoscopic findings, fecal calprotectin, blood inflammatory markersEstablished only by testing
Disease courseAdmission history, how often steroids were neededThe clinical record is the source

The 2025 update to the American College of Gastroenterology guideline organises this structure into four domains: patient-reported outcomes, inflammatory burden, disease course, and impact on quality of life. What is worth noticing is that no single box decides severity. Stool frequency can be high while systemic indicators stay clean, and the reverse happens too.

So the conclusion of this section is about posture rather than numbers. The bottom three rows of that table are boxes you cannot fill in alone. Grading your own severity from the top two rows is not the use the guidelines intended, and using it to rate yourself as mild and postpone an appointment produces the worst outcome. The point of knowing the frame is knowing what to say in the consulting room.

Reported triggers - what is established and what is folklore

This disease attracts an unusual amount of talk about triggers, and much of it has hardened into apparent fact through repetition. Here they are sorted by the level of evidence.

Reported triggerLevel of evidenceWhat has actually been established
Stopping maintenance therapy on one's ownHandled in guidelinesThe second Korean guideline states that 60 percent of untreated patients in remission had a relapse
Clostridioides difficile infectionHandled in guidelinesA 2025 meta-analysis reported a pooled prevalence of 8.84 percent (5.91 to 13.03) in inflammatory bowel disease. Asia was higher at 11 percent
Other infectious colitisWidely accepted clinicallySymptoms overlap with a flare, so it must be distinguished. Guidelines put excluding infection into the pathway when disease worsens
Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugsWeaker evidence than the folkloreA 2018 meta-analysis reported a relative risk of exacerbation of 1.52 (0.87 to 2.63), which was not statistically significant
Change in smoking status, including quittingWidely repeated but not supported by evidenceIn a study of 6,754 UK adults, flare risk did not differ between former smokers and never-smokers, with an odds ratio of 1.07 (0.92 to 1.25)
StressNo settled conclusionA 2020 review pooling 19 prospective cohorts found no significant association between depression, anxiety or perceived stress and symptom exacerbation

The last three rows deserve unpacking. On non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, that meta-analysis explicitly stated that, contrary to generally accepted belief, it found no consistent association. That does not mean any painkiller can be taken freely. Heterogeneity between the studies was substantial, and the choice of analgesic is tangled up with each person's other conditions, so this part is settled by asking the clinician who treats you.

Smoking is the item whose direction is most often reversed in retelling. The observation that smokers develop this disease less often is old, but there is no confirmed evidence that quitting worsens the disease in someone already diagnosed. In that same study the hazard ratio for hospitalisation was 0.92 (0.72 to 1.18) and for colectomy 0.78 (0.50 to 1.21) — no difference. These data give no reason to postpone quitting because of the disease. Stress is the item people feel most strongly, but feeling is not proof of causation. The review above judged that four of the seven Bradford Hill criteria were met, while still describing its conclusion as inconclusive. Honestly stated, this is closer to not known.

Stopping maintenance therapy on your own

This is the one item in that table with clear evidence that also sits within the patient's control. The second Korean guideline from the IBD Study Group of the Korean Association for the Study of Intestinal Diseases states that among ulcerative colitis patients in remission, 60 percent of those who were not treated had a relapse.

Stopping on one's own is not unusual either. A 2025 systematic review with meta-analysis reported a mean non-adherence rate to oral mesalamine of 32.3 percent. The distribution within it is interesting: 46.6 percent in observational studies against 19.6 percent in clinical trials. That reads as the size of the difference between an environment where someone is watching and one where nobody is.

The reasons for stopping are fairly fixed too. The body feels fine, taking something daily is a nuisance, and there is nothing visible getting better. All three are understandable, and all three rest on the premise that no symptoms equals no inflammation. As noted above, that premise does not hold.

  • Maintenance therapy is not taken to remove today's symptoms but to lower the probability of the next flare. Seeing no visible effect is normal
  • If side effects or the burden of taking it make continuing hard, the real option is not stopping but saying so in clinic
  • Steroids are used to settle a flare and are not a drug to continue as maintenance. The 2025 update states this explicitly
  • If you missed a few days, a record beats self-reproach. When and how much was missed becomes material for interpreting symptom change
  • Reducing or stopping medication is not itself off the table. It is simply a decision made in clinic with test results alongside, not from symptoms

The question each test answers

The tests ordered when a flare is suspected answer different questions. Knowing which says what makes the results sheet less bewildering.

TestThe question it answersIts limit
Fecal calprotectinIs there actually inflammation in the colon? It lets symptoms and inflammation be looked at separatelyNot perfect. A 2023 meta-analysis reported sensitivity 0.720 and specificity 0.740 at a cutoff of 152 micrograms per gram
Blood inflammatory markers and haemoglobinIs inflammation reaching the whole body? This is the systemic axis of severityThere are flares where markers do not rise. It cannot rule out a flare on its own
Stool studies and difficile testingIs this worsening a flare or an infection?Both can be present at once. Finding one does not exclude the other
ColonoscopyWhat state is the mucosa actually in, and how far does involvement reach?During severe inflammation the procedure itself can be a risk factor, so timing needs judgement

Of these, fecal calprotectin is the test whose standing has risen in recent guidance. The 2025 update to the American College of Gastroenterology guideline recommends it strongly for assessing treatment response, evaluating suspected flares, and monitoring during remission. Being non-invasive and repeatable counts for a lot. But as those performance figures show, it is not a test that settles anything on one result. Rather than being reassured by a low number or certain of a high one, comparing against your own previous values is the more realistic use.

Diet - remission and flare ask for different things

Few areas carry so much information on so little evidence. Let us be clear first. There is no confirmed strong evidence that any particular diet cures this disease. Dietary research is genuinely active, but the firmer evidence on inducing remission is concentrated on the Crohn's disease side. That does not make diet meaningless. What it asks for simply differs by phase.

  • During a flare, symptom relief is often the goal. Using a low-residue diet briefly is not treatment but a temporary measure for getting through symptoms
  • In remission the goal changes. Loosening restrictions favours nutrition, and unnecessary restriction leaves cost without benefit
  • The most common mistake is carrying a flare-time restricted diet into remission. Run for months or years, deficiencies accumulate
  • Prolonged restriction can make deficiencies of iron, vitamin D, folate and vitamin B12 a problem. This is an area to establish by testing, not by guesswork
  • Personal experience that a specific food made things worse is worth recording. Just do not expand it into a general rule. Varying by person is the default here
  • Do not use dietary change as a substitute for medication. Trading one for the other walks straight into the trigger described in the earlier section

In short, diet is a means of handling symptoms and protecting nutrition, not a confirmed means of removing inflammation. Whether tightening restrictions or loosening them, if it is going to run long, going through a clinical and dietetic consultation is safer.

Long-term management - where surveillance colonoscopy sits

Separately from managing flares, something follows as time accumulates. The National Health Information Portal notes that persistent inflammation can rarely produce complications such as strictures and perforation, and that colorectal cancer may arise as a result of chronic inflammation. So guidelines recommend surveillance colonoscopy from a certain point regardless of symptoms. The European Crohn's and Colitis Organisation puts the first screening examination at six to eight years after symptom onset and then separates intervals by risk.

Risk groupQualifying conditionsInterval
High riskStricture within the past 5 years, dysplasia within the past 5 years in someone declining surgery, colorectal cancer in a first-degree relative under 50Annually
Intermediate riskPost-inflammatory polyps, colorectal cancer in a first-degree relative over 50, extensive colitis with moderate or severe inflammationEvery 3 years
Low riskColitis affecting less than 50 percent of the colon surface, extensive colitis with mild active inflammationEvery 5 years
With primary sclerosing cholangitisFrom the point the diagnosis is confirmedAnnually, irrespective of activity, extent and duration

Two points. First, these intervals differ between societies. United States guidance splits into one to three years, or into a wider band depending on risk factors. Your own interval follows what your clinician sets, not the table above. Second, the starting point is often anchored to symptom onset rather than the date of diagnosis. For anyone whose diagnosis came late, that may fall earlier than it feels.

How a bowel diary is actually used in clinic

Plenty of articles recommend keeping records; few say how those records get used. The use is concrete. Of the three severity axes above, the two boxes only the patient can fill are stool frequency and bleeding. Filled from memory, those values wobble. Memory pulls towards the last few days and the worst day, so people describe things as worse than they were and also as better.

There do not need to be many columns. Something like the following covers most of it.

Date    Stool  Blood  Night  Urg  Pain  Temp   Med  Note
09-01     3      0      0     1     1   36.5    Y
09-02     4      1      0     2     1   36.7    Y   first week, new job
09-03     6      2      1     2     2   37.4    Y
09-04     7      2      2     3     2   37.6    N   missed evening dose
09-05     7      2      2     3     2   37.8    Y   appointment booked

Blood 0=none 1=mixed in 2=clearly mixed 3=blood alone
Urg   0=none 1=manageable 2=barely held 3=could not hold
Pain  0=none 1=mild 2=interferes with the day 3=severe
Med   Y=taken N=missed
  • The most important column is the night column. The others wobble with meals and schedules; this one wobbles less
  • Slope carries more information than the absolute value. In the example above, the reason to bring the appointment forward is not the number seven but the four-day upward direction
  • Do not drop the medication column. The relationship between missed days and worse days is among the things checked most often in clinic
  • Put life changes into the note column, one line each. Antibiotics, travel, starting another drug — these become clues later
  • Paper or a notes app, it makes no difference. It only has to be in a form you can pull out in the consulting room
  • Do not discard it once the flare has passed. The opening shape of the next flare often resembles the last one

Summary

In ulcerative colitis a flare is not an exception. A cumulative relapse rate of 88.4 percent at ten years says exactly that. So the goal is not eliminating flares but noticing early and responding in time.

What to watch on the noticing side is change from your own baseline. Stool frequency, blood, mucus, urgency, and especially nocturnal bowel movements. The response splits two ways: situations that mean bringing the appointment forward, and situations that mean the emergency department now. The latter are severe abdominal pain with distension, high fever, tachycardia, diarrhoea stopping as the person gets worse, and massive bleeding. Here, deferring the judgement is itself the risk.

One thing is within your control: not stopping maintenance therapy on your own. It is effectively the only item that is both clearly evidenced and yours to decide. For anti-inflammatory painkillers, smoking and stress, the evidence does not match the volume of the talk, and none of them substitutes for medication.

Once more. This article is general information; diagnosis and treatment follow the judgement of the clinician who examines you. The criteria and figures quoted here are meant to explain the frame used in practice, not to serve as a self-assessment. Decisions about which drug, what dose and how to take it belong in clinic, and if any situation in the warning-signs section applies, get seen without delay.

What I Could Not Verify

  • The original figures in the severity classification table of the Korean guideline could not be verified. The guideline states that it uses a modification of the Truelove and Witts score, but the table itself would not open, and the cutoffs in this article are taken from sources on those criteria
  • The full text of the 2019 American College of Gastroenterology guideline would not open; the server refused access. So the individual cutoffs in its activity index table were not carried over, and only the structure confirmed in the society's own summary of the 2025 update is described here
  • The 2003 original paper that first quantified the link between mesalamine non-adherence and relapse risk could not be retrieved. Its hazard ratio is not used here, and only the non-adherence rate from the 2025 meta-analysis is given
  • National recommendations on surveillance colonoscopy intervals in Korea could not be verified. The intervals above are from the European society, and the actual interval should follow the treating clinician
  • For infectious colitis in general as a trigger, no data as quantified as that for difficile infection was found, so no prevalence or effect size was entered in the table
  • Individual results from randomised controlled trials on diet inducing remission in ulcerative colitis could not be verified. Only the point that the relevant guidance leans on Crohn's disease evidence is stated
