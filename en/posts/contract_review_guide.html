Complete Contract Review Guide — Key Clauses, Red-Flag Terms, and What to Check Before You Sign

What every contract needs, the clauses that deserve a second look, and a practical checklist before you sign (2026)

Jul 9, 2026 Law & Legal
⚠️ Legal information notice — This article is for general information only and is not legal advice. Consult a lawyer for your specific case. It describes the law of the Republic of Korea, which may change.

Introduction: Why You Should Actually Read a Contract Before You Sign

Whether you are signing a sales agreement, a service or freelance contract, or a lease, the same rule applies: your signature fixes your rights, obligations, and exposure to risk for the life of that agreement. When a dispute arises later, the contract text is usually the first and most powerful evidence either side can point to. Yet many people still treat contract signing as a formality and skim past the clauses that matter most. This guide does not focus on one contract type. Instead, it lays out review principles that apply broadly across sales, service, lease, and freelance or independent-contractor agreements, based on practices commonly recommended in contract-review guidance as of July 2026.

One caveat up front: contract law and how specific clauses get interpreted vary significantly by country, state, and contract type. This article avoids citing specific statutes or case law and instead summarizes general principles that are broadly applicable. Before you sign anything meaningful, have the actual document reviewed by a qualified lawyer in the relevant jurisdiction.

1. Core Clauses Every Contract Review Should Cover

Regardless of contract type, confirm that the following are stated clearly and specifically.

1.1 Parties and Subject Matter

Verify that the parties named in the contract are the actual counterparties you intend to deal with. For a company, check the full legal entity name, not just a brand name, along with the signatory's authority to bind that entity. For an individual, confirm the legal name and contact details match the person who is actually signing. The subject matter — the goods being sold, the scope of services, or the leased property — should be defined specifically, not with open-ended language like "services as needed" or "all related work," which tends to become a source of dispute later.

1.2 Price and Payment Terms

The total price, payment schedule, payment method, any deposit or interim or final payment breakdown, whether tax is included, and late-payment terms should all be spelled out. Vague payment terms are one of the most common causes of later disputes over money.

1.3 Term and Renewal

Check the start date, end date, and how renewal works. Whether renewal is automatic or requires a new agreement changes what you need to do and when. If there is a deadline to give notice of non-renewal, mark it somewhere you will not miss it.

1.4 Termination Clause

Understand the grounds on which either party can end the contract and the process required — notice period, method of notice, and any post-termination obligations. Contracts commonly distinguish between termination for cause, triggered by a breach, and termination for convenience, which ends the deal without a specific reason. Check whether both exist, and whether the notice periods and conditions attached to each are reasonable.

1.5 Damages and Liquidated Damages Clauses

Review how damages are calculated if either party breaches, and whether there is a liquidated damages, or pre-agreed penalty, clause. Watch for damages provisions that are lopsided, for example a cap on damages that protects only one side, or amounts set far above what a genuine loss would look like.

1.6 Dispute Resolution, Governing Law, and Jurisdiction

Check how disputes are meant to be resolved: litigation, arbitration, or mediation first. Governing law, meaning whose law applies, and jurisdiction, meaning which court or forum hears the case, should be reviewed together. This matters especially in cross-border deals; check that the governing law and forum are not set up to favor only the other party, such as a distant venue that is impractical for you to attend.

2. Common Red-Flag Clauses Hiding in Plain Sight

The following clause types often blend naturally into the rest of the contract, which makes them easy to miss, even though they can produce results that unfairly favor one side.

2.1 Excessive Liquidated Damages or Penalty Amounts

A liquidated damages figure that is far larger than any reasonable estimate of actual loss is a red flag. Liquidated damages are meant to be a genuine pre-estimate of loss, not a punitive amount meant to intimidate. Provisions that set a fixed multiple of the total contract value as an automatic penalty for any breach deserve close scrutiny.

2.2 Unfair or One-Sided Indemnification Clauses

An indemnification clause that makes you responsible for losses caused by the other party's own conduct is a classic red flag. Watch for unlimited scope, such as agreeing to indemnify and hold harmless against any and all claims, or an obligation that runs only one way. In a balanced contract, indemnification obligations are typically mutual and limited to clearly defined triggers such as a party's own negligence or breach.

2.3 Auto-Renewal Clauses

Automatic renewal on the same terms is not inherently unfair, but it becomes a problem when the notice window to opt out is very short, for example only usable up to 60 days before renewal, or buried where it is easy to miss. Getting locked into a contract you no longer want is a common real-world consequence.

2.4 Unilateral Amendment Clauses

A clause letting the other party change price, scope, or terms after signing, without your consent, is particularly risky in ongoing agreements such as subscriptions or long-term service contracts. Check whether any right to amend is paired with advance notice and a right to object or terminate.

2.5 Overbroad Confidentiality or Non-Compete Clauses

Confidentiality and non-compete obligations are common and often legitimate, but when the duration or scope is excessive, such as no time limit, worldwide reach, or industries unrelated to the actual relationship, they can restrict your future work far beyond what is reasonably needed. The key question is whether the duration, geography, and scope are proportionate to a legitimate purpose.

3. A Pre-Signing Checklist

3.1 Put Every Verbal Promise in Writing

Anything promised verbally during negotiation, such as a discount, a special condition, or an extra service, is hard to prove later if it never made it into the contract text or a side letter. Being told something verbally that the contract does not mention is a weak position in any dispute.

3.2 Review Side Letters and Attachments Too

Check whether special terms, addenda, or attached documents, such as quotes, specifications, or side agreements, form part of the contract, and read them together with the main body. If an attachment conflicts with the main text, check which one is stated to prevail.

3.3 Keep a Copy and Confirm Proper Execution

Always keep your own original or copy of the fully signed contract. If it runs multiple pages, check whether pages are initialed or otherwise linked so the content cannot be altered later without detection.

3.4 When in Doubt, Delay and Get It Reviewed

Pressure to sign immediately, such as being told the deal needs to close today, can itself be a warning sign. If a clause is unclear, the amount is large, or the commitment is long-term, it is safer to delay signing and have the document reviewed by a lawyer or other qualified professional. If a deal cannot survive the time it takes to review it properly, that itself may say something about how reasonable the deal is.

4. What to Watch for With Electronic Contracts

Electronic signatures are recognized as legally valid alongside handwritten signatures in many jurisdictions, but that validity generally depends on several conditions being met together: clear intent by the signer to sign electronically, informed consent to conduct the transaction electronically, a way to reliably attribute the signature to the actual signer, such as identity or email verification, and a tamper-evident record of the signing event, such as a timestamp, document hash, and audit trail. Certain document types, such as wills and some family-law documents in various jurisdictions, may be excluded from electronic-signature rules. For anything significant, it is worth confirming that the e-signature platform you use meets these requirements.

5. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. What should I check first in any contract?

Start with the parties, the subject matter, and the price and payment terms. If these are inaccurate or vague, even the most carefully drafted later clauses will not save you from a dispute.

Q2. Am I always bound by a liquidated damages clause?

If the amount is far larger than any realistic estimate of actual loss, it may be adjusted or held unenforceable depending on the jurisdiction and contract type. This varies case by case and cannot be generalized, so get specific advice.

Q3. How should I handle a contract with auto-renewal?

Record the notice deadline for opting out somewhere you will actually see it, and do not miss it. If the notice window is unreasonably short or hard to find, consider raising it during negotiation.

Q4. Are electronic contracts legally valid?

In many jurisdictions, yes, provided requirements such as consent, identity verification, and record-keeping are met. Some document types may be excluded, so it is worth checking.

Q5. What should I do if I do not understand a clause?

Pause before signing, ask the other party for a written explanation, or have it reviewed by a lawyer. Once you have signed, saying you did not understand it rarely helps in a dispute.

Conclusion

Reviewing a contract is less about memorizing specific legal provisions and more about building a habit: consistently checking the same backbone of elements — parties, subject matter, price, term, termination, damages, and dispute resolution — and learning to spot red flags such as excessive penalties, unfair indemnification, auto-renewal traps, unilateral amendment rights, and overbroad non-compete or confidentiality terms. Because the specifics differ by country and contract type, treat this checklist as a starting point, and have significant contracts reviewed by a qualified professional before you sign.
