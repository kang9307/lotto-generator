Hometax: What Korea's Tax Portal Does, and Where People Get Stuck

Start from the task, not the menu — the branches, the authentication, the deadlines, and how the Sontax app divides the work (as of August 2026)

Aug 18, 2026 Tech & IT
This article is general guidance on using a public service and is not tax advice. Whether you have a filing obligation, which deductions apply to you, and how much you owe or get back all depend on your individual circumstances. For your specific situation, consult your district tax office or a licensed tax professional such as a certified tax accountant.

Why people get lost in Hometax: the names, not the features

Hometax is the online portal of Korea's National Tax Service, the agency that administers national taxes for the Republic of Korea. If you live, work or run a business in Korea, this is where income tax returns, value-added tax returns, tax certificates and cash receipt records live. Everything below describes that Korean system specifically; other countries run entirely different portals.

Anyone who has wandered around Hometax tends to stall at the same point. The task is clear enough, but which drawer holds it is not. You came for a single certificate and the screen offers filing, payment and data submission side by side, none of them using the word you were looking for.

That is not a user failure. The National Tax Service overhauled Hometax in August 2023, and the stated reason was exactly this problem. Roughly 840 services were scattered under abstract top-level headings such as Inquiry/Issuance and Application/Submission, and the redesign replaced them with names you could recognise on sight, including Tax Filing, Tax Credits and Year-End Settlement, and Electronic Tax Invoices, Cash Receipts and Credit Cards. A profile-aware menu that shows only the items relevant to individuals, sole traders or corporations arrived at the same time.

Renaming a portal does not, however, rename every document that points at it. Even now the tax agency's own website describes the same portal using two different naming systems. The page explaining how to file a comprehensive income tax return uses the new wording, giving the route as Hometax, then login, then Tax Filing, then comprehensive income tax return. The page explaining business registration still uses the pre-redesign wording, giving it as Hometax, then Application/Submission, then business registration. Neither page is trying to mislead. They were simply written at different times.

The practical conclusion is that memorising menu names does not last. What lasts is deciding, before you log in, which branch your task belongs to. Once the branch is settled, a rename only moves the target a little. The structure and names described here were confirmed from public National Tax Service and Hometax guidance as of August 2026, and everything that could not be confirmed is collected in its own section near the end.

The six branches of what Hometax does

Hometax runs hundreds of services, but almost every reason a person opens it falls into six branches. They differ in character, in the level of authentication they demand, and in the hours they are open.

  • Filing — calculating what you owe and submitting a return. Comprehensive income tax, value-added tax and capital gains tax sit here.
  • Payment — actually paying an assessed or self-declared amount. A different screen from filing, with different hours.
  • Inquiry — looking at what you paid, what you owe, and what records the tax office holds on you. The year-end settlement service is fundamentally an inquiry.
  • Certificates — obtaining a document to hand to somebody else: a business registration certificate, a tax payment certificate, an income amount certificate.
  • Cash receipts and electronic tax invoices — creating transaction records and transmitting them to the tax office. Consumer-side and business-side tasks are mixed into one branch.
  • Business registration — starting a business, amending a trade name or address, and reporting suspension or closure.

The table below maps common tasks onto those branches. The right-hand column carries only wording that appears verbatim in public official guidance. A vague entry does not mean the function is missing; it means the exact label could not be pinned down.

Year-end settlement versus the income tax return — the most common confusion

Of all Hometax questions, these two tangle most often. Not because the names resemble each other, but because both sound like tidying up your taxes. They are nothing alike.

The simplification service is a lookup, not a filing

The year-end settlement simplification service is where the tax office gathers deduction records in advance from hospitals, pharmacies, card issuers, financial institutions and schools so an employee can view and download them in one place. It is an inquiry service. For 2026 it opened on 15 January with 45 categories of records, and finalised data reflecting additions and corrections became available from 20 January. There is also a bulk-provision route in which an employer registers a list of employees in advance and collects the records of those who consented.

The point that matters: seeing your records on that screen files nothing. For someone with wage income only, the employer performs the settlement as withholding agent. The employee's job in Hometax ends at gathering the records and passing them along.

The income tax return is a document you submit yourself

A comprehensive income tax return, by contrast, is a filing you make personally, adding together a year of income from several sources. Official guidance sets the filing and payment window at 1 May to 31 May of the following year, rolling to the next business day when the deadline falls on a Saturday or public holiday. Business owners who submit a compliance verification report get until 30 June. Separate rules apply when a resident dies, in which case the deadline runs to six months from the end of the month containing the date of death, and when a resident leaves the country, in which case it is the day before departure.

Here is where the two meet. If you have wage income plus business income or another category subject to aggregate taxation, you may have to file again in May even though your employer already ran a year-end settlement. Much of the paperwork you need is already sitting in the simplification screen, which is precisely why people assume the job is finished.

If the tax office has sent you a pre-filled return notice, the process gets much shorter. Recipients can review and submit through Hometax or the Sontax app, or file by automated phone service on 1544-9944. Official guidance is blunt about one thing, though: if you do not file within the deadline, no refund is paid. Pre-filled does not mean automatic.

Which side you fall on depends on the types and size of your income, and no Hometax screen makes that determination for you. Confirm it with your district tax office or a tax professional. The arithmetic of each tax is covered separately in our comprehensive income tax guide and year-end settlement guide.

Your authentication method decides what you can do

About half of all Hometax blockers happen before the login completes. Official guidance describes three broad families.

  • Joint and financial certificates — the successors to what used to be called accredited certificates. Some official documents are still written assuming this method.
  • Simple authentication — identity confirmation through a private-sector authentication app, approved on your phone without installing desktop software.
  • ID login — a username and password registered with Hometax in advance.

The catch in practice is that being logged in and being able to use a given screen are not the same thing. Getting through to a lookup page and then being asked to authenticate again at the submission step is a common experience, because different screens demand different levels of assurance.

The rule that follows is simple: do not log in for the first time on the deadline. An expired certificate, a simple-authentication registration that needs redoing after a phone change, a forgotten password — all of these announce themselves only at the moment you try. Spending five minutes logging in on the day a filing season opens removes most of that risk.

One more thing. Authentication proves that you are you; it grants no access to anyone else's records. To view a dependent's year-end settlement data, that dependent has to consent to providing it. For a tax agent to see your records, you have to consent to the engagement.

Certificates — what to obtain and where

The need for a tax certificate usually arrives without warning. A lender asks for proof of income, a client asks for a business registration certificate, a public contract requires a tax payment certificate. Hometax groups this branch under civil certificates.

Official guidance splits them into two kinds: those issued on the spot, and those that need review by the responsible division first.

CertificateCommonly requested forIssuance
Business registration certificateVendor onboarding, signing contractsOn the spot

Income amount certificateLoan and lease screening, benefit applicationsOn the spot

Closure or suspension certificateProcedures needing proof of ceasing tradeOn the spot
VAT tax base certificateSubstantiating turnoverOn the spot
Residence certificateTax treaty and cross-border mattersAfter divisional review
Fact confirmationFacts that no standard form coversAfter divisional review

The channels are plural too. Official guidance lists online routes such as Hometax and Government24, a visit to the district tax office, and unmanned certificate kiosks. Kiosks handle a subset of the on-the-spot certificates. At a tax office, you need only identification for yourself, while an agent acting for you needs a power of attorney and a copy of your identification.

Wasted trips almost always come from the same mistake: issuing the document before asking what the recipient actually wants. Some accept an electronically issued file as is; others insist on paper or a stamped original. How long they consider a certificate valid varies too. One question to the recipient before you press issue saves the second trip.

Hometax as a business owner — registration, cash receipts, invoices

Using Hometax as an individual and using it as a business are nearly different products, because business use brings recurring obligations with deadlines attached.

Business registration — 20 days from the start

Official guidance sets business registration at before commencing business, or within 20 days of the day you started. Online application and document submission through Hometax are available, and in-person application at a district tax office civil affairs desk runs in parallel. As noted, the official page still carries the pre-redesign route wording, so if you cannot find a heading called Application/Submission, look around the business-related menus instead.

Cash receipts — consumers and businesses in the same branch

Cash receipts confuse people because consumer-side and business-side tasks sit together. The rules that appear in official guidance are these.

  • When a consumer declines a receipt or their details are unknown, the business must self-issue within five days of receiving the cash, using the designated code 010-000-1234.
  • A receipt self-issued to that designated code can later be claimed under a consumer's own name through Hometax.
  • Consumer identifiers are a card number, resident registration number, business number or mobile phone number.
  • Businesses in mandatory-issuance industries are exposed to fines or penalty tax for non-issuance even if they never joined the merchant scheme.
  • For issuance-duty breaches from 1 January 2019 onward, penalty tax of 20% of the unissued amount applies, with transactions under 5,000 won excluded.

For consumers, the single most useful step is registering a mobile number in advance. Once it is registered, saying the number at the till issues the receipt in your name. Leaving it unregistered and later hunting down individual self-issued receipts to claim is far more work.

Electronic tax invoices — issuing and transmitting are two acts

This is where accidents cluster. Issuing means a digitally signed invoice reaches the counterparty; transmitting means the record goes to the tax office. Official guidance separates them clearly.

  • A monthly aggregate tax invoice must be issued by the 10th of the month following the supply date.
  • Transmission must be completed by the day after issuance.
  • If the final day is a Saturday or public holiday, it rolls to the next business day.
  • Sending a text with a link is not issuance. The signed file has to actually land in the recipient's inbox or be entered into Hometax.

Issuing on time and then forgetting to transmit genuinely happens, usually because nobody realised the two deadlines were different. Checking transmission status the day after issuance eliminates the problem.

The calendar comes before the menu — deadlines and payment

Where to click matters less than when. Miss the deadline and no amount of navigational skill prevents penalty tax. The main dates confirmable from public official guidance are below.

Individual general taxpayers receive interim assessments in April and October. Official guidance describes this as paying 50% of the previous period's tax by assessment notice, with exclusions where the amount falls below 500,000 won or the taxpayer's classification has changed. The assessed amount is deducted at the final return.

Payment is a different screen, on a different clock

Filing a return does not move money. From official guidance on electronic national tax payment:

  • Internet payment through Hometax and Internet Giro runs 07:00 to 23:30, every day including public holidays.
  • Automated phone payment through a bank runs weekdays 09:00 to 17:00 and is closed at weekends and on holidays.
  • Bank ATMs are also available.
  • After filing electronically you can still print or fill in a payment slip and pay at a bank or post office counter.
  • Official guidance states that the Cardrotax service closed on 22 May 2026, while Internet Giro continues.

One combination reliably goes wrong: finishing a return after 23:30 on the deadline and expecting to pay immediately. An open filing screen does not imply an open payment screen. If you expect to be filing near the deadline, work backwards from the time payment closes.

Where people get stuck — browsers, popups, certificates, deadline crowds

When Hometax refuses to cooperate, the cause is usually in the browser rather than the tax code.

Blocked popups

Hometax opens confirmation dialogs and issuance screens in new windows in several places. When a browser blocks them, pressing the button appears to do nothing at all — not even an error message, which makes the cause hard to spot. Hometax's own guidance tells users to add https://www.hometax.go.kr to the Chrome popup allow list explicitly, alongside a cookie setting that keeps local data only until the browser closes. Both an automated setup file and manual steps are described.

Cache and stale screens

A familiar symptom on a frequently redesigned site: part of the page keeps an older structure, or scripts tangle and buttons stop responding. Clearing cache and cookies and reconnecting resolves it more often than not. Try a second browser as well. If only one browser fails, the browser is the problem; if all of them fail, look at the account, the authentication or the service itself.

Security software and extensions

Antivirus tools, firewalls and ad-blocking extensions can suppress scripts and leave a screen blank, particularly where authentication modules are involved. If every other cause is ruled out and the same single screen keeps failing, disable extensions briefly and retry.

Deadline crowding

No setting fixes this one. Around the end of May and around the 25th of January and July, the whole country files within the same few days. Slow screens and timeouts concentrate here. There are only three useful responses.

  • Avoid the final two days. Filing windows are usually open for close to a month.
  • Do not refresh right before submitting, and save work in progress as you go.
  • Remember that payment completes the job, and finish inside payment hours.

Who to ask when you are stuck

National tax enquiries go to 126, dialled without an area code, with general consultation on weekdays from 09:00 to 18:00 according to official guidance. One caveat is worth stating plainly, because the tax agency states it itself: telephone consultation is not a binding interpretation and carries no evidentiary weight in a filing or an appeal. It is good for asking how a screen works, not for settling a question of tax law. Tax credit enquiries have a separate line on 1566-3636.

Dividing the work with Sontax

Sontax is the official mobile app published by the National Tax Service; the developer listed in the app stores is the agency itself. And here an interesting fact surfaces. The desktop portal renamed its top-level menus in the 2023 redesign, but the official store listing still describes the service in the older set of six groupings — inquiry and issuance, civil certificates, application and submission, filing and payment, consultation and reporting, and My Hometax.

The effect on users is concrete: the same task feels as if it lives in differently named drawers on desktop and on mobile. What you reach through Tax Filing on the desktop you look for under filing and payment in the app. Nothing is missing; the labels differ.

Based on that official listing, Sontax covers the following.

  • Inquiry and issuance — business registration status, electronic tax invoice issuance, cash receipt lookups, year-end settlement data
  • Civil certificates — on-the-spot certificate requests, fact confirmations, status of requests
  • Application and submission — earned and child tax credit claims, business registration, various document requests
  • Filing and payment — simplified VAT returns, comprehensive income tax, capital gains tax, inheritance tax, suspension and closure reports, tax payment
  • Consultation and reporting — mobile consultation, visit booking, tax evasion reports, borrowed-name account reports
  • My Hometax — tax points, exemplary taxpayer status, tax agent information, payment, refund and assessment history, audit history

So when should you move to a desktop? Judge by the nature of the task rather than by a feature list.

  • Better on mobile — a pre-filled return you only need to review and submit, tax credit claims, cash receipt lookups, requesting a single certificate.
  • Better on desktop — returns with many input fields, submissions requiring attachments, work that means cross-checking several screens, anything you need to print or archive as a PDF.

With a pre-filled notice you may not need the app at all: official guidance offers filing by automated phone service on 1544-9944. Tax credit claims likewise list 1544-9944, a QR code on the notice, and direct claiming through a mobile notice, with the claim service stated as running from 06:00 to 24:00.

Delegation and tax agents — letting someone else see your records

Hiring a tax accountant does not instantly give that person sight of your national tax records. Hometax has a separate step in which the taxpayer personally consents to the engagement, and only then can the agent view records and file on your behalf. Terminating the arrangement is likewise done by the taxpayer.

Several practical frictions follow from that design.

  • If you have handed over bookkeeping and the accountant reports that no records are visible, the engagement consent has usually not gone through.
  • Switching agents without terminating the previous engagement leaves the old permission in place.
  • A change of representative or of authentication method can require the consent steps to be repeated.

The same principle governs family records. A dependent's year-end settlement data is visible only if that person consents to providing it. Listing an adult relative as a dependent does not by itself produce their records — which is why the same complaint recurs every January.

There is one more branch worth knowing even without an agent: the tax administrator you may appoint when circumstances stop you handling matters yourself. Hometax treats this alongside tax agency.

What Hometax does not finish

Hometax handles national taxes. That single line resolves a surprising share of the confusion.

  • Local taxes are elsewhere. Property tax, vehicle tax, acquisition tax and the local income tax attached to income tax belong to local governments and use separate systems. That is why an income tax return filed on Hometax still leaves local income tax to handle.
  • Social insurance is not the tax agency's remit. Health insurance, national pension, employment insurance and industrial accident insurance are administered by their own agencies. Income data links across, which makes them look connected, but the filing and payment counters differ.
  • Some certificates come faster elsewhere. Official guidance itself lists Government24 and unmanned kiosks beside Hometax.
  • Anything requiring the establishment of facts goes through a person. Certificates needing divisional review, and matters where you must substantiate individual circumstances, do not end on a screen.
  • No screen interprets tax law. Whether a deduction applies to you, or which category an item of income belongs to, is not something Hometax decides.

That last point is the important one. Hometax is a tool for executing procedures, not for making judgements. A figure appearing on screen does not confirm that the assumptions behind it match your situation. A return built on wrong assumptions is wrong however smoothly it was submitted. What could not be confirmed

These items were left out — not because they are untrue, but because public first-hand guidance did not state them outright. Screen structures and operating policies change frequently, so check these against the live Hometax screens or on 126.

  • The complete list and exact wording of desktop top-level menus. Only three of the post-redesign names appear verbatim in public guidance; the rest vary between documents.
  • Baseline service hours for Hometax as a whole. Secondary sources widely give 06:00 to 24:00, but that could not be pinned to first-hand guidance. What was confirmed is 06:00 to 24:00 for the tax credit claim service and 07:00 to 23:30 for electronic payment.
  • The current full roster of simple-authentication providers. Partnerships come and go, so no provider names were fixed in the text.
  • The rule determining which screens accept which authentication level.
  • Online issuance hours and fees for civil certificates. Even kiosk operating hours appear with more than one description in official material.
  • A complete feature comparison between Sontax and desktop Hometax. Only the scope stated in the official listing is described here.
  • The exact sub-menu path for tax agent engagement consent. Only the existence and nature of the step were confirmed.
  • The current list of officially supported browsers. The Chrome configuration guidance was confirmed; the wider support range was not.

Conclusion: decide what kind of task it is before you look for the menu

What separates people who use Hometax comfortably from people who wander is not knowing where the menus are. It is settling the nature of the task before logging in. Inquiry, filing or certificate? National or local? As an individual or as a business? Fix those three and a redesign only moves the target slightly.

Four habits sit on top of that. Never log in for the first time on a deadline. Remember that anyone else's records require a consent step first. Treat filing and payment as separate acts with separate hours. And when a screen does nothing at all, suspect the browser before the tax code.

What Hometax will not do for you is equally clear. Procedures end on a screen; judgement does not.

To repeat: this article is general guidance on using a public service and is not tax advice. Whether you must file, which deductions apply, and how much is payable or refundable depend on your income structure and the facts of your case, and both the rules and the screens change from time to time. Consult your district tax office or a licensed tax professional for your specific situation; responsibility for filing and payment decisions based on this article rests with you.
