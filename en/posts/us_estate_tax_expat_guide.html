US Estate Tax on American Stocks - The 60,000 Dollar Trap

Why nonresident investors get a threshold 250 times smaller, and what a treaty gap actually costs

Jul 27, 2026 Finance

Introduction: The tax hiding inside your US brokerage account

Buying American stocks from outside the United States has become ordinary. A few taps in a domestic brokerage app and you own shares of the largest technology companies on earth, or an index fund tracking the whole market. Yet one risk in that arrangement is almost never discussed: if you die while holding a meaningful amount of US stock, the United States may collect tax before your own country ever runs its inheritance calculation.

The usual reaction is disbelief. I am not American, I have never lived there, I have never even visited, so on what basis would the US tax my estate? The answer is that the US federal estate tax does not scope itself by the nationality or residence of the deceased alone. For a person who is neither a US citizen nor a US tax resident, it scopes itself by where the assets are considered to be located. And stock issued by a US corporation is treated as located in the United States regardless of which country's brokerage account holds it.

The threshold is where it becomes uncomfortable. The exclusion available to US citizens and residents is reported at roughly 15 million dollars per person for 2026. The equivalent figure for a nonresident alien is 60,000 dollars of US-situs assets. That is not a typo. Two numbers roughly 250 times apart live inside the same tax.

Note on perspective. This article is written primarily from the standpoint of a Korean resident investing in US markets, because Korea has no estate tax treaty with the United States and therefore represents the harshest common case. If you are resident elsewhere, the US-side rules described here are the same, but your country may have an estate tax treaty that changes the outcome substantially, and your domestic inheritance tax will work differently. Check your own jurisdiction before drawing conclusions.

1. US estate tax is a different animal from your domestic inheritance tax

1.1 Estate tax versus inheritance tax

The US federal regime is an estate tax. It treats everything the decedent left as a single pool, taxes that pool, and distributes what remains. The party liable is the estate itself, through its executor or administrator, not each individual heir. Several US states levy their own separate inheritance taxes assessed on recipients, which is a different concept again. This article concerns only the federal estate tax.

Korean inheritance tax, by contrast, is charged on the estate of a decedent who was a Korean resident and reaches worldwide assets. So a Korean resident holding US stocks is caught twice, once by residence and once by situs. That double structure is covered in section 5.

1.2 Two completely different exclusions

The mechanism is the unified credit. Citizens and residents get an enormous one. Nonresident aliens get a very small one.

The number to remember is 13,000 dollars. Run the estate tax rate schedule backwards and that credit precisely cancels the tax on a 60,000 dollar taxable estate, which is why practitioners speak of a 60,000 dollar threshold. Strictly there is no separate exemption at all, only a credit that produces that result. IRS guidance states plainly that Form 706-NA is required once US-situs assets exceed 60,000 dollars.

2. What counts as a US-situs asset

2.1 Included - stock follows the issuer, not the custodian

Drawing on IRS guidance, the gross estate of a nonresident non-citizen typically includes:

  • US real estate, where situs is beyond argument.
  • Tangible personal property physically located in the United States, such as art, precious metals or physical cash.
  • Stock issued by a US corporation. This is the decisive one. The test is the nationality of the issuing company, not where the shares are held. Shares sitting in a brokerage account in Seoul, Tokyo or Singapore are still US-situs if the issuer is a US corporation.
  • Shares in US-registered mutual funds and US-domiciled ETFs, because these are interests in a US investment company. Most of the famous US-listed index ETFs fall here.
  • Debt obligations of a US person or of the United States, including states and subdivisions, though many are excluded by the exception below.
  • A US trade or business, and bank accounts connected with it.

2.2 Excluded - bank deposits and much US debt

IRS material explicitly excludes several categories from the gross estate, and these exclusions matter more than most investors realise.

  • Bank deposits not connected with a US trade or business. Simply holding dollars in a US bank account is not, by itself, a US-situs asset for this purpose.
  • Debt securities generating so-called portfolio interest. A large share of US Treasuries and corporate bonds fall here, though the terms of issue govern and individual review is warranted.
  • Life insurance proceeds on the life of a nonresident non-citizen.
  • Stock of a foreign corporation, even when it is listed on a US exchange. The prevailing reading follows the place of incorporation.

One grey area deserves flagging. Idle cash inside a US brokerage account is not obviously the same thing as a bank deposit, and if that cash is swept into a money market fund it becomes an interest in a US registered investment company, which points toward US situs. Assuming cash is automatically safe is not a defensible position.

3. Rates and what the bill actually looks like

The federal estate tax rate schedule is graduated, running from 18% up to 40%, with 40% applying above one million dollars of taxable estate. For US citizens and residents the huge exclusion swallows the entire lower portion of the schedule, so in practice only the flat top rate is visible. A nonresident alien, holding only a 13,000 dollar credit, genuinely pays through the lower brackets.

4. The treaty gap, and why an income tax treaty does not help

4.1 Two separate kinds of treaty

This is the most common misunderstanding. Korea has an income tax treaty with the United States, which is why the withholding rate on US dividends drops from 30% to 15% for Korean residents who have filed the right form. It is natural to assume the same treaty softens the estate tax. It does not. Income tax treaties and estate or gift tax treaties are distinct instruments, negotiated separately, and having one implies nothing about the other.

4.2 Who is on the list

The IRS publishes its estate and gift tax treaty partners: Australia, Austria, Canada, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Ireland, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, South Africa, Switzerland and the United Kingdom. Korea is not among them. The consequences for a Korean resident are concrete.

  • The pro-rata unified credit used by treaty-country residents, which scales the credit by the ratio of US assets to worldwide assets, cannot be claimed.
  • Treaty situs overrides and expanded marital relief are unavailable.
  • The plain 60,000 dollar threshold applies without softening.
  • Two people holding identical US portfolios can face materially different US estate tax purely because one lives in Japan or the UK and the other in Korea.

If you are reading this from a treaty country, your position is genuinely better, but the relief is not automatic. Treaty positions typically require disclosure on the appropriate IRS form, and the calculation still starts from the same rules.

5. Double exposure with your domestic inheritance tax

5.1 Two countries looking at the same shares

Korea taxes a resident decedent on worldwide property. The United States taxes a nonresident on US-situs property. American shares held by a Korean resident sit in both nets simultaneously.

5.2 The foreign tax credit and its limits

Korea provides a cushion. Article 29 of the Inheritance Tax and Gift Tax Act allows inheritance tax imposed abroad on foreign-situated inherited property to be credited against the Korean computed tax. US estate tax paid can therefore reduce Korean tax. Most countries have some analogue, though the mechanics vary. The limits, however, are real.

  • The credit is capped at the Korean tax attributable to the foreign property. Pay more abroad than that and the excess is simply absorbed.
  • The timing does not line up. The Korean deadline runs six months from the month end of death, while a US filing and transfer certificate take far longer. The foreign amount is frequently unsettled when the domestic return is due.
  • Evidence is burdensome, requiring certified translations and proof of foreign payment.
  • There is no mirror credit on the US side. Relief flows in one direction only.

6. What actually happens after death

6.1 Accounts held directly with a US broker

This is where the risk becomes visible immediately. On notice of death the account is frozen. A US financial institution will generally not release assets until the executor produces an IRS transfer certificate, Form 5173, because releasing assets while tax is unpaid can leave the institution on the hook for that tax. The sequence usually runs:

  1. Notification of death and account freeze.
  2. Preparation of certified and translated death certificate, proof of heirs, and any will.
  3. Valuation of US-situs assets at the date of death, which is the governing valuation date.
  4. Preparation and filing of Form 706-NA, due nine months after death with extension available.
  5. Payment of tax, then application for the transfer certificate.
  6. Delivery of the certificate to each institution, then release of assets.

Published practitioner material indicates the IRS typically takes six to nine months after receiving complete documentation. Add document preparation and assets can be immobilised well beyond a year. For a surviving family, that liquidity freeze often hurts more than the tax itself.

6.2 Accounts held through a local broker

Most non-US retail investors never open a US account. They buy US shares through a domestic broker, and the securities are held through a local depository structure in which individual investors do not appear on any US register. In consequence, such estates are frequently settled entirely under domestic procedure.

An important distinction. The fact that a custody chain does not surface an individual death to the US side is not the same as an absence of legal obligation. The US filing requirement is driven by the character of the asset, and where no return is filed the limitations period is generally understood not to start running. Exposure can surface later when heirs acquire a US connection or attempt to move assets into a US account. This article does not describe how to avoid filing. If you are in this position, obtain a formal professional determination of whether a filing is required.

The practical summary is that local custody carries less immediate freeze risk but is not a legal safe harbour, while a direct US account converts the risk into an immediate, unavoidable process. Where portfolios are large, the choice of account structure is itself a planning decision.

7. Comparing responses, with the downsides stated

Nothing below is a guaranteed saving. Each option trades one problem for another.

7.1 Ireland-domiciled UCITS ETFs

  • Benefit - Shares are interests in an Irish company and are generally outside US-situs treatment, so the estate tax question falls away. Accumulating share classes also simplify distribution mechanics.
  • Limit 1 - Fund-level US withholding on dividends still applies, at the rate available under the US-Ireland treaty. The drag does not vanish.
  • Limit 2 - Access through local brokers is often restricted, and where available, trading hours, minimum sizes, commissions and currency conversion tend to be less favourable.
  • Limit 3 - Expense ratios frequently exceed those of comparable US-listed products, and that gap compounds over decades.
  • Limit 4 - Liquidity and spreads can be worse, which bites on large single transactions.
  • Limit 5 - Your own country's treatment of the fund may differ from its treatment of US-listed holdings. Confirm before switching.

7.2 Locally listed foreign-index ETFs

  • Benefit - Interests in a domestic collective vehicle are not US-situs. Access is easiest, currency conversion is unnecessary, and such funds are often eligible inside tax-advantaged retirement accounts.
  • Limit 1 - Domestic taxation differs. In Korea, gains and distributions from locally listed foreign-index ETFs are taxed as dividend income and are aggregated into comprehensive financial income taxation, whereas gains on directly held US shares are taxed separately as overseas capital gains.
  • Limit 2 - Consequently the structure can become worse as the amount grows, especially for investors with other financial income pushing them into higher aggregate brackets.
  • Limit 3 - Physically replicating and synthetic structures behave differently on tracking error and cost.
  • Limit 4 - It is no answer at all for someone who wants individual stocks.

7.3 Capping the size of US-situs holdings

  • Benefit - Stay below 60,000 dollars and the filing obligation never arises. Simple and certain.
  • Limit - For anyone investing in the US market seriously, this amounts to abandoning direct US exposure. It is less a strategy than reassurance for small investors that the issue does not concern them.

7.4 Splitting ownership between spouses

  • Benefit - The 60,000 dollar figure is per decedent, so separate accounts attract separate thresholds.
  • Limit 1 - Moving assets to a spouse can itself trigger domestic gift tax once spousal allowances are exceeded.
  • Limit 2 - Transfers between nonresident aliens also need review under US gift tax rules. The treatment of intangibles differs, but blanket assumptions are unsafe.
  • Limit 3 - An unlimited marital deduction is not available where the surviving spouse is not a US citizen. Leave it to the spouse is not a solution here.
  • Limit 4 - Two thresholds still total only 120,000 dollars, which resolves nothing at scale.

7.6 What not to do

  • Manipulating accounts or ownership to defeat a filing obligation is not planning, it is evasion. This article does not cover it.
  • Switching products on the strength of forum posts. Escaping one tax can cost more in another.
  • Creating offshore entities or trusts without advice. Formation and maintenance costs, foreign account reporting obligations and controlled foreign corporation rules attach quickly.

8. Dividend withholding and estate tax are separate issues

Discussion of Irish-domiciled funds almost always merges these two topics. They differ in when they arise, which treaty governs, and who bears them.

The takeaway: the appeal of an Irish-domiciled fund is not that dividend withholding disappears. The fund still suffers US withholding on the dividends it collects. Its structural advantages are that no further withholding is layered on when the fund pays investors and that the shares sit outside the US estate tax net. Collapsing both into no tax produces false expectations.

Note also that the reduced 15% treaty rate on directly held US dividends depends on a valid certification form being on file. Local brokers usually handle this automatically, but investors with direct US accounts must track renewal themselves. Let it lapse and 30% can apply.

9. Where the problem actually bites

Not every investor needs to worry. Sorted by size:

  • Under 60,000 dollars of US-situs assets - No filing obligation arises. Spend your attention elsewhere.
  • 60,000 to 200,000 dollars - Filing territory. Simplified tax runs from a few thousand up to the low forty thousands. Worth knowing about.
  • 200,000 to 500,000 dollars - Effective rates move into the twenties. A good moment to review portfolio structure.
  • Above 500,000 dollars - Simplified tax exceeds 140,000 dollars. There is no reason to postpone professional advice.
  • Above 1,000,000 dollars - Everything above the million mark is taxed at 40%. Succession should be designed, not discovered.

One further variable deserves emphasis. Equities are bought in the expectation that they will grow. A position worth 250,000 dollars today may be worth a million in twenty years, and the date of death cannot be chosen. Worse, restructuring later is itself a taxable disposal in most systems. The cheapest time to choose a structure is while the position is small.

Conclusion: the most expensive tax is the one you never heard of

US federal estate tax is an unusual risk for foreign investors. It attaches to an event that is certain to occur, at a moment when you cannot respond, and the burden lands entirely on the people you leave behind. The 60,000 dollar nonresident threshold sits roughly 250 times below the citizen and resident figure, and for residents of non-treaty countries such as Korea there is no estate tax treaty to soften it. Domestic inheritance tax then applies on top, with a foreign tax credit that helps but is capped and badly timed.

None of which is an argument to sell your US holdings. The purpose of this article is narrower: to make sure the tax is known to you. Once it is, several responses are reasonable, including doing nothing while documenting your accounts and the required procedure so that your family is not blindsided. Knowingly accepting a risk and being unaware it exists are entirely different positions, even when the action taken is identical.

Three concrete steps are available today. Calculate what your US-situs assets are worth right now. Identify which band above that figure falls into. And if it exceeds a couple of hundred thousand dollars, speak once to a tax professional experienced in cross-border estates. That fee is far cheaper than a few percentage points of estate tax. Figures here reflect July 2026 and move with indexing and legislation, so reconfirm current numbers whenever you actually act.
