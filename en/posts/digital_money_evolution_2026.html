Money in the Digital Age - How Currency Evolved and the Institutional Choices Behind Digital Money

Seen as a social institution of trust rather than a commodity, money makes clear why CBDC, stablecoins, and crypto are fundamentally different choices

Introduction: Why We Need to Ask What Money Is, Again

As of July 2026, the Bank of Korea is expanding its digital won experiment, Project Hangang, to nine banks; the European Parliament has cleared the final legislative hurdle for a digital euro; and China has converted the digital yuan into an interest-bearing, deposit-like form of money. Meanwhile, the United States is moving its GENIUS Act — which brings stablecoins into the regulated financial system — into implementation. On the surface, all of this looks like the same headline: "digital money is coming." In reality, each is a different answer to a different question: who issues money, who guarantees the trust behind it, and how that trust gets verified.

Rather than simply listing recent news, this article analyzes what money originally was, and how that essence is being tested again by the shift to digital forms. It does not recommend any specific coin or financial product, and it is not investment advice.

1. What Is Money - The Three Functions That Define Its Essence

1.1 Money as a Medium of Exchange

The oldest function of money is as a medium of exchange. Barter suffers from the "double coincidence of wants": a trade only happens if I want what you have and you happen to want what I have. Money removes that constraint. If there is something everyone will accept, I can sell what I own for money and use that money to buy whatever I actually want. This is the precondition that lets a market economy operate at scale.

1.2 Store of Value and Unit of Account

Money is also a store of value — a way to move purchasing power into the future instead of spending it now — and a unit of account, a common yardstick for comparing the worth of different goods. These three functions are intertwined. When the store-of-value function fails (hyperinflation, for instance), people become reluctant to use that money as a medium of exchange either, and its credibility as a unit of account erodes with it. When evaluating CBDCs, stablecoins, and cryptocurrencies, the ultimate test is how reliably each fulfills these same three functions.

2. The Historical Evolution of Money - From Commodities to Digital

2.1 Commodity Money and Metal Coins

Early societies used commodity money such as shells, livestock, and grain. Precious metals like gold and silver later became the standard medium thanks to their durability, scarcity, and divisibility. Metal coinage is believed to have first appeared around the 7th century BCE in the Kingdom of Lydia in Asia Minor, before spreading across the Mediterranean and Asia.

2.2 The Rise of Paper Money and the Break from the Gold Standard

Paper money began around the 7th century in China as merchant promissory notes, evolving into the first true state-issued paper currency under the Song Dynasty in the 11th century. Paper money was lighter, easier to transport, and could be issued in larger volumes than metal coins. In the 20th century, the shift toward fiat currency — money not backed by convertibility into gold — accelerated, and especially after the collapse of the Bretton Woods system in 1971, major currencies moved entirely to a structure where value rests on sovereign credit alone, with no link to gold.

2.3 From Electronic Money to Digital Currency

In the latter half of the 20th century, electronic money via credit cards and bank computer networks became routine. This was already "money that exists only as numbers," yet the issuers remained the state and commercial banks. With the 2020s came three new branches — CBDC, stablecoins, and cryptocurrency — which raised in earnest the question of who is qualified to issue digital money.

3. Money Is an Institution of Trust - Sovereign Credit and Legal Tender

Economics has a tradition — the credit theory of money and chartalism (the state theory of money) — that treats money not as a commodity but as credit and a social relation. In this view, money is an implicit contract between an individual and society; holding money means trusting society's collective promise that it can later be exchanged for other goods and services of equivalent value.

Legal tender works because the state designates a currency as the sole valid means of paying taxes and settling debts, and backs that designation with law and coercive power. In other words, the value of money is not tied to gold or any physical object — it is tied to the continuity of the state as an institution and to society's trust in that institution. Viewed this way, the digital-money debate becomes clearer. A CBDC is an attempt to extend sovereign credit directly into digital form. A stablecoin puts forward a private issuer's reserves and reputation as collateral for trust. Cryptocurrency tries to shift the object of trust away from both the state and any issuer, toward the verification rules of a decentralized network itself. The three branches don't differ in technology so much as in whom they delegate trust to.

4. The Three Branches of Digital Money - CBDC, Stablecoin, Cryptocurrency

4.1 CBDC - Digital Money Directly Backed by the Central Bank

A CBDC is a model in which the central bank provides its own money directly in digital form. The Bank of Korea is running Phase 2 of Project Hangang, a live trial of deposit tokens involving nine banks — KB Kookmin, Shinhan, Woori, Hana, NH Nonghyup, IBK, BNK Busan, plus Kyongnam and iM Daegu — and is testing the use of the digital won for real government subsidy payments such as EV charging infrastructure grants. The goal is to finalize the design by the end of 2026, with plans to test NFC-based offline payments among 100,000 users in Seoul and Busan.

In Europe, the European Parliament's Economic and Monetary Affairs Committee cleared the legislative framework for a digital euro in June 2026, targeting an online and offline launch by 2029. By design, offline payments aim for cash-like privacy so the central bank cannot directly see individual purchase histories, and a holding limit of roughly €500 to €3,000 is under discussion.

China converted the digital yuan from a cash-like instrument into deposit-style money on January 1, 2026, becoming the first CBDC in the world to pay interest. As of November 2025, 230 million personal wallets and 18.84 million institutional wallets had been opened, with cumulative transactions reaching 3.48 billion worth 16.7 trillion yuan — yet in everyday retail use it still trails Alipay and WeChat Pay.

4.2 Stablecoins - Privately Issued Money Backed by Reserve Assets

A stablecoin is a privately issued digital token designed to be pegged 1:1 to a fiat currency. In the United States, the GENIUS Act, signed in July 2025, formally brought payment stablecoins into the regulated system, requiring issuers to hold reserves only in Federal Reserve account credits, insured bank deposits, or short-term Treasuries maturing within 93 days. Final rules are expected in 2026, with the full regime taking effect in January 2027.

South Korea is debating its own Digital Asset Basic Act. The Bank of Korea holds that only entities in which banks own at least 51% should be allowed to issue won-denominated stablecoins, while the Financial Services Commission worries such a restriction could stifle innovation. In practice, digital-asset custodian BDACS is already partnering with Woori Bank to prepare a won stablecoin called KRW1. Still, the 2022 collapse of Terra/Luna made clear that stablecoins are vulnerable to reserve-quality problems and run-like mass redemptions.

4.3 Cryptocurrency - A Different Trust Model With No State or Issuer

Cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin adopt a fundamentally different trust structure from CBDCs and stablecoins. Instead of a sovereign or issuer guaranteeing trust, the consensus rules of a decentralized network (proof-of-work, proof-of-stake, and so on) take that place. Because its price is not pegged to any fiat currency, it is highly volatile and is not recognized as legal tender in most countries. In practice, it is often used more as an investment or speculative asset than as a means of payment, which is why it should be understood separately from CBDCs and stablecoins.

5. Institutional Trade-Offs - Privacy, Control, and Stability

5.1 Privacy Versus Traceability

Cash guarantees anonymity, but a CBDC, depending on its design, may allow the central bank or payment providers to see transactions. The digital euro is being designed to offer cash-like privacy specifically for offline payments, while China's digital yuan uses a "controlled anonymity" model where small transactions are anonymous and large ones are traceable. How much traceability a society allows ultimately comes down to a policy choice between privacy and priorities like anti-money-laundering enforcement and tax fairness.

5.2 The Scope of Monetary Policy Control

A CBDC gives central banks more direct policy tools — "programmable money" restricted to specific uses, such as the EV subsidy example above, is a prime case. But this also carries the risk that, in a crisis, deposits could flee commercial bank accounts en masse into CBDC holdings, which is exactly why Europe is debating holding limits.

5.3 Stability and Risk

Stablecoins carry stability risk tied to reserve quality and run dynamics; CBDCs carry a different kind of risk tied to technical failures and cyber threats in a centralized system; cryptocurrency's price volatility is itself the risk, with no safety net like deposit insurance. None of the three models is risk-free — the nature of the trade-off simply differs.

6. Will Cash Disappear - The Cashless Society Debate

About 90% of transactions in Sweden are already cashless, and South Korea's digital payment adoption rate reaches 98%, meaning cash use is already rare in practice. Yet even Sweden's Riksbank acknowledges the risk of financial exclusion for the elderly and rural populations, and an over-reliance on private payment infrastructure, and continues to work on guaranteeing separate access to cash. China supplements this with offline QR payments through Alipay and WeChat Pay so that transactions remain possible even where networks are down. Many point out that fully abolishing cash undermines resilience during crises such as power outages or cyberattacks, which is why most central banks maintain a dual-track approach: expanding digital money while separately guaranteeing continued access to cash.

7. Implications for Korea and the Global Landscape

Interestingly, South Korea is navigating both the CBDC debate (Project Hangang) and the stablecoin debate (the Digital Asset Basic Act) at the same time. The disagreement between the Bank of Korea and the Financial Services Commission is, at its core, the institutional question of who should issue the digital won: the central bank directly, banks via deposit tokens, or private stablecoin issuers.

Globally, the picture is splitting: the United States is leaning toward a private, stablecoin-centered model through the GENIUS Act, while Europe and China are leaning toward a central-bank-led CBDC model. The European Parliament has explicitly framed the digital euro as a response to US dominance over payment rails, showing that this is not merely a technology race but also a geopolitical contest over monetary sovereignty and payment-network influence. For individuals, what matters is that these are institutional and infrastructure choices, not investment products — and they should not be conflated with highly volatile, speculative crypto assets.

Conclusion: A Balanced View of Digital Money

Money is not a tool but a system of trust that society maintains together. What the digital shift changes is not the essence of money but the structure of who verifies and guarantees that trust, and how. A CBDC delegates trust to the state; a stablecoin, to an issuer and its reserves; a cryptocurrency, to a decentralized network. No single model has been declared the answer, and countries are each testing different balance points among privacy, control, and stability. This article does not recommend any specific coin or product — what readers need is an understanding of each model's institutional trade-offs, and a perspective from which to judge the policy debates still to come.
