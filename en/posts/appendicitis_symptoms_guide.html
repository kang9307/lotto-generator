Early Signs of Appendicitis and What to Do - When to Go to the Emergency Room

Do not try to judge it yourself - learn the signs you must not miss

2026-07-31 Health & Wellness

Introduction: is this a stomach ache to sit out, or one to go in with?

It started as heaviness after dinner. A dull, blocked feeling below the breastbone, no appetite, so you decide it must be indigestion and lie down. You wake again in the small hours and the sore spot is not where it was. It is lower and to the right now, sharp, and it gets worse when you turn over or cough. Is this a reason to go to an emergency department at this hour, or something that can wait until a clinic opens?

This article is for the person at that fork. And its argument is not let us work out whether this is appendicitis but let us not try to work it out ourselves. That sounds backwards until you see why. The acute appendicitis document from Korea's National Health Information Portal records that about one third of all patients present with an unclear picture. MedlinePlus, from the US National Library of Medicine, states that not everyone with appendicitis has all these symptoms. The NHS page on appendicitis puts it even more bluntly: there is no test that can always show if you have appendicitis. If a hospital with imaging and bloodwork does not settle it on a single result, no amount of pressing your own abdomen at home is going to.

So the purpose of this article narrows to one thing: not missing the moment to go. Everything below is arranged around that.

  • The signs that mean do not wait come first. If you read nothing else, keep that list
  • Then why this condition in particular defeats self-assessment, using the sources. The typical sequence is here, and so are the common ways it fails to appear
  • Finally, the preparation that makes a consultation faster. When the pain started and how the location shifted are things clinicians actually use

Disclaimer. This article is for information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Nothing in it should be used as a basis for self-diagnosis or self-treatment. If you have symptoms, the rule is to be seen by a clinician; whether to take medication and which treatment to pursue are decided by clinicians after examination and testing. In Korea the emergency number is 119; wherever else you are, use your local emergency number.

1. Go to the emergency department without delay

The list comes first. Every item below is something the public health sources checked here tie to an urgent response; nothing has been added on my own authority. If even one applies, the better move is to stop narrowing down the cause yourself.

  • The abdominal pain is severe. The NHS lists severe abdominal pain as a call-now situation
  • The pain keeps getting worse, or will not settle. The NHS directs people to urgent care in that case
  • Pain that started around the navel has moved to the lower right side. The migration itself is treated as a sign
  • It hurts more when the abdomen is pressed and then released. Korea's portal calls this rebound tenderness and describes it as a finding of progressing appendicitis
  • Walking, coughing, sneezing, or breathing deeply makes it worse. This is a characteristic NIDDK highlights
  • There is a fever. A low fever that climbs gradually counts
  • Vomiting continues and nothing will stay down
  • The pain has spread past the lower right across the whole abdomen. Or it eased off briefly and then came back far worse

That last item deserves its own emphasis. The NHS explains that after the appendix bursts, the pain may improve briefly before becoming severe pain that spreads across the whole abdomen. In other words, sudden relief is not a reason to relax. What follows it is the part to watch.

1.1 Three things that mean call emergency services now

The NHS lists the following three as possible signs of sepsis and says to call emergency services straight away rather than looking for an appointment.

  • Confusion. This includes slurred speech or not recognising people
  • Blotchy skin, or skin paler than usual
  • Difficulty breathing

Numbers. In Korea there is a single emergency and advice number, 119. The older emergency medical information line was merged into 119 and is no longer in use. 119 handles the call and the dispatch, and also phone advice and directing you to a receiving hospital. To locate an emergency department in advance, the emergency medical portal run by the National Emergency Medical Center lists them. If you are outside Korea, use your local emergency number. The Korean number does not work abroad.

2. What the appendix is, and why it becomes inflamed

People often say the caecum burst, and in Korean the condition is popularly named after the caecum, but the National Health Information Portal notes that the popular term is not medically accurate. What actually becomes inflamed is not the caecum itself but the narrow tube attached to it, the appendix.

MedlinePlus describes the appendix as a small, tube-like organ attached to the first part of the large intestine, sitting in the lower right part of the abdomen, and adds that it has no known function. That is why the lower right is talked about so much.

Inflammation starts with a blockage. MedlinePlus explains that a blockage inside the appendix causes appendicitis, and that the blockage leads to increased pressure, problems with blood flow, and inflammation. What does the blocking varies a little between sources.

  • Korea's portal records overgrowth of the lymphoid tissue around the appendix as the most common cause, at 60 percent
  • The same source attributes 35 percent to hardened stool blocking the opening
  • It also lists foreign bodies, inflammatory strictures, parasites, and tumours
  • NIDDK cites hardened stool or growths, and enlarged tissue in the wall of the appendix caused by infection
  • Those percentages come from the Korean portal document, and that document does not separately state a survey year or study population

Knowing the structure makes the time pressure intuitive. Inside a blocked tube, pressure keeps rising and blood flow deteriorates, and there is a limit to how long the wall holds. The section on perforation and peritonitis later on follows directly from this.

3. The typical sequence - and the third that does not follow it

Start with the typical case. The National Health Information Portal describes the progression as follows. First appetite falls and nausea begins, and then upper abdominal pain appears. That pain shifts by way of the area around the navel into the lower right abdomen. Then tenderness in the lower right becomes distinct, pain on releasing pressure appears, and a low fever gradually rises, sometimes with chills.

The NHS account matches in outline. It describes pain starting in the middle of the abdomen, around the navel, and moving to the lower right side after a few hours. NIDDK is more specific about the character of the pain: it may begin near the belly button and move lower and to the right, may start suddenly, gets worse when you move around, take deep breaths, cough, or sneeze, and may happen before other symptoms and worsen in a matter of hours.

3.1 But many cases never follow that sequence

This is the most important passage in the article. The sequence above is a reference map, not a decision rule, and the sources are explicit about how large the exception is.

  • Korea's portal records that about one third of all patients present with an unclear picture
  • Children are described as becoming drowsy and irritable with a poor appetite early on, then progressing rapidly to vomiting, fever, and pain
  • Older adults are described as often not showing the typical picture, with a faster course and more frequent complications. The NHS adds that sudden confusion can appear in older people
  • In pregnancy, as the fetus grows the appendix is pushed upward, so that in late pregnancy it comes to sit in the upper right abdomen. The painful location itself changes
  • The NHS likewise notes that in pregnant women, young children, and older people, pain may be milder, slower to develop, or felt in a different place

Put plainly: following the typical sequence makes it easier to recognise, but the converse does not hold. Not following the sequence is no evidence against appendicitis. And the people in whom the exceptions cluster are precisely children who cannot describe symptoms, older adults whose signals are faint, and pregnant patients whose pain has relocated. That is the case against self-assessment.

4. Things it gets confused with - and why that means being seen

Let me state the purpose of this section first. The table below is not an invitation to sort it out yourself. The opposite. It shows how much overlaps, so that the conclusion lands: separating these is the job of examination and testing.

NIDDK names abdominal adhesions, inflammatory bowel disease, intestinal obstruction, pelvic inflammatory disease, and kidney stones as conditions that can mimic appendicitis. Urinalysis is done in hospital precisely to rule out other conditions such as a bladder infection or a kidney stone, and a pregnancy test may be added for women.

Reading the table makes clear why concluding it is just a stomach bug is dangerous. Not because symptoms overlap, but because there is barely a symptom that does not. Nausea and vomiting, diarrhoea and constipation, a low fever, all appear on both sides. What is left is the location and character of the pain and how it changes over time, and those are exactly the pieces of information that only acquire meaning when someone examines the abdomen and reads them alongside test results.

5. What the hospital actually does

An emergency department can feel like a place where nothing happens slowly. Knowing why helps. The pathway NIDDK describes has four layers, history, physical examination, laboratory tests, and imaging, and they are used to complement one another.

StepWhat is doneWhy
HistoryWhen the pain began, how bad it feels, exactly where, plus other conditions and past operationsChange over time forms the backbone of the assessment
Physical examinationChecking for pain in the lower right abdomen, gently pressing or jiggling all areas, pressing the right knee as the leg is liftedTo locate the inflammation and check for peritoneal irritation
Blood testWhite blood cell countNIDDK describes a high white cell count as a sign of infection
Urinalysis and pregnancy testRuling out other causesTo exclude a bladder infection, a kidney stone, and similar
ImagingUltrasound, MRI, CTUltrasound uses no radiation and is the first imaging test in infants, children, young adults, and pregnant women

The Korean portal notes that on examination, tenderness at McBurney's point matters. It sits at the outer third of a line drawn from the navel to the bony prominence at the front of the pelvis. Even so, this is a finding a clinician confirms by hand, not an item to press and grade at home.

Imaging has an order too. NIDDK presents MRI as a safe, reliable alternative to CT, and states that in children CT is recommended only after ultrasound and MRI, to limit radiation exposure. That answers the natural question of why nobody sends you straight for a CT.

  • The reason for multiple layers is simple: no single layer settles it
  • The NHS states outright that there is no test that can always show if you have appendicitis
  • So a normal blood count on its own does not license the conclusion that all is well
  • Conversely a raised count on its own does not confirm appendicitis either
  • The waiting in hospital is generally those layers being stacked

5.1 On scoring tools

Clinical practice does use tools that score history, examination, and test findings item by item to gauge risk. This article does not list the items of any such tool, for three reasons.

  • They are designed on the assumption that a clinician who has palpated the abdomen is using them. Items like tenderness and rebound change meaning the moment you score them yourself
  • A score is a reference, not a verdict. Delaying care because the number came out low inverts the entire purpose of this article
  • The public sources checked here describe diagnosis as the combination of history, examination, laboratory tests, and imaging, and none of them walks a patient through scoring themselves

6. Treatment options - surgery and antibiotics

Treatment is set out here without recommending either route. That decision belongs to a discussion with the clinicians who have seen the examination and test results, and it is not something this article can settle.

  • Korea's portal states that surgery is the principle in treating acute appendicitis
  • MedlinePlus states that treatment almost always involves removing the appendix
  • NIDDK states that some mild cases of appendicitis may be treated with antibiotics alone
  • The NHS notes that antibiotics may be considered if the infection has not spread and surgery carries high risk, and that where a mass has formed around the appendix the approach is antibiotics first with surgery later

Surgery is not one thing either. NIDDK sets out the laparoscopic approach through a small incision alongside the open approach through a single larger incision in the lower right abdomen. The Korean portal likewise notes that laparoscopic surgery is used in some cases to leave a smaller scar and speed recovery. Where perforation has already caused peritonitis, NIDDK describes surgery performed immediately to clean the inside of the abdomen, and where an abscess has formed, draining the pus to allow it to heal.

With that many branch points, what a patient can usefully prepare is not a conclusion but questions. These are worth asking in the discussion.

  • What stage does my case appear to be at, and is there any sign of perforation or abscess
  • Why this approach rather than another
  • Is there a reason the alternatives were not considered
  • How do the options differ in recovery time and return to normal activity
  • What changes if the decision is deferred, and how long can it be deferred

7. What changes if it is left

This section is not written to frighten anyone. It sets out why time is a variable, in the words of the sources.

MedlinePlus puts it most concisely: if the blockage is not treated, the appendix can burst and spread infection into the abdomen, and this causes a condition called peritonitis. The NHS describes peritonitis as an infection of the inner lining of the abdomen, and adds that a burst appendix can produce a painful build-up of pus, an abscess. Korea's portal records that on perforation the pain is no longer confined to the lower right but spreads across the lower or whole abdomen, with high fever and a faster heartbeat.

  • The Korean portal records that complications occur in 5 percent of non-perforated appendicitis, while with tissue necrosis or perforation the rate can reach 30 percent
  • The same source gives wound infection rates of 4 to 8 percent in simple appendicitis and 10 to 20 percent in perforated cases
  • Overall perforation is given as about 25 percent, rising to 35 to 60 percent under age 10 and 60 to 75 percent over age 50
  • The same document states that if surgery is not performed within three days of symptom onset the appendix ruptures. That is the document's own wording, reproduced here, and it is not a figure to use for predicting an individual course
  • All of the above appear in the Korean portal document, and that document does not separately state a survey year or study population

For balance, another sentence from the same source belongs here. Most patients reach a medical facility within 12 to 18 hours of symptom onset. This is, in other words, a condition people generally do present with in time. The problem lies with the cases that fall outside that majority, and as the previous section showed, those cluster among the unclear third and among children, older adults, and pregnant patients. That perforation rates are recorded as highest under 10 and over 50 is the same observation from another angle.

How to read this section. The numbers above are not your personal probability. They are group-level descriptions from a document that does not state its survey year or population, and that limitation travels with them. What to take away is the direction, not the figures: options narrow as time passes, and time runs faster for children and older adults.

8. Recovery, and the signs that mean going back

After surgery the next question is when things go back to normal. The sources are broadly reassuring.

  • NIDDK states that after surgery you will likely recover completely and will not need to change your diet, exercise, or lifestyle
  • The same source gives activity limits of 3 to 5 days after laparoscopic surgery and 10 to 14 days after a laparotomy
  • The NHS says most people recover within 1 to 2 weeks, and longer where the appendix burst
  • Because recovery depends on the approach and on whether perforation occurred, the instructions you were given on discharge take precedence over this article

On the signs that mean going back, I will be careful, because what the sources name explicitly and what belongs to discharge instructions are different things.

  • The NHS lists an abscess forming where the appendix was removed as a post-surgical complication. Pus collecting is not something to leave
  • Fever returning or abdominal pain worsening again should be treated as a departure from the expected course
  • Surgery or not, if the emergency signs in section 1 reappear, the same criteria apply unchanged
  • If the case was treated with antibiotics alone, it is worth asking in advance what to do if symptoms return
  • Where the line falls between an expected course and a problem varies with the approach and the individual, so the discharge criteria you were given are the ones to follow

9. What to prepare before the consultation

As section 5 showed, the first layer of diagnosis is the history. The questions NIDDK describes are when the abdominal pain began, how bad it feels, exactly where you feel it, and what other conditions and operations you have had. Yet arriving at an emergency department in real pain is exactly the situation in which those basics will not come to mind. Writing them down first speeds the whole visit up.

The row worth extra care is medication taken. This article gives no instruction to take or avoid medication; whether to take something is a matter to discuss with a clinician. What is definitely important is reporting it exactly if you already have. Diagnosis leans on the intensity and location of pain and how it has changed, and a clinician can only read that properly if they know whether a drug has affected it.

  • Notes on your phone are enough. Memory blurs as pain intensifies
  • If someone is going with you, share the same notes in case you cannot speak for yourself
  • For a young child, record when they stopped eating and what is different from usual, with times
  • If pregnancy is possible, knowing the first day of the last period speeds up decisions about testing
  • If you have already been tested elsewhere, bring those results and their timing too

10. Summary - the one line this article wants to leave

The most searched question about appendicitis is some version of is this appendicitis. Follow the sources, though, and what becomes clear is why that question is hard to answer at home. Hospitals with full imaging do not settle it on a single test, roughly one third of patients do not follow the typical sequence, and the exceptions cluster among children, older adults, and pregnant patients.

  • What you need to know is not the diagnosis but the moment to go
  • If even one sign from section 1 is present, stop narrowing it down yourself
  • Pain suddenly easing is not a reason to relax
  • The most practical preparation is writing down when it started, where it is, and how it changed

Disclaimer, again. This article was written for information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Judgements about individual symptoms and decisions about medication or surgery must be discussed with a clinician. If you judge the situation to be an emergency, use 119 or your nearest emergency department in Korea, and your local emergency number elsewhere.

# Appendicitis — Emergency Room Decision Notes and a Consultation Prep Sheet

Source: braindetox.kr — Early Signs of Appendicitis and What to Do
Evidence: KDCA National Health Information Portal 「Acute Appendicitis」 · NHS Appendicitis · MedlinePlus Appendicitis · NIH NIDDK Appendicitis (all verified 2026-07-31)

Note: this document is for information only and does not replace medical care. It is not a self-diagnosis tool.
Judgment belongs to clinicians. The single purpose of this sheet is to keep you from missing the moment to go.

## 0. How to use this sheet

- These tables are not a tool for working out whether you have appendicitis. They exist to move your visit earlier.
- If even one of the signs below applies, stop trying to sort it out yourself and hand it to a clinician.
- In Korea the emergency number is 119, which handles the call, the dispatch, phone guidance, and directing you to a receiving hospital.
- Outside Korea, use your local emergency number. The Korean number does not work abroad.
- Find out where your nearest emergency department is before you need it. Searching once the pain is bad is already late.

## 1. Go to the emergency department without delay — if any one applies

- [ ] The abdominal pain is severe
- [ ] The pain keeps getting worse over time, or will not go away
- [ ] Pain that started around the navel has moved to the lower right side
- [ ] It hurts more when the abdomen is pressed and then released
- [ ] Walking, coughing, sneezing, or breathing deeply makes it worse
- [ ] There is a fever
- [ ] Vomiting continues and nothing will stay down
- [ ] The pain has spread past the lower right and across the whole abdomen
- [ ] The pain eased off briefly and then came back far worse

## 2. Call emergency services immediately — if any of these three appear

- [ ] Confusion, slurred speech, or not recognising people
- [ ] Blotchy skin, or skin paler than usual
- [ ] Difficulty breathing

The NHS lists these three as possible signs of sepsis and tells people to call emergency services straight away.

## 3. Cases where mild symptoms should not be discounted

- Children — early drowsiness, irritability, and loss of appetite are reported, followed by rapid progression
- Older adults — the typical picture often does not appear, the course can be faster, and complications are more common
- Pregnancy — as pregnancy advances the appendix is pushed upward, so the painful spot can sit somewhere else
- Anyone who has already taken a painkiller — report the drug and the time exactly as it happened

## 6. What not to do

- Take a painkiller and wait it out — if you took one, say so. Do not decide alone whether to take one, discuss it with a clinician
- Obtain and take antibiotics on your own — the treatment plan is set after examination and tests
- Try to settle it with home remedies or a diet — none of the sources checked support the idea that an inflamed appendix resolves that way
- Relax because the pain suddenly eased — after a rupture the pain can improve briefly before spreading across the abdomen
- Score yourself with a checklist found online — the tools used in practice assume a clinician who has examined the abdomen
- Trust a single test result — the NHS states there is no test that can always show whether you have appendicitis

## 7. Numbers as the sources state them

The values below all appear in the KDCA National Health Information Portal document 「Acute Appendicitis」 (verified 2026-07-31).
That document does not separately state a survey year or study population.

- Roughly one third of all patients present with an unclear picture
- Blockage of the appendix is attributed to overgrowth of surrounding lymphoid tissue in 60 percent and to hardened stool in 35 percent
- It occurs often in the teens and twenties, and is most common in the early twenties
- Patients under 10 or over 50 make up about 10 percent of all cases
- Overall perforation is given as about 25 percent, rising to 35 to 60 percent under 10 and 60 to 75 percent over 50
- Non-perforated appendicitis leads to complications in 5 percent, while tissue necrosis or perforation can reach 30 percent
- Most patients reach a medical facility within 12 to 18 hours of symptom onset

MedlinePlus states it is more common among people 10 to 30 years old. That page likewise does not state a period or study population.

## 8. One line

Appendicitis is not a condition you can sort out on your own. Unclear presentations are common, so what you need to know is not the diagnosis but the moment to go.
