1. What Is braillify? - The Library In One Paragraph

braillify is an open-source JavaScript library that converts Korean text into Unicode Braille Patterns (U+2800 to U+28FF). It was built by the Korean developer community in direct response to the 2024 specification overhaul. Its identity card reads as follows.

  • License: MIT - commercial use permitted without royalties
  • Spec support: 2024 Korean Braille Specification, fully implemented
  • Verification: 2,419 unit-test cases passing in CI
  • Runtime: Browser (ES modules) and Node.js from the same package
  • Dependencies: zero - no runtime packages required

In one sentence, braillify is the lightest Korean text-to-braille library you can install today. Traditional Korean braille software was either Windows-only desktopware or a heavyweight server stack. braillify shrinks the surface area down to something that runs comfortably inside a static HTML file. That portability is the headline.

2. Braille Fundamentals - Understanding the Six-Dot Cell

For readers who have never inspected braille closely, here is the structural primer. A braille cell is a 2-by-3 grid of raised dots - two columns, three rows. A dot is either raised (1) or flat (0), so a single cell encodes 2 to the power of 6 - sixty-four possible patterns. That is wide enough to cover the Latin alphabet, digits, punctuation, and many contractions.

Unicode allocated 256 codepoints to braille in 1999 - U+2800 to U+28FF - which covers both the six-dot cell and the eight-dot computer-braille cell. Pure six-dot output only uses U+2800 to U+283F. braillify defaults to six-dot output but can emit eight-dot when configured.

ConceptDescription
CellOne braille character unit, 2 columns by 3 rows
DotSix positions numbered 1 to 6, top-left to bottom-right
ContractionCommon words compressed into one or two cells
AbbreviationLong words mapped to a predefined short pattern
Number signMarks the following cells as digits
Capital signMarks the following cell as uppercase

2.1. Why Six Dots, and Why Two Columns

When Louis Braille designed the system in 1825, the constraint was the human fingertip. The discriminable area under one index finger is roughly six millimeters by nine millimeters. Within that footprint a reader can reliably distinguish six dots but not eight. Make the cell larger and the finger must lift and reposition more often; make it smaller and the dots fuse. That ergonomic constant has stood for two hundred years and still anchors every digital braille display sold today.

3. The Particularities of Hangul Braille

English braille is almost trivially direct - one Latin letter, one cell. Korean is structurally different. Hangul is a composed script - each visible syllable block is built from an initial consonant (choseong), a medial vowel (jungseong), and an optional final consonant (jongseong). The word for hello, two syllable blocks on screen, decomposes into six jamo for braille purposes. Each jamo gets its own cell.

Layered on top of decomposition are several Korean-specific rules.

  • Position-sensitive consonants: the consonant looks the same on screen but takes different braille cells depending on whether it appears in the initial or final slot.
  • Contractions: very common words such as the Korean equivalents of "however" and "and" collapse into single dedicated cells.
  • Abbreviations: long phrases like the formal name of South Korea map to fixed short cell sequences.
  • Foreign-word transitions: embedded English requires explicit mode-switch markers before and after the foreign substring.
  • Stricter spacing: braille requires more whitespace than printed Hangul to avoid ambiguity.

Implementing all of that correctly is closer to morphological analysis than to a lookup table. This is why earlier converters scored well on isolated nouns but failed on real prose. braillify's 2,419 regression tests exist precisely to lock down these collision cases.

4. The 2024 Korean Braille Specification Revision

The 2024 revision is the first Korean braille update written with digital pipelines in mind. The previous rules assumed embossed-paper output and tolerated ambiguity that human transcribers could resolve. Automated tools cannot. The key changes break down as follows.

DomainOld rule2024 revision
Foreign wordsTransition markers ambiguousTransition points and scope explicit
NumbersNumber-sign scope disputedContinuous digits, decimals, commas unified
ContractionsCollisions possibleTie-breaking rules added
PunctuationMany quote and bracket variantsStandardized to five canonical patterns
Digital outputPrint onlyUnicode output explicitly endorsed

The last row is the quiet revolution. Until 2024 the canonical pipeline produced BRF files and sent them to braille embossers. The revision blesses Unicode Braille Patterns as a first-class output target. That single change unlocks rendering braille directly inside a webpage, streaming braille over the network to a refreshable display, and shipping braille as data inside an accessibility API. braillify rides that change.

5. Five Strengths of braillify

Let us evaluate braillify against five concrete axes.

5.1. Full 2024 Specification Support

Every novelty introduced in the 2024 revision is implemented - explicit foreign-word transitions, unified number-sign scope, contraction tie-breaking, the five canonical punctuation patterns, and Unicode output. The GitHub commit history shows the maintainers shipping spec-tracking patches within weeks of the official gazette. Rapid spec response is a structural advantage of open source over closed government tooling.

5.2. 2,419 Test Cases

Korean braille is the textbook example of a domain where "the devil is in the details". The intersection of jamo decomposition, contractions, foreign loanwords, digits, and punctuation creates an enormous combinatorial space of edge cases. braillify holds 2,419 unit-test cases - plain words, contraction-eligible words, mixed English, mixed digits, punctuation variants, abbreviations, multi-contraction collisions, every regression they have ever fixed. That number is the most defensible proof that "open source" does not mean "unreliable" here.

5.3. Painless JavaScript Integration

Installation is one line - npm install braillify. CDN users import the ES module directly. The public API is essentially one function - feed Korean text in, get a Unicode braille string out. Optional flags toggle contractions, abbreviations, foreign-mode handling, and output format. The learning curve is measured in minutes, not days.

5.4. Open Source and MIT

Charging royalties on accessibility tools is morally inconsistent - the moment your library has a price tag, the population that needs it most cannot afford the products that embed it. MIT permits any company to integrate braillify into a paid kiosk, any school to ship it inside a tutoring app, any individual to fork and improve it. The license is a small line in package.json but it is the most important political decision the maintainers made.

5.5. Browser and Node from a Single Codebase

The same compiled output runs in Chrome, in Safari, in Firefox, in Node.js, in Bun, in Deno, and on AWS Lambda. Conversion output is byte-for-byte identical across runtimes. That matters because braille content is often produced server-side for archival, then re-rendered client-side for live display - if the two stacks disagreed even by a single cell, the spec would be violated in transit. braillify's portability is therefore not a convenience, it is a correctness guarantee.

7. Developer Integration - Five-Minute Quickstart

Enough theory. Here is the working code.

7.1. Install

npm install braillify
# or via CDN
#

7.2. Basic Usage

import { toBraille } from 'braillify';

const text = '안녕하세요. 점자 변환 테스트입니다.';
const braille = toBraille(text);
console.log(braille);
// Output: a Unicode braille string in the U+2800-U+28FF range

7.3. Options

const result = toBraille(text, {
  contraction: true,       // apply contractions
  abbreviation: true,      // apply abbreviations
  foreignMode: 'auto',     // auto-switch foreign-word mode
  format: 'unicode',       // 'unicode' | 'brf' | 'numeric'
});

7.4. React Example

import { useState } from 'react';
import { toBraille } from 'braillify';

export function BrailleConverter() {
  const [input, setInput] = useState('');
  const braille = input ? toBraille(input, { contraction: true }) : '';
  return (
    <div>
      <textarea value={input} onChange={(e) => setInput(e.target.value)} />
      <div className="braille-output" aria-label="Braille output">{braille}</div>
    </div>
  );
}

7.5. Server-Side (Next.js Route Handler)

// app/api/braille/route.ts
import { toBraille } from 'braillify';

export async function POST(req) {
  const { text } = await req.json();
  const braille = toBraille(text, { contraction: true });
  return Response.json({ braille });
}

Five minutes is not marketing. Once the five steps above are in place, your Korean text is being converted to braille on demand, in any runtime your stack already uses.

8. Five Real-World Use Cases

A library that has no application is academic. Here are five concrete deployments where braillify changes the cost structure of accessibility.

Use case 1 - Automated learning material for blind students

Extract text from textbook PDFs, teacher worksheets, or workbook scans, run it through braillify, and ship the output to an embossing printer or to a refreshable braille display. The traditional pipeline involved publisher commissions measured in months. braillify reduces the cycle to minutes. Special-education programs gain back enormous amounts of teaching time.

Use case 2 - Braille learning apps

For blind children learning braille, and for sighted readers learning the system as adults, braillify can generate flashcards on demand - a "word of the day" mode, a quiz mode, a contractions drill. Wrap it in React Native and you have a mobile braille tutor that ships with the latest spec out of the box.

Use case 3 - Public-sector document accessibility

Every announcement issued as a PDF by a city office can be automatically duplicated as a braille PDF. Korean government accessibility audits increasingly weight braille availability, so this automation translates directly into measurable compliance scores. braillify's BRF output mode plugs into existing print workflows.

Use case 4 - Accessible kiosks

Pair self-service kiosks at subway stations, hospitals, and airports with a refreshable braille display, and pipe the screen text through braillify in real time. Blind users complete transactions independently. Korea's 2026-onward kiosk accessibility mandates align cleanly with this architecture.

Use case 5 - Multilingual braille services

braillify itself is Korean-only, but it composes cleanly with liblouis for English, BES-style libraries for Japanese, and Pinyin-then-liblouis pipelines for Chinese. Put all four behind one screen and you have a multilingual braille service - exactly the configuration tourist-area kiosks need.

Korean braille is roughly 2.5 times as cell-dense as English braille for the same content. That is why Korean braille books are physically heavier than their print equivalents and why refreshable displays for Korean users are recommended at eighty cells or more. Chinese is harder still - Hanzi cannot map to dots directly, so transliteration to Pinyin must happen first, with the inherent information loss of homophone collisions. Korean has the architectural advantage of consistent jamo-to-cell mappings.

10. The Future of Accessibility - AI Meets Braille

Braille is two hundred years old, but its operating environment is changing fast. Three trends will redraw accessibility tooling over the next five years.

  • Speech-to-braille pipelines: real-time speech recognition feeds braillify, which streams to a refreshable display. Deafblind users can read live conversations.
  • OCR-plus-braille apps: snap a photo of a printed page, extract text via OCR, route through braillify, save as braille PDF - the entire workflow inside one mobile app.
  • LLM-generated braille: as large language models become primary content creators, accessibility libraries must accept their output. braillify's deterministic, spec-compliant conversion is a stable foundation for that pipeline - the model can hallucinate text, but the braille mapping itself will be correct.

In an AI era, accessibility is no longer a bolt-on feature. It is core infrastructure, and tools like braillify are the foundation that infrastructure is built on.
