Korea Basic Pension - Who Qualifies and How Much It Pays in 2026

Eligibility thresholds, the single figure that decides everything, three reductions, and the application route

Aug 18, 2026 Finance & Economy

Introduction: One thing to do a month before the birthday

This article is about a South Korean welfare programme called the basic pension, or gicho yeongeum. It is a tax funded benefit paid to older residents of Korea and it has nothing to do with state pension systems elsewhere. All amounts here are in Korean won and all rules are Korean.

As a 65th birthday approaches in Korea, the same advice circulates. Go to the community centre and apply. Ask for specifics and the answers scatter. You cannot get it if you own a home. You cannot get it if you draw the National Pension. Your children earning well will disqualify you. None of the three is accurate.

The decision actually rests on a single number, the recognized income amount. It is not what arrives in a bank account. It is a monthly figure built by pressing income and assets through a fixed formula. If that number sits below the threshold published for the year, the applicant may qualify. If it sits above, they do not. Homes, children and pensions come up in conversation because each feeds into that number in its own way, not because they are separate pass or fail conditions.

So this piece is organised around that mechanism. Who falls in scope, what the 2026 figures are, how income and assets get squeezed through deductions into one number, how three separate reductions cut the payment afterwards, and what the application and appeal routes look like.

Please read first. This article is for information purposes only. Actual entitlement and payment amounts are determined by the standards in force at the time of application and by the review of the competent authority. Korea revises the basic pension thresholds, the standard pension amount and every deduction figure each year by ministerial notice. The amounts below were confirmed as of August 2026. For your own case, contact the National Pension Service on 1355, the Ministry of Health and Welfare counselling line on 129, or your local community service centre.

1. The basic pension is not the National Pension

Both carry the word pension and both are handled by the same agency, so they get mistaken for two branches of one scheme. They are closer to opposites.

The National Pension is insurance. You pay contributions while earning and later draw a benefit proportional to that record. No contributions, no benefit. The basic pension is a welfare payment made even to people who never contributed a won. It is funded from taxation and assessed on present circumstances rather than past participation. One looks at your contribution history, the other at your recognized income amount.

  • Funding. The National Pension runs on member contributions and its fund. The basic pension comes from central and local government budgets.
  • Qualification. The National Pension needs a minimum contribution period and a qualifying age. The basic pension needs age 65 and a recognized income amount below the threshold.
  • How the amount is set. The National Pension is calculated individually from contribution years and earnings. The basic pension starts from a fixed standard amount and subtracts reductions.
  • Application and dual receipt. Both are granted only on application, so doing nothing produces nothing. You can hold both at once, though National Pension recipients may face the linkage reduction described later.

The genuinely confusing point in that relationship is one thing. It is true that drawing the National Pension can shrink the basic pension, but the trigger is not the National Pension payment itself. That distinction gets its own section below.

2. Who is in scope - age 65 and the bottom 70 percent

Three conditions apply together: age 65 or over, Korean nationality, and residence in Korea. Those are the formal requirements. The substantive one is the recognized income amount.

People describe the scope as the bottom 70 percent by income, which is misleading if taken literally. Nobody ranks applicants against each other. The government works backwards each year to publish a single won figure that lets roughly 70 percent of the older population through, and the assessment simply compares your recognized income amount against that figure. That figure is the selection criteria amount.

Exclusions exist. Holders of occupational pensions, meaning the civil service, private school teacher, military and post office schemes, and their spouses, are in principle out of scope. The exceptions are numerous enough that blanket statements fail. Five years having elapsed since a lump sum payment, or receipt of only a disability or survivor lump sum, changes the position. Anyone with an occupational pension in the picture should take their own facts to the agency rather than reason from a general rule.

The whole test is one line.
Recognized income amount ≤ selection criteria amount → may qualify
Recognized income amount > selection criteria amount → out of scope

One point deserves emphasis. Children's income and assets do not enter the calculation. This is not a means test on family support capacity. There is a narrow exception where a claimant lives rent free in an expensive property owned by a child, which produces an imputed rental income line covered below.

3. The recognized income amount - collapsing income and assets into one figure

Understanding why this device exists makes everything after it easier. Judge by monthly income alone and a person with no earnings but substantial wealth sails through. Judge by assets alone and a high earner does. To weigh both you have to put them in the same unit, and the way to do that is to convert assets into a monthly income equivalent.

Recognized income amount = assessed income + income-converted value of assets

The critical feature is that neither term is a raw figure. Earned income of three million won a month does not enter as three million, and an apartment worth 500 million won does not enter as 500 million. The deductions are large enough that a sizeable gap opens between how comfortable a household looks and what its recognized income amount actually is. People run a rough calculation in their head, conclude they are out, and later discover they were in scope all along.

For an accurate figure, the simulator on the official basic pension site is the right tool. What follows explains why the simulator returns the number it does.

4. Assessed income - the earned income deduction is generous

Assessed income comes in two parts. Earned income receives a large deduction and other income mostly enters as it stands.

Assessed income = 0.7 × (earned income − 1,160,000 won) + other income
The 1.16 million won deduction is the 2026 figure and moves each year with the minimum wage.

Follow the order. First 1.16 million won comes off entirely, then a further 30 percent of the remainder is stripped, leaving 70 percent to count. Someone earning two million won a month contributes (2,000,000 − 1,160,000) × 0.7 = 588,000 won to assessed income. Two million earned, 588 thousand counted. The rationale is plain enough. If working past 65 were the reason people lost the benefit, nobody would work.

Note that this deduction attaches only to regular employment income. Daily labour income and payments from public senior employment schemes are handled differently. Other income covers the following.

  • Business income. Other business income and rental income, net of allowable expenses.
  • Property income. Interest income and income arising from pension savings and similar vehicles.
  • Public transfer income. The National Pension, occupational pensions, industrial accident benefits, unemployment benefit and other regularly paid public benefits. The basic pension itself is not counted here.
  • Imputed rent from free occupancy. An amount recognized as income where the claimant or spouse lives rent free in an expensive property owned by a child.

That last item generates constant confusion, so it needs unpacking. It applies only where the child's property has a taxable standard value of 600 million won or more, and the conversion rate is 0.78 percent a year expressed monthly. The official worked examples put a 600 million won property at 390 thousand won a month and a one billion won property at 650 thousand won a month. Below the 600 million won line, living in a child's home produces no entry at all.

5. Converting assets - the basic asset allowance and 4 percent

The asset side takes two deductions. A regional basic asset allowance comes off general property and 20 million won comes off financial assets. Debt is then subtracted, the balance is multiplied by an annual conversion rate of 4 percent, and the result is divided by twelve.

Income-converted value of assets =
[ (general property − basic asset allowance) + (financial assets − 20,000,000) − debt ] × 0.04 ÷ 12
+ value of luxury vehicles and memberships

The basic asset allowance varies by where you live, which is why an identically valued home produces different outcomes in Seoul and in a rural county.

The 4 percent rate is hard to feel until you invert it. Thirty million won of remaining property equals 100 thousand won a month. One hundred million equals roughly 333 thousand won, and 300 million equals one million won. A single person in a large city owning one home with a published price of 400 million won and no debt gets (400m − 135m) × 0.04 ÷ 12, or about 883 thousand won a month. Against the 2026 single household threshold of 2.47 million won, that leaves considerable headroom.

Three items are commonly overlooked in this calculation.

  • Debt genuinely counts. A mortgage or a lease deposit liability is deductible. It requires documentation, and if you do not submit it at application it may not be captured automatically.
  • Luxury vehicles and memberships receive no basic asset allowance. Worse, they convert at 100 percent monthly, so a vehicle valued at 40 million won is effectively counted as 40 million won of monthly income and functions as a disqualifier on its own.
  • Property gifted or disposed of on or after 1 July 2011 may be captured as other property and still counted as yours. Allowable consumption is deducted, including debt repayment and the claimant's or spouse's medical, education, funeral, wedding, alimony and child support expenses.
This article does not cover ways of moving assets to fit under the threshold. As shown above, gifts and transfers of title are captured back into the calculation as other property. Where a benefit is obtained by false or otherwise improper means, Article 19 of the Basic Pension Act requires repayment of the amount received, with interest calculated at the three year fixed deposit rate added on top. If your asset position is borderline, the correct direction is not concealment but assembling the documentation for legitimate deductions such as debt and medical expenditure.

6. The 2026 thresholds and standard pension amount

This is the part that changes annually. Keep two numbers apart. The selection criteria amount is the gate you have to get under, and the standard pension amount is the ceiling on what you receive once inside.

The ministry explained the 190 thousand won rise in the single household threshold directly. Among people aged 65 and over, public pension income rose 7.9 percent and business income 5.5 percent, while housing and land values rose 6.0 percent and 2.6 percent respectively. When the income and assets of older people rise as a group, the line dividing the bottom 70 percent rises with them. That is why a rejection last year does not settle the question this year even if nothing in your own situation changed. Reapplying has real value here.

A threshold of 2.47 million won does not mean recipients cluster near it. As of September 2025, roughly 86 percent of actual recipients had a recognized income amount of 1.5 million won or less. Borderline assessments affect a minority. Most recipients sit comfortably inside.

7. Three reductions - couple, pension linkage, and reversal prevention

Qualifying does not mean receiving 349,700 won. Three reductions of quite different character apply for quite different reasons.

ReductionWhen it appliesHow it is computedFloor
Couple reductionBoth spouses are recipientsEach individual amount cut by 20 percent—
Pension linkage reductionNational Pension benefit exceeds a set levelStandard amount less two thirds of the A benefit, plus the supplementary amount, among other stepsAround the supplementary amount
Reversal prevention reductionRecognized income plus the pension exceeds the thresholdCut by the excess so no reversal occursTen percent of the standard amount

The couple reduction

Article 8 of the Basic Pension Act provides the basis. Where both spouses receive the pension, each individual amount is cut by 20 percent. Applying the 2026 standard amount directly gives 279,760 won each and 559,520 won for the household.

A frequent misreading arises here. People assume that having a spouse automatically triggers the 20 percent cut. It does not. The cut applies only when both are actual recipients. If one spouse is not yet 65 or holds an occupational pension so that only one person receives, the full amount is paid without reduction. The couple threshold of 3,952,000 won still governs eligibility in that case.

The National Pension linkage reduction

Of the three, this one is explained wrongly most often. It is true that a larger National Pension can shrink the basic pension. But the reference value is not the total National Pension payment, it is the A benefit inside it. The A benefit is the redistributive component tied to the average earnings of all members. Because of that distinction, two people receiving identical National Pension amounts can face different reductions depending on their contribution histories.

The calculation compares two routes and takes the larger, capped at the standard pension amount.

① (standard pension amount − two thirds × A benefit) + supplementary pension amount
② 250 percent of the standard pension amount − National Pension benefit
→ whichever is larger, but never above the standard pension amount

In practice there is one line worth memorising. If the monthly National Pension benefit is at or below 150 percent of the standard pension amount, the full standard amount is paid with no reduction. For 2026 that line is 524,550 won a month. Below it, the linkage reduction is not a concern.

It is also worth listing the cases where the linkage reduction never bites at all.

  • People receiving no National Pension.
  • People whose monthly National Pension benefit is 524,550 won or less.
  • Recipients of National Pension survivor or disability benefits.
  • Recipients under the National Basic Living Security scheme.

One more point. Even where the linkage reduction applies, an amount around the supplementary pension level survives. A large National Pension does not drive the basic pension to zero, and that is where it differs from the reversal prevention reduction.

The reversal prevention reduction

The purpose is exactly what the name says. It stops someone who scraped under the threshold from ending up better off, after adding the basic pension, than someone who just missed it. Where recognized income plus the basic pension exceeds the threshold, the excess is cut away.

The arithmetic runs like this. Say a single household has a recognized income amount of 2.3 million won a month. The headroom to the 2.47 million won threshold is 170 thousand won. Add the full standard amount of 349,700 won and the total reaches 2,649,700 won, exceeding the threshold by 179,700 won. That excess is removed, leaving a payment of roughly 170 thousand won.

The effect is a gentle slope near the gate rather than a cliff. The closer you sit to the threshold, the less you receive, so the difference between passing and failing is not abrupt. Floors exist. A single household, or a couple where only one spouse receives, keeps at least ten percent of the standard amount, meaning 34,970 won a month in 2026. A household where both spouses receive keeps at least 20 percent of the standard amount.

8. Applying - when, where and what to bring

The basic pension is granted only on application. Meeting the criteria without filing produces nothing, and filing later does not automatically backdate the intervening months. That single fact causes more financial loss in practice than any calculation error.

The window opens one month before the month containing the 65th birthday. Intake runs year round, so missing that window does not mean waiting a year.

  • Where. Any community service centre or administrative welfare centre nationwide, regardless of registered address, and any National Pension Service branch. Online filing through the Bokjiro portal works too, so with authentication ready no visit is needed.
  • By proxy. A spouse, child, sibling or other relative may file on the applicant's behalf. Where mobility is limited, the agency offers a home visit service on request.
  • Core documents. An identity document such as a resident registration card, driving licence, disability card or passport, plus a bank passbook copy in the applicant's own name.
  • If you have a spouse. A financial information consent form for the spouse is required, even where the spouse is not themselves applying.
  • If you rent. Submit the lease contract, because the deposit enters the asset calculation and the associated liability is recognized.

Payment lands on the 25th of each month, or the day before when that is a holiday. Most application and consent forms are stocked at the intake office and can be completed on the spot. Debt documentation is the exception worth preparing in advance. A loan balance certificate cannot be produced at the counter, and without it your assets are counted higher than they are.

A decision takes time because income and asset verification has to run, which is another reason not to delay filing. If you are sequencing later life finances more broadly, the retirement preparation guide puts this piece in context.

9. After the decision - appeals, reporting duties, recovery

Objecting to a decision

Where you disagree with a decision or an amount, you may file an objection. Article 22 of the Basic Pension Act sets the route: in writing within 90 days of learning of the disposition, addressed to the mayor or county or district head. Where you can show a legitimate reason for missing that period, the objection may be filed within 60 days of that reason ceasing.

Objections that actually succeed usually rest on facts having been captured wrongly. A handful of causes recur.

  • Property already disposed of still recorded under the applicant's name.
  • Debt left undocumented, so assets were counted gross.
  • The residential area classified differently from reality, applying the wrong basic asset allowance.
  • Occasional income classified as regular employment income, or a vehicle valued inconsistently with its actual grade.

For that reason an objection works better when it sets out which line was captured at which value and what the true value is, with supporting paperwork, rather than expressing dissatisfaction.

Changes you have to report

Reporting duties continue after payment starts, and periodic verification reviews take place.

  • A significant increase or decrease in income or assets.
  • A change in household composition through marriage, divorce or death.
  • A change of address, particularly where the regional classification changes and with it the basic asset allowance.
  • Extended time abroad. Payment is suspended after 60 days or more outside the country.

Recovery and sanctions for improper receipt

Wrongly paid amounts are recovered. Under Article 19 of the Basic Pension Act this covers payments made during a suspension period and payments made in error for any other reason. Where the benefit was obtained by false or otherwise improper means, repayment carries interest at the three year fixed deposit rate.

In practice the problem is rarely elaborate forgery. It is a change that went unreported. Property was sold and nobody told the office, or income rose and the file stayed as it was until the next review. When the verification review surfaces it, everything received in the interim becomes recoverable at once. Reporting a change when it happens is far cheaper than the alternative.

10. Things people commonly get wrong

The same questions surface repeatedly, so here they are together.

  • Well off children disqualify you. No. This is not a family support means test. Children's income and assets do not enter the calculation. The sole exception is the imputed rent line where you live rent free in a child owned property valued at 600 million won or more.
  • Owning a home disqualifies you. No. The regional basic asset allowance is deducted first and only the balance converts at 4 percent a year. In a large city, 135 million won comes off before anything else.
  • Drawing the National Pension means no basic pension, or a cut of the same size. Neither is right. Dual receipt is possible, the reduction keys off the A benefit rather than the headline payment, and below 524,550 won a month there is no reduction at all. Even where it applies, an amount around the supplementary level remains.
  • Having a spouse means an automatic 20 percent cut. No. It applies only when both spouses are recipients.
  • One rejection settles it. No. The threshold rises annually and personal circumstances shift. The 2026 single household threshold sits 190 thousand won above the 2025 figure.
  • It starts automatically at 65. No. It is granted on application only.
  • Receiving the basic pension cuts basic living security benefits by the same amount. The interaction between the two schemes has been the subject of long running policy debate, so this article does not state a conclusion. Anyone receiving both should confirm their own case at the community service centre.
  • Splitting the registered household helps. No. The assessment unit is the claimant and spouse, not the registered household, so restructuring registration does not move the recognized income amount.

To place this within the wider picture, the National Pension guide and the pension savings fund guide cover the contributory and private layers respectively. The basic pension is a third layer that overlaps neither.

11. What could not be confirmed

These items could not be settled against official sources while writing. Rather than fill them with estimates, they are collected here.

  • Timing of the increase to 400,000 won. A plan was announced to raise the standard amount to 400,000 won a month for lower income seniors, defined by reference to 50 percent of the median income standard, before extending it to everyone in 2027. As of August 2026 the standard amount confirmed in force is 349,700 won a month. When and to what scope the separate lower income tier takes effect could not be confirmed.
  • Rounding unit for the reversal prevention reduction. The unit to which reduced amounts are rounded could not be confirmed in the source guidance. The worked example above illustrates the mechanism and may not match an actual payment to the won.
  • Whether the imputed rent parameters changed for 2026. The 600 million won threshold and the 0.78 percent annual rate come from the official site, but that page does not state an applicable year, so identity with 2026 could not be asserted.
  • The full list of occupational pension exceptions. Some exceptions were confirmed, such as five years elapsing after a lump sum, but the complete set was not enumerated. Cases involving occupational pensions need agency confirmation.
  • Interaction with basic living security livelihood benefits. How each scheme feeds into the other is an area under active policy review, so the current handling could not be stated with confidence.

Conclusion

Assessing the basic pension really takes three steps.

  1. Build the recognized income amount. Take earned income, remove 1.16 million won, count 70 percent of the rest, add other income. On the asset side remove the regional basic asset allowance and 20 million won of financial assets, subtract debt, convert at 4 percent a year and divide by twelve.
  2. Compare it to the threshold. For 2026 that is 2,470,000 won for a single household and 3,952,000 won for a couple.
  3. Check the reductions. Twenty percent each where both spouses receive, the linkage reduction where the A benefit is large, and the reversal prevention reduction near the gate.

Once that sequence is clear, the common misconceptions resolve themselves. Owning a home comes after a deduction, children's income never enters, and drawing the National Pension is measured through the A benefit rather than the headline amount. A rejection last year says little once the threshold moves.

Above all, nothing starts until an application is filed. If your own calculation is ambiguous, file rather than rule yourself out. The assessment is the agency's job anyway.
