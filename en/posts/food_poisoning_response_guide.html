Food Poisoning: What to Do Right Now and When to See a Doctor

First Steps for Vomiting and Diarrhea, and Where the Line Into Emergency Sits

Jul 26, 2026 Health
⚠️ Health information notice — This article is for general information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Consult a clinician if you have symptoms or need a decision.

🚑 Do This Now (30-second summary)

  1. Fluids and electrolytes first. Use an oral rehydration solution (ORS) rather than plain water alone, in small, frequent sips. Gulping tends to trigger more vomiting.
  2. Do not self-prescribe anti-diarrheals. Diarrhea is how the gut clears toxins, and blocking it on your own can delay recovery.
  3. Watch for dehydration. Much less urine, dry mouth and tongue, dizziness when standing up.
  4. Eat bland food once you can keep it down. Rice, porridge, dry toast, crackers, banana. Skip greasy food, alcohol and caffeine.
  5. Get help immediately for bloody stool, high fever, vomiting that stops you keeping any liquid down, or confusion and neurological symptoms - call your local emergency number.

The right action varies considerably with your symptoms, underlying conditions, and current medications. The suspicion that dinner was a mistake usually arrives in the middle of the night. Cramps, repeated trips to the bathroom, and a search box that gets typed into with one hand. What is needed at that moment is not a long essay but a short answer about what to do and what not to do right now.

This article is organized as questions and answers for exactly that situation. It sticks to guidance that public health bodies broadly agree on, and deliberately avoids definitive claims in an area where individual variation is large. When a judgment call is ambiguous, getting seen is the safer option.

1. I am vomiting and having diarrhea. What comes first?

Replacing fluids and electrolytes comes first. Across public health sources, the dehydration that follows food poisoning is consistently flagged as the faster and more dangerous problem, more so than the infection itself in otherwise healthy adults.

Drinking a lot at once irritates the stomach and often provokes another round of vomiting. The commonly suggested pattern is a mouthful at a time, every five to ten minutes. When vomiting is intense, resting the stomach for 20 to 30 minutes before restarting with very small amounts is frequently mentioned.

There is no need to force food. Early on, focus on drinking, and move to soft foods once the nausea settles. Telling someone nearby what is going on is also worth doing, so help is available if things deteriorate quickly.

2. The diarrhea is severe. Why not just take an anti-diarrheal?

Korea's national health information portal, run by the KDCA, describes diarrhea as the gut flushing out toxins and bacteria. On that basis it warns that reaching for an anti-diarrheal simply because symptoms feel severe can slow that clearance, delaying recovery and worsening the course.

The same source is explicit that such medication should be used under a doctor's direction. Antibiotics follow the same logic: depending on the organism and the clinical picture, they are useful in some cases and counterproductive in others. This is not a self-assessment area.

The risk is sharpest when bloody stool or high fever is present, because suppressing symptoms can mask exactly the signs that should be prompting a visit. That combination is a reason to be examined, not a reason to medicate.

3. Which meal caused this? Working backward from incubation periods

Blaming the most recent meal is often wrong, because the interval between exposure and symptoms varies enormously by cause. The rough ranges reported by the CDC and outbreak epidemiology literature look roughly like this.

Common causeApproximate incubationFrequently noted features
Staphylococcus aureus (toxin)About 30 min - 8 hoursFastest onset. Vomiting prominent; often settles within a day or two
NorovirusAbout 12 - 48 hoursVomiting and diarrhea together; highly contagious within households
Vibrio (incl. V. parahaemolyticus)About 1 - 2 daysCommonly linked to raw seafood in warm months
SalmonellaAbout 6 hours - 6 daysFever and abdominal pain commonly reported
CampylobacterAbout 2 - 5 daysLong lag makes the source meal hard to pin down

Treat this table as an approximate reference range; it shifts with immune status and how much was consumed. Rather than trying to identify the organism yourself, it is more useful to check whether anyone who ate the same meal has the same symptoms.

If you do get seen, being able to state what you ate, when, and when symptoms began, in order, helps. Advice to keep any leftover food refrigerated rather than throwing it out also appears frequently.

4. Is plain water enough? Why ORS exists

Diarrhea carries away sodium, potassium and other electrolytes along with water. That is why drinking large volumes of plain water alone may not restore the balance adequately, a limitation noted in rehydration guidance.

The oral rehydration salts formula standardized by the WHO sets glucose and electrolyte concentrations at levels designed for efficient absorption. KDCA material makes the same point more simply: liquid containing glucose and electrolytes is absorbed faster than pure water.

4.1 How to drink it

  • Use commercial ORS sachets or ready-made drinks from a pharmacy, mixed with exactly the stated volume of water.
  • Small amounts, often. Sipping steadily keeps more down than drinking in bulk.
  • Cool or lukewarm - pick whichever makes you less nauseated.
  • Think of it as topping up a little extra after each episode of diarrhea.

4.2 Can I mix my own sugar and salt solution?

Official sources do not treat homemade mixtures as the recommended route. The reasons given are that concentrations are easy to get wrong and that excess sugar can actually worsen diarrhea.

They appear only as a stopgap when commercial preparations are unavailable, and even then the advice is to use boiled and cooled water and to move to a proper preparation or medical care as soon as possible.

Filling up on soda or high-sugar juice is likewise discouraged. A high sugar concentration can draw water into the gut and make diarrhea worse, which is the standard explanation given.

5. Should I eat, or is fasting better?

The case for complete fasting is weak. The UK NHS suggests trying small, light, bland meals once you feel able to keep them down - with the caveat that there is no need to force it.

CategoryExamplesReason usually given
Try firstRice, porridge, dry toast, crackers, banana, boiled potatoLow irritation, easy on digestion
Avoid for nowGreasy food, spicy food, alcohol, caffeine, fizzy drinksIrritate the gut or push dehydration further
Varies by personMilk and dairyLactose digestion can dip temporarily; postpone if uncomfortable

6. When to seek care immediately - the signs that matter

⚠️ Contact a doctor without delay if you have

  • Bloody diarrhea or bleeding from the bottom
  • Fever above about 102F (38.9C)
  • Vomiting so frequent you cannot keep any liquid down
  • Diarrhea lasting more than three days, or no improvement at all
  • Marked dehydration: passing very little urine, or none for 6 to 8 hours, a parched mouth and tongue, severe dizziness on standing, unusual lethargy
  • Dehydration that persists despite oral rehydration sachets
  • Severe, unrelenting abdominal pain

🚨 Call emergency services for neurological symptoms: confusion, slurred speech, blurred or double vision, drooping eyelids, difficulty swallowing, or muscle weakness. These point to a different and potentially more serious course than ordinary gastroenteritis, so do not wait it out.

The US CDC lists bloody diarrhea, diarrhea lasting more than three days, fever above 102F, vomiting frequent enough that liquids cannot be kept down, and signs of dehydration as reasons to contact a doctor. The NHS adds dehydration that does not improve with oral rehydration sachets, along with bleeding from the bottom.

Both bodies advise a lower threshold for people who are pregnant or immunocompromised. In other words, the point at which waiting stops being reasonable is not the same for everyone.

7. Are the rules different for children, older adults, and pregnancy?

Yes. Infants and older adults dehydrate faster and signal it later, a point raised repeatedly in guidance. A situation an adult might watch for a couple of days warrants earlier assessment in these groups.

For young children, commonly cited markers include a nappy that has stayed dry for six hours or more, crying with few or no tears, sunken-looking eyes, and unusual floppiness or lethargy. Older adults may not feel thirst reliably and so will not seek fluids on their own, which makes prompting them important.

People who are pregnant, undergoing chemotherapy or immunosuppressive treatment, or living with chronic liver or kidney disease are also classed as higher risk for complications. For them, an early consultation beats trying to ride it out at home.

8. Why is it worse in summer? Prevention still wins

Heat and humidity speed bacterial growth. The time cooked food sits on the counter, the time groceries spend in a warm car, and the time a delivery bag waits at the door all become risk windows.

Korea's Ministry of Food and Drug Safety frames its guidance as three core habits - washing hands, cooking thoroughly, and boiling water - extended to six with separating raw and cooked items, washing and sanitizing, and holding storage temperatures.

8.1 The numbers worth memorizing

  • Handwashing: at least 30 seconds with soap under running water
  • Cooking: meat to a 75C core temperature, seafood to 85C, for at least one minute
  • Storage: refrigerate at or below 5C, freeze at or below minus 18C
  • Cross-contamination: separate knives and boards for raw versus ready-to-eat food

8.2 The commonly missed points

Over-trusting the refrigerator is the classic one. Chilling slows growth rather than stopping it, and a fridge that is opened constantly or packed full can run warmer inside than its setting suggests.

Reheating is similar. Warming the outside is not the same as heating all the way through, and toxins already formed in food are not always destroyed by heat - a point that comes up alongside the reheating advice.

9. Common misconceptions

  • Boiling makes anything safe. Bacteria drop, but some preformed toxins tolerate heat. Not letting food sit out in the first place is the real control.
  • Spoiled food tastes spoiled. Taste, smell and color can all be normal in contaminated food. Judging by the senses is not reliable.
  • Stopping the diarrhea speeds recovery. Forcibly blocking the clearance response is not the recommended approach; medication is a clinical decision.
  • You must fast to rest the gut. Rehydration is separate from eating. Once you can keep food down, bland options are generally encouraged.
  • It always clears in a day. Depending on the cause it can run for several days. Judge by warning signs, not by the calendar.

10. Conclusion - three things to remember

To summarize. First, replace fluids and electrolytes in small, frequent amounts. Use ORS, and treat homemade mixtures as a fallback only when nothing else is at hand.

Second, do not decide on medication yourself. Anti-diarrheals and antibiotics can delay recovery or worsen the course, which is why these are a doctor's call.

Third, memorize the warning signs. Bloody stool, high fever, vomiting that stops you keeping any liquid down, severe dehydration and neurological symptoms are not in the wait-and-see category. If you are in a higher-risk group, lower that threshold further.

After recovery, prevention is where the leverage is. Washing hands, cooking to core temperature, and holding storage temperatures are unglamorous rules that remove most of a summer's misery.
