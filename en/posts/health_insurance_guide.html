Korean Indemnity Health Insurance - Generations, Coverage and How to Claim

Start by finding out which generation your own contract is, and what its wording actually says

2026-07-31 Life & Practical

Opening: you have the receipt, but not the number

You tap your card at the hospital desk and take the receipt. The bill is 320,000 KRW. Someone asked whether you have indemnity cover, and you do, and you have been paying premiums for years. Yet you have no idea how much of it comes back. Open the app and the product name runs for a line and a half, the wording file is over two hundred pages, and every search result gives a different number.

This is where most people turn the wrong way. They start comparing products, or start asking whether they should switch. That question is out of order. The questions that come first sit much earlier.

  • Which generation is my contract. Coinsurance rates differ sharply by generation. Even counting only what is confirmed, the range runs from contracts that carried a 0 percent coinsurance rate to a generation where non-severe non-covered care sits at 50 percent
  • What does my wording cover, and what has it left out. Within one generation and one insurer, a different rider set produces a different outcome
  • Am I in a position to claim right now. Documents, deadlines, coinsurance and deductibles all stack on top of one another

This article does not say buy indemnity cover, and it does not say switch it. Generations differ sharply and the policy wording is the final authority, so judging by a general rule is usually wrong. The purpose here is one thing: to hand you the list of items to check and the places to check them. 1. Where indemnity cover fits - covered care, non-covered care, and two patient shares

To understand indemnity cover you have to look at the layer beneath it. It sits on top of Korean national health insurance; it is not a separate free-standing layer.

A Korean hospital receipt splits the amount into two broad streams.

  • Covered care. Items national health insurance applies to. The public insurer pays part of the cost, and the remainder the patient pays is the patient share
  • Non-covered care. Items national health insurance does not apply to. There is no public contribution, so the patient pays all of it

Indemnity cover addresses both streams. It reimburses the patient share of covered care and the cost of non-covered care, within the coinsurance rate, the deductible and the limits set by the wording. It does not reimburse the receipt total; it takes an already filtered amount and removes your share from it again.

1.1 The confusing part - there are two patient shares

This is the most common reason arithmetic goes wrong. Two things that sound like the same phrase operate on different layers.

TermWho sets itA percentage of whatRelation to indemnity cover
National health insurance patient shareThe public health insurance systemThe part of covered care the public insurer does not payThat amount is what indemnity cover addresses
Indemnity coinsurance rateThe individual policy wordingThe part of the addressable amount the policy does not payVaries sharply by generation and rider
DeductibleThe individual policy wordingNot a rate but a flat amount taken off firstEspecially significant for outpatient care
Non-covered careOutside national health insuranceNo public contribution, so the patient pays allAddressable, but at a higher coinsurance rate

So a 20 percent coinsurance rate on covered care is not 20 percent of the whole bill. Public insurance pays its part first, and the calculation runs on the patient share that remains. Conversely, non-covered care has no public contribution ahead of it, so the felt amount is far larger. That is why every round of reform debate circles around non-covered care.

On top of this sits the deductible. For outpatient care it is common for a flat amount to be removed before any rate is applied, which means a small bill can calculate down to zero. That is not a denial; it is an outcome. Section 6 returns to it.

2.1 What changed at the fourth generation

The terms confirmed in the Financial Services Commission press release of 29 June 2021 are as follows.

  • Coinsurance of 20 percent on covered care and 30 percent on non-covered care
  • Outpatient deductible on covered care: clinic and hospital level minimum 10,000 KRW, tertiary and general hospital minimum 20,000 KRW
  • Outpatient deductible on non-covered care: minimum 30,000 KRW
  • Re-enrollment cycle shortened from 15 years to 5 years
  • Premium differentiation on non-covered claims: five grades based on the previous year's non-covered benefits paid, applied three years after launch

The item that matters most in practice is the five-year re-enrollment cycle. Re-enrollment is not renewal. Renewal continues the contract on the same coverage terms; re-enrollment is a structure in which the standard wording in force at that moment may newly apply. In other words, good terms today do not guarantee good terms tomorrow.

2.2 The fifth generation - severe and non-severe were split

According to the Financial Services Commission press release of 6 May 2026, fifth generation products went on sale from 6 May 2026, with one insurer starting on 1 June. The largest structural change is that non-covered care was divided into severe and non-severe.

3. What falls outside cover, and how limits stack - the wording decides

The assumption that anything paid for at a hospital must be covered is the one that breaks most often. Indemnity cover reimburses what the wording defines as within scope, not simply money spent at a medical institution.

The categories most often cited as falling outside cover look like this. This is an illustrative list; whether something is actually excluded is decided by your own wording.

  • Procedures for cosmetic or aesthetic purposes, where the aim is appearance rather than treatment
  • Acts for preventive purposes, such as a health screening itself or tests in the absence of disease
  • Procedures or treatments whose connection to treatment is not recognized
  • Cases where the same procedure falls differently depending on purpose and diagnosis. This is the crux, and it is why a question like is this procedure covered cannot be answered as a general rule

3.1 There is never only one limit

Even when something is covered, the full amount does not necessarily come out. Limits are layered, and the smallest one becomes the effective limit.

  • Annual benefit limit. The total available across a year
  • Separate limits per rider. In the fifth generation, 50 million KRW for severe non-covered care and 10 million KRW for non-severe are confirmed
  • Per-visit limits and visit count caps, which attach most often to non-covered riders
  • Outpatient day limits. A cap on the number of outpatient days covered in a year
  • Deductibles. Not a limit but an amount removed first, which in effect filters out small bills
  • Annual caps on your own out-of-pocket amount. Present in some contracts and absent in others. The fifth generation's 5 million KRW annual cap on severe non-covered care is confirmed
  • Waiting periods and reduced-benefit periods. Structures where nothing, or a reduced amount, is paid for a set time after cover begins

3.2 The clauses you actually need to open and read

You do not have to read two hundred pages. A handful of clauses with fixed names answer most questions.

  • Losses covered — what is in scope
  • Losses not covered — every exclusion lives here. Read this one first
  • Grounds for payment and payment limits
  • Coinsurance and deductible clauses
  • Waiting period and reduced-benefit period
  • Renewal and re-enrollment clauses
  • Duty of disclosure before contract — directly linked to section 6
  • Duplicate insurance and proportional payment clause — directly linked to section 4

Worth repeating. Every sentence in this section is a general tendency, and the final authority is the wording of your own contract. Where an app summary, a sales explanation, or this article differs from the wording, the wording wins. That is why you need the full document, not the summary.

4. Why holding several indemnity policies achieves little

Some insurance does pay several times over if you hold several policies. Fixed-benefit products, such as a lump sum on diagnosis, work that way. Indemnity cover does not.

Indemnity means what the name says: it reimburses a loss actually borne. As a general principle it does not pay beyond the actual loss, and where several contracts apply they share the amount between them. The consequences split like this.

  • Premiums multiply with the number of contracts. Two contracts cost two premiums
  • The benefit does not multiply. The principle is that payment does not exceed the medical cost actually borne
  • In other words, you can end up in a situation where only the premium doubled

Needs checking. This article could not open and verify the duplicate insurance and proportional payment clause of the standard policy wording. The description above follows from the structure of indemnity cover as reimbursement of an actual loss, and how the shared amount is calculated is set by each contract's wording. Read the duplicate insurance or proportional payment clause in your own wording.

4.1 So the first job is confirming whether you actually overlap

Before concluding that overlap is wasteful, confirm whether you actually overlap. There are a few routes by which cover doubles up without anyone noticing.

  • Group indemnity cover provided by an employer sitting alongside a personal policy. This is the most common case
  • A contract a family member took out on your behalf
  • A travel policy that includes medical expense benefits — that relationship is covered in the complete guide to travel insurance
  • For how to consolidate overlapping cover across a whole portfolio, see the complete guide to insurance review and remodeling. This article does not restate that general material

The order of work is the same as section 9. Pull the list of contracts first, then find the relevant clause in each contract's wording. The important part is not cancelling the moment you find an overlap. Which contract to keep depends on coinsurance rates, limits, renewal terms and health status together, and every risk in section 7 applies.

5. The claim process - documents, electronic transmission, deadlines

There are two broad routes. The traditional one, where you obtain paper documents and submit them to the insurer, and the electronic one, where the medical institution transmits them to the insurer.

5.1 Claiming with documents

The documents generally required are as follows. Requirements differ by insurer, product and amount, so confirming with your own insurer before claiming is the accurate route.

Simplified small claims. Below a certain amount, many insurers accept a reduced document set through a photo submitted in an app. However, the threshold differs by insurer and product. This article could not confirm a common threshold, so it is left as needing checking. Check with your own insurer's app or call center.

5.2 Electronic claim transmission - sending it straight from the hospital

What is confirmed in Financial Services Commission material is as follows. The legal basis is an amendment to the Insurance Business Act that passed the National Assembly in October 2023.

  • Hospitals with 30 or more beds from 25 October 2024
  • Clinics and pharmacies from 25 October 2025
  • Claims are filed through a dedicated app or website with no visit to the hospital desk and no paper documents
  • The documents transmitted electronically are the statement and receipt, the itemized medical fee breakdown, and the prescription
  • 10,920 medical institutions were connected as of 21 October 2025. Put the other way, it does not work at every hospital
  • The transmission agent is the Korea Insurance Development Institute, and under the Insurance Business Act aggregating the data for other purposes is prohibited and criminally punishable. Treatment data you did not claim is not transmitted to insurers

In practice the order is simple. Check first whether the hospital you visited is connected, and fall back to a document claim if it is not. The electronic route did not remove the paper route.

5.3 Deadlines

The deadline rests on statute. Article 662 of the Commercial Act, on prescription, as confirmed at the Korea Law Information Center, provides the following.

  • The right to claim insurance benefits is extinguished by prescription if not exercised for three years
  • The right to demand the return of premiums or reserves is also three years
  • The right to claim premiums is two years
  • These periods were each extended by one year when the article was amended on 11 March 2014 by Act No. 12397
  • On the starting point, running the period from the moment of the event would be too harsh where the claimant could not have known without fault that an event had occurred, so the period is taken to run from when the claimant knew or could have known of the insured event

That said, holding out to the deadline is not a good strategy. Medical records and itemized statements become harder to reissue, and verifying facts gets harder too. The claim procedure and document requirements of your own contract are set separately by the wording, so check both together.

Do not game it. Preparing documents that differ from the treatment you actually received, or asking a provider to relabel an item as a covered one, can violate the Act on Special Measures for the Prevention of Insurance Fraud. Even if a payment goes through, it comes back as clawback and criminal exposure. Claim exactly what you received. This article is not about getting claims past assessment; it is about stopping a legitimate claim from failing on missing paperwork.

6. Common reasons a claim is denied or reduced

Non-payment comes in three broad shapes. Something is wrong with the contract itself, the event is outside the scope of cover, or the arithmetic produces zero. The third is frequently mistaken for a denial when it is not one.

  • Breach of the duty of disclosure before contract. Answering the application questionnaire in a way that differs from the facts. It can be a problem even without any intent to conceal. If your memory is vague, it is safer to check and write it down than to let it pass as unknown
  • An event within a waiting or reduced-benefit period. Some structures pay nothing, or a reduced amount, for a set time after cover begins
  • Not recognized as being for treatment. If judged cosmetic or preventive, it lands in the exclusions from section 3
  • The non-covered item is outside the scope. Whether the item printed on the itemized breakdown falls within the wording's scope, and whether medical necessity is recognized, become the issue
  • Limit exhausted. The annual limit, a rider limit or a visit cap has already been reached
  • Missing documents. Submitting only the receipt without the itemized breakdown is the classic case
  • The calculation comes to zero. After the coinsurance rate and the deductible there is nothing left. This is arithmetic, not a denial. It shows up often on small outpatient bills

Of these, the first is the hardest to undo. Even where disclosure looks disadvantageous at the time, stating the facts is safer in the end. Poor disclosure can go beyond non-payment to become grounds for terminating the contract, at which point the premiums already paid and the future cover disappear together. The choice that looks briefly advantageous at signing is the structure that becomes the most expensive.

7. Before switching - a risk list, not a recommendation

To be clear at the outset: this section says neither switch nor stay. Terms differ so much between generations that no single answer fits everyone. What follows is a list of the things that are hard to undo once changed.

  • Have you written the existing contract's terms down as numbers. Coinsurance rate, deductible, annual limit, renewal cycle, re-enrollment cycle. Take them from the certificate and the wording, not from memory
  • Have you looked at the premium as a future path rather than today's figure. Renewable premiums rise with age and loss ratio. Deciding purely because something is cheap now changes the arithmetic five years out
  • Switching and new enrollment are different acts. The Financial Services Commission stated that a fifth generation switch happens without separate underwriting at your own insurer. However, taking out a product from a different insurer is a new contract, not a switch, and underwriting applies
  • With a medical history there is risk on the new-enrollment route. An exclusion rider removing a body part or disease from cover may attach, the premium may be loaded, or the application may be rejected
  • Cancelling first and shopping afterwards is dangerous. If the new application is rejected there is nowhere to return to. The order of operations is itself the risk
  • Have you factored in the re-enrollment cycle. It has been five years since the fourth generation, and at re-enrollment the standard wording in force at that time may apply
  • Is a family member attached to the same contract. If a spouse or child is included, the blast radius is wider
  • Have you confirmed the withdrawal or reversal conditions. A Financial Services Commission news page dated 9 May 2026 stated that a policyholder who has received no benefits after switching may withdraw within six months and return to the previous product. The scope and exceptions of that condition must be confirmed directly with your own insurer

The conclusion is single. Which option is better is a calculation that requires age, health status, medical usage pattern and current contract terms all together. This article cannot run that calculation for you, and an explanation offering to run it for you deserves careful listening. An old contract with a low coinsurance rate may carry a high premium; a new contract with a low premium may carry a high coinsurance rate. Comparison only works when both axes are on the table.

8. If a dispute arises

When a claim is denied or cut far below expectation, following the order helps more than reacting emotionally.

  1. Demand the reason in writing. An explanation given over the phone is weak evidence. Ask the insurer to specify which clause of the wording the denial or reduction relies on. That single step becomes the starting point for everything after it
  2. File through the insurer's complaint channel. This is the stage of internal reconsideration
  3. Apply for financial dispute mediation at the Financial Supervisory Service. The routes are the Financial Supervisory Service website and the consumer information portal it operates
  4. Consider litigation. Manage the timing so as not to exceed the three-year prescription period under Article 662 of the Commercial Act

Material to prepare is as follows.

  • The full policy wording — the document, not a summary
  • Copies of the application and the questionnaire — decisive in disclosure disputes
  • Medical records and certificates
  • Statements and receipts, and the itemized medical fee breakdown
  • Every document exchanged with the insurer, including messages, emails and notices

On listing contact numbers. This article does not print institutional telephone numbers. Main numbers can be merged or changed, and a number frozen into an old article turns into misleading guidance. Go to the Financial Supervisory Service (fss.or.kr) and the consumer information portal FINE (fine.fss.or.kr) and use the number and procedure currently published there.

9.1 What to look at - the checklist

Once you have the certificate and the wording, check in this order. Writing these lines into a note and filling them in one by one is the fastest method.

  • The formal product name — exactly as printed on the certificate, not the casual nickname
  • Application date and coverage start date — first, because they determine the generation
  • Coinsurance rate on covered care and coinsurance rate on non-covered care
  • Outpatient deductible and how it varies by institution type (clinic, hospital, tertiary hospital)
  • Annual benefit limit, outpatient day limits, per-visit limits
  • Whether you hold three major non-covered riders, and if so their separate limits and visit caps
  • Renewal cycle and re-enrollment cycle, and the date of the next re-enrollment
  • Whether any exclusion rider attaches to a body part or disease
  • Whether you also hold group indemnity cover through an employer — the starting point for section 4
  • Waiting period and reduced-benefit period clauses
  • What you answered on the application questionnaire — directly linked to section 6
  • Premium payment end date and maturity, and any family members attached to the same contract

Only once that list is filled in can you ask a question that fits your situation. Before that, most questions are unanswerable in form. How much do I get back has no answer, but how does this treatment calculate under a third generation contract with a 10 percent coinsurance rate on covered care and a 10,000 KRW outpatient deductible does have one.

Final emphasis. Even after checking all of the above, the final authority is the full policy wording. Where a lookup screen's summary differs from the wording, the wording wins. A summary screen is where checking starts, not the evidence it rests on.

| Existing policyholders | May switch to the 5th generation **at their own insurer**, **without separate underwriting**. **Switching is not compulsory** |

## 4. Claim Document Checklist

These are the documents generally requested. **Requirements differ by insurer, product and amount, so confirm with your own insurer before claiming.**

- [ ] **Medical fee statement and receipt**
- [ ] **Itemized medical fee breakdown** — the document that lists non-covered items line by line
- [ ] **Prescription**, or pharmacy statement and receipt
- [ ] Medical certificate or treatment confirmation (may be required depending on the amount and the case)
- [ ] Identification document
- [ ] Claim form on the insurer's template
- [ ] Bank account details **in the claimant's own name**
- [ ] Admission and discharge certificate for inpatient stays
- [ ] Power of attorney and family relation documents for a proxy claim
- [ ] Threshold for simplified small claims — **needs checking** (differs by insurer and product). Confirm in your insurer's app or call center

⚠ **Never ask for documents that differ from the treatment actually received, and never ask a provider to relabel an item as a covered one.** That can violate the Act on Special Measures for the Prevention of Insurance Fraud. Claim exactly what you received.

## 5. Electronic Claim Transmission (source: Financial Services Commission)

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Legal basis | Amendment to the Insurance Business Act passed the National Assembly in **October 2023** |
| Hospitals | Hospitals with **30 or more beds** from **2024-10-25** |
| Clinics and pharmacies | From **2025-10-25** |
| Channel | The official electronic claim app or website — no visit to the hospital desk, no paper documents |
| Documents transmitted | 1. statement and receipt, 2. itemized medical fee breakdown, 3. prescription |
| Coverage | **10,920 medical institutions connected as of 2025-10-21** — **not every hospital participates** |
| Transmission agent | Korea Insurance Development Institute. Aggregation of data for other purposes is prohibited and criminally punishable; treatment data you did not claim is not transmitted |

## 6. Time Limits (source: Korea Law Information Center, Commercial Act Article 662)

- **Right to claim insurance benefits: 3 years**, extinguished by prescription if not exercised
- Right to demand return of premiums or reserves: **3 years**
- Right to claim premiums: **2 years**
- Article amended on 2014-03-11 by Act No. 12397, extending each period by one year
- Starting point: case law runs the period from **when the claimant knew or could have known that the insured event occurred**
- Practical advice: do not wait until the limit. **Obtaining documents and verifying facts gets harder with time.**

## 7. Ten Steps to Check Your Own Contract

1. [ ] Obtain the **policy certificate and the full policy wording (PDF)** from your insurer's app or call center
2. [ ] Write down the **application date and coverage start date** — these determine the generation
3. [ ] Write down the full product name, not the colloquial nickname
4. [ ] Find the **covered-care coinsurance** and **non-covered-care coinsurance** in the wording
5. [ ] Check the **outpatient deductible** and how it varies by institution type (clinic, hospital, tertiary hospital)
6. [ ] Check the **annual benefit limit**, outpatient day limits and per-visit limits
7. [ ] Check the separate limits and visit caps of the **three major non-covered riders**, if you have them
8. [ ] Check the **renewal cycle**, the **re-enrollment cycle** and the date of the next re-enrollment
9. [ ] Check whether any **exclusion rider** applies to a specific body part or disease
10. [ ] Check whether you also hold **group indemnity cover through an employer**

Also confirm:
- [ ] **What you answered on the application questionnaire** (your disclosure)
- [ ] The **waiting period and reduced-benefit period** clauses
- [ ] Premium payment end date and maturity
- [ ] Whether a spouse or child is attached to the same contract

## 8. Clauses You Must Actually Open and Read

- [ ] Losses covered
- [ ] **Losses not covered** — the exclusions live here
- [ ] Grounds for payment and payment limits
- [ ] Coinsurance and deductible clauses
- [ ] Waiting period and reduced-benefit period
- [ ] Renewal and re-enrollment clauses
- [ ] Duty of disclosure before contract
- [ ] Duplicate insurance and proportional payment — **this document could not verify the standard policy clause directly (needs checking)**

## 9. Common Reasons for Denial or Reduction

| Reason | Where to look |
|---|---|
| Breach of the duty of disclosure | Copy of the application questionnaire, medical history at the time of disclosure |
| Event within a waiting or reduced-benefit period | Waiting period clause, coverage start date |
| Judged not to be for treatment purposes | Diagnosis, medical records, the losses-not-covered clause |
| Non-covered item outside the scope | Itemized fee breakdown, scope of coverage in the wording |
| Payment amounts to zero | Coinsurance and deductible arithmetic — **an outcome, not a denial** |
| Limit exhausted | Annual limit, per-rider limits, visit caps |
| Missing documents | The list of documents the insurer requested |

## 10. Risk Checklist Before Switching — **a risk list, not a recommendation**

This section says neither switch nor stay. It collects the items that are **hard to undo**.

- [ ] Have you written down the existing contract's coinsurance, limits, renewal cycle and re-enrollment cycle **as numbers**
- [ ] Have you looked at the premium **as a future path**, not only today's figure (renewable premiums rise with age and loss ratio)
- [ ] Do you understand that although the regulator stated the 5th generation switch happens without separate underwriting, **buying a product from a different insurer is a new contract, not a switch**, and underwriting applies
- [ ] If you have a medical history, do you understand that a new application may attract an **exclusion rider, a loading, or a rejection**
- [ ] Do you understand that **cancelling first and shopping afterwards is dangerous**
- [ ] Do you understand that at re-enrollment **the standard wording in force at that time may apply**
- [ ] Have you checked whether family members are attached to the same contract
- [ ] Have you confirmed any withdrawal or reversal conditions **directly with your own insurer**

⚠ Which option is better is a calculation that requires age, health status, medical usage pattern and current contract terms together. **A general rule cannot produce that answer.**

## 11. If a Dispute Arises — Order of Steps

1. [ ] Ask the insurer for the reason for denial or reduction **in writing**, and ask **which clause of the wording it relies on**
2. [ ] File a complaint through the insurer's complaint channel
3. [ ] Apply for **financial dispute mediation** at the Financial Supervisory Service — see fss.or.kr and the consumer portal fine.fss.or.kr
4. [ ] Consider litigation if that fails, staying within the 3-year prescription period

Prepare:
- [ ] The full policy wording
- [ ] Copy of the application and questionnaire
- [ ] Medical records and certificates
- [ ] Statements, receipts and the itemized fee breakdown
- [ ] Every document exchanged with the insurer

⚠ **No telephone numbers are printed in this document.** Numbers change; read the current number from each institution's own website.
⚠ **What you were told verbally is weak evidence.** Get it in writing.

## 12. Official Places to Check

| Channel | What it shows | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Your insurer's app and call center | **The policy wording and the certificate** — the most reliable route | Screens differ by insurer |
| Policy locator service (cont.insure.or.kr) | The **list of contracts** where you are policyholder or insured | **Does not show coverage detail** |
| Coverage detail service (fine.fss.or.kr) | **Coverage detail** | Life and general insurance only from **2006**, mutual aid only from **2009** |
| FINE (fine.fss.or.kr) | Financial Supervisory Service consumer information portal | — |
| Electronic claim service | The electronic claim channel | **Only participating institutions** |
| National Health Insurance Service | Covered care, patient share, out-of-pocket ceiling scheme | Does not handle private policy wording |

## 13. What This Document Could Not Confirm (needs checking)

- Exact sales periods of the 1st to 3rd generations
- Renewal and re-enrollment cycles of the 1st to 3rd generations
- The numeric renewal cycle of the 5th generation
- The standard policy clause governing proportional sharing across duplicate contracts
- The threshold amount for simplified small claims, by insurer and product
- The national health insurance annual out-of-pocket ceiling amount
- How out-of-pocket ceiling refunds interact with indemnity payouts
- Main telephone numbers of each institution

Where to check: your own certificate and wording, then your insurer's call center, then the Financial Supervisory Service (fss.or.kr), then the National Health Insurance Service.

## 14. Do Not

- ❌ Assume everything comes back because you hold indemnity cover — coinsurance and deductibles stack
- ❌ Decide your generation by **guessing** — fix it from the application date and the certificate
- ❌ Judge from the summary screen alone — **the wording is the final authority**
- ❌ Dress up documents to differ from the treatment received — this can violate insurance fraud law
- ❌ **Cancel before checking** — with a medical history the road back may be closed
- ❌ Hold **several indemnity policies** for the same loss — payment does not exceed the actual loss, so you may only double the premium
- ❌ Accept a denial explained **only by telephone** — get it in writing with the clause number
- ❌ Use this document **instead of the policy wording** — it is only an order of checking
