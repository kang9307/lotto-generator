Introduction: Why AI Coding Measurement Keeps Fooling Us

AI-assisted coding effectiveness reports keep falling into the same trap. A slide proudly announces "35% increase in lines of code after AI adoption," "40% improvement in ticket throughput," "developer satisfaction at 9.2 out of 10," or "PR merge rate doubled" (quoted figures like these throughout this article are illustrative examples). Read those numbers in isolation and AI-assisted coding looks like the obvious next standard for every engineering organization in the world. Look closely, however, and you find a more uncomfortable truth: almost none of those numbers actually measure business value.

The starting point for this piece is Greg Wilson's "Twelve Ways to Be Wrong About AI-Assisted Coding," published on his blog The Third Bit on May 20, 2026, and picked up by the Korean tech community news.hada.io (GeekNews). Wilson's twelve are: counting lines of code, timing artificial tasks, before/after comparisons with no control group, asking developers whether they feel more productive, counting commits, PRs and tickets, measuring only the easy half, treating adoption rate as success, comparing volunteers to non-volunteers, measuring the individual instead of the system, measuring during the novelty period, treating suggestion acceptance rate as a quality signal, and comparing AI to nothing. The twelve traps below overlap with several of those, but the list itself is this article's own. The broader pattern is the same across regions: organizations measure AI coding the wrong way, and then make multimillion-dollar tooling decisions based on those wrong measurements. The problem is even sharper in markets where activity-based metrics dominate, but the underlying mistakes are global, not local.

This article does two things. First, it walks through twelve common ways people get AI coding measurement wrong, with concrete examples and the downstream cost each one creates. Second, since giving up on measurement is not an option, it proposes seven outcome-based alternatives that actually correlate with business value, and explains why most engineering organizations are not yet equipped to track them. This is not an argument that AI-assisted coding does not work. It clearly does. The argument is that the way we measure it is making executives confident about the wrong things, and that confidence is shaping the next five years of investment in a misleading direction.

Trap 1: Lines of Code (LoC) - More Lines Are Not More Value

The most common and most damaging trap. When a slide announces "35% more lines of code after AI adoption," it misses two fundamental things. First, lines of code measure volume, not value. The same feature implemented in 200 lines or 1,000 lines delivers the same user value, and every additional line increases the surface area that needs to be read, reviewed, tested, deployed, and maintained. Second, AI tools are particularly good at producing boilerplate and repetitive code, so the line count tends to inflate in exactly the categories where line count has the weakest connection to business outcome.

Take a hypothetical example. A team reports a 2.3x increase in average lines changed per PR six months after AI adoption and frames that as "doubled productivity." If, during the same window, the defect rate rose by 1.7x and average PR review time grew by 1.5x, then counted only by lines the launch looks like a triumph, while counted by downstream cost the net effect can be negative. Real data points in a similar direction. GitClear's January 2024 report, which analyzed 153 million changed lines of code authored from 2020 to 2023, projected that code churn (lines reverted or updated within two weeks of being written) would double in 2024 compared with its pre-AI 2021 baseline, and reported more copy/pasted code and less code reuse. The old analogy that measuring programming progress by lines of code is like measuring aircraft building progress by weight (often attributed to Bill Gates, though no original source has been confirmed) applies just as sharply to AI-generated code.

Trap 2: Ticket Throughput - Closing More Tickets Is Not Real Progress

The second trap is treating "tickets closed per sprint" from Jira, Linear, or any other issue tracker as if it were a proxy for AI coding effectiveness. When AI tools land, developers can close small tickets faster, and the headline ticket count rises. The mistake is assuming a rising ticket count means rising business value. A ticket is a unit of activity, not a unit of value.

A common pattern follows AI adoption. When throughput rises, product managers and engineering managers start slicing work into smaller tickets, on the logic that "if AI is helping us move faster, we can also tackle all those small improvements we used to defer." The ticket count balloons, but the share of tickets that move a real business needle shrinks. One hundred button-color changes look enormous in a throughput chart and trivial in a user-behavior chart. Counting tickets counts busyness, not value, and once teams optimize for the count, they tend to make the value side worse.

Trap 3: Developer Satisfaction Surveys - Happiness Is Not Output

The third trap is treating a survey result like "developer satisfaction is 8.7 out of 10 after AI rollout" as the headline metric for AI coding effectiveness. Satisfaction is genuinely useful for predicting adoption: if satisfaction is low, people stop using the tool. But the link between satisfaction and the value of the produced artifact is much weaker than the headline suggests.

A further uncomfortable point is that the more developers trust a tool, the less carefully they may verify its output. No study has measured satisfaction directly on this, but there is close evidence. In an experiment by Stanford researchers (Perry et al.) published at CCS 2023, participants with access to an AI assistant wrote significantly less secure code than those without, and were more likely to believe their code was secure. Participants who were more skeptical of the AI and refined their prompts more produced code with fewer vulnerabilities. That is the path from a "looks fine, ship it" reflex to defects. Satisfaction belongs in the dashboard as a secondary indicator that tells you whether to keep paying for a tool, but it is the wrong primary metric for deciding whether AI coding is creating business value.

Trap 4: PR Merge Count - Merging Is Not the Same as Delivering Value

The fourth trap is treating "PRs merged per week increased 60%" as a productivity headline. A merge is the event of code reaching main. It is not the event of users receiving value. Two very common reasons that merge counts rise after AI adoption, neither of which improves outcomes, are worth naming.

  1. PRs get smaller. AI-assisted refactoring makes it easy to split work into many small PRs. One engineer who used to ship one larger PR per day now ships three smaller PRs. The merge count triples without any change in the underlying business work.
  2. Hotfix and rollback PRs increase. Less rigorous verification of AI output leads to more production incidents, which produce more hotfix PRs and more rollback PRs. The merge count climbs, but a growing fraction of it is engineers fixing problems that the previous merge caused.

Both patterns inflate the merge count while the business outcome stays flat or moves backward. The more decision-makers cheer the merge count, the more both patterns are reinforced, and the easier it becomes for an organization to fall into the "busy but not valuable" failure mode.

Trap 5: Self-Reported Time Savings - Cognitive Bias Inflates Everything

The fifth trap is taking self-reported numbers like "I save two hours per day with AI" at face value. Self-reported data tends to lean in one direction: people can feel that AI saves them more time than it actually does. One study showed this with measurements.

It is METR's randomized controlled trial, published in July 2025. Sixteen experienced open-source developers worked on 246 real issues in repositories they had contributed to (averaging 22k+ stars and 1M+ lines of code). Tasks where AI was allowed took 19% longer. Before the study, participants expected AI to speed them up by 24%, and even afterward they estimated it had made them 20% faster. Perception and measurement pointed in opposite directions. METR stressed that this measured early-2025 tools (Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet) in one specific setting and is not evidence that AI fails to speed up most developers. In February 2026 METR said it was changing its experiment design because selection effects had grown, with developers declining to participate without AI, and that its newer figures are only very weak evidence and the true speedup could be much higher. The lesson that perception cannot replace measurement still stands. The reason perception inflates is not deception; it is memory. People remember the moments of fast typing while AI is suggesting code, and forget the time spent verifying, correcting, and re-verifying that output. Self-reports are good for satisfaction and useless for effectiveness. (When first published, this passage attributed the finding to GitClear's 2024 report. GitClear analyzed code change history, not self-reported time, so it has been corrected to the METR trial.)

Trap 6: AI Usage Rate - A Ratio Does Not Guarantee Quality

The sixth trap is treating "28% of our code is AI-generated" or the suggestion acceptance rate shown on an AI coding tool's dashboard as a KPI. The only thing these ratios reliably measure is "developers accepted AI suggestions often." Whether the result is durable, secure, maintainable, or aligned with the product strategy is not encoded in the number at all.

The danger sharpens the moment an executive sets "reach 50% AI usage rate" as an OKR. From that moment, developers have a measurable incentive to accept more suggestions, which means accepting them with less verification. This is the textbook case of Goodhart's Law: once a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure (the widely quoted wording is anthropologist Marilyn Strathern's 1997 restatement of economist Goodhart's 1975 formulation). AI coding metrics are unusually prone to this trap because the activity being measured (accepting a suggestion) is exactly the activity whose quality the metric is supposed to assess.

Trap 7: Star Counts and Adoption Numbers - Popularity Is Not ROI

The seventh trap is using the popularity of AI coding tools as evidence of their effectiveness inside your organization. "Millions of users" or "tens of thousands of GitHub stars" prove one thing: that the market is paying attention. They prove nothing about the ROI any specific company will realize from adopting that tool.

The most dangerous form of this trap is FOMO-driven adoption. An executive decides to adopt the tool because "everyone else is," and then retroactively cites popularity metrics as justification. The effectiveness of a tool depends on company context, domain, codebase maturity, and team culture, all of which vary widely between organizations. Popularity is a signal worth investigating, never evidence of ROI worth booking.

Trap 8: Cycle Time Reduction - Faster Cycles Are Not Better Outcomes

The eighth trap is treating cycle time reduction as direct evidence of AI coding effectiveness. When first published, this article called cycle time "a DORA Four Key metric," which is not accurate. As of 2026-09-29, DORA's official guide lists five software delivery metrics: change lead time (from commit to production), deployment frequency, failed deployment recovery time, change fail rate, and deployment rework rate. The closest to what people usually call cycle time is change lead time. Shorter cycle time looks unambiguously good. The catch is that the cycle time reduction created by AI adoption is often produced by shortening or skipping verification steps, not by genuinely accelerating the underlying work.

As a hypothetical example, suppose a team's average cycle time fell from three days to one and a half days after AI adoption and was announced as a "50% improvement," while mean time to detect production defects rose from four hours to eighteen hours in the same window. Defects piled up as fast as the deployments did. Cycle time reduction accompanied by rising defect rate, rising operational cost, or worsening MTTR is not improvement; it is borrowing. Organization-level research shows the same risk. The DORA 2024 report, announced by Google Cloud in October 2024, found that a 25% increase in AI adoption was associated with better documentation quality, code quality and code review speed, but was accompanied by an estimated 1.5% decrease in delivery throughput and an estimated 7.2% reduction in delivery stability. DORA's guidance is to read throughput and instability together, but most AI effectiveness reports extract cycle time and leave the rest behind.

Trap 9: Measuring Only Bug Detection Time - Defending Hourly Cost, Not Quality

The ninth trap is using "30% more bugs caught in code review after AI adoption" as evidence of AI effectiveness. On the surface it sounds good: catching bugs earlier is better. But this number really means one of two things. Either (a) AI-generated code introduces more bugs to catch, or (b) the AI-assisted review tooling surfaces more issues than before. Neither is evidence of business value improvement.

A far better signal is the six-month trend of defects that reach production. Bugs caught in review protect engineer hours, but they do not measure the defects that real users encountered. To measure AI coding effectiveness honestly, you need downstream metrics: production defect count, severity-weighted defect impact, MTTR, customer-facing incident rate. Evaluating AI coding solely from review-stage numbers is like a doctor evaluating patient health by counting their own prescriptions written, not patient outcomes.

Trap 10: Single-Project, Single-Window Comparisons - Statistically Invalid

The tenth trap is generalizing from a before-and-after comparison on a single project. "Project A was 30% faster after we adopted AI" is almost always statistically invalid. Even for the same team and same project, work mix, complexity, external dependencies, and team energy vary by quarter, and all of those variables move the measurement. Maybe the AI adoption window happened to overlap with a stretch of unusually simple work, or with a period when a new hire was rapidly climbing their learning curve.

A defensible measurement needs at least three comparison groups, an observation window of six months or longer, and explicit controls for major confounding variables. Full academic RCT rigor is rare in industry, but an A/B-style rollout where some teams use AI and some teams do not, observed for two to three quarters, is realistic and is what every serious AI coding effectiveness claim should be built on. A single project, single window comparison has roughly the evidentiary weight of "I ate dumplings for lunch yesterday and my afternoon coding went well."

Trap 11: Generalizing From Personal Experience - "It Worked for Me, So It Works for Everyone"

The eleventh trap is the most common and the least recognized. An engineer has a personal win with AI tooling and immediately extrapolates to "every developer should use AI." That is sample-size-one reasoning, the weakest statistical inference there is. AI coding effectiveness varies dramatically across cohorts, and ignoring that variation produces advice that is right for some and badly wrong for others.

Commonly cited tendencies (tendencies, not measurements):

  • Junior developers: Short-term output rises, but learning often stalls. They are more likely than seniors to accept AI code without understanding the underlying reasoning. Long-term skill development is the casualty.
  • Senior developers: Use AI selectively. Heavy use for boilerplate, scaffolding, and tests, near-zero use for core design decisions. There is a real benefit, but headline numbers like "30% faster" often do not show up as such in their actual work.
  • Legacy codebases: AI effectiveness tends to be weaker. Context is large, domain knowledge is specialized, and AI suggestions can be wrong in subtle ways that take longer to debug than to write from scratch. The METR trial above also studied experienced developers working in large, mature repositories they knew well, and in that setting AI-allowed tasks took longer.
  • Greenfield projects: AI effectiveness tends to be stronger. Context is small, the work leans heavily on standard libraries, and AI output is comparatively even in quality.

If you do not know which cell your personal experience came from, generalizing from it gives people in other cells the wrong advice. "It worked for me" should always carry the qualifier "in my context."

Trap 12: Measuring Only Coding, Ignoring Business Value - Missing the Whole Point

The twelfth and most fundamental trap. The shared root of the previous eleven is this: they all measure coding activity and ignore business value. The point of AI-assisted coding is not faster typing. It is faster delivery of better products to users. That value is measured by revenue, conversion, retention, NPS, cost reduction, and operational reliability, not by lines, tickets, or merges.

Yet most AI coding effectiveness reports stop at the coding step. Lines, tickets, PRs, cycle time are all proxies for business value, and proxies regularly disagree with the underlying metric they pretend to represent. The single most useful discipline an engineering organization can adopt is requiring every AI coding effectiveness report to be paired with at least one business KPI. The finding that "after AI adoption, lines of code rose but conversion is flat" is worth far more than the finding that "lines of code rose 35%."

What to Measure Instead - Seven Alternative Metrics

If activity metrics distort the picture, what should we measure? Seven alternatives, all outcome-oriented, all designed to be read over months rather than weeks.

Alternative 1) User Satisfaction Change

NPS, CSAT, or qualitative interview data tracked quarter over quarter. If AI is helping ship better features faster, user satisfaction should move up. If it stays flat, AI is at best delivering the same value faster. If it moves down, AI-introduced shortcuts in verification are eroding user experience and the team needs to slow down.

Alternative 2) Production Defect Rate (Six-Month Trend)

Defects that real users encountered, weighted by severity, plotted over six-month windows. Ignore review-stage catches; they protect engineering hours, not customer experience. A stable or improving production defect rate after AI rollout is a real success signal. A rising one is a directive to strengthen verification practices, not a directive to abandon the tool.

Alternative 3) Reliability SLO Attainment

Availability, latency, error rate, all expressed as SLO attainment percentages. AI rollouts that affect system reliability will show up here whether the engineering organization wants them to or not. SLO trends are also harder to game than activity counts, which is exactly why they belong in the dashboard.

Alternative 4) Net New Value vs. Maintenance Ratio

The share of engineering hours spent on net new value compared to the share spent on maintenance and technical debt service. If AI accelerates short-term shipping but also accelerates technical debt accumulation, this ratio will tilt toward maintenance over time. A declining new-value share is one of the clearest leading indicators that AI rollout is creating downstream cost.

Alternative 5) Correlation With Business KPIs

Time-series correlation between AI adoption milestones and core business KPIs (revenue, conversion, retention, cost reduction). Causal inference is hard in industry, but a near-zero or negative correlation over a year is a strong signal that the AI coding investment is not producing the value the headline metrics claim.

Alternative 6) Developer Learning Curve (Long Term)

Time for juniors to reach senior level, time for new hires to reach full productivity, domain knowledge assessment scores. Teams that lean heavily on AI tend to show good short-term output and flat long-term learning curves. This signal only becomes visible at twelve to twenty-four months, which is exactly why most organizations miss it.

Alternative 7) Organizational Decision-Making Speed

How long it takes to decide whether to build a feature, and how many people are involved in that decision. As AI shrinks implementation time, the organizational bottleneck moves to decision-making. If decision-making speed does not improve in parallel, the AI coding gain is capped at the speed of the slowest meeting.

Why Many Organizations Are Especially Prone to These Traps

Three structural reasons make organizations fall into measurement traps more often than they should. Naming them makes them easier to resist.

The first is billing structures that reward activity. Outsourced engineering work historically priced on person-months and lines of code creates a direct financial incentive to count activity as value. When AI raises the line count, the billing system happily converts that into "1.4x productivity," even when the underlying business value has not moved. Internal organizations that mirror this billing pattern, even unofficially, inherit the same distortion.

The second is short reporting cycles. Startups operating on quarterly investor updates need numbers before six-month trends have had time to stabilize. The metrics that fit the available time window are short-term activity counts, exactly the metrics most prone to the traps above. Long-term outcome metrics never get started because the first reporting cycle arrives before they would produce a result.

The third is hierarchical decision cultures. When an executive asks for "the AI effectiveness report," the team builds the report the executive wants to read. Lines of code and satisfaction are executive-friendly numbers; rising defect trends are not. The structure systematically degrades measurement honesty over time, and the only counter is a discipline of pairing every activity metric with a business KPI so the executive sees the full picture whether they want to or not.

Conclusion and Three Things You Can Do Tomorrow

The one-sentence summary of this article is this: most AI coding measurement confuses activity with value. Lines, tickets, satisfaction, PR counts, and cycle time are activity metrics. An increase in activity is not an increase in value, and treating it as one is leading organizations to make confident decisions in the wrong direction.

Three concrete actions you can adopt immediately:

  1. Pair every AI coding effectiveness report with at least one business KPI. Whenever a slide says "lines of code rose 30% after AI adoption," the next line must say "during the same window, conversion rate moved X% and production defect rate moved Y%." One additional line dramatically improves the honesty of the entire report and is almost free to implement.
  2. Make six-month trend the default reporting unit. Drop single-point comparisons. Report every metric on a six-month or longer trend line. Short-term fluctuations stay visible but stop driving decisions. This is the single most effective intervention against decision-makers being whipsawed by short-term numbers.
  3. Quarterly review what you are not measuring. Defect trend, SLO attainment, learning curve, decision-making speed, and similar long-horizon metrics will stay invisible forever if no one is tracking them. A quarterly fifteen-minute review answering "what important thing did we fail to measure last quarter?" shrinks the blind spots considerably.

This article does not deny that AI-assisted coding works. It clearly does, in measurable ways, in many contexts. The argument is narrower and more important: the numbers we are using to prove that it works are mostly the wrong numbers, and the wrong numbers are pointing organizations in a wrong direction. Escaping the measurement traps is the prerequisite for seeing AI coding's real value, and seeing real value is the prerequisite for spending the next five years' tooling budget wisely. The single discipline of asking "is the number on this slide actually measuring business value?" once per quarter is enough to lift an engineering organization's measurement maturity a full step.

# AI Coding Effectiveness Report Checklist - Not Reading Activity as Value

As of: 2026-09-29.
Research figures come from the public releases of METR, DORA (as announced by Google Cloud), GitClear and Perry et al. (CCS 2023). The seven alternative metrics and the six-month and twelve-to-twenty-four-month windows are this article's recommendations, not research findings.

---

## 1. First checks when a report lands

- [ ] Is the headline number an activity metric (lines, tickets, PR merges, cycle time, AI usage rate, acceptance rate, satisfaction) or an outcome metric?
- [ ] Are defect rate, review time and MTTR for the same window shown alongside it?
- [ ] Is at least one business KPI (revenue, conversion, retention, cost reduction) attached?
- [ ] Is there a comparison group, or only a before/after on one project?
- [ ] Is the observation window six months or longer?
- [ ] Are time-savings figures self-reported or measured?
- [ ] Are popularity numbers such as user counts or GitHub stars being used as evidence of effectiveness?
- [ ] Is "reach X% AI usage" set as a target (OKR)? If so, it creates pressure to verify less

---

## 2. What to pair with each activity metric

| Metric in the report | What it actually measures | What to read with it |
|---|---|---|
| Lines of code | Volume of code | Defect rate, review time, share of code rewritten within two weeks |
| Tickets closed | Busyness | Share of items with real business impact |
| Developer satisfaction | Whether to keep the tool | Production defect rate (satisfaction does not guarantee output value) |
| PR merge count | Number of merge events | Change in PR size, share of hotfix and rollback PRs |
| Self-reported time saved | Perception | Measured task time |
| AI-generated share, acceptance rate | How often suggestions were accepted | Defects, security defects, maintenance cost six months later |
| User counts, stars | Market attention | Outcome metrics inside your own organization |
| Cycle time | Speed to deployment | Change fail rate, failed deployment recovery time, time to detect defects |
| Bugs caught in review | What review caught | Six-month trend of defects that reached production |

## 4. Seven alternative metrics

| Metric | What to look at | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| User satisfaction change | Quarterly trend of NPS, CSAT, interviews | Flat or falling |
| Production defect rate | Count and severity of defects users actually hit, in six-month windows | Rising |
| SLO attainment | Availability, latency, error rate | Falling |
| New value vs. maintenance ratio | How engineering time is split | Tilting toward maintenance |
| Correlation with business KPIs | Time-series correlation of adoption with revenue, conversion, retention, cost | Zero or negative |
| Developer learning curve | Time to senior level, time for new hires to reach full productivity | Flattening when tracked for twelve months or more |
| Decision-making speed | Time and number of people needed to decide whether to build | Implementation got faster but decisions did not |
