Angina Symptoms and Causes - Stable vs Unstable, and When to Call for Help

How to read a tight, squeezing chest - from mechanism to differential diagnosis and red flags

Aug 16, 2026 Health & Wellness
Stated up front. This article is for information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. If you have chest pain, seek medical care without delay. Nothing below should be used to diagnose yourself or to postpone a visit to a clinician.

Only when climbing stairs does the middle of the chest feel heavy and pressed. Rest for a few minutes and it clears. So it gets brushed aside. That pattern is the story angina tells most often, and it is also the one most often missed.

Angina is less the name of a disease than a signal that the heart muscle is not receiving as much oxygen as it needs. The clinical reference literature of the US National Library of Medicine defines it as chest discomfort resulting from myocardial ischaemia. So the axis of this article is not the label on the pain but two questions: what is out of balance with what, and is that imbalance happening right now. The answer to the second question decides whether this is an emergency.

What follows covers causes and symptoms, the conditions that must be told apart from angina, and what the tests are looking for. The choice of medicines and procedures belongs to whoever prescribes them, so it is not covered here.

Call emergency services now if any of these apply

There is a reason this comes first rather than last. People do read up on angina and then hesitate at the exact moment when hesitation costs the most. If any of the following is present, the call comes before the reasoning.

Call emergency services immediately — if even one of these applies, an acute coronary syndrome including myocardial infarction cannot be ruled out. Do not wait it out. Call your local emergency number, describe the situation, and follow the dispatcher instructions.
  • Chest pain has lasted more than 20 minutes and is not settling
  • The pain began at rest, or was severe enough to wake you from sleep
  • You are breaking out in a cold sweat or turning pale
  • Nausea or vomiting comes with it
  • You feel confused, or you have fainted
  • The pain spreads to the jaw, neck, shoulder, either arm, back or upper abdomen
  • You are short of breath even while sitting still
  • You have known angina but it now comes more often, more severely, or with less exertion
An ambulance is preferred over driving yourself. Treatment can begin during transport, and your condition may worsen on the way. The emergency number differs by country - 911 in North America, 999 in the United Kingdom, 112 across the European Union, and 119 in Korea and Japan.

Korean public health information advises strongly suspecting acute myocardial infarction when chest pain lasting 30 minutes or more comes with sweating. That does not mean waiting out 30 minutes before calling. The figure is a threshold of certainty, not a waiting period.

The American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology chest pain guideline points the same way. It puts near the top of its take-home messages the fact that the term chest pain does not mean pain confined to the chest. Pressure, tightness, squeezing, heaviness or burning counts within the same category whether it appears in the chest, shoulder, arm, neck, upper abdomen or jaw. So a numb arm or an aching jaw should never be closed off with the reasoning that the chest does not hurt, therefore it is not the heart.

What actually goes wrong - the supply and demand mismatch

The heart is a muscle that never stops, and that muscle needs its own separate blood supply. The vessels that deliver it wrap the surface of the heart like a crown, which is where the name coronary arteries comes from. The mechanism of angina reduces to one thing happening in those vessels: demand and supply falling out of step.

  • What pushes demand up — brisk walking, inclines, stairs, heavy loads, cold exposure, a large meal, and emotional load such as anger or tension. All raise heart rate and blood pressure, increasing the oxygen the heart muscle consumes.
  • What pulls supply down — atherosclerotic plaque built from lipids and inflammatory cells narrows the channel inside the coronary artery. A temporary constriction of the vessel, called spasm, produces the same result.

At rest, enough still gets through a narrowed vessel. The problem appears when the reserve is gone. Clinical reference sources describe lesions narrowing the lumen of a major vessel by 70% or more as flow-limiting. At that point everything is fine at rest, and the shortfall only surfaces the moment exertion loads the system. This is where the familiar account — it only hurts on the stairs and rest fixes it — comes from. Not because the disease is mild, but because the deficit only shows under load.

Atherosclerosis is not the whole story. Ischaemia can arise from spasm or microvascular dysfunction while the large vessels look fine. That case gets its own section below.

Stable versus unstable - the line that decides whether it is an emergency

The most practical distinction in angina is not severity but whether the pattern is settled. Stable angina describes a state where the trigger and the duration stay within a predictable range. Unstable angina is the state where that predictability has broken, and it belongs to the acute coronary syndromes. In other words, it is an emergency.

The conditions clinical references set out for unstable angina are met if any single one applies: newly in onset, changing in severity or frequency, or occurring at rest or lasting longer than 20 minutes. The second of those deserves particular attention. The fact that a long-standing symptom is changing in the direction of worse is itself the warning.

The long-standing scale for grading stable angina is the Canadian Cardiovascular Society classification. Class I means ordinary activity causes no symptoms and angina appears only with strenuous or prolonged exertion. Class II means slight limitation, with symptoms on walking quickly or uphill, after meals, in cold or wind, or under emotional stress. Class III means marked limitation, with symptoms after one or two blocks on the level or one flight of stairs. Class IV means being unable to carry out any physical activity without discomfort, with angina possibly present at rest. If that grade is moving upward, that movement is itself a change in pattern, and it is not something to postpone.

What the pain actually feels like - typical patterns and quiet symptoms

Patterns reported more often

  • Pressure, tightness or squeezing in the centre or left side of the chest, sometimes described as burning or as a heavy weight resting on it
  • A location hard to pinpoint with a fingertip, which people tend to indicate with a flat palm across a broad area
  • Radiation to the jaw, neck, shoulder, inner left arm, back or upper abdomen
  • Accompanying shortness of breath, cold sweating, palpitations, lightheadedness or profound fatigue
  • A course that is reproducibly triggered by exertion, cold, large meals or emotional arousal and eases with rest

Patterns less likely to come from the heart

  • A sharp jab that passes in seconds
  • Pain you can pinpoint exactly with one fingertip
  • Pain reproduced by pressing on the spot or by a particular posture
  • Pain that worsens only on breathing in

These lists adjust probability. They are not exclusion criteria, and the next section explains why.

Quiet symptoms - women, older adults and diabetes

Dismissing the heart because the textbook chest pain is absent leads to delayed diagnosis over and over. Several strands of evidence explain why.

  • Women — chest discomfort is still the most common symptom, but the chest pain guideline records that women with acute coronary syndrome frequently have nausea, palpitations and shortness of breath alongside it. When upper abdominal pain, vomiting, shoulder or back pain, or unexplained severe fatigue lead the picture, it is easily filed as a digestive problem.
  • Diabetes — autonomic neuropathy is repeatedly reported to blunt the perception of ischaemic pain, so a larger share of ischaemia progresses without pain at all.
  • Older adults — breathlessness, weakness, sudden confusion or loss of appetite may come before pain, if pain appears at all.
  • Silent ischaemia generally — clinical references state that roughly 70% to 80% of transient ischaemic episodes occur without anginal chest symptoms, and that older people, those with diabetes, and those with prior infarction or revascularisation are particularly susceptible.

One terminology point is worth flagging here. Such presentations were long called atypical chest pain, and the chest pain guideline explicitly discourages that term. It was meant to denote angina without the classic chest symptoms, but in practice it came to be read as this is not cardiac, which caused harm. The guideline recommends describing pain as cardiac, possibly cardiac, or noncardiac instead. It looks like a proposal about wording, but the substance is this: a symptom differing from the textbook is not grounds for ruling out the heart.

Vasospastic and microvascular angina - blocked arteries are not the only cause

This section carries particular weight for East Asian readers. Vasospastic angina, also called variant or Prinzmetal angina, arises when a coronary artery constricts transiently, and it is reported more frequently in East Asian populations. An international comparative study reported spasm in 24.3% of Japanese, 19.3% of other Asian and 7.5% of Caucasian patients, with multivessel spasm also higher in Japanese patients at around 23% against 7.5%. One proposed background factor is the reduced-activity variant of aldehyde dehydrogenase 2, which is common in Japanese, Korean and Chinese populations at roughly 30% to 50%.

What stands out in vasospastic angina is the time of day. Symptoms are reported to arise not on exertion but at night or in the early morning hours, while at rest. Read alongside the red flag list above, which includes pain at rest, this makes judgement harder rather than easier. That is precisely why it is not a matter for self-assessment. Chest pain that starts at rest cannot have its cause sorted out on the spot, so treating it as an emergency is the safer side of the error.

The second case is ischaemia with clear large vessels. The European Society of Cardiology guideline on chronic coronary syndromes has expanded this into a section of its own. Among patients sent for angiography because of angina symptoms, a substantial proportion have no obstructive disease, and in a share of those, ischaemia is genuinely demonstrated. Proposed causes include microvascular dysfunction, spasm, myocardial bridging and diffuse atherosclerosis, alone or in combination.

The practical implication is single. A report of clear arteries does not mean there is no symptom. If the symptom persists, saying so plainly is the better move.

Risk factors - what you can change and what you cannot

The atherosclerosis underlying angina does not appear overnight. The factors that Korean public health information and international guidelines both list divide as follows.

  • Modifiable — smoking, high blood pressure, dyslipidaemia, diabetes, obesity and physical inactivity, with chronic psychological load also cited
  • Not modifiable — age (thresholds of 45 and over for men and 55 and over for women are cited), sex, ethnicity and family history

The unmodifiable column is listed not to invite resignation. For the same symptom, the pre-test probability that the heart is the source rises in someone carrying several stacked risk factors. That information is genuinely used in deciding which way to test. It is also worth remembering that vasospastic angina is reported in people without the traditional risk factors at all. An absence of risk factors is likewise not grounds for exclusion.

Other reasons a chest can hurt - the differential

The causes of chest pain are broad. Not every aching chest is angina, and neither can it be declared not to be. The purpose of this section is not to help you tell them apart yourself but to show that either way, several entries on the list are emergencies.

The top three rows of the right-hand column make the point of this section. Concluding that it is not angina does not amount to reassurance. Aortic dissection and pulmonary embolism are not coronary artery problems at all, yet each threatens life on its own. Self-triage does not work for severe chest pain of sudden onset.

What the tests are actually looking for

There is no need to memorise a list of investigations. But knowing what each test is trying to establish makes it clear why several are done and why one normal result is not the end of it.

  • Electrocardiogram — looks for traces of ischaemia or injury in the electrical signal at this moment. The chest pain guideline places it as fundamental in both acute and stable chest pain. A recording taken when symptoms are absent is often normal
  • High-sensitivity cardiac troponin — establishes from blood whether heart muscle has actually been injured. The guideline states a preference for this assay in detecting myocardial injury, and this is the practical basis on which angina and infarction are separated
  • Stress testing — raises demand through exercise or medication to expose a deficit that does not show at rest
  • Coronary computed tomography angiography — shows anatomical narrowing non-invasively
  • Invasive coronary angiography — opacifies the vessels through a catheter to confirm the site and severity of lesions, with provocation testing sometimes used to confirm spasm

The guideline also stresses that stacking tests layer upon layer is not always a benefit. It recommends selecting investigations according to risk, and holds that in acute chest pain assessed at very low risk of a major cardiac event within 30 days, further urgent cardiac testing may not be required. Which tests are done and in what order is a judgement for the treating clinician, integrating symptoms, risk and other results. The choice of medicines and procedures sits outside this article for the same reason.

How strong is the evidence for risk factor management

Here it is more honest to separate the strength of the evidence by question. Reducing symptoms and reducing later cardiovascular events are different questions, and the evidence is not equally thick for both.

Frequently asked questions

How is angina different from a heart attack?

Both come from the heart muscle receiving too little blood, but the degree of damage differs. In angina the shortage is temporary and discomfort usually settles once the trigger is removed. In infarction an artery is abruptly blocked and muscle actually begins to die. The catch is that telling them apart by symptoms alone is close to impossible in the moment. What separates them is the electrocardiogram and the cardiac biomarker test, not how the pain feels. That is why chest pain starting at rest, or lasting more than 20 minutes, should be treated as a possible heart attack and emergency services called immediately.

How many minutes of chest pain should make me suspect angina?

There is no threshold that settles it by duration alone, though reference points help. Stable angina is generally described as easing within a few minutes, while chest pain persisting beyond 20 minutes despite rest is commonly seen in acute coronary syndrome. Korean public health information advises strongly suspecting acute myocardial infarction when chest pain of 30 minutes or more comes with cold sweating. At the other end, a jab that passes in seconds or pain you can pinpoint comes from the heart comparatively less often. Either way, the diagnosis has to be confirmed by testing.

Do women experience angina differently from men?

Chest discomfort remains the most common symptom in women too. What differs is the weight of the accompanying symptoms. Guidelines note that women with acute coronary syndrome frequently have nausea, palpitations and shortness of breath alongside it, and upper abdominal pain, vomiting, shoulder or back pain, or unexplained fatigue may lead instead. These are easily written off as digestive. The important point is not to rule out the heart because chest pain is not the loudest symptom.

My coronary arteries were reported as clear, so why does it still hurt?

That is not rare. Among people who undergo angiography for angina symptoms, a substantial proportion have no significantly narrowed large vessel, and in some of them ischaemia is still demonstrated. The European Society of Cardiology treats this separately under the heading of ischaemia with non-obstructive coronary arteries. Proposed mechanisms include microvascular dysfunction, coronary spasm and myocardial bridging. A clear large vessel does not by itself mean the heart is not the source, so if symptoms persist it is worth saying exactly that to your clinician.

Does managing risk factors make angina better?

The evidence is strongest for reducing later cardiovascular events rather than for symptom relief itself. The chronic coronary disease guideline places lifestyle modification including diet and exercise at the top recommendation class for all patients, and sets a reduction of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol by at least half from baseline at the same class. Which targets and which medicines fit a given person depends on overall health and coexisting conditions, so those decisions belong with your treating clinician.

Conclusion

What understanding angina requires is not a list of disease names but two questions. Where did supply and demand fall out of step, and is that pattern settled. The first leads out to atherosclerosis, spasm and microvascular dysfunction. The second divides a scheduled appointment from an emergency department.

If only one thing survives from this article, let it be this. Chest pain that begins at rest, chest pain lasting more than 20 minutes, chest pain with cold sweating, nausea or fainting, and a long-standing symptom that has changed for the worse all mean calling emergency services before working out the cause. A symptom that differs from the textbook is no grounds for ruling out the heart. In women, older adults and people with diabetes especially, pain often does not lead the picture at all.

Stated once more. This article is for information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. If you have chest pain, seek medical care without delay. Where your own symptoms fall, which tests you need and what treatment you receive are matters for your treating clinician to decide. Nothing here should be used to adjust a prescribed medicine or to postpone a visit. If a red flag is present, call emergency services immediately.
