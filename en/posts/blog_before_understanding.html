Introduction: the shelf life of I will write it once I have looked into it more

If half-written drafts are piling up in a folder, most of them stalled at the same sentence: I will write this once I have looked into it more. It sounds diligent, but it is a promise with no deadline. Because nobody defined when more is enough, that moment does not come.

This article is about the opposite working order. Start writing while you still do not understand, and mark the not-understanding precisely inside the piece. The first half is why that order helps learning. The second half is the machinery that keeps such a piece from harming other people. Without the second half, this method is just scattering unverified text across search results.

The general case for dropping perfectionism, and the value of sharing a learning process, is already covered in blogging is allowed to state the obvious. Here I skip straight to the next question: if you write from ignorance, what exactly has to be marked for the result to still count as information?

1. Why the bar of full understanding postpones writing forever

The core problem is that the bar cannot be tested. Understanding is not a switch that flips; it is a continuum, and nowhere along it is there a tick mark reading understood from here. Set an untestable condition as your starting gate and you get one of two outcomes: you never start, or you declare yourself ready with no basis at all.

The second outcome is the dangerous one, because self-assessed understanding is biased in a single direction. The 2002 Cognitive Science study by Rozenblit and Keil on the illusion of explanatory depth lays out the structure. Participants rated how well they understood everyday devices such as a zipper or a flush toilet on a seven-point scale, then wrote a step-by-step causal explanation of how each one works, then rated themselves again. Nearly everyone's rating dropped. It dropped again after they answered diagnostic questions, and again after they compared their account with an expert explanation.

The important detail is that nothing new was learned between the first rating and the second. What fell was not knowledge but the estimate of it. Put differently, the feeling of having understood something is another name for the state of not having tried to explain it yet. Make that feeling your starting condition and the moment it is satisfied is precisely the least accurate moment available.

A replacement bar has to be something you can actually check. This one works: can you write down what you do not know, in sentences? If the gap reads as generally unclear on this whole topic, it is too early. If you can point at it - I do not know where this number comes from, I do not know whether A or B happens first - you can start. That list gets used twice: first as the research plan, later as the section of the finished piece titled what I could not verify.

2. Three mechanisms by which writing produces understanding

The claim that writing helps you learn is repeated so often it has stopped meaning anything. What actually operates is three specific pressures, and all three come from the form of the sentence itself.

2.1 Compression exposes the blanks

In your head, roughly it works like this passes inspection. A sentence refuses it, because a sentence demands a subject and a verb. The moment you try to write when a request comes in, it gets processed, you have to name what does the processing - and if you cannot name it, that spot is the blank. Diagrams and bullet lists apply this pressure only weakly. An arrow indicates direction without committing to causation.

2.2 You are forced to establish an order

Prose is linear. You must decide what comes first, and that decision embeds a claim about the direction of causation. Understanding in your head needs no order; several fragments can float simultaneously and the sensation of knowing survives intact. When you are drafting an outline and cannot decide which of two sections goes first, that is usually not an editorial problem. It is a signal that you do not know how the two relate.

2.3 You start hunting for counterexamples yourself

Make one claim and the question but what about this case follows on its own. Imagining the objections in advance naturally generates research items. This effect grows stronger the sharper the claim. A neutral survey piece that takes no position invites no counterexamples, and therefore invites no research either. But sharpening a claim is not the same as asserting without grounds. What you sharpen is the claim, never the level of confidence.

Worth noting: none of the three mechanisms requires publication. They operate identically on a piece that stays in a drawer. Whether to publish is a decision on a different axis, handled separately in section 8.

3. The learning pyramid has no evidence - and what survives anyway

One diagram shows up almost inevitably in discussions like this: the pyramid claiming five percent retention from lectures, ten percent from reading, and ninety percent from teaching others. Do not cite it. Those numbers have never been verified.

  • The diagram is generally attributed to the US NTL Institute in the early 1960s, but the original research no longer exists. Method, sample, and measurement conditions are all unavailable.
  • Critics repeatedly point out that every figure is a multiple of five and that the set resolves far too neatly.
  • Edgar Dale's Cone of Experience, usually attached as supporting evidence, is not a retention model. It classified learning methods by degree of abstraction, and none of the 1946, 1954, or 1969 versions contained percentages at all.
  • The rebuttals were not a one-off. Letrud (2012), Subramony (2003), Lalley and Miller (2007), and Masters (2020) on the medical education side each reached the same conclusion in different venues.

So this article makes no claim about what percentage sticks when you write. When you meet material offering such figures, the safer move is to doubt the material itself - this is a case of unverified numbers circulating through educational resources for more than forty years.

Does anything remain once the numbers go? Yes. The illusion of explanatory depth experiments cited above do not measure retention. What they measure is the discovery of your own ignorance: attempt an explanation and your self-rating falls, which is to say you find out that you did not know. That is the ground this article stands on, and it is a different question from how long you remember something. There is no need to oversell the learning effect. As an instrument for locating the places you do not understand, writing is already worth the trouble.

4. How to encode the level of confidence in the sentence

This is where the article's real subject starts. There is exactly one reason writing from ignorance is permissible: making it possible for a reader to see where the verified part ends and the rest begins. That marking has to live in the shape of the sentences, not in a separate notice. Nobody reads the disclaimer paragraph at the bottom.

In practice four levels are enough.

LevelSentence formWhat the support actually isWhat must not be written at this level
Verified It is X, plus a source link You read the primary source yourself Second-hand citations you never opened
Reported X announced that, it was reported that A source exists but you could not verify it Deleting the announcing party and stating it as fact
Inferred It appears that, if A then B would follow Logic and circumstance. Not a fact Figures, rates, statutes, dosages
Unverified I could not confirm this Nothing. Left blank Filling the blank with a plausible value

Two rules. First, do not mix two levels in one sentence. It is stated in the official documentation and probably behaves this way glues two different levels together with a conjunction, and the reader cannot tell where the documentation stops. Split it into two sentences.

Second, state verified things flatly. Attaching a hedge to every sentence is the failure in the opposite direction. When everything is hedged, the hedge stops being a marker and becomes noise, and you end up with an article that asserts nothing. Hedging only carries information when it is selective. The four levels have to genuinely differ for any of them to mean something.

5. Formats that separate the verified from the guessed

If confidence marking works at the sentence level, this works at the level of page layout. Rather than mixing inside sentences, it is far safer to separate by position. Readers register block boundaries before they register the ending of a clause.

  • Body text carries only what is confirmed. Push inference into a quote block or a separate paragraph that opens with an explicit marker that what follows is your guess.
  • Never fill a table cell with an estimate. Cells read as measurements. If the value is missing, leave it empty and add a one-line footnote. Fill it and that number gets cited in someone else's article later.
  • Separate what you ran from what you did not. When commands, code, or configuration are involved, attach output only to the things you actually executed. Establish the convention that a command with no output means it was not run.
  • Stamp the date. Anything that changes gets the date you checked it. With no date, readers treat it as the current value.
  • Include a what I could not verify section every time. This is a list, not a display of modesty. It is the fill queue for the next revision, and it is also the channel through which other people tell you the answer.

That last item carries the most weight in practice. When the gaps are named in the article, the people who know them fill them in. Conversely, an article that hides its gaps gets back not corrections but quiet distrust - unable to point at what is wrong, readers mark down the whole thing.

6. What to do after being wrong - keep the correction visible

If you have decided to write from ignorance, being wrong is not an exception but a scheduled event, so it is better to prepare the slot in advance. The worst available choice here is editing silently. Everyone who already read it keeps the wrong version, anyone who cited it is left with vanished support, and the person who pointed out the error has no way to tell whether it landed.

Scope of the errorActionWhat to leave behind
The conclusion flips Revise title, summary, and body The old conclusion and why it was wrong, at the top
A figure or value is wrong Fix the cell and refresh the checked-on date The old value, its source, and the date changed
An inference breaks on a counterexample Keep the paragraph and append the counterexample The original inference in full. Do not delete it
Wording or typos Fix immediately No record needed

A changelog section needs three columns: date, what changed, why. Whether to name the person who flagged it is their call. And it is better not to erase the original wording wholesale, because readers need to see what was wrong in order to correct their own memory. An article that keeps its corrections does not lose trust. If anything, the presence of that record signals more reliability than its absence.

The limit should be stated plainly, though. Once a wrong statement has spread, there is essentially no way to recall it beyond fixing the original. Articles that cited it, screenshots, and summaries carried off elsewhere all remain. A correction mechanism is insurance, not a licence. Not spreading it in the first place is always cheaper.

7. Where this method is forbidden - YMYL

Learning by writing holds under one condition: the cost of being wrong stays with the writer. Where that cost transfers to the reader, do not use this method. It is a trade in which the learning accrues to the writer and the risk accrues to the reader.

Concretely: diseases and symptoms, medication and dosage, emergency response, investment decisions and product selection, tax rates and filing procedures, the interpretation and application of statutes, and any work procedure where a person can get hurt. A wrong value here is executed as written. And since a reader capable of verifying it would not have been searching in the first place, the assumption that readers will filter it themselves does not hold.

Search guidance points in the same direction. In its content quality documentation, Google places trust as the most important of experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trust, and states explicitly that it gives even more weight to topics that could significantly affect health, financial stability, safety, or societal welfare. The same document offers as a self-assessment question whether the content contains easily verified factual errors. Publishing a learning note in this territory amounts to preparing a wrong answer to that question in advance.

That does not mean nothing can be written in these fields. Two forms hold up.

  • A record of a procedure you actually went through. Which counter you brought which documents to, how many days it took. Only someone who did it can write this, and it is easy to check. But writing about a procedure you did not go through as if you had stops being a matter of style: it becomes a factual claim, and the claim itself is misinformation.
  • Directions to the primary sources. Statute numbers, links to the official text, where the application form lives. Pointing at locations without adding interpretation is something a beginner can do accurately.

The line not to cross is judgment, interpretation, and numbers. Three questions help draw it. If a reader follows this article exactly, does their body, money, or legal standing move? Does a value I wrote get executed as written? Is the next place a reader can verify this actually named in the text? If either of the first two is yes, do not publish.

8. What to publish and what to leave in the drawer

Writing from ignorance and publishing that writing are two separate decisions. The learning effects in the earlier sections arrive identically for a piece that stays in the drawer. So publication is judged on its own, and the test is not am I embarrassed but is this article a net gain in the slot it occupies?

Shape of the pieceVerdictReason
A generic what is X explainer, written by a beginner Drawer A less accurate article displaces a more accurate one already in that search slot
I tried X, hit error Y, worked around it with Z Publish Irreplaceable and easy to reproduce. Only a beginner can write this form
A verdict on someone else's claim you cannot support Drawer Past accuracy, this turns into a reputation dispute
Negative statements about a company or product Conditional Only within reported or published facts, with speculation marked as speculation
A case carrying employer or client context Drawer Anonymised details still identify by combination. Narrowing the industry alone creates specificity
An in-progress learning note Publishable Say in the title that it is a note, and exclude it from indexing if needed

The feedback usually cited as the reward for publishing deserves a precise statement too. Feedback arrives only if someone is reading. With no audience yet, publishing gives you almost nothing beyond self-discipline - and that self-discipline is equally available by showing the draft to someone who knows the area, or by having a language model review it. The author of the original essay makes the same point about model review of private drafts.

One more thing: if you decide to publish learning notes, there are two platform capabilities worth checking. Can an individual post be excluded from search indexing, and can edit history be displayed? Not the brand or the fashion - these two determine how safe a learning note actually is.

9. What happens to the gaps when an AI writes the draft

All three mechanisms in section 2 use friction as their signal: the point where the sentence will not come, the point where you cannot fix the order, the point where a counterexample surfaces. That resistance is the coordinate system of learning.

Generative tools are built precisely to remove that resistance. Places you do not understand come out as smooth, plausible prose, and as a result the diagnostic signal disappears. As drafting gets easier, the gap-revealing effect does not weaken - it switches off. Finished text with unchanged understanding is now a reachable state. The structural version of this, on the code side, is covered in understanding is the new bottleneck; the same thing happens in writing.

The answer is to change the order.

  1. Write the outline from what you know, first. Before opening the tool. Whatever is missing here is the real study list.
  2. Write the gaps down explicitly. Later, when generated prose covers those spots naturally, the record that they were once blank survives.
  3. Bring in generation after that. Take the sentences, but change the review question from is this right to where is the support for this.
  4. If you cannot name the support, the gap is yours. Write it off as a tool error and that spot never gets filled.

There are uses these tools are genuinely good at, though: generating a list of objections to your draft, flagging where a term's definition wobbles, proposing counterexamples you missed. That direction creates friction rather than removing it, so it does not conflict with this working method. But such review is closer to structural critique than to fact checking. Figures and statutes still have to be confirmed against primary sources. Where generated prose diverges from human writing is treated separately in AI smells.

10. What catches you when the source language is not yours

Writing about technology or regulation in a language other than the one the primary sources use adds a layer. Because the authoritative documents are often in English, a piece written while learning effectively becomes translation plus commentary. Here a misreading of the source does not end as one person's misunderstanding. If few documents explain that topic in the local language, the wrong explanation occupies the position of the standard explanation for a while.

  • Keep the original term in parentheses. Readers need to be able to cross over to the source for a misreading to get corrected. Leaving only the translated term severs the verification path.
  • Link to the source, not the local re-report. Linking a local secondary article puts verification one step further away, and if that article comes down the support vanishes entirely.
  • Fold corrections into the body when they arrive. A correction that lives only in the comments means the next reader takes away the same misunderstanding from the body. Fold it in, and say that you did.
  • Translationese itself reads as a trust signal. A sentence that preserves the source's structure verbatim gives the impression it was moved rather than understood. Understanding changes the word order.
  • Fix one term per concept within a piece. Call the same concept by three different translations and readers will treat them as three concepts.

One community habit is worth noting as well. Criticism of a wrong article often circulates somewhere other than the comments on the original, so the writer never gets the chance to correct while the article stays up for a long time. Putting the changelog from section 6 somewhere visible is the simplest device for routing those corrections back to the original.

11. Five lines to check before publishing

No need to make this long. These five confirm whether the machinery above actually engaged.

  1. Can you point at which sentences you verified and which you inferred? If not, the confidence marking from section 4 did not engage.
  2. Is there a table cell filled with an estimate? Empty it and move the value to a footnote.
  3. If a reader follows this exactly, is any cost transferred to them? If so, go back to section 7.
  4. Is the slot for a correction ready? If there is no changelog section, build it now.
  5. If the what I could not verify section is empty, did you really verify everything, or did you just not write it down? Usually the latter.

12. What I could not verify for this article

  • No credible effect size for how much writing accelerates learning turned up. The commonly cited learning pyramid figures are not used, for the reasons given in section 3.
  • Among the rebuttal literature on the learning pyramid, the 2018 Cogent Education paper by Letrud and Hernes could not be opened. It is therefore not cited in the body, and the rebuttal list plus the loss of the NTL original research are stated only at the level of bibliographic information compiled by an encyclopedia entry.
  • No study directly comparing whether published writing beats private notes for learning was found. The judgment in section 8 rests on the risk side of publishing instead.
  • There is no measurement of how long wrong explanations persist at the top of search results in a given language market. Section 10 is a statement based on observation and structural reasoning.
  • The effect of displaying a correction history on search ranking has no published criteria. The recommendation in section 6 rests on reader trust, not ranking.

Conclusion: write without understanding, and write that you do not understand

Two things need to change. Move the starting bar from once I understand to once I can write down what I do not know, and then spend half the page on markings: the level of confidence, the separation of verified from guessed, the slot where a correction will go. Without those three, what remains is unverified text occupying one more position in search results.

And the method has its own territory. Records of using a tool, notes organising a concept, failed experiments, readings of a cultural shift - the places where the writer pays for being wrong. Anywhere bodies, money, and law are at stake sits outside this method, and holding that boundary is the condition on which you get to keep using it.

One thing you can do today: open a stalled draft, make a list titled what I do not know, and write down three questions you cannot currently answer. Once those three lines exist, that article has already started.
