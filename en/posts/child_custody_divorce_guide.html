Parental Authority and Custody in Korea - How Courts Decide and How Decisions Change

Not who takes the child, but what the court looks at - the designation standard and the routes to change it, as of July 2026

2026-07-29 Life & Law

Introduction: change the question and the answer appears

This article describes the law of the Republic of Korea. Family law is one of the least portable areas of law there is, so if you live outside Korea, or if any part of your family situation touches another country, the rules that actually govern your case are likely to be different. Treat what follows as a map of the Korean system rather than as advice for wherever you happen to be standing.

The most common search phrase around divorce and children is some version of who gets to take the child. It is a natural question and a poor one, because the law contains no such concept. What the law allocates is which powers are entrusted to whom, and the yardstick is not which parent has suffered more but what is better for the child.

So this article replaces that question with two. First, what does the court actually look at. Second, what can be done after a decision has been made. Answering either requires clearing one threshold first: the two things usually lumped together in ordinary speech, parental authority and physical custody, are separate rights with different statutory homes. Parental authority comes from Article 909 of the Civil Act; matters of custody come from Article 837. They may be given to the same parent, and they may be split.

Missing that distinction causes real trouble. A parent who lives with the child may be told at a counter that they cannot open an account in the child's name or sign a particular form. In the other direction, a parent who does not live with the child sometimes concludes that they are now a stranger in the eyes of the law. Both misunderstandings dissolve on a single reading of the statute.

One more thing about scope. The axis of this article is designation and change. The mechanics of actually collecting unpaid support, and the enforcement procedure for blocked visitation, are covered in separate articles, so here they get their order and their statutory basis and then a link. This is also not an article that predicts amounts, because there is no honest answer to that beyond saying it depends. Finally, it is written on the assumption that a child might read it. No parent is cast as a villain, and no real case or real person is used as material; every situation described is hypothetical.

1. Parental authority and custody are not the same word

The Ministry of Government Legislation, through its plain-language legal information service, describes parental authority as the bundle of personal and property rights and duties that parents hold with respect to a minor child, grounded in Article 909 of the Civil Act. Physical custody, by contrast, is the narrower right to protect, raise and educate the child. The same source treats parental authority as the broader concept, with custody sitting inside it.

Reading the provisions makes the gap concrete. Article 913 of the Civil Act covers the right and duty of protection and education; Article 914 covers the power to determine the child's place of residence; Article 916 covers management of property the child has acquired in their own name; and Articles 911 and 920 cover statutory representation. Of these, property management and statutory representation have nothing inherently to do with living under the same roof. That is precisely why a custodial parent and a parental authority holder can be different people, and why almost all of the friction in a split arrangement lands here.

The last row of that table is where most disputes in this area actually live. A parent designated as custodian, sharing a home with the child every day, still needs the other parent's cooperation for anything that requires the name of a legal representative. Conversely, holding parental authority does not create a right to bring the child home. Neither power substitutes for the other.

2. Splitting them is possible - with a condition attached

In its decision of 13 April 2012 in case 2011Meu4719, the Supreme Court of Korea held that parental authority and custody need not always vest in the same person, and that arrangements giving custody to one parent and parental authority to the other, or to both jointly, are permissible so long as the applicable standards are met. The Ministry of Government Legislation likewise explains that on divorce the two roles may be assigned to one parent or to different parents.

Possible and comfortable are different words, though. A split means the two parents have to move together in the situations below. These are not drawn from any real case; they are generic illustrations of the kinds of paperwork that call for a legal representative.

  • Banking in the child's name - opening or closing an account, or withdrawals, where a legal representative must be involved.
  • Dealing with property held in the child's name - the territory of the management power in Article 916 and the representation power in Article 920.
  • Passports and travel documents - wherever the consent of a legal representative is required.
  • Insurance contracts - policies naming a minor as the insured or as the policyholder.
  • Inheritance and gift procedures - any legal act in which the child is a party.

Because institutions differ in what they ask for, anyone contemplating a split is better off checking in advance. It also helps to write into the agreement who decides what, by what method, and what happens if one parent cannot be reached; the fewer gaps in the text, the fewer arguments later. The exact wording is something to work out with a lawyer.

For completeness, Article 909-2 of the Civil Act, headed designation of the holder of parental authority, deals separately with the situation where a sole holder of parental authority dies or becomes unable to exercise it. It falls outside the scope of this article, so the reference is noted and left there.

3. How it gets decided - there are two routes

The route by which matters concerning children are settled differs between a consensual divorce and a judicial divorce. In a consensual divorce the parents decide as a matter of principle, but the court intervenes if that agreement would harm the child. In a judicial divorce the court designates the holder of parental authority on its own initiative.

Start with the consensual procedure. Article 836-2 paragraph 2 of the Civil Act provides that confirmation of the intention to divorce may be obtained after three months where there is a child to be raised, and one month where there is not, counted from the day the statutory guidance is given. Paragraph 4 of the same article requires the parties, where there is a child to be raised, to submit an agreement on custody and on designation of the holder of parental authority, or an authenticated copy of the family court's ruling. Paragraph 5 requires the family court to prepare a child support payment record.

What the agreement must contain is listed in Article 837 paragraph 2 of the Civil Act: who the custodian will be, how the cost of raising the child will be borne, and whether and how visitation will be exercised. Article 837 paragraph 3 then provides that where the agreement would be contrary to the welfare of the child, the family court may order it corrected, or may determine the necessary matters on its own initiative, taking into account the child's wishes and age, the parents' financial situation and other circumstances. The parents' agreement, in other words, is not the final gate.

The child support payment record deserves a closer look. After requiring the court to prepare it, Article 836-2 paragraph 5 of the Civil Act adds that Article 41 of the Family Litigation Act applies to its effect. Article 41 provides that a ruling ordering the payment of money or the performance of another obligation constitutes a title of execution. Put together, the support record produced during a consensual divorce can itself serve as the basis for compulsory execution. That is the most practically useful point in this article on the subject of support.

4. What the court looks at - a single standard called the child's welfare

The standard is written into the statute. Article 912 paragraph 1 of the Civil Act provides that the welfare of the child shall be considered as a matter of priority in exercising parental authority, and paragraph 2 provides that the family court shall likewise give priority to the welfare of the child when designating the holder of parental authority, adding that for this purpose the court may seek advice from experts in relevant fields or from social welfare institutions. Neither parental convenience nor parental fault is the yardstick, and the statute says so directly.

What that means in practice was set out by the Supreme Court of Korea in case 2011Meu4719. The court held that a decision must comprehensively weigh the sex and age of the minor child, the presence of parental affection and of a genuine intention to raise the child, the financial capacity needed for caregiving, the closeness of the relationship between each parent and the child, and the child's own wishes, and must be made in the direction most helpful and suitable for the child's growth and welfare.

The table contains only items confirmed in a statute or a Supreme Court decision. Online guidance often adds that siblings should not be separated, or that the primary caregiver should not be displaced as a matter of principle. Much of that is a summary of practical tendencies rather than a standard written anywhere, and presenting an unverified tendency as a settled rule would leave readers with false expectations. So it has been left out.

Continuity of caregiving is worth one more paragraph. In case 2008Meu380 the Supreme Court of Korea took the view that changing an already established caregiving arrangement requires it to be apparent that the change would better serve the child's growth and welfare. That principle reads in two directions. It gives stability to whoever is currently raising the child, but it does not mean that removing a child in order to manufacture a settled situation is thereby justified. Continuity is one factor among several, and how the present state of affairs came about is itself part of what is assessed.

5. The child's wishes - what the line at 13 actually means

This is the most misunderstood point in the whole area. Article 100 of the Family Procedure Rules provides that when a family court determines matters of parental authority, custody or visitation for a minor child, it must hear the opinion of a child aged 13 or over, subject to the proviso that this does not apply where the opinion cannot be obtained or where there are special circumstances in which hearing it would instead harm the child's welfare.

Material released by the Supreme Court of Korea on 29 October 2020, in connection with its guide on hearing minors who are the subject of family cases, describes the same structure: hearing is the rule from age 13, and a child under 13 is heard where necessary. The same material explains that the guide was produced with the child's best interests as its standard, and with the burden the hearing process places on minors clearly in view.

Two things follow, and both are worth stating plainly.

  • Thirteen is the floor for being asked, not the start of a right to decide. The child's wishes are handled as one of the factors listed in Article 837 paragraph 3 of the Civil Act. There is no rule anywhere providing that the outcome follows what the child said.
  • Being under 13 does not mean being ignored. Where a hearing is found to be necessary, it takes place regardless of age.
  • The statute allows the hearing to be skipped. Where special circumstances make hearing the child's opinion harmful to the child, the hearing may be dispensed with.
  • A hearing is not a place where a choice is demanded. The burden minors experience in this process was among the reasons the court gave for producing the guide at all.

This section may matter more to children than to adults. Being asked to pick one parent is not an easy question at any age. The reason the law requires the child to be heard is not to hand the responsibility over, but to find out where the child actually stands, outside the competing accounts of the adults. Coaching a child toward a particular answer, or asking them for one, therefore runs directly against the purpose of the procedure.

6. Once decided is not decided forever - applications to change

The belief that a decision is permanent is simply wrong. Korean law keeps two routes open, and they differ in who may use them.

Changing the holder of parental authority falls under Article 909 paragraph 6 of the Civil Act. Where the family court finds it necessary for the welfare of the child, it may change the designated holder to the other parent on the application of a relative of the child within the fourth degree of kinship. The word relative looks unfamiliar in this context, but a parent is within the first degree of the child and so is plainly included; the provision simply also opens the door to people who are not the parents, such as grandparents or siblings.

Changing matters concerning custody falls under Article 837 paragraph 5. Where the family court finds it necessary for the welfare of the child, it may change matters concerning custody or make another appropriate disposition, on the application of the father, the mother, the child or a public prosecutor, or on its own initiative. It is notable that the child appears there as an applicant in their own right. In a judicial divorce, Article 843 of the Civil Act applies this provision.

  • A custodian can be changed by agreement. The Ministry of Government Legislation explains that the parties may change the custodian by agreement after divorce. Given how such things are later disputed, recording the agreement in writing is the safer course.
  • Parental authority always goes through the court. The fact that the parents agreed does not by itself move parental authority.
  • A confirmed ruling brings a reporting duty. The same source explains that under Articles 58 and 79 paragraph 2 of the Act on Registration of Family Relations the change must be reported within one month of the ruling becoming final.
  • Changeable is not the same as easily changed. As case 2008Meu380 indicates, altering the present caregiving arrangement requires it to be apparent that the change would be better for the child.
  • Records are what carry the argument. A claim that circumstances have changed has weight when it rests on documents rather than feeling: medical records, school paperwork, and anything showing how caregiving has actually been shared.

So if the arrangement fixed at the time of divorce has stopped fitting the child, enduring it is not the only option available. Equally, filing repeated applications in order to pressure the other parent does not match the standard either. The standard is always the same single question: what is better for this child now.

7. Visitation - a right of the parent and a right of the child

Article 837-2 paragraph 1 of the Civil Act provides that the parent who does not directly raise the child and the child have a mutual right of visitation. The provision was newly inserted on 13 January 1990, and the amendment of 21 December 2007 made the child's position as a right holder visible in the text. The change of wording looks small and is not. It treats visitation as a right belonging to the child too, rather than a convenience exchanged between adults.

That is also where the answer lies to why simply not sending the child is a problem. When the parent with custody blocks contact, they block not only the other parent's right but the child's own. Unpaid support does not change that. The two obligations are not tradeable, and each has its own enforcement route.

Grandparents appear in the statute as well. Article 837-2 paragraph 2, newly inserted on 2 December 2016, provides that a lineal ascendant of the parent who does not directly raise the child may apply to the family court for visitation with the child where that parent has died or cannot visit the child owing to illness, residence abroad or other unavoidable circumstances. The Ministry of Government Legislation explains that the court then weighs the child's wishes, the relationship between the applicant and the child, the motive for the application and other circumstances. It is not a rule that grandparents may always visit; it is a route that opens when the requirements are met.

The provision also runs the other way. Article 837-2 paragraph 3 allows the family court, where necessary for the welfare of the child, to restrict, exclude or vary visitation on application or on its own initiative. The Ministry of Government Legislation gives as examples situations where the child does not wish to meet the parent, or where grounds for loss of parental authority exist. Visitation is the rule, then, but it is not absolute, and the test for restricting it is once again the welfare of the child.

  • Right holders - both the parent who does not raise the child and the child (Art. 837-2 para. 1).
  • Forms it takes - besides meeting in person, the Ministry of Government Legislation lists letters, telephone calls, exchanging gifts and stays of a set period.
  • How it is settled - by agreement, and failing agreement by a family court ruling (Art. 837 para. 2 subpara. 3, Art. 843).
  • Grandparents - may apply where death, illness, residence abroad or another unavoidable circumstance applies (Art. 837-2 para. 2).
  • Restriction and exclusion - decided by the court where necessary for the child's welfare (Art. 837-2 para. 3).
  • Full adoption - the Ministry of Government Legislation explains that where the child is adopted as a full adoptive child, the birth parent's visitation right is extinguished (Civil Act Art. 908-3).

The enforcement order procedure for cases where contact is blocked despite an existing agreement, and the limits of that procedure, are covered separately. See the guide to enforcing visitation rights.

8. Child support - deciding it and collecting it are different jobs

Support is not the axis of this article, but the order of operations belongs here, because many people assume that a judgment or an agreement is the end of the matter and discover only after payments stop that one more procedure was waiting. Support divides into two stages: obtaining a title of execution, and using that title to secure performance.

The first stage has already appeared. In a consensual divorce it is the child support payment record under Article 836-2 paragraph 5 of the Civil Act, whose effect is governed by Article 41 of the Family Litigation Act, making it a title of execution. In a judicial divorce a ruling or a mediation protocol plays the same role. The first stage, in other words, usually completes inside the divorce process itself.

9. What remains without custody - the position of the non-custodial parent

This section is short and is the part most often misunderstood. Article 837 paragraph 6 of the Civil Act states that the preceding provisions bring about no change in the rights and duties of the parents other than in matters concerning custody. Not being designated as custodian does not stop a person being a parent.

  • The legal relationship between parent and child is unchanged. The Ministry of Government Legislation explains that the blood relationship between parent and child continues even after custody matters are settled.
  • Inheritance is unchanged. The standing of each to inherit from the other is unaffected.
  • The duty of support remains. Separately from paying child support, the parental duty of support does not disappear.
  • The right to consent to a minor child's marriage remains. This is one of the examples the Ministry of Government Legislation names explicitly.
  • Visitation exists. Civil Act Article 837-2 paragraph 1.
  • Surname and origin do not change automatically. Article 781 paragraph 6 of the Civil Act provides that where it is necessary for the welfare of the child to change the child's surname and origin, this may be done with the permission of the court on the application of the father, the mother or the child. A parent's remarriage does not by itself change anything.

Compressed into one sentence, those provisions say this: what a divorce dissolves is the marriage, not the relationship between parent and child. If a child is reading this, that sentence alone is enough to take away.

10. Six misconceptions worth clearing up

  1. One parent is bound to be favoured. Nothing in Article 912 of the Civil Act or in the factors listed in case 2011Meu4719 refers to the sex of a parent. What the court looks at is the child's welfare and the concrete factors bearing on it.
  2. At 13 the child chooses. Article 100 of the Family Procedure Rules requires only that the opinion be heard. The opinion is one factor, and the proviso even permits the hearing to be skipped where it would harm the child.
  3. Once decided, it cannot be changed. Article 909 paragraph 6 and Article 837 paragraph 5 of the Civil Act both keep a route open, subject to it being apparent that the change would be better for the child.
  4. No support means no visits. Visitation is a right of the child as well (Art. 837-2 para. 1). Each issue belongs in its own procedure.
  5. Parental authority means living with the child. Living arrangements belong to the designation of a custodian. The two concepts differ in scope.
  6. Without custody you are a stranger. Article 837 paragraph 6 of the Civil Act says the opposite outright: outside matters of custody, parental rights and duties remain.

11. Frequently asked questions

Can parental authority and physical custody be given to different parents?

On the Ministry of Government Legislation's explanation and the reasoning in case 2011Meu4719, yes. The cost is that anything needing a legal representative, such as an account, an insurance policy or a passport in the child's name, will require the other parent's cooperation each time. If a split is contemplated, it is better to record who decides what in the agreement in advance.

Does a child get to choose which parent to live with at 13?

No. Article 100 of the Family Procedure Rules requires that the opinion be heard from age 13; it does not transfer the decision to the child. The opinion is handled as one of the factors listed in Article 837 paragraph 3 of the Civil Act.

Can a parental authority or custody order be changed later?

There is a route. Parental authority may be changed on the application of a relative of the child within the fourth degree under Article 909 paragraph 6, and custody matters on the application of the father, mother, child or a public prosecutor under Article 837 paragraph 5, or by the court on its own initiative. As case 2008Meu380 indicates, however, changing an existing caregiving arrangement requires it to be apparent that the change would be better for the child.

If support goes unpaid, can visitation be refused?

No. Because visitation is also a right of the child, blocking contact blocks the child's right as well. Nor does a missed visit justify withholding support. Each belongs in its own enforcement procedure.

What has to be filed about the children in a consensual divorce?

Under Article 836-2 paragraph 4 of the Civil Act, an agreement on custody and on designation of the holder of parental authority, or an authenticated copy of the ruling. Under paragraph 5, the family court prepares the child support payment record, whose effect is governed by Article 41 of the Family Litigation Act. Under paragraph 2, where there is a child to be raised, confirmation of the intention to divorce follows three months after the guidance is given.

Conclusion: there is only one standard

Compressed into one paragraph: parental authority and custody are different rights, both are decided by reference to the welfare of the child, and both can be changed afterwards where the child's welfare requires it. In statutory terms, Articles 909 and 837 of the Civil Act handle designation, Article 912 supplies the standard, Article 909 paragraph 6 and Article 837 paragraph 5 handle change, and Article 837-2 handles visitation.

Three things can be done today. First, find out what is currently in place: reread the agreement or the judgment sentence by sentence to see who is named as holder of parental authority, who is named as custodian, and how visitation is described. Second, make a list of the friction points: which documents get blocked, which arrangements are not being kept, each with a date. Third, take that list into a consultation. A prepared list buys far more from the same hour of advice, and free consultation is available if cost is a barrier.

And finally, the sentence that governs all of it. Article 912 paragraph 1 of the Civil Act says that the welfare of the child shall be considered as a matter of priority in exercising parental authority. That sentence does not apply only to courts. It is the same standard at the table where an agreement is drafted, and on the day one parent goes to collect a child.
