What the Never Get Angry at Work Advice Misses — The Signal and the Cost of Anger

Feeling and expressing are different problems — how status, culture and institutions change the outcome of the same anger

Aug 23, 2026 Trends & Culture

This article is a general discussion of organizational communication. It is not medical or psychological counseling, nor is it legal advice. Individual cases should be assessed by a qualified professional.

After the meeting where someone raised their voice

Someone raised their voice in a meeting. In the room, the objections disappeared and the decision went their way. The problem shows up the following week. Drafts reach that person late, and the decisions that matter are already settled in another channel before the meeting opens. Nobody says they were excluded. The calendar simply worked out that way.

Anger is expensive in organizations not because of the noise on the day, but because of that quiet aftermath. So the advice to never get angry at work is broadly correct. Strip it down to the conclusion alone, though, and it reads as just put up with it, which is a different claim altogether. Telling people not to feel something is an unenforceable demand, and swallowing it carries its own bill.

What follows is about what anger actually does inside an organization. Its function as a signal that flags something out of joint, the cost billed in return, how differently the same expression reads depending on who voices it and in which culture, and finally where the line sits beyond which no amount of individual composure helps.

The original argument — anger turns you into someone to route around

The starting point here is an essay by software engineer Sean Goedecke, titled You should never be angry at work. The core claim is compact and largely persuasive.

  • In a healthy organization, an angry person stops being someone to persuade and becomes someone to manage. Decisions migrate to informal channels that exclude them, and collaboration thins out
  • Caring about the work and getting angry about it are separable. The author holds that caring a great deal is by itself enough to make you a competent engineer
  • The famous figures who stayed angry and kept their seats are not exceptions but a matter of authority. As the author puts it, once you are the one in charge you can get away with being emotional at work

The author grants his own exceptions. Anger early in a career can be a mistake worth making, anger at injustice is legitimate, and in the short run anger does sometimes work. He simply calls it settling for a local maximum.

None of that is in dispute here. The aim is not to rebut the argument but to restore the layers that fall away when only the headline survives. The first of those layers is the difference between the feeling and its expression.

Feeling it and showing it are not the same problem

Feeling anger is not a choice. When a deadline moves without notice, or when two weeks of work is discarded in a single meeting, the odd response would be feeling nothing. What can be chosen is what comes next: the form in which it leaves you. Blur the two and the advice immediately becomes stop having the emotion, which nobody can comply with.

The clearest statement of that distinction in organizational research is the dual threshold model that Deanna Geddes and Ronda Roberts Callister published in Academy of Management Review volume 32, issue 3, in 2007. It is a conceptual model rather than an empirical study, but it reshaped the conversation. The model draws two lines.

  • The expression threshold — crossed the moment felt anger is voiced instead of swallowed
  • The impropriety threshold — crossed when that expression violates the organization's display norms
  • The band between them, anger that is expressed but stays inside the norms, is where the organization actually gets a chance to fix the underlying problem

What makes the model interesting is the other side. Losses do not only come from outbursts past the norms. Anger that never crosses the expression threshold at all, swallowed in silence or leaked only to people with no authority to fix anything, also bills the organization. The signal never reaches anyone who could act on it, so the problem persists while the grievance spreads through hallways and chat threads. That is why just put up with it is not a harmless piece of advice.

What anger does in an organization — signal and invoice

Treat anger purely as something to eliminate and the information it carries goes out with it. Treat it purely as honest expression and the bill that follows stays invisible. Splitting the two is more accurate.

1. A signal that something has gone wrong

Anger usually follows a judgment that something regarded as one's own has been encroached upon. Trace back to where it started and you can see which joint in the organization has slipped. Reading emotion as information does not mean acting on it. It means checking the direction it points.

In practice, the structures that manufacture anger repeatedly narrow down to a few.

  • Agreements get reversed silently — settled items change without notice, and the person affected finds out last
  • Responsibility and authority are misaligned — you carry the consequences but never join the decision. Contributions that get renamed on the way up the reporting chain belong in this category too
  • The same point is ignored for the third time — concerns are received and nothing changes

None of these three dissolve through emotional-regulation training. They dissolve when scheduling agreements, decision records and escalation paths change. That is why looking at an individual's temperament first, when anger keeps surfacing in a team, is usually the wrong order.

The signal is noisy, though. Fatigue and private circumstances amplify the intensity, and it often aims at the nearest person rather than the actual cause. Hence the rule of looking at where the anger started rather than at what it was pointed at.

2. The invoice attached to that signal

Sending the signal costs something, and the cost is usually split across the whole organization rather than paid by the sender.

The Price of Incivility, which Christine Porath and Christine Pearson published in the January–February 2013 issue of Harvard Business Review, gathers fourteen years of North American data on the traces rudeness leaves behind. Its central evidence is a poll of 800 managers and employees across 17 industries, and the reactions reported on the receiving end look like this.

  • 48% deliberately decreased their work effort
  • 38% deliberately decreased the quality of their work
  • 66% said their performance declined
  • 78% said their commitment to the organization declined

The same article notes that 98% of respondents had experienced incivility, and that the share reporting it at least weekly rose from a quarter in 1998 to half in 2011. The incivility in question sits far below verbal abuse. Cutting people off, taking credit, belittling someone in front of others — that level is enough to generate these numbers, which is the point.

The costs that never appear as figures come in three strands. Defensiveness — the person facing the anger spends resources protecting themselves instead of examining the content, so the point never lands. Information shutdown — someone who was met with anger for delivering bad news delivers the next one later and softer. Late problem reports around a short-tempered colleague travel this route. Trust withdrawal — this accrues as reputation rather than as a single incident, and recovery takes several times as long.

So anger is a signal and an invoice at once. Look at one side only and you tilt toward either express it freely, emotion is information too, or bottle it unconditionally. Neither works well in practice.

The same sentence reads differently depending on rank

This is the layer most often dropped from the topic. What decides the outcome of anger is not the intensity of the expression but who directed it at whom.

Three experiments by Victoria Brescoll and Eric Uhlmann, published in Psychological Science volume 19, issue 3, in 2008, confront that asymmetry directly. US adult participants were randomly assigned to watch videotaped mock interviews and then assign the applicant a status level and a salary. The samples were 69 participants in Study 1 (39 men, 30 women), 180 in Study 2 (70 men, 110 women), and 133 in Study 3 (51 men, 82 women).

  • Men who expressed anger were granted higher status than men who expressed sadness
  • Women who expressed anger were granted the lowest status of all, a result that held regardless of the evaluator's own gender and regardless of whether the applicant was an intern or an executive
  • In Study 3, women who attributed their anger to external circumstances recovered significantly in both status and salary compared with women who offered no explanation

The axis studied there is gender, but the mechanism is broader. An observer watching anger chooses, largely without noticing, whether to locate the cause in the situation or in the person's disposition. Read as situational, it becomes they named a problem. Read as dispositional, it becomes they cannot control themselves. Which way it tips depends heavily on the seat the speaker already occupies. For someone with decision rights the default reading is situational; for everyone else the default is dispositional. That is exactly the point the original essay makes when it invokes Linus Torvalds and notes that you have to be the one in charge before emotional behavior is tolerated.

The second row is the heart of it. Anger directed upward is unlikely to be handled on its merits no matter how sound the merits are. So never get angry lands as a counsel of restraint for those above and, for those below, as a constraint on the permissible form of speech. Same sentence, different weight.

Culture adds another layer on top

Rank alone splits the outcome, and the culture in question can flip the direction of that split.

A study by Jiyoung Park, Shinobu Kitayama and colleagues, published in Emotion volume 13, issue 6, in 2013, compared middle-aged and older samples in the United States and Japan. The US side drew on 1,054 MIDUS II participants aged 34 to 84, collected as a national probability sample; the Japanese side on 1,027 MIDJA participants aged 30 to 79 from the Tokyo metropolitan area. Anger expression was measured with an eight-item STAXI scale in a cross-sectional survey design. The results ran in opposite directions.

  • In the United States, people who saw themselves as lower in status expressed more anger, and frustration mediated the link
  • In Japan, people higher in objective status expressed more anger, and decision-making authority mediated the link
  • In one place anger is the expression of the blocked; in the other it looks closer to a prerogative that comes with rank

There is material on Korea directly. A 2024 study by Seoungbeom Hong, Rosemary Hyejin Moon, Jae Yoon Chang, Jin Nam Choi and Jiyoung Park in International Journal of Cross Cultural Management volume 24, issue 2, runs in three strands: a survey of employees at the Korean and US subsidiaries of a multinational technology firm, a Japanese and US sample from the same firm, and an online-recruited sample asked about hiring intentions. Respondents from Confucian Asian cultures (Korea and Japan) rated anger expressed by a higher-status counterpart as more appropriate and effective than Anglo respondents did. For anger expressed by an equal-status coworker, that difference reversed.

Translated into the language of Korean organizations, the picture is familiar. Anger flowing down is rendered as a standard for the work; anger arriving from the side or from below is rendered as crossing a line. Face compounds it. Being corrected in front of others is a loss in itself, independent of the substance, so anger voiced in the open damages the relationship long after even when the point was right. The same words delivered quietly, in another room, land far better.

Reading these cultural differences as better and worse is a misreading. Where direct expression is the norm, indirectness reads as evasion; where it is not, bluntness reads as attack. Push honesty without knowing the norm and the cost is always paid by the weaker party.

The alternative — splitting it into fact, impact and request

If everything so far is diagnosis, what is needed now is a usable form. The goal is not to lower the intensity but to keep the content intact while stripping away the other side's defensiveness.

Cutting it into three pieces

Angry speech usually fuses three things into one lump: what happened, what it broke, and what should change. Fused, it sounds like a verdict on a person. Separated, it sounds like a matter to settle.

  • Fact — only what is observable. Times, counts, documents, in a form the other side cannot simply deny
  • Impact — the consequence that fact produced, written in work units such as schedule, cost or rework
  • Request — one sentence on what should be done differently. Ask for behavior, not an apology. None of the three pieces should contain a verdict on the person. The moment a word like careless appears, the matter becomes an argument about character
Said in angerRewritten in three pieces
Why do you keep changing things without telling anyone?The criteria changed at 5pm yesterday and I heard about it this morning (fact). Yesterday's output has to be rebuilt (impact). Could changes be posted to the shared channel on the day they are decided (request)?
This is just careless work.Two of the five requested items are missing (fact). As it stands another review round is needed (impact). Could the remaining two be filled in by tomorrow morning (request)?
How many times do I have to say this?The same item has been requested three times and the record is there (fact). The time spent on repeat requests is accumulating (impact). A reply on whether it will be handled, some time this week, would help (request).

The right-hand column is not the softer one. It is harder to escape. The left column can be turned into a debate about tone with a single that is no way to talk to people, whereas rebutting the right column means disputing facts. The intensity has not dropped; the point of attack has moved.

How to buy time

Sleep on it is common advice, and rarely available. What is actually needed is not to postpone the matter but to open a gap between reaction and response.

  • In the room, confirm the facts and defer the judgment. Let me check and come back to you this afternoon is enough
  • If an immediate answer is unavoidable, at least change the channel. Do not litigate a personal matter in front of an audience
  • If a draft is written, delete the other person's name and reread it before sending. It is the fastest way to check whether only the matter is left

How to keep a record

A record is evidence for later, but before that it is the tool that converts a present emotion into a matter. Writing it out separates fact from interpretation almost automatically.

  • Agreements reached verbally should be summarized into the shared channel the same day. Here is how I understood what you said is enough
  • For recurring issues, stack one line each for date, what was asked and whether a reply came. Once the third line lands, it is a pattern rather than a private grievance
  • Be clear about the purpose from the start. Collecting material to trap someone and organizing a matter so it can be reproduced are different kinds of document

There are still places where anger is the right answer

If all of this lands as so the point is to grin and bear it, the article has failed. There are situations where concealing anger simply leaves the situation running.

  • Safety — work that will injure someone unless it stops now. Politeness is not the priority here. Immediate, clear and loud is correct
  • Harassment or verbal abuse in progress — a line not drawn at the time becomes the later argument that nobody objected then
  • Repeated dismissal — if a concern has gone through normal channels three or more times with no response, the problem is not how it was delivered but that the channel does not work
  • Clear wrongdoing — data manipulation, rule violations, anything where silence functions as agreement

Even here the mode remains a choice. Aim at the behavior and its consequences rather than at the person, state the demand in one sentence, and leave a record on the spot. All three survive any intensity of anger, and holding them makes later procedure overwhelmingly easier. The point is not to bottle the feeling but to give words carrying that feeling a shape that can be used as evidence afterwards.

What the receiving side can do

This topic is almost always framed as a problem belonging to the person expressing. Yet one conclusion of the dual threshold model above is that the observer's reaction decides the outcome. Depending on how the receiving side responds, the same anger becomes either the entrance to a fix or the end of a working relationship.

  • Separate content from form while listening — a rough tone does not make the point wrong. Discard the content because of the form and the same problem returns
  • Do not adjudicate on the spot — ruling on right and wrong immediately pushes the other side into defense. Proposing a fact check first works better. With an audience present, simply suggesting a change of room lowers the temperature
  • If it repeats, look at the channel rather than the person — when the same person keeps getting angry about the same topic, the normal route for that topic is probably blocked

One boundary is worth stating plainly. The items above apply to anger about a matter, and not to situations involving personal attacks or humiliation. That is not something to handle with conversational technique; it is behavior to stop.

The institutional share — harassment is not an emotion problem

There is a domain that individual expression style never resolves. Conduct that is repeated, one-sided and leverages a position of advantage is not a failure of self-control but a matter institutions were designed to handle. Rename it and the responsible party disappears.

  • In Korea, Article 76-2 of the Labor Standards Act prohibits using positional or relational advantage to inflict physical or mental suffering beyond the appropriate scope of work, or to worsen the working environment (in force since 16 July 2019)
  • Article 76-3 allows anyone to report such conduct and obliges the employer to conduct an objective investigation without delay
  • Article 116 sets an administrative fine of up to 10 million won where the employer is the one committing the harassment, and up to 5 million won for breaches of the investigation and remedial duties under Article 76-3
  • Reports keep rising. Ministry of Employment and Labor figures released through a lawmaker's office and reported in February 2025 show 2,130 cases in 2019, then 5,823 in 2020, 7,774 in 2021, 8,961 in 2022, 11,038 in 2023 and 12,253 in 2024

Having an institution and having it work are separate matters. Figures released by the office of lawmaker Lee Yong-woo of the National Assembly environment and labor committee show that of 42,896 cases filed between 2022 and August 2025, 11,442 named the employer as the perpetrator, or 26.7%. One in four. When the employer is the accused and also the investigating body, impartiality is structurally hard to expect. That gap is not one individual technique can close.

Three layers, then. Expression style is chosen by the individual, the structure that manufactures recurring anger is fixed by teams and managers, and harassment belongs to institutions. Advice that drags the third layer down to the first is the most dangerous kind. Whether a specific case qualifies as harassment, and which procedure applies, depends on the workplace and the facts, so the labor authority or a qualified labor or legal professional should make that call. The procedure itself is covered separately in the workplace harassment response guide.

What could not be verified

The following items behind this article were not confirmed down to the primary source. Worth keeping in mind when weighing them.

  • The full HBR article by Porath and Pearson sits behind a paywall, so the sampling method and fielding dates of the 800-person poll are not verifiable from the open text. The percentages here are the ones widely cited from that article
  • Salary figures assigned by participants in the Brescoll and Uhlmann studies are reported inconsistently across secondary coverage, so no specific amount is quoted here and only the direction is described
  • For Hong et al. (2024), the national composition and design of the samples appear in the abstract, but the sample size of each study is not available in the openly accessible material. Korean workplace harassment report counts for 2025 also vary across coverage depending on the counting basis, so figures here stop at 2024

In short — this is not a counsel of endurance

What survives from never get angry at work is clear enough. Anger aimed at a person fails to deliver its content, and the cost tends to return to the person who expressed it. That much the original essay gets right.

The advice is complete only with three additions. First, feeling and expressing are different, and swallowed anger carries a price of its own. Second, the same expression produces different outcomes depending on whether it travels up or down, and that difference flips again across cultures. Third, harassment is an institutional matter, not a failure of self-control.

So the practical conclusion runs a little longer than the slogan. When anger rises, check the point it is aiming at first; put what has to be said into fact, impact and request and leave it in writing; and in front of conduct that crosses the line, do not retreat, but arrive with a record. And if you run a team, watching whether the same anger recurs in the same spot will always be faster than examining someone's temperament.
