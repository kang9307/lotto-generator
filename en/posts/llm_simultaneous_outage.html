Failing at the Same Time Does Not Mean Failing for the Same Reason - The Day Several AI Services Went Down Together

What to read on a status page, what to check in a postmortem, and what your own logs can and cannot separate

2026-09-05 Dev & Infra

When three services built by three different companies stop working within an hour or so of each other, a line gets drawn almost instantly in everyone's head. This cannot be a coincidence, there must be something shared underneath. The reaction is natural, and it is wrong often enough to be worth taking seriously.

This piece is about what to check before drawing that line. It is not about which company handled its day well or badly. It is about procedure. Reading correlation as a common cause is the single most frequent misreading in incident analysis, and the usual consequence is that the follow-up engineering work gets aimed at a layer where nothing was actually wrong.

Every factual claim below is marked at the sentence level as either something confirmed by opening a public status page directly, or something that could not be confirmed there and is reported second-hand. What could not be confirmed at all is collected in the second-to-last section along with the reason. Where something is a guess, it is labelled a guess.

The trigger - three services down inside eighty minutes

On 3 September 2026, three widely used conversational AI services began returning errors one after another inside a window of about one hour and twenty minutes. Communities picked it up immediately, and the conversation was not really about the outages themselves. It was about whether there was a single cause behind all three.

It helps to state the problem precisely before answering it. There are three separate questions here. First, what actually happened and when. Second, what did each provider say about it. Third, is there a basis for believing that one cause connects those statements. The third question only becomes answerable after the first two have been written down. Reverse the order, and you end up selecting the facts that fit the conclusion you already reached.

And in events of this shape, a story almost always finishes forming before the evidence does. It did here too. The story is that users abandoned the first service that broke, piled onto the next one, and overwhelmed it in turn. That story gets its own section further down, but the short version is that right now it has a shape rather than any supporting evidence.

What the primary sources say, and what they leave out

Start by putting every timestamp on one clock. Status pages often render in the reader's local time zone, and news coverage uses yet another. Mixing zones scrambles the ordering, and a scrambled ordering points causation in the wrong direction. Everything below is in Coordinated Universal Time, and the date throughout is 3 September 2026.

Time (UTC)What happenedNature of the source
12:37 to 12:56Elevated errors on a single model at one provider. Marked resolved after nineteen minutesStatus page opened directly
13:26Elevated errors across several models at the same provider. Posted as a separate incidentStatus page opened directly
Around 13:30A second provider's conversational service begins failingPress coverage. That status page could not be opened
13:41The earlier incident moves to the identified stage. What was identified is not written downStatus page opened directly
14:43A third provider posts elevated errors on its chat service and coding toolStatus page opened directly
15:17That third provider reports a mitigation applied and recovery being monitoredStatus page opened directly
16:16 and 16:23The first provider marks impact ended, then marks the incident resolved. Seven minutes apartStatus page opened directly
16:55The third provider marks its incident resolvedStatus page opened directly
Around 17:05The second provider's service recoversPress coverage

The first thing this table shows is that the start times did not cluster. They were 13:26, 13:30 and 14:43. The first two are four minutes apart, which is close enough that ordering is meaningless at that resolution, but the third is seventy-seven minutes later. The recoveries did not cluster either: 16:16, 16:55 and 17:05.

What matters more is what is not written on those pages. The third provider's incident had exactly three updates and not one of them names a cause. There is a sentence saying the issue is being investigated, a sentence saying a mitigation has been applied and recovery is being monitored, and a sentence saying the issue has been resolved. The widely quoted phrase routing error does not appear there. It appears in press coverage, attributed to the company. The first provider is the same story in a different form. There is an explicit note that the cause was identified, and no statement of what the cause was.

That distinction looks pedantic and is not. The moment an explanation that lives only in press coverage gets cited as though it came from the status page, every inference built on top of it becomes impossible to check.

The three explanations sit at different layers

Line the public explanations up and the striking thing is that they point at three different layers of the stack. One describes a problem in the path traffic takes. One describes a problem at a specific physical facility. One says the cause was found and fixed and does not go further.

How should that be read? Two readings are available, and both of them hold.

  • Reading A - different explanations, different causes. The simplest interpretation. Three companies each had an unrelated failure at a different layer, and the timing overlap is coincidence
  • Reading B - different explanations, possibly the same cause. Every company observes only inside its own boundary. When something shared upstream wobbles, one operator sees path failures and another sees compute nodes disappearing. The same event showing up differently on different dashboards is ordinary, not exotic
  • So the difference in explanations does not refute the common-cause theory. What it does is raise the burden that theory has to carry, because someone now has to explain how one mechanism produced three distinct symptoms
  • And in the material published so far, that connecting explanation does not exist. Absent is not the same as false. It means not yet present

Stopping the judgement here is the accurate move. But stopping the judgement is not the same as doing nothing. It is still perfectly possible today to work out which observations would narrow each theory down, and that is what the rest of this article does.

Counting how unlikely that overlap really was

The intuition that this cannot be coincidence usually comes from a gut calculation with the window set to one day. But the day is not what actually surprised anyone. What surprised people is that such a day existed at all this year. Those two framings produce very different numbers.

A rough calculation gives a sense of scale. The inputs come from what was observable on the status pages over two days, so this is an estimate and not a measurement. It is still useful for getting the order of magnitude right.

Assumptions (crude values pulled from two observed days, not measurements)
  Incidents per provider     roughly 1 per day
  Duration of one incident   roughly 3 hours
  Probability that a given provider is impaired at a random instant
      p = 3 / 24 = 0.125

Probability all three are impaired at the same instant (assuming independence)
      0.125 ^ 3 = 0.00195      -> about 0.2% on any given day

Probability that at least one such day occurs in a year
      1 - (1 - 0.00195) ^ 365 = 0.51   -> about 51%

What the result says is this. The chance of it happening on one particular day is small, but the chance of it happening at some point during a year is close to a coin flip. Widen the overlap window to the eighty minutes actually observed and the number climbs further. This is structurally the same trap as the birthday problem, where intuition consistently underestimates how likely a shared date is in a room of modest size.

  • This calculation is not a verdict of coincidence. It only shows that the coincidence theory is not absurd, and therefore that refuting it requires actual evidence
  • Change the inputs and the answer moves a lot. One-hour incidents push the annual figure down sharply, two incidents a day push it up. Read the order of magnitude, not the digits
  • The independence assumption is itself something to be tested. If the providers genuinely share an upstream, they are not independent, and overlaps become far more frequent than this model predicts
  • The place where this calculation earns its keep in practice is elsewhere: estimating how likely it is that two of your own dependencies fail in the same month. If that number is larger than you assumed, the priority order of your fallback work changes

Five signals that argue against a shared upstream

The first theory anyone reaches for is shared infrastructure: several services riding the same content delivery network, the same name-resolution system or the same cloud region, and that upstream wobbled. The good news is that this theory can largely be tested from outside. There are five observable signals.

SignalWhat a shared cause would look likeWhat was actually observed
The upstream's own reportThat upstream posts an incident of its ownA major delivery network explicitly denied any significant disruption
Shape of the blast radiusUnrelated services on the same upstream go down tooNo broad simultaneous failure elsewhere. The damage landed selectively on AI services
Layer of the symptomConnection failures, name lookup failures, handshake failuresRequests arrived and came back as errors. An application-layer symptom
Spread of start timesClustered within seconds or a few minutesSpread across seventy-seven minutes
Spread of recovery timesRecovery clusters once the cause is removedSpread across forty-nine minutes

Four of the five run against a shared network upstream, and the second and third carry the most weight. When a delivery network or a name-resolution system falters, it does not pick out AI services and leave everything else alone. The shopping site and the banking app sitting on the same upstream wobble along with them. Failures of that kind also tend to break before the request reaches an application at all, which produces a very different symptom from a server returning an error response.

It is worth adding that the upstream's denial alone would be weak. Status pages are self-reported, and they sometimes reflect trouble late. The distance between published uptime and lived experience is covered separately in the piece on the gap between official uptime and real incidents. So the denial counts as one signal out of five and gets read alongside the other four rather than on its own.

The theory that survives

As the shared-network theory weakens, a different one is left standing. Shared compute. This one passes the signals above rather than failing them.

The public apology from the operator that named a physical facility included, alongside its apology to its own users, an apology to the compute partners affected. That is a fact established through press coverage, and it means the facility in question was not exclusively its own. What was not published is who those partners were. Whether the other two providers were among them is unknown.

  • When compute capacity shrinks, requests still arrive normally and then fail to be served. The symptom surfaces as an error response, which passes the third signal
  • Shared compute is concentrated in one industry. The organisations renting large training and inference capacity are overwhelmingly AI services, so a selective blast radius is not a contradiction here. That passes the second signal
  • A capacity reduction does not become a total outage instantly. Headroom absorbs it until a threshold is crossed, which means start times can legitimately spread out. That does not conflict with the fourth signal either
  • And that is as far as it goes. A theory surviving is not a theory being confirmed. Surviving only means it has not yet been ruled out
  • Confirming it would take something specific: the partner list, or a statement from the other two providers that they were drawing capacity from that facility. Neither exists today

What the cascading-overload theory would need

The story that spread furthest is cascading overload, often described with the term for a herd bolting all at once at a clap of thunder. The structure is simple. A goes down, A's users move to B, B cannot absorb the sudden load and goes down, and those users move on to C.

The mechanism itself is entirely real. Caches expiring together and stampeding the database behind them, or queued requests flooding in the instant a service comes back, are things every operator has seen. The question is whether there is any basis for applying that mechanism here. Laid out requirement by requirement, it splits like this.

RequirementWhy it is neededCan it be checked today
Ordering of start timesA stampede has a direction. The receiving side must fail laterPartly. The first two are four minutes apart, too close to order
Ratio of scaleThe shifting traffic must exceed the receiving side's headroomNo published figures. Concurrent user counts are not disclosed
Rising load indicatorsRequest volume, queue depth and rejection rate must actually climb firstNot checkable. This lives inside the provider and was not published
Shape of the errorsCapacity limits show up as queuing, refusal and partial degradationPoints the other way. Certain model families failed selectively
Duration profilePure overload usually resolves quickly once autoscaling reactsAll three ran two to three hours. Long for pure overload

The fourth row matters most. One provider's status page listed the affected models, and that list changed while the incident was running. Some models recovered early and others stayed impaired longer. Overload does not fail selectively like that. When resources run short the effect is usually broad, and latency climbs before anything starts failing outright. A failure that spares some model families and not others looks much more like a deployment or configuration problem.

So the position is that the third requirement cannot be checked at all, and the fourth requirement points in the opposite direction. That is why this article treats cascading overload strictly as a hypothesis. It is not being declared false either. It means demonstrating it would take data that nobody outside those companies currently has.

A theory with no disproof condition is not an explanation

The cascading-overload story has one more property worth naming. It survives any observation you throw at it. If the ordering is wrong, only part of the traffic moved. If the load metrics look ordinary, the system was already near its threshold and a small nudge tipped it. If the error shape is wrong, the stampede exposed some other latent defect.

A story with that property is comfortable, because nothing can embarrass it. And that is exactly why it is useless. You cannot build a countermeasure on it, because there is no observation that would tell you whether the countermeasure removed the cause.

  • When you form a hypothesis, write down the observation that would kill it at the same time. One sentence: if this value comes back like this, we drop this theory
  • If you cannot write that sentence, what you have is not a hypothesis but a narrative. Narratives are useful for retelling and useless for remediation
  • The most dangerous moment in a review meeting is the one where a single explanation arrives and everybody nods. Consensus is not verification
  • In practice, keep at least two live hypotheses. Once only one is left, the only evidence anybody notices is the evidence that fits it
  • And allow yourself to conclude that you do not know. Being unable to determine a cause from outside information is a common and honest state, and forcing a conclusion there is how bad countermeasures get built

How to read a status page

From here on, this is a tool you can use next time. Status pages mostly share a skeleton. One incident carries a series of updates, and each update steps through a status label. Both pages opened for this article showed exactly that progression: investigating, identified, monitoring, resolved.

  • Identified is not published. The incident that reached the identified stage here never said what had been identified. That label is closer to an internal signal that the responders know their next action
  • The end of impact and the resolved marker are different timestamps. One incident here wrote the moment impact ended into the update body, and the resolved marker came seven minutes later. Which one you use changes your availability arithmetic
  • The affected component list grows during the incident. Not seeing your path in the first post is not reassurance. That list usually only settles near the end
  • The first post is not the start of the outage. Detecting, confirming and drafting a public update all take time. When the two timestamps are identical, that often means they were never separated in the first place
  • Mitigated and resolved are different words. Mitigated means the symptom is suppressed, resolved means the cause is gone. Systems can sit in the mitigated state for days
  • Read the other incidents from the same day. One provider here posted three incidents across the two days and only one of them became the story. Reading only that one misrepresents what the company actually went through

What a status page structurally cannot tell you

The limits of a status page come from what kind of artefact it is, not from any lack of diligence by the people writing it. It is a live noticeboard, not an analysis document. Knowing what not to expect from it removes most of the misreading.

First, it is self-reported. A person publishes it by hand. It is not a raw feed from monitoring; a responding team judges the situation, picks a label and writes the text. So there is always a gap between the event and the post.

Second, the purpose of the notice is not to explain. Text published mid-incident exists to tell users what to expect right now. The cause is still being worked out, and writing an unconfirmed guess means retracting it later. That is why the sentences are short and dry. Reading that dryness as concealment is usually a mistake.

Third, the unit of impact is generally coarse. Phrases like some users or intermittent errors carry no magnitude. Five per cent and forty per cent can be published under identical wording. Which means the size of the impact has to be measured on your side, not theirs.

Fourth, absence is not evidence. Nothing on the upstream cloud or delivery network's status page does not prove nothing happened. It might reflect late, it might have affected only certain customers, or it might have sat below the publishing threshold. What happens when a whole pipeline hangs on one provider is covered separately in the piece on the price of putting deployment in one place.

What to check in a postmortem

The document that does what a status page cannot is the postmortem. Google's site reliability engineering book defines it as a written record of an incident, its impact, the actions taken to mitigate or resolve it, the root causes, and the follow-up actions to prevent recurrence. The same book lists the usual triggers for writing one: user-visible downtime or degradation past a threshold, data loss of any kind, intervention by an on-call engineer, a resolution time above some threshold, and a monitoring failure.

When reading someone else's postmortem, the checklist is fairly stable.

  • Are the time of occurrence and the time of detection separated? If they are identical, they were usually never separated. Detection taking longer than recovery is extremely common
  • What is the unit of impact? A fraction of requests, a count of users, a region, a feature. Without a unit, magnitude cannot be compared to anything
  • Are the trigger and the cause distinguished? A deployment is usually the trigger, and the cause is the latent defect that the deployment exposed. A report that names only the trigger will meet the same defect again through a different trigger
  • Does it answer why it happened then? The defect may have existed for a long time. The real answer is what condition finally crossed the threshold
  • Do the follow-up actions have an owner and a date? Items without them do not get done. This is also the first part of the document to go vague
  • What will be different next time? Written as a conditional, so that it can actually be verified afterwards
  • Does it avoid naming individuals? The same book defines a blameless review as one that identifies contributing causes without indicting any individual or team. Reading someone else's report as a hunt for who erred leaves you with nothing to carry back into your own system

What your logs separate and what they do not

There is work to do while waiting for external statements. The first step is to divide your own records into what they can separate and what they cannot. Knowing where that boundary sits is what stops you from manufacturing a conclusion out of data you do not have.

What your logs separateWhy it separatesWhat they do not separate
The failing layerName lookup, connection, handshake and response each leave a different traceWhat went wrong inside the other system
Distribution of response codesRate limiting, capacity exhaustion and internal faults tell different storiesWhether users from another service piled in
Time to first byteSeparates waiting-then-failing from failing instantly. Instant failure is not overloadWhether capacity was short or configuration was wrong
Failure rate per endpointSeparates total failure from selective failure. Selective points at deploymentWhether a physical facility was shared
Load your own retries createdWhether your client made things worse is your problem, not theirsExactly when the outage began
State of an unrelated upstream at the same momentThe cheapest way to separate your network from theirsWhat happened during intervals in which you sent nothing

The last row on the right is the one people forget. An interval with no requests in it is not a healthy interval, it is an unobserved one. Reading a gap in the failure record as a period of normal operation, and therefore dating the outage wrongly, happens constantly. The trap is worst during low-traffic hours, which is exactly when many of these events start.

The last row on the left requires preparation. If you only probe the upstream you care about, then when that upstream fails you cannot distinguish it from your own connectivity. Probing one unrelated external endpoint on the same schedule gives you that distinction for free. Which signals to keep in an agent or automated pipeline is covered in more depth in the piece on tracking what production agents get wrong.

A procedure for the next simultaneous outage

Folding all of the above into an order gives the following. This sequence is not designed to reach a conclusion quickly. It is designed so that a wrong conclusion is still catchable later.

Several upstreams fail inside the same window  ->  where did it break?

  Cannot resolve the name   -> name resolution layer. Suspect shared upstream
  Cannot open a connection  -> path layer. Suspect your own network too
  Cannot complete handshake -> encryption layer. Check certificates, inspection
  A response does come back -> application layer. Look at the code mix
       rate limit / capacity -> consistent with the overload theory
       instant internal error-> inconsistent with the overload theory
       only some paths fail  -> look at deployment and configuration first
  1. Put every timestamp on one clock. Rewriting each one in Coordinated Universal Time is the single line that protects the ordering
  2. Write down your own observations before reading any announcement. Read the announcement first and your memory quietly rearranges itself to match its wording
  3. Split the layer. Settling just the first branch of the diagram above discards half the candidate theories
  4. Probe one unrelated upstream. It is the cheapest way to rule your own side out
  5. Record status page labels and timestamps separately. The label is progress reporting; the timestamp is the evidence
  6. Hold at least two hypotheses and pre-write what would kill each. This step is the whole point of the article
  7. Turn your retries down. Do not add load to the other side while you are observing it. Check that your backoff is exponential and capped
  8. Defer the conclusion. Revisit the hypotheses when a postmortem appears. Deferring is procedure, not incompetence

What adding vendors buys, and what it does not

Days like this usually end with the same resolution: stop betting everything on one provider. The direction is right, but unless you write down precisely what you are buying, cost goes up and safety does not.

  • What it reliably buys - one vendor's bad deployment, one vendor's configuration error, one vendor's regional failure. Those three account for a large share of real incidents, so this alone pays for itself
  • What it does not buy - shared compute, a shared name-resolution or authentication upstream, and a fallback path that does not actually work. The last item is the one that collapses most often in practice
  • This event is a reminder of exactly that. Your fallback target can be down at the same moment you need it. A fallback is not simply another company, it has to be a company unlikely to fail alongside the first, and since that correlation is never published you are left estimating it
  • Fallback paths need a trickle of real traffic in normal times. A path that is never used does not work when it is needed. Expired credentials, changed response formats and unrequested quota all surface at once, at the worst possible moment
  • Routing several providers through one interface addresses half of this. What such a design actually solves and what it leaves untouched is examined in the piece on a gateway that unifies many providers
  • The most dependable protection is not on the infrastructure side at all, it is on the product side: a degraded mode in which the service still works without that capability. Decide in advance what the user sees when the call fails, and your side stays up even when every upstream is down
  • How far to hand operations over to automation is discussed separately in the piece on putting automation into reliability work. During a simultaneous outage, automatic failover can end up shuttling load between systems that are all already struggling

One closing point. The real cost of a day like this is not the few hours of downtime, it is the countermeasures built afterwards. Get the cause wrong and expensive redundancy goes into the wrong layer, while the next incident arrives somewhere else entirely. Which is why the procedure comes first.

What could not be confirmed

  • One of the three providers' official status pages could not be opened. Two attempts were made and both were refused. That service's start time, recovery time and the statement about the physical facility are therefore all sourced from press coverage, and the body text marks them as such
  • The widely quoted routing error explanation could not be found on the relevant status page. The three updates there contain no word pointing at a cause. It is reported as a company statement, and until a postmortem is published nothing firmer can be said
  • One incident's duration differs between sources. Subtracting the status page's start marker from its end-of-impact note gives two hours and fifty minutes; press coverage reported three hours and six minutes. Which moment was taken as the start could not be determined, so the body uses the status page figures
  • None of the three providers has published a detailed postmortem for this event as of writing. Every conclusion here about causes is therefore provisional and may change once one appears
  • The list of partners using that compute facility was not published. The shared-compute theory therefore remains neither ruled out nor confirmed
  • The central evidence for the cascading-overload theory, the change in request volume at each service immediately before failure, is internal provider data and cannot be checked externally. The judgement in the body rests only on the published error patterns
  • The probability calculation in the fourth section is an estimate using crude inputs pulled from two observed days, not a measurement. Each provider's actual annual incident frequency and mean duration could not be confirmed
  • The official definition document for status page status labels could not be opened. The description of those labels in the body is based on the wording and ordering observed on the two pages read directly for this article
