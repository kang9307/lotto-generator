Introduction: The Background of the Dubai Chewy Cookie Craze

The Dubai Chewy Cookie craze that spread through Korea from the second half of 2025 was treated as more than a dessert trend: it became a social phenomenon. The dessert derives from the "Dubai chocolate" that drew attention overseas after 2022, and it stood out on social media for its texture of pistachio cream and kadayif (a Middle Eastern vermicelli pastry).

Alongside the craze, several issues were discussed: ingredient price swings, retail price increases, labelling of place of manufacture and origin, advertising disclosure, and hygiene control. This article separates what government announcements and news reports confirm from what consumers can check for themselves before buying. Figures with no official statistics behind them, such as cost and profit-margin estimates, are not included.

In 2026 the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety carried out intensive inspections of delivery restaurants and similar outlets handling trend desserts, and the subject widened into a larger agenda of consumer protection and food safety.

1. What is the Dubai Chewy Cookie?

1.1 Product Features and Secrets to Popularity

The key features of Dubai chewy cookies include:

  • Kadayif: A thin, vermicelli-like pastry originating from the Middle East, providing a crispy yet chewy texture.
  • Pistachio Cream: Made from ground pistachios, characterized by a deep green color and nutty flavor.
  • Tahini (Sesame Paste): Added to some products to enhance flavor.
  • Chocolate Coating: Wrapped in milk or dark chocolate to add sweetness.

The visual impact on social media helped the product become known quickly. Videos of cutting the cookie in half to reveal the pistachio cream and kadayif, combined with ASMR, were widely shared on TikTok and Instagram Reels, and the overseas "Dubai chocolate mukbang" trend carried over to Korea.

1.2 Distribution Channels in the Korean Market

Dubai chewy cookies are distributed through various channels in Korea:

  • Direct Import: Direct orders of products from the original overseas brand
  • Convenience Store Products: Dubai-themed desserts released by convenience store chains as their own products
  • Bakeries/Dessert Cafes: Individual businesses selling handmade Dubai cookies
  • Online Marketplaces: Sales through large online shopping malls, open markets and smart-store style shops

With so many channels, quality and labelling standards can differ from seller to seller. Even when "Dubai" is in the product name, the place of manufacture differs by product, so it is safest to check the manufacturer and origin on the package.

2.2 Types to Check in Labelling and Advertising

Food labelling and advertising in Korea fall under the Fair Labelling and Advertising Act and the Food Labelling and Advertising Act. For trend foods, the types consumers may want to check are:

  • "Direct Import from Dubai" type wording: whether the place of manufacture or import matches what is stated
  • "Limited Quantity" wording: whether the basis for the limit is given
  • "Local Recipe" wording: whether there is verifiable support
  • Ingredient content labels: whether a claim such as "pistachio XX%" matches the stated content

These items are not findings of violations; they are points to check before buying. Whether a specific case is a violation is for the competent authority to decide.

2.3 Food Safety Inspection Results

The MFDS inspection was reported to have found breaches of hygienic handling standards and similar items. Points to check are:

  • Hygienic handling standards: basic hygiene rules such as wearing hygiene caps and masks
  • Consumption deadline: whether products past the deadline are stored or sold
  • Health examinations: whether operators and staff have had them
  • Allergen labels: labelling of allergens such as tree nuts, wheat and milk

On 27 January 2026 the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety announced an intensive inspection of dessert delivery restaurants and related outlets, and from 2 to 6 February it inspected 4,180 sites with local governments: 2,947 dessert delivery restaurants and 1,233 unstaffed ice-cream shops. Results released on 25 February 2026 identified 81 sites in violation of the Food Sanitation Act (60 delivery restaurants and 21 unstaffed shops), and among 128 prepared-food samples tested, one Dubai chewy cookie sample exceeded the standard for Staphylococcus aureus (reported as 2.5 times the standard). Administrative action against that restaurant was announced as planned.

3. Points to Examine in Marketing Strategy

3.1 Influencer Marketing and Advertising Disclosure

In a trend driven by social media, influencer posts become a channel of information. Consumers can check the following:

  • Advertising disclosure: whether the post states that it is sponsored or an advertisement
  • Unverifiable modifiers: whether it relies on subjective phrases such as "best dessert ever" or "legendary"
  • Balance of reviews: whether both positive and negative reviews are visible
  • Interests: whether the poster discloses any financial relationship with the business

The Korea Fair Trade Commission's "Guide to Disclosing Economic Interests" directs that posts made with sponsorship or compensation state that fact clearly. Disclosure differs post by post, so this article does not generalise; please check each post yourself.

3.2 Scarcity Wording and FOMO Marketing

FOMO (Fear Of Missing Out) is a psychological factor that pushes buyers to hurry, through wording such as limited edition or deadline approaching. Points to check for trend foods are:

  • Stock indication: whether a "sold out" label matches actual stock, using the seller's notice
  • Purchase time limits: whether the reason for selling only at set times is explained
  • Resale prices: whether there is any reason to accept a resale price above the list price
  • "Now or Never": whether urgency wording is standing in for price comparison

Such wording can prompt impulse buying, so it is worth comparing price and labelling once more before purchase. This article found no basis to say that any particular seller intended it.

3.3 Origin and Identity

On the identity of Dubai cookies, the following can be said:

  • Place of manufacture differs by product: even with "Dubai" in the name, check the manufacturer and origin on the package
  • Origin of Dubai chocolate: the dessert brand known as the original is reported to have been started in Dubai by a British-Egyptian founder, and it is not a traditional Emirati confection
  • Relation to kunafa: it is described as a variation on elements of the Middle Eastern dessert kunafa (kadayif and pistachio)
  • Use of a place name: whether a place name in a product name can cause confusion about where it is made should be judged together with the labelling

4. What Consumers Should Know

4.1 Checklist for Smart Consumption

Before purchasing Dubai cookies, check the following:

  1. Verify Manufacturer: Confirm the business has a food manufacturing permit
  2. Ingredient Labels: Check pistachio content and allergen indicators
  3. Expiration Date: Products containing cream tend to have short deadlines, so check the label
  4. Price Comparison: The same product may have different prices across platforms
  5. Review Authenticity: If there are only overwhelmingly positive reviews, check other sources as well
  6. Advertising Status: Check for "ad" or "sponsored" labels on influencer posts

Later Developments (Checked 2026-09-29)

The change confirmed since the first version (2026-01-11) is in import indicators. According to Customs Service and MFDS data cited in a Seoul Shinmun report of 2026-08-23, pistachio imports were 372 tonnes in December 2025, 594 in January 2026, 731 in February (the peak), 470 in March, 482 in April, 161 in May, 119 in June and 54 in July. July imports were 92.6% below February, and import value fell 93.6%, from USD 15.469 million in February to USD 990,000 in July.

Import inspections of kadayif-related processed foods also went from 168 in January to 340 in February (the peak), 138 in March, 36 in April, 30 in May and 22 in June, a 93.5% fall from February to June. These figures are only an indirect indicator of how demand for the trend changed and are not sales of Dubai cookies themselves (pistachios are used in other foods too).

What Could Not Be Confirmed

  • No follow-up announcement was found on the administrative action against businesses caught in the February inspection or on further inspection plans.
  • The MFDS press release body (attached files) could not be opened, so the inspection figures were cross-checked against a Newspim article reporting the same content.
  • Kadayif and pistachio prices after August 2026 could not be confirmed from official statistics. The price table above is as of January 2026.
  • The founding date of the original brand could not be checked against the source and is described on a report basis.
  • The Fair Trade Commission guide and the Food Safety Korea allergen labelling page were not reopened this time to check for revisions.

Conclusion: Recommendations for a Healthy Dessert Culture

The Dubai chewy cookie craze shows a cross-section of consumer culture in the social media era. Visual impact, viral spread and the fear of missing out combined to turn a single dessert into a social phenomenon.

The risks consumers may meet in this craze can be summarised as:

  • High retail prices reflecting higher ingredient costs
  • The possibility that labelling and advertising differ from the actual product
  • Quality differences between sellers
  • Food safety problems found in hygiene inspections

For a healthy dessert culture, wise consumer judgment is necessary. Rather than being swept up in trends, it helps to weigh the actual value of a product and compare labelling and price. Dubai cookies themselves are not the problem. Checking labelling, advertising and hygiene information before buying lets you enjoy the trend safely.
