This article rests on the summary of the privacy study Prompt like a Butterfly, Sting like a Tracker that GeekNews introduced in September 2026, on the author's site notice, and on a secondary write-up of the method. The paper PDF itself was not opened. The names of the analyzed services are not discussed here, and they are referred to only as the nine analyzed services. This article makes no judgment on whether any service or business is unlawful.

What the study covered — scope and key figures

This article is not meant to say that AI is watching us. A conversational service is in the end a web page and an app, and web pages and apps have carried external code for advertising and analytics for a long time. A study has now measured what that external code can see on the screens of conversational services, so the results are relayed here with their numbers and connected to checks a user can run on their own screen.

People who use conversational AI type into the input box about health, money, work and relationships. The web page or app wrapped around that screen may carry scripts for advertising and analytics, and what those scripts can see is the question of this study. It is described as carried out by IMDEA Networks researchers with co-authors from UC3M and an independent researcher, and accepted for publication at PoPETs 2027. The paper is titled Prompt like a Butterfly, Sting like a Tracker: A Privacy Analysis of Web and Mobile Conversational AI Agents.

The scope is 9 web clients of conversational AI services and 8 Android apps. The study examined how advertising and tracking services (ATSes) are attached, where data flows, and how consent choices, subscription tiers and access-control mechanisms change the exposure of a conversation to third parties.

What ad and analytics scripts can see in a conversational service

The scripts meant here may have been put in by the service operator, or they may have been pulled in along with the advertising and analytics tools the operator uses. From the user side it is hard to tell which, and a browser runs the code a page carries without distinction. So the starting point of a check is not who put it in but where my browser sends requests.

An advertising or analytics script on a web page runs inside the browser that opened the page. It can therefore reach what the page shows on screen and values such as the page address and the title. That is a general web principle and not a finding of this study alone. What differs in conversational AI is that the content of a conversation can seep into that page address and title. The paper introduction summarizes that existing tracking infrastructure — tracking pixels, SDKs, persistent identifiers and server-side forwarding — has been carried into conversational AI, exposing conversation artifacts such as prompts, generated titles and permalinks to third parties.

On the traditional web, what an advertising script knew was roughly which page was viewed. In a conversational service the page address differs per conversation, the title is made from the conversation content, and the input text is on screen as it is. That is why the nature of the exposed information changes even when the same kind of script is attached.

  • Page address: in a service that gives each conversation a unique address, the address itself becomes a key that points to that conversation.
  • Title: a title automatically made by summarizing the conversation appears on screen and on the tab.
  • Input text: the sentences a user types are text on the screen, so they fall within reach of code that reads the screen.
  • Screen capture: the study also describes cases in which screenshots were transmitted.
  • Persistent identifiers: if an advertising ID or a tracking cookie goes along, the information can be tied to a specific user.

This structure is especially a problem for conversational services because of the nature of what users type. Unlike a one-line search query, the input of a conversation often holds a long account of a situation, a worry or a personal circumstance, and that can be processed into the form of a title or an address and leave the screen. That seems to be why the study chose topics such as health and salary for its test inputs.

The transfer types the study observed

The reason for separating transfer types is that the size of the risk differs by type. A one-line conversation title and a whole input text differ in how much information is exposed, and a transfer with an identifier and one without differ in how far they lead to a specific user. The study reports these differences separately, but this article relays only the range that appears in the summaries.

The core results of the study can be summarized in three strands. The first is how common advertising and tracking integration is. All nine analyzed services were integrated with at least one advertising or tracking service. The second is the transfer of conversation-derived information. 6 web clients and 3 apps sent conversation URLs, titles, prompts and screenshots to third parties. The third is conversation links left public without access control.

These figures should be read with their denominators. The web is 6 of 9 and the apps are 3 of 8. If they are lumped together as 6 of the services leaked conversations, the difference between web and app disappears. And what the study reports is transfers observed under its test conditions, not that the same thing always happens to every user.

  • The share of web clients that transferred was 6 of 9 and of apps 3 of 8, so the web side was higher.
  • Even for the same service web and app behavior can differ, so 6 of 9 and 3 of 8 count clients and not services.
  • Persistent identifiers are described as often going out together with conversation-derived information.
  • What data went out and how a third party used it are separate questions, and only the former was observed.

Public share links — a path for trackers to read a whole conversation

The most striking part is the share link. The study reports that some providers left conversation share links public without access control, and in that case a tracker can read the entire conversation. The principle is simple. If a conversation can be opened by anyone who knows the link, a third party that receives that address can also open the address and fetch the content. The moment an address is carried in an advertising or analytics request, the address becomes a passage to the conversation body.

Even if the user who made a link sent it to no one, the same passage opens if the structure is such that the address is carried in an advertising or analytics request the moment the link is opened in a browser. What the study describes is the existence of such a passage, and which service did so under what conditions is not discussed in this article.

  • Whether a share link opens without logging in is the first thing to check.
  • See how each service displays the visibility of a link (anyone with the link, specific users, private).
  • See whether there is a menu to stop sharing or delete the link.
  • Assume that content already viewed or copied is not recalled by deletion.

It helps to have one standard to ask yourself before making a share link. Would it be acceptable if this conversation were searched or passed to others. If not, it is safer to copy over only the needed part instead of making a link. A public link, from the moment it is made, gives its content an address outside the range the service controls.

How much consent settings and pricing tiers changed the result

A consent banner stands on the premise that users can control tracking. The study varied the consent state while measuring in order to see how far that premise holds in actual transfers. How far the figures in this section generalize can only be judged by looking at the tables in the paper body.

The study reports that the cookie consent choice affects the scope of tracking. The secondary write-up of the method says three consent states were compared: the banner ignored, all non-essential cookies rejected, and all accepted. The same write-up says that even after choosing reject all, 80.8 percent of third-party trackers remained active, and that tracking behavior of the free and paid tiers was nearly the same.

This figure comes from the secondary write-up that summarizes the paper and could not be checked against the paper body. So rather than reading that 80.8 percent remain even if you reject as a value that applies to ordinary users, it is safer to read it as a qualitative conclusion that under the study conditions cases were observed in which the reject choice did not eliminate tracking completely.

  • The choice in the consent banner is described as affecting the number and kinds of third-party requests.
  • That a considerable share of trackers stayed active even after choosing reject all is the report of the secondary write-up.
  • The difference in tracking behavior between the free and paid tiers is described as not large.
  • The implication of this result is that it is hard to assume that raising the pricing tier makes tracking disappear.

Steps a user can take to check alone

There is one thing to know before starting. What browser DevTools shows is only the requests that the user's browser sends out. Server-side forwarding, in which the service's server passes information from its own server to a third party, does not appear in DevTools. Since the paper introduction mentions server-side forwarding as one branch of tracking infrastructure, seeing nothing on screen does not mean nothing goes out.

Whether or not one believes the conclusions of the study, there are things a user can check on their own screen. The table below divides the check items into what is looked at, how it is confirmed, and the limits of that method. Every step is read-only, and no commands that change settings and no workarounds are included. The DevTools screen may contain conversation content, so care is needed when sharing the screen or posting a capture.

  • The first thing to do is to sort the domain column in the Network tab and count requests to places other than the service's own domain.
  • Next, click one request and read whether its address or body contains a conversation title.
  • After choosing reject in the consent banner, open the same screen again and compare the requests that remain.
  • Open a share link in a window that is not logged in after making it, and turn sharing off if it is not needed.
  • Before entering sensitive matters, look first at the service's visibility settings and its delete menu.

The results of a check can be read in three ways. First, if there are almost no third-party requests or only requests that look necessary to run the service, exposure on the browser side is small. Second, if there are many third-party requests but no conversation content is visible in their addresses and bodies, it may be at the level of only the fact of a visit being known. Third, if a conversation title, conversation address or input text is visible in a request, it overlaps with the types the study pointed out, so asking the service's contact channel for confirmation is reasonable. In every case the result is an observation at that time, on that account, in that browser.

The response after getting a result is broadly two things. One is changing how you use the service so that sensitive matters are not entered, and the other is inquiring by the method described in the service's settings and privacy policy. Using ad and tracker blocking tools is also an option, but its cost may be that parts of the screen break, as covered in the article introduced earlier.

Measurement method and limits

What people who quote study results often miss is the measurement conditions. Figures are the result of an experiment with set conditions, and when the conditions change the values change. The method description and the list of limits below are for judging how far the figures can be carried over.

According to the secondary write-up of the method, the study used network capture in Chrome for the web and instrumented phones that recorded outbound connections, permission access and identifier reads for Android. It measured while varying account type (guest, free, paid) and the three consent states mentioned above, and used deliberately sensitive conversations, such as questions about a health condition and salary, as test inputs. This description is from the secondary write-up and not from the paper body.

This method has structural limits. What follows is commentary in this article and not from the paper, and each item is a general caution that comes from the nature of the method.

  • It is a measurement at one point in time. The script composition of web services changes often. There is no guarantee that the result at the time of measurement is the same now, and the measurement date of the paper is not stated in the material seen here.
  • The conversations were test conversations. Because the researchers deliberately entered sensitive topics, whether the same information goes out at the same rate in everyday conversations is a separate matter.
  • The platforms are limited. Chrome is described for the web and Android for mobile, and iOS is not mentioned as within scope.
  • Transfer and use are different. What the study observed is information that went out to third parties, and whether the third parties stored it or used it for advertising is another question.
  • The sources of the figures overlap. 44 and 9, 6 of 9 and 3 of 8 come from the GeekNews summary and the paper introduction, while 124 domains and 80.8 percent come from the secondary write-up.

Legal assessment and disclosure — what the study said

When the words legal assessment come up, it is easy to read them at once as who was at fault. But what the study did was to hold observed practices up to a legal standard, and reaching a conclusion is a matter for the supervisory authorities. This article relays the procedure the study described and adds no judgment on top of it.

The study is described as assessing observed practices from the viewpoint of the European personal data protection framework, that is, the GDPR and the ePrivacy Directive. It is also said to have followed a responsible disclosure process that included the providers of the services concerned and the competent European data protection authorities. The summaries seen here contain no statement that any service was determined to be unlawful, and no judgment about the reason or intent behind how a service was designed.

Accordingly, this article also says nothing about unlawfulness. Lawfulness varies with jurisdiction and purpose, the validity of consent and the processing structure, and it is a matter that is settled only after the judgment of a supervisory authority. What is actually useful to users is less a verdict than a way to see for themselves what goes out of their own screen.

How far to raise ad and tracker blocking is covered by other articles on this site. What breaks at each blocking strength can be seen in What Breaks When You Raise Blocking Strength, and smartphone app permissions and location-tracking settings in Smartphone Location Tracking Block Guide. This article does not explain those axes again.

The last resort a user has is to inquire. A service privacy policy usually gives a contact channel and a method for access and deletion requests. Through that channel one can ask on what terms conversation data is provided to advertising and analytics partners. The form and scope of the answer differ with the service and jurisdiction, so this article does not predict the result.

What could not be verified

  • The paper PDF body. Because it was not opened, figures and wording depend on the GeekNews summary, the paper introduction and the secondary write-up of the method.
  • The 124 domains, the count of 34 organizations classified as advertising or tracking services, and the 80.8 percent still active after reject all. These are figures of the secondary write-up and could not be checked against the paper body.
  • The time at which the measurement was carried out. No material states a date.
  • Individual results for the analyzed services. Because this article chose not to name services, comparison by service is not covered.
  • How third parties actually used the information they received, and the intent on the service side. It could not be confirmed from the materials whether the study determined this.
  • Whether the services improved after disclosure, and any follow-up judgment by supervisory authorities.
