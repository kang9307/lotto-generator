Why Security Is Unusually Hard to Learn - Where the Problem Is Structure, Not Knowledge

The target belongs to someone else, the law has drawn a line, and the information ages fast

Sep 4, 2026 Tech & IT

What stops someone in the first week of learning security is not a hard concept. It is the discovery that there is nothing to practise on. Programming begins when you open an editor. Data analysis begins when you download a public dataset. Security needs a target in that same slot, and almost every target in the world belongs to somebody else.

The barrier in this field gets treated as a question of diligence, when in fact a large share of it comes from structure. This piece splits that structure into four strands, and for the legal strand it writes down the article numbers. It does not cover attack techniques or tool usage. What is here is what to learn, not how to break in.

This is a general account of how people learn security, not legal advice. All statutes cited are the law of the Republic of Korea. Whether a specific act is unlawful depends on the concrete facts, and the bodies that decide are investigators and courts. In a real matter you need a lawyer or the Korea Legal Aid Corporation. Statutes and statistics quoted are current as of September 2026.

The trigger - what one high school student pointed at

In early September 2026 a security learning site was introduced on a Korean developer community. The person who built it wrote that he is in his first year of high school, that he first touched programming in fourth grade, and that he has been teaching himself security and development ever since. He handles the development, the maintenance and the course production alone, has built a payment flow but has no plan to charge, and funds it himself. The front page lists nine curricula, eighteen specialist tracks and forty three topic tags, marked as started in 2026 and version 1.0.

The reason this piece does not review that site is written on the site's own introduction. Most of the material currently posted came from his own notes split and normalised by a language model, which he says makes it no different from AI slop, and he has announced that he will rewrite much of it by hand. Grading the polish of a project that started months ago is not a useful exercise. It is even less useful against someone who has published an accurate self-assessment.

The interesting part is not the site but the difficulty he named. Two things. Explanations were obscure and abstract, and the prerequisite knowledge he needed, networking in particular, was scattered across many places so that he had to go around collecting fragments. Anyone who has studied this alone recognises both sentences. And behind them sit several layers of conditions that diligence does not resolve.

The real shape of the barrier - four things overlapping

Every technical field has a barrier to entry. The one in security is a different kind, because most of the difficulty comes from the learning conditions rather than from conceptual depth. Four constraints apply at once.

ConditionHow it appears in securityHow it appears elsewhere
Ownership of the practice targetThe target is usually someone else's. The moment you test something that is not yours, this stops being study and becomes a legal questionAn editor, a public dataset and local execution cover most of it
Dual use of the knowledgeThe same material serves defence and attack, so how openly it can be written down is always constrained, and questions often go unansweredKnowledge points one way, so explanations carry almost no constraint
Shelf life of the informationWhen versions and defaults change, yesterday's explanation becomes today's wrong one. Undated material drops a grade in trustFoundational theory lasts, so old material largely still holds
Breadth of prerequisitesYou need some networking, operating systems, web and cryptography before one sentence lands. Law is added on topGoing deep on one strand carries you for a long time

What produces the actual difficulty is that these four are not separate but stacked. The prerequisites are wide, so you go hunting for material. The material is written cautiously because of dual use. Cautious writing reads as abstract. Checking something abstract requires a target, and there is no target. The obscurity and the fragmentation named in that introduction are two points on this same loop.

Why the lab is the first gate

The most underestimated cost in learning security is building the environment. Standing up a few virtual machines inside your own hardware and wiring them onto one network so they can talk already demands networking and operating system knowledge. That is where the chicken and egg appears: you have to know the thing in order to start learning the thing.

Faced with that wall, the most common beginner move is turning to a live target. It is already on the internet, it needs no setup, and it responds immediately. The size of the temptation scales with how hard the lab is to build. And that is exactly where the law has drawn its line. So the order here does not bend. Learning to build an environment is the first subject in security, and whoever skips it is not buying learning, they are buying risk.

  • Handling virtualisation, segmenting networks and rolling back with snapshots are not side chores but the syllabus. All three reappear verbatim in operations work later
  • A single old laptop covers most foundational exercises. Being able to roll back matters more than specifications
  • Rented capacity is not ownership. For a leased server or a cloud instance, what the terms of service permit comes first
  • The router at home and a family member's device also need that person's consent if they are using it. Owning and using are different words

How to check the source of permission

There is more legal ground to practise on than people assume. But that ground extends exactly as far as the source of permission is clear. Reduced to one test: can you say in a single sentence who permitted what, and when, for the thing you are currently touching?

  • Your own environment — virtual machines and labs inside your own hardware. Target and owner are the same person, so permission does not arise. The freest ground, and the one that teaches most
  • Platforms built for training — the ones that publish permitted and prohibited actions in writing. Being described as a training environment is not enough on its own
  • Capture the flag events — the organiser defines targets and rules. But the event infrastructure itself is usually out of scope, and people are disqualified over that distinction every year
  • Vulnerability reporting programmes and bug bounties with a stated scope — the scope document is the boundary of permission. It names the domains, the permitted actions, the prohibited actions and the reporting route

One sentence here admits no exceptions. Outside the scope is off limits for any reason at all. That you meant well, that you caused no harm, that you intended to report it the moment you found it, that somebody else could have found it anyway - none of those is permission. Permission is given by whoever runs the target, and the moment you assume a permission you were not given, the judgement has already left your hands.

Put differently, the first skill a security learner needs is reading a scope document. What is in, what is explicitly forbidden, where and how to report a finding. That habit later turns into the working ability to read contracts and authorisation letters, which is exactly what professional testing runs on.

The line the law draws - what the articles say

In South Korea the central provision is Article 48 of the Act on Promotion of Information and Communications Network Utilization and Information Protection, commonly called the Network Act. The version in force took effect on 7 July 2026 as Act No. 21305. Paragraph 1 prohibits intruding into an information and communications network without legitimate access authority or beyond permitted access authority. Paragraph 2 prohibits transmitting or distributing malicious programs. Paragraph 3 prohibits causing a fault in a network by sending mass signals or data. Paragraph 4, newly inserted on 23 January 2024, prohibits installing, transmitting or distributing programs or technical devices that allow access by bypassing normal protection and authentication procedures.

Two cells in that table deserve attention. One is the attempt provision in Article 71(2). The text leaves room for liability even where no intrusion resulted. Whether a given act amounts to commencement of execution turns on the facts of the individual case and is decided by investigators and courts, so no line can be drawn for you here. What the text does say plainly is that liability does not hinge on a result alone.

The other is that there is rarely just one offence. A single act can engage several provisions of the Network Act and the Criminal Act at once, and which ones are actually applied depends on the facts. That is precisely why the private judgement of this much is probably fine is so dangerous. The provisions were split up in order to catch a range of forms that do not reduce to one description.

Why curiosity is not a defence

This is where beginners misread the situation most often. The text of Article 48(1) contains no element about motive or purpose. The elements are whether legitimate access authority was absent, or whether permitted access authority was exceeded. Whether you were curious or malicious is not inside those elements. Motive can of course matter at sentencing or disposition, but that is a different layer from whether the offence is made out.

So who decides what access authority is? On that question the holding in Supreme Court of Korea, 14 November 2024, 2021Do5555 states that the party who grants access authority and sets its permitted range is the service provider, and that where a third party who is not a user authorised by the service provider connects to a network, whether that third party has access authority must be judged by reference to the access authority granted by the service provider. The same holding records that intrusion covered by the prohibition includes entering by improper means, such as using another person's identifier or entering improper commands that evade the restriction imposed by protective measures, even where no infringement or damage to those protective measures accompanies it.

Translated into a learner's language: your own assessment that something was harmless is not the standard. The standard is what the operator of the target permitted, and the body that later rules on that is neither you nor them but a court. An unlocked door does not become an invitation.

  • I was going to report it immediately — reporting is a later fact. The element is whether permission existed at the moment of access
  • I only looked at public pages — where public ends is also set by the service provider. Editing a parameter to pull up another person's resource moves that judgement out of your hands
  • Nobody was harmed — harm is a separate element or a sentencing factor. It does not drop out of the statutory elements automatically
  • It was already a known issue — reconfirming a known issue on someone else's system still requires permission
  • I am a minor — age changes the procedure and the type of disposition. It does not change the character of the act

What to learn first - principles, not tools

Once the target question is settled, the next question is order. The split here is sharp. People who start from tools usually run out of road. The reason is structural rather than about talent. Tool output changes with versions and the tools themselves get replaced every few years, while the ability to read that output comes from principles.

Four blocks are worth securing first. The first is request and response: how state gets simulated on top of a structure that does not remember state, and what headers and cookies do in that simulation. The second is how a name becomes an address: where a query travels, where caching sits, and where trust is delegated. The third is the distinction between authentication and authorisation: confirming who someone is and deciding what they may do are different jobs, and a large share of real incidents come from gluing them together. The fourth is what cryptography does and does not guarantee: confidentiality, integrity and non-repudiation are separate properties, and having one does not bring the others along.

These four overlap heavily with the material in the network security fundamentals series, and the question of how far a client can be trusted is set out separately in trust boundaries and web security controls. For defence at the individual level, personal cybersecurity basics is the right entry point.

  • The way to confirm a principle is observation. Watching the requests and responses inside your own machine turns most of the abstraction concrete on its own
  • Tools stick better when picked up in the middle of confirming a principle. A tool you reached for because you needed it stays; a tool memorised from a list does not
  • The order in one line is principle, observation, reproduction, defensive design. Drop the last box and the study stays a hobby
  • The ability to reproduce a failure arrives later than the ability to reproduce a success, and lasts far longer

What certifications do and do not do

Certification arguments usually burn out in a needed versus not needed loop. In practice what they do and what they cannot do divides fairly cleanly. Three things they do. They act as a signal that clears the minimum bar at paper screening, they work as a map of a syllabus that shows you what you do not know, and they fill a box an organisation has to fill for contractual or compliance reasons. That third one is very practical: sometimes it is required on paper regardless of the person's actual skill.

What they cannot do is equally clear. Judgement in an unfamiliar situation, the ability to show your reasoning, and the ability to read the context of a system someone else built are not filtered by multiple choice or a standardised practical. So signal and skill correlate without being the same thing. Hiring teams also tend to use certifications to screen out rather than to select. It moves you to the next round, where something else is examined.

The reason no specific certification is named here is the same. Requirements differ by role, organisation and country, and the posting for the job you actually want is the most accurate answer. Lining up ten postings you care about and counting which preferred qualifications repeat beats copying someone else's conclusion about which certificate is best.

How to grade the reliability of information

Security material goes stale or turns out wrong unusually often. It ages fast, and each round of summarising strips conditions off. A sentence that ends with then you are safe is best treated as one that originally carried conditions.

TierWhat it isWhat it lets you confirmWhat to watch
PrimaryStandards documents, vendor security advisories, vulnerability records, statutory textExact conditions and scope, applicable versions, publication dateIt costs time to read. But it is the terminus of every trace-back, so you end up here anyway
SecondaryInstitutional analyses, academic papers, survey statisticsContext and scale, comparison with other casesCheck the survey population and sample. A different population changes what a number means
TertiaryBlog summaries, course material, community postsA fast overview, hints about where to lookConditions have often been stripped. Trace every number and every flat claim upward

Not reading a score as risk is another habit worth forming. The CVSS v4.0 specification states that the Base Score reflects the severity of a vulnerability according to its intrinsic characteristics which are constant over time and assumes the reasonable worst-case impact across different deployed environments. It then instructs consumers to enrich the base metrics with threat and environmental values specific to their own use, in order to produce a more comprehensive input to risk assessment specific to their organization. Regulatory requirements, the number of customers impacted, monetary losses from a breach, threats to life or property and reputational impact are explicitly named as outside the scope of CVSS. The score is severity; risk has to be recalculated per organisation.

There is also a separate source for whether something is actually being exploited. The catalogue maintained by the United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency presents itself as the authoritative source of vulnerabilities that have been exploited in the wild and asks organisations to use it as an input into their vulnerability prioritisation framework. Scoring high and being used right now are different questions, and you only get an order of work by reading both.

The defensive path is wider

The picture in a beginner's head is usually penetration testing. The distribution of actual seats is not that. That said, the popular claim that demand is exploding is not straightforwardly supported by the numbers either. Laying out the Korean information security industry survey as a time series gives this.

How you read it matters. Information security revenue in 2024 was roughly 1.57 times its 2021 level, while over the same window the workforce has sat around twenty four thousand for three years since 2022. An industry growing is not the same statement as seats growing in proportion. One caveat is essential: this survey covers information security businesses, so security staff inside ordinary companies are not counted here. The table describes the size of an industry, not the size of total demand.

The reason the defensive path can still be called wider lies in the nature of the work. Defence is standing work and assessment is project work. Standing work creates standing seats. Laid out as actual roles with people in them, penetration testing is one box among several.

  • Logging and detection — deciding what to record and picking anomalies out of what was recorded. Only someone who knows normal can see abnormal
  • Incident response — reconstructing what happened in order of time. The handling sequence is itself the craft, and that part is set out separately in acquiring digital evidence
  • Vulnerability management — deciding the order of fixes rather than finding things. This is where the score, the real exploitation signal and the organisational context sit on one table
  • Design review and architecture — blocking things before they are built. The cheapest place to block, and the last place to get credit
  • Governance and certification — reconciling rules with evidence. It looks dull and it is a box organisations reliably staff
  • Privacy — where law meets systems. Questions like how far an access or deletion request must be honoured are covered in personal data access requests

None of this means defence is the easy route. If anything the reverse. Reproducing an attack only needs one thing to work, whereas defence has to know the whole surface. Wider means more seats, not lighter work.

What AI changed and what it did not

What changed is clear. The cost of getting a first explanation of an unfamiliar concept, of skimming a long document, and of reading an unfamiliar log format has dropped a lot. The person who built the site above wrote that he split and normalised his own notes with a language model, which is the same story. Making form consistent is something it genuinely does well.

What did not change is equally clear. Judgement and responsibility. Whether the thing you are touching is inside the permitted scope, whether an answer holds in your organisation, and what a change will break are still carried by a person. And the same author calling his own courses no different from AI slop is an accurate diagnosis. A model-normalised document has even form and uneven grounding. Every paragraph carries a similar air of confidence while the actual certainty varies wildly.

  • Take article and standard numbers and compare them against the source. The more plausible the number looks, the harder you check. Non-existent provisions do come back with convincing numbers
  • Demand a version and a date. Advice that cannot say which version it applies to is advice without a point in time, and in security that is half wrong already
  • Ask separately for the counter-argument. A topic that only ever comes back one way has usually had its conditions cut off
  • Follow execution instructions only in your own environment. A model telling you to go and check something does not change who owns the target
  • Hold any conclusion that lacks a reproduction path. As AI-written vulnerability reports pile up, intake criteria are shifting toward reproducibility, which is set out in bug bounty intake in the age of AI reports

Traps beginners fall into

None of these is about talent or effort. They come from getting the order wrong, which means almost all of them are reversible. All but one.

  • Collecting tools — the number of tools installed has no relationship to depth of understanding. One person who can explain a single line of output from first principles is ahead of someone with ten installs
  • Theory without practice — repeating concepts never builds the ability to observe. Knowledge that has never met a real screen collapses immediately at work
  • Testing outside scope — the one item that cannot be undone. What you lose is incomparably larger than what the exercise teaches
  • Believing hacking is security — reproducing attacks is one branch. This misconception narrows the career path, and a narrowed path feeds the temptation to test without permission
  • Following a write-up and stopping there — reproducing after seeing the answer is a different experience from arriving by observation. Without noting what you did not know, nothing is left
  • Asking how to break in — answers do not come back because of dual use, not because people are unfriendly. Rephrase the question as how does this work and answers arrive
  • Chasing vulnerability news only — news is an outcome and learning is a process. Understanding the news sends you back to principles anyway
  • Collecting certificates — buying several maps is not the same as walking. One map is often enough

Five tests for the first three months

No concrete curriculum is written here because it differs person to person. What lasts longer, whichever path you take, is five tests you can apply to yourself. Answer them at the end of the third month and the direction usually becomes visible.

  • Who owns what you are practising on right now? If you cannot answer in one sentence, stop where you are. This test outweighs the other four
  • What is the date and the target version of the material you are reading? Without both, that document is a reference and not a basis
  • Can you explain one line of a tool's output from principles? The line where the explanation breaks is what you learn next
  • Can you reproduce a failure? Making a working thing work again is easier than making a broken thing break the same way, and the second skill lasts longer
  • Can you restate what you learned as a defensive sentence? Turning this is how it breaks into this is how it is blocked and detected is what gives the knowledge a seat

The check at three months is not how many more tool names you know either. It is whether you know where to go when something unfamiliar appears. The people who last in this field are not the ones who memorised the most but the ones who trace back fastest.

Summing up

Saying that security is hard to learn usually gets heard as saying the concepts are obscure, but most of the actual difficulty comes from the structure of the learning conditions. The practice target belongs to someone else, dual use constrains how things are explained, information ages fast, and the prerequisites are wide. Stacked together, those four keep beginners circling between fragments.

Only one of them cannot be undone: testing outside a permitted scope. Article 48 of the Network Act with its penalty provisions, and the related articles of the Criminal Act, draw that line. Access authority is set by whoever runs the target, and the body that rules on it is a court. Everything else on the list yields to time and sequence. This one does not.

So what has to be settled in the first three months is not what to learn but where to learn it. Being able to build your own environment, forming the habit of reading a scope document, and taking principles before tools. With those three in place the rest largely runs itself. And any attempt to lower the barrier to entry can be judged the same way: by which of those three it actually makes cheaper.

Restating it once more. This is a general account of a learning path, not legal advice, and all statutes cited are the law of the Republic of Korea. Whether a specific act is unlawful depends on concrete facts, and the deciding bodies are investigators and courts. The provisions quoted are in force as of September 2026, and a real matter needs a lawyer or the Korea Legal Aid Corporation. Testing outside a permitted scope is not justified by any learning objective.
