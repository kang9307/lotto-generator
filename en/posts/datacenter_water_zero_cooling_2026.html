45°C Cooling Design Brings Data Center Water Use Near Zero — AI Infrastructure Sustainability

Higher coolant temperatures eliminate evaporative water loss — a structural break in the WUE-PUE tradeoff

2026-06-26 Tech

Introduction: The Water Crisis the AI Boom Created

As of June 2026, the quietest yet most consequential environmental problem in the data center industry is not electricity — it is water. AI training and inference workloads generate thermal densities several times higher than conventional servers, and the evaporative cooling towers used to manage that heat consume water at an alarming rate. Researchers estimate that U.S. AI data centers will consume between 200 and 300 billion gallons of water annually between 2024 and 2030. The problem is compounded by location: roughly two-thirds of data centers built in the U.S. since 2022 are sited in high water-stress areas.

Against this backdrop, NVIDIA released its DSX (Data Center eXperience) AI factory reference design, featuring a closed-loop liquid cooling system that allows coolant temperatures up to 45°C. The result: water consumption reduced to near zero, while energy efficiency simultaneously improves. The paradox — cooling better by running hotter — deserves careful examination.

This article analyzes how the traditional WUE-PUE tradeoff collapses under this design, what technical prerequisites and limitations remain, and what it means for the environmental sustainability of AI infrastructure. All data references are based on publicly available sources as of June 2026.

1. How Data Centers Consume Water — The Evaporative Cooling Structure

Understanding the problem requires mapping the thermal path. In a conventional data center, servers heat the surrounding air. Indoor cooling units (CRAC/CRAH) transfer that heat to chilled water, which is then circulated to outdoor cooling towers. At the tower, water evaporates directly into the atmosphere, carrying heat away. This evaporation step is where water disappears. Conventional cooling towers consume roughly 2.6 million gallons per megawatt per year. For a 50 MW AI factory, that is 130 million gallons annually — just for cooling.

1.1 WUE: Measuring Water Efficiency

WUE (Water Usage Effectiveness) measures liters of cooling water consumed per kWh of IT equipment energy processed. Developed by The Green Grid as the water counterpart to PUE, lower values indicate less water used. Microsoft reported an average WUE of 0.30 L/kWh for its fiscal year 2026 — a 39% improvement over 2021. However, energy analysts warn that expanding AI workloads could push the industry average WUE higher despite per-facility improvements. The target for top-tier new builds in 2026 is below 0.2 L/kWh. NVIDIA DSX targets WUE ≈ 0.

1.2 PUE: The Energy Efficiency Counterpart

PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness) is total facility power divided by IT equipment power. The ideal is 1.0; overhead from cooling, lighting, and power conversion raises it. The global average PUE has stagnated between 1.55 and 1.59 since 2020, while leading hyperscalers achieve below 1.2. For AI factories where cooling overhead is particularly high, PUE becomes a critical cost lever.

2. The Traditional Tradeoff — Better WUE Means Worse PUE, and Vice Versa

The classic dilemma: evaporative cooling exploits the latent heat of vaporization, making it highly energy-efficient (good PUE) but water-intensive (bad WUE). Dry cooling or high-powered air cooling saves water (good WUE) but consumes more fan energy (bad PUE). Designers historically had to sacrifice one for the other.

This tension is sharpest in water-stressed arid regions. Phoenix-area data centers face a stark choice: save water and burn more electricity, or save electricity and consume scarce desert water. AI accelerators, with power densities far exceeding conventional servers, make this tradeoff dramatically more consequential.

3. The 45°C Solution — How the Tradeoff Collapses

NVIDIA DSX's answer sounds simple but is technically demanding. The core idea: raise the coolant temperature threshold to 45°C.

3.1 Direct Cold-Plate Liquid Cooling

NVIDIA's Rubin-generation AI infrastructure runs 100% liquid cooling across every component — GPUs, CPUs, networking, storage. Coolant (75% water, 25% propylene glycol) flows through cold plates in direct contact with processors, absorbing heat. The loop is sealed and filled once; no evaporation, no fans anywhere in the rack.

3.2 Why 45°C? Leveraging the Natural Cooling Threshold

The 45°C threshold is the key insight. Because cold plates directly contact chips, the coolant can be significantly warmer than air-cooling setpoints while still keeping processors within safe operating limits. Critically, in most temperate and subtropical climates, outdoor air temperatures stay well below 45°C for most of the year. This means dry coolers can reject heat to outdoor air without chillers — no evaporation, no cooling towers, no ongoing water replenishment. The loop runs perpetually on the same charge of coolant.

The structural consequence: WUE approaches zero while PUE simultaneously improves, because chiller plants and cooling towers — major energy consumers — are eliminated. This is not a marginal optimization; it is a categorical change in the cooling architecture.

3.3 Quantified Savings

NVIDIA's published figures: compared to conventional evaporative tower cooling, the DSX design can reduce facility water consumption by approximately 2.6 million gallons per MW per year — essentially 100% elimination in suitable climates. Microsoft reports that its zero-evaporation closed-loop design avoids 125 million liters per facility per year. An independent analysis estimates that a 50 MW hyperscale facility can save over $4 million annually in combined cooling energy and water costs.

4. Technical Challenges of High-Temperature Operations

The principle is elegant, but three substantive challenges remain.

4.1 Climate Constraints — The Phoenix Problem

Natural cooling at 45°C fails when outdoor air temperatures approach or exceed that threshold. Phoenix, Arizona — one of the densest data center markets — regularly sees summer temperatures above 45°C, requiring supplemental chillers during peak heat periods. Year-round WUE≈0 is climate-contingent; temperate regions benefit fully while arid, high-demand markets see only partial gains.

4.2 Infrastructure Conversion Capital

Retrofitting existing air-cooled facilities to 100% liquid cooling is effectively a rebuild. Cold plate infrastructure, leak detection systems, coolant distribution units, and fully compatible components across every rack system require substantial upfront capital. For greenfield AI factory design, liquid cooling is increasingly the baseline. For existing legacy facilities, the economics are more complex.

4.3 Coolant Quality and Long-Term Maintenance

Even a sealed loop requires management. Corrosion, biofouling, and scaling can develop over time, particularly in the microscopic channels of cold plates where thermal resistance is critical. Propylene glycol mitigates some risks, but periodic fluid analysis and planned replacement intervals add operational overhead not present in air-cooled systems.

5. The Structural Collapse of the WUE-PUE Tradeoff — An Analysis

The more important insight here is not just "less water" but that a long-assumed architectural tradeoff has been made obsolete by a technology choice.

Traditional cooling design optimized around natural physics: evaporative latent heat was the most energy-efficient cooling mechanism available, so using water was rational. AI accelerators broke that logic by generating thermal densities that air simply cannot handle at scale. Liquid cooling became mandatory — and that mandatory transition happens to resolve the water problem as a side effect.

In other words, AI's thermal intensity forced a cooling evolution, and that evolution happened to converge on the solution that also addresses water scarcity. Environmental and technical imperatives pointing in the same direction is rare. When they align, adoption speed is typically much faster than when sustainability is the sole driver — because operators choose liquid cooling to keep their AI factories running, and water savings follow automatically.

6. AI Infrastructure Sustainability — Optimism and Caution

Two simultaneous reactions to this trend are warranted.

6.1 Grounds for Optimism

The trajectory is clear: NVIDIA DSX, Microsoft's zero-evaporation closed-loop design, and 2026 pilot projects in Phoenix and Wisconsin are all converging on the same architecture. If 45°C liquid cooling becomes the baseline design for new AI factories — which appears increasingly likely — the marginal water impact of each incremental gigawatt of AI compute approaches zero.

6.2 Grounds for Caution

Efficiency improvements do not guarantee absolute reductions when the underlying demand is growing exponentially. Even if WUE trends toward zero, total AI compute capacity is expanding faster. The Jevons paradox applies: efficiency gains often enable more consumption, not less. Additionally, if the electricity powering dry coolers comes from carbon-intensive sources, solving the water problem may redistribute rather than resolve the environmental burden. Sustainability requires examining the entire system, not one metric in isolation.

7. Industry Status as of June 2026

  • NVIDIA DSX: Rubin-generation hardware, 100% liquid cooling across all components, theoretical WUE≈0, reference design publicly available.
  • Microsoft: Zero-evaporation closed-loop cooling deployed in all new data center designs from August 2024. Target: 125M+ liters saved per facility per year. Phoenix and Wisconsin pilots expanding in 2026.
  • Industry WUE benchmark: Top-tier new builds targeting below 0.2 L/kWh; global average still has substantial room for improvement.
  • Conversion barrier: Existing air-cooled facility retrofits are capital-intensive; greenfield designs have the strongest economic case.

8. Conclusion — Zero-Water Data Centers Are Achievable, With Conditions

The 45°C liquid cooling design represents a genuine structural break in the WUE-PUE tradeoff that has constrained data center design for decades. The convergence of AI thermal demands and sustainable cooling architecture is one of the rare cases where business necessity and environmental benefit point in the same direction.

Three important qualifications must be stated clearly. First, the full benefit is climate-contingent and applies primarily to greenfield designs — arid high-demand regions and existing infrastructure conversions face additional constraints. Second, efficiency improvements do not automatically reduce absolute water consumption if AI deployment volumes grow faster than the technology diffuses. Third, water savings must be paired with clean electricity to represent genuine environmental progress rather than a shifted burden.

A zero-water data center is now technically achievable and economically rational. Whether that achievement translates to a genuinely more sustainable AI infrastructure depends on deployment rate, energy sourcing, and how honestly the industry measures total system impact rather than optimizing individual metrics.
