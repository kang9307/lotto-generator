Korea's Pension Savings Fund Account - A Complete Guide

Limits, credit rates, investment rules, payout taxation, and early-exit math, checked against the statutes as of July 2026

Jul 28, 2026 Finance & Economy

Introduction: Start by Splitting the Name Apart

Every year-end filing season in Korea, the same sentence circulates: put KRW 9 million into a pension account and get KRW 1.48 million back. The arithmetic is not wrong. But four assumptions are hidden inside that single line. Not all of the KRW 9 million can go into a pension savings account. The credit rate splits along an income threshold. If there is no tax to pay in the first place, no refund appears. And the money is locked away until at least age 55. Follow the number without the assumptions and, a few years later when cash is needed urgently, closing the account can cost more in tax than the refunds ever delivered.

This guide focuses on one specific vehicle: the pension savings fund account, known in Korean as yeongeum jeochuk pundeu. Korea's pension savings comes in three forms, namely trust, fund, and insurance, and the fund form is performance-based, which means capital loss is possible. It carries the same tax treatment as the other two but behaves entirely differently as a product, and a surprising amount of guidance blurs that line. So this article separates the three forms first, then verifies the tax credit structure at the level of the statute, calculates the actual savings by income band, sets out criteria for splitting contributions between a pension savings account and an IRP, walks through the investment restrictions and the transfer procedure, and finally covers the tax on both taking money out as a pension and taking it out early.

All limits, rates, and conditions below reflect July 2026 and were verified where possible against Korea's Income Tax Act, its Enforcement Decree, and guidance published by the National Tax Service and the Ministry of Government Legislation. Figures that could not be verified against a primary source were left out, and those gaps are flagged in the text. This describes the Korean system and assumes a Korean resident taxpayer.

1. What a Pension Savings Fund Account Is, and How It Differs from Insurance and Trust

Pension savings is defined in Article 20-3, paragraph 1, item 2 of Korea's Income Tax Act as a pension savings account: a tax-advantaged account where contributions made over a period are taxed as pension income when withdrawn in pension form. The critical point is that pension savings is not the name of one product. It is a container holding three distinct forms.

1.1 The Decisive Differences Are Return Structure and Protection

Using the comparison published by the Korea Ministry of Government Legislation in its plain-language law service, which cites Financial Supervisory Service product guidance, the three forms break down as follows.

ItemPension savings trustPension savings fundPension savings insurance
ProvidersBanks acting as trusteesSecurities firms, banks, insurersSecurities firms, banks, insurers
Return structurePerformance-basedPerformance-basedDeclared interest rate
Principal protectionOnly contracts opened through 2017Not protectedProtected
Deposit insuranceCoveredNot coveredCovered
Character in one linePerformance-based yet coveredInvestment results pass straight throughSteadier, with a different return structure

One cell in that table is the starting point for this entire guide. A pension savings fund account has no principal protection and no deposit insurance coverage. It holds funds and exchange-traded funds inside the wrapper, so if markets fall, the balance can drop below the total contributed. Having received a tax credit does not offset an investment loss. The insurance form, by contrast, applies a declared rate and is covered by deposit insurance, but in exchange the return structure differs and acquisition costs are typically deducted in the early years.

Neither form is objectively better. Whether to accept the possibility of loss in exchange for long-run investment results, or to reduce volatility and accept a different return structure, depends on risk tolerance, time horizon, and the rest of a household's assets. This article does not recommend either one.

1.2 The Contribution Limit and the Credit Limit Are Different Numbers

Two figures are constantly confused, so let us fix them first.

  • Contribution limit: KRW 18 million a year, under Article 40-2, paragraph 2, item 1 of the Enforcement Decree of the Income Tax Act. This is a combined ceiling across all pension savings and retirement pension accounts a person holds.
  • Tax credit limit: the portion of that KRW 18 million that actually reduces tax, which is the KRW 6 million and KRW 9 million discussed in the next section.
  • Contributions above the credit limit do not reduce that year's tax, but they still grow tax-deferred inside the account and, thanks to the withdrawal ordering rule, they come out first and untaxed later. Section 7 covers this in detail.

1.3 Two Special Routes That Sit Outside the Normal Limit

Separately from the ordinary contribution limit, the National Tax Service describes two additional routes.

  • Rolling over a matured Individual Savings Account (ISA): moving matured or terminated ISA proceeds into a pension account within 60 days is permitted regardless of the ordinary contribution limit, and 10% of the transferred amount qualifies for an additional tax credit of up to KRW 3 million.
  • Housing downsizing contribution: per National Tax Service guidance, where the resident or spouse is aged 60 or over, the couple owns a single home, the published price of the home being sold is KRW 1.2 billion or less, and they either buy a cheaper home or buy none, the difference may be contributed to a pension account within six months of the sale date. The cumulative ceiling is KRW 100 million.
  • The downsizing route carries follow-up conditions. Failing them later can require withdrawing the amount and reversing the benefits received, so confirm with the National Tax Service or a tax professional before acting.

2. The Tax Credit: The KRW 6 Million and 9 Million Ceilings and the Rate Structure

The governing provision is Article 59-3 of Korea's Income Tax Act. Read alongside National Tax Service guidance, the structure is simple.

2.1 The Ceiling Applies in Two Layers

  • Where contributions to a pension savings account exceed KRW 6 million a year, the excess is disregarded.
  • Where the sum of pension savings contributions up to that KRW 6 million plus contributions to a retirement pension account, such as an IRP or a defined contribution plan, exceeds KRW 9 million a year, that excess is disregarded too.
  • So contributing KRW 9 million entirely to a pension savings account still leaves only KRW 6 million eligible. Filling the full KRW 9 million requires the remaining KRW 3 million to sit in a retirement pension account.
  • The reverse works, however. The KRW 9 million ceiling can be filled by a retirement pension account alone.

2.2 The Rate Splits on a Single Income Line

The National Tax Service publishes the rate table below. Figures in the right-hand column add the 10% local income tax surcharge to give the effective refund rate.

Two points get missed here. First, the threshold is KRW 45 million of aggregate income but KRW 55 million of total wage and salary income, and they are not interchangeable. Someone with employment income only looks at the wage figure; someone with business or other income looks at aggregate income. Second, the local income tax surcharge moves with the national tax at 10%, which is why the amount actually received works out to 16.5% or 13.2%. The statute says 15% and 12%; the money in hand follows the higher pair.

2.3 Amounts Excluded from the Credit

The National Tax Service specifies that the following are excluded.

  • Income on which tax has been deferred, such as retirement income not subject to withholding
  • Amounts that arrived through a contract transfer between pension accounts
  • In other words, moving a severance payment into an IRP does not make that amount eligible for the credit, and neither does moving an account between institutions. The credit applies only to new contributions the taxpayer makes.
Let us settle the most common misunderstanding right here. A tax credit is subtracted from computed tax, so if the tax owed for the year is small, filling the contribution ceiling will not produce the full refund. The classic case is a taxpayer whose determined tax is already close to zero after other deductions. Checking your expected tax liability first is the correct order of operations.

  • KRW 6 million a year is KRW 500,000 a month. At the lower income band that produces KRW 990,000 of annual tax savings, roughly KRW 82,500 a month.

  • At the 13.2% band the same KRW 9 million yields KRW 1,188,000. That number reappears in the early-exit calculation below.
The amounts above are illustrations produced by simple multiplication. Actual refunds depend on your determined tax, other credits and deductions, when the account was opened, and the tax law in force that year. No specific refund is guaranteed in any case.

4. Splitting Contributions Between a Pension Savings Account and an IRP

Filling the combined KRW 9 million ceiling requires both accounts. So how should the split be decided? The answer depends on individual circumstances, but the criteria can be derived from the differences between the two regimes.

4.1 How the Two Accounts Differ

ItemPension savings fundIRP (individual retirement pension)
Governing lawIncome Tax Act (pension savings account)Employee Retirement Benefit Security Act plus Income Tax Act
Tax credit ceilingKRW 6 million standaloneUp to KRW 9 million combined with pension savings
Risk asset limitNo separate capUp to 70% of assets, with at least 30% in safe assets
Early withdrawalUnrestricted, though tax appliesOnly on statutory grounds
Account feesOften none, though product fees still applyOperating and asset management fees may apply
Receiving severance payNot possiblePossible, with tax deferral

4.2 Three Questions That Decide the Split

  1. Is there any chance you will need the cash? A pension savings account can be tapped at any time if you accept the tax, whereas an IRP permits early withdrawal only on statutory grounds such as a home purchase or rental deposit by someone who owns no home, medical care of six months or more, personal rehabilitation or bankruptcy, or a declared disaster. If illiquidity is a concern, filling the KRW 6 million pension savings ceiling first is the natural order.
  2. How much do you want in risk assets? An IRP allows at most 70% of assets in risk assets, with the remaining 30% held in principal-and-interest guaranteed products or similar. If you intend to keep an equity-heavy allocation, that portion faces fewer constraints inside a pension savings fund account. This is a description of the rules, not a suggestion that a higher equity weight is better.
  3. Do you need somewhere to hold severance pay? Receiving retirement benefits as a pension requires an IRP. For that purpose the IRP is not optional but a precondition.
As of July 2026 the 70% risk asset cap for IRPs is described as being in force, though there have been reports that supervisory authorities are discussing relaxing or removing it. Rules can change, so confirm the current standard with your financial institution before committing to an allocation.

4.3 A General Note on Ordering

  • Where flexibility matters most, filling the KRW 6 million pension savings ceiling first and putting the remaining KRW 3 million into an IRP is a commonly cited combination.
  • Where managing severance pay matters more, or where the plan calls for a low risk asset weight, tilting toward the IRP is equally defensible.
  • Either way, contributions above the combined KRW 9 million generate no credit that year. The tax deferral and withdrawal ordering advantages nonetheless remain.
  • Holding both accounts open leaves room to manage withdrawal ordering later, because the ordering rule is applied to each account individually.

5. What Can Be Held Inside: Investment Rules and the Possibility of Loss

A pension savings fund account is not a deposit box. It is a wrapper you fill with assets and manage. The same KRW 6 million produces different outcomes twenty years later depending on what goes inside, and those outcomes include losses.

5.1 What Is Allowed and What Is Not

  • Generally available: domestically established and listed funds and exchange-traded funds, bond and balanced products, target date funds, and principal-and-interest guaranteed products.
  • Restricted: financial institutions describe pension accounts as excluding leveraged and inverse products and others with a high derivative risk valuation. The intent is to screen out high-volatility instruments given the retirement purpose of the account.
  • Buying exchange-traded funds listed on overseas exchanges directly is generally not supported in these accounts. Domestically listed funds that track overseas indices are generally available.
  • For IRP and defined contribution plans, direct investment in individual Korean listed shares is generally described as not permitted. Coverage varies by institution and over time, so confirm before opening an account.

5.2 Costs Accumulate in Two Layers

  • Account-level fees: securities firm pension savings fund accounts often carry no separate fee, while IRPs may charge operating and asset management fees.
  • Product-level fees: management and distribution fees on the funds and exchange-traded funds themselves are deducted daily from assets. This layer remains even when the account fee is zero.
  • Over a long holding period the gap between these two layers compounds. Checking both the account fee and the product fee before opening is a habit worth forming.
  • The Financial Supervisory Service integrated pension portal publishes comparative disclosures of returns and fee ratios by institution, which is a way to check without relying solely on a provider's own sales material.
To repeat. Assets held in a pension savings fund account can fall in value with markets, and the balance can end up below the total contributed. A tax credit reduces tax at the time of contribution; it does not compensate for an investment loss. This article does not say that any category of product is advantageous, and specific product selection should be made by you in line with your own risk tolerance and objectives, or in consultation with a professional.

6. Tax on the Way Out: How Age, Duration, and Amount Change the Rate

The tax reduced on the way in is settled on the way out. Because the payout rates are designed to be low, a deferral benefit generally remains. The complication is that the rate depends on how the money is taken, and that design produces the largest practical differences.

6.1 The Three Conditions for a Withdrawal to Count as a Pension

Article 40-2, paragraph 3 of the Enforcement Decree of the Income Tax Act sets three conditions. All three must be met.

  1. The holder applies to the pension account provider to begin pension payments on or after age 55, and then withdraws.
  2. Withdrawal occurs after five years have passed since the account was opened. Accounts holding deferred retirement income are excluded from this condition.
  3. Withdrawal stays within the pension withdrawal ceiling.

The ceiling is calculated as follows.

Pension withdrawal ceiling = account valuation / (11 - pension withdrawal year) x 120/100

The pension withdrawal year counts the tax period containing the first date on which a pension could be received as year one, accumulating thereafter. According to National Tax Service guidance, holders who opened accounts before March 1, 2013 start at year six, which gives them a materially larger ceiling. As the formula shows, a higher year number shrinks the denominator and raises the ceiling, and from year eleven the constraint effectively disappears.

6.2 Rates by Age and Payout Type

Amounts received within the ceiling are subject to withholding of pension income tax under Article 129, paragraph 1 and Article 143-2 of the Income Tax Act.

Retirement benefits taken as a pension are calculated differently. The deferred retirement income tax is the base, and 70% of it applies; per National Tax Service guidance, 60% applies once the payout period exceeds ten years, and 50% applies to the portion beyond twenty years, with the over-twenty-year band applying from January 1, 2026. The direction is clear: spreading payments over a longer period reduces the burden.

6.3 The KRW 15 Million Fork in the Road

  • Where total private pension receipts are KRW 15 million a year or less, the low rates in the table above can be elected as separate taxation. Before December 31, 2023 the threshold was KRW 12 million.
  • Above KRW 15 million, the taxpayer may elect whichever is more favorable between aggregate taxation and separate taxation at 15%, or 16.5% including the local surcharge. The basis is Article 64-4 of the Income Tax Act.
  • Someone with substantial other income facing a high marginal rate may prefer separate taxation, while someone with almost no income outside the pension may prefer aggregate taxation. Which is better depends on the whole income picture and cannot be generalized.
  • Crucially, the test is applied to the annual amount received. Stretching the payout period lowers the annual figure and can bring it under the threshold.

  • For the same period, starting later shifts more of the payout into the age 70 and 80 bands, lowering the average rate.

  • The picture changes again if the national pension, other private pensions, or rental income are in play. Payout design is best reviewed with a tax professional against total income at retirement.

7. Early Closure and Withdrawal: Doing the Math Yourself

The biggest risk in a pension account is often not the market but your own cash position, because breaking the account inverts the arithmetic.

7.1 How Non-Pension Withdrawals Are Taxed

  • A withdrawal that fails the pension conditions is a non-pension withdrawal. Taking money before 55, taking it before five years have passed, and taking more than the ceiling all fall into this category.
  • Credited contributions and investment gains are then taxed as other income at 15%, or 16.5% including the local surcharge, assessed separately.
  • Contributions that never received a credit are not taxable. That distinction connects directly to the ordering rule below.

7.2 The Ordering Rule Changes the Outcome

Article 40-3 of the Enforcement Decree of the Income Tax Act sets the order in which amounts are treated as withdrawn. Per National Tax Service interpretation, the order is applied separately to each account.

  1. Non-taxable amounts: contributions made without receiving a tax credit come out first, with no tax.
  2. Deferred retirement income: amounts that arrived as retirement benefits with tax deferred come next.
  3. Credited contributions and investment gains: last in line, and this is where the other income tax lands.

The practical significance is large. If contributions were made above the credit ceiling, a modest withdrawal may draw entirely on that layer and come out without tax. Conversely, someone who contributed exactly up to the ceiling every year reaches the third layer with the very first withdrawal.

7.3 Early Closure Math in Two Cases

Assume KRW 6 million contributed each year for five years into a pension savings fund account, KRW 30 million in total, all of it credit-eligible, no investment gain or loss, and no contributions above the ceiling.

Item16.5% band13.2% band
Total credits received over five yearsKRW 4.95 million (KRW 990,000 x 5)KRW 3.96 million (KRW 792,000 x 5)
Other income tax on closure at 16.5%KRW 4.95 millionKRW 4.95 million
DifferenceZeroNet loss of KRW 990,000

The conclusion is unambiguous. Anyone who received the credit at 13.2% pays back more than they received. Even at 16.5% the result is merely break-even, and the opportunity cost of having the money locked up is never recovered. If there were investment gains, those are taxed at 16.5% too, so the burden rises. If there were losses, the taxable amount shrinks, but that is a consequence of having lost money and is not a benefit.

7.4 Unavoidable Causes: There Are Exceptions

Article 20-2 of the Enforcement Decree of the Income Tax Act applies the lower pension income tax rate to withdrawals made for unavoidable causes. Within what could be verified, the causes include the following.

  • Natural disaster
  • Death or emigration of the account holder
  • Illness of the holder or a dependent requiring medical care of three months or more
  • A disaster requiring hospitalization of 15 days or more
  • A declaration of bankruptcy, or commencement of personal rehabilitation proceedings, for the holder
  • Procedural requirement: documents evidencing the cause must be submitted to the financial institution holding the pension account within six months of the date the cause is confirmed. Missing that window can forfeit the exception.
The detailed requirements and supporting documents differ by cause and can change as the Enforcement Decree is amended. The list above covers only items that could be verified and may not be exhaustive. Whether a specific situation qualifies must be confirmed with the financial institution and the National Tax Service.

8. Contract Transfer: Switching Without Closing

Sooner or later the fees disappoint, the product lineup falls short, or you want to shift from an insurance form to a fund form. Closing the account is the worst way to handle that, because a contract transfer system exists.

8.1 The Core of a Contract Transfer

  • A pension savings account can be moved to pension savings at another financial institution, and the move is treated as continuation rather than cancellation, so tax treatment carries over.
  • Receiving institutions include banks, securities firms, and insurers that offer pension savings.
  • Amounts arriving through a transfer do not qualify for a new tax credit. The National Tax Service explicitly excludes them.
  • The original opening date generally carries over, so the five-year condition is not usually reset. Confirm the handling with the institution to be safe.

8.2 The General Sequence

  1. Open a pension savings account of the same type at the receiving institution first.
  2. File the transfer request through that institution's app, website, or branch, entering the details of the existing provider.
  3. The new institution requests the transfer from the existing one. In most cases there is no need to visit the original provider.
  4. Assets in the old account are liquidated into cash and moved, then repurchased in the new account. This can leave a gap of several days out of the market.
  5. After completion, confirm that the balance, the opening date, and the non-taxable contribution records transferred correctly.

8.3 Check These Before Transferring

  • Some contracts cannot be transferred. Annuity insurance contracts already in payout and contracts subject to attachment are the commonly cited examples.
  • Transferring out of an insurance form can reduce the amount. The transfer is based on the surrender value at that moment, so if early acquisition costs have been deducted, less than the total contributed may move across. The shorter the holding period, the larger this gap.
  • Transfers between a pension savings account and an IRP are described as carrying separate conditions, so do not assume they work like a same-type transfer. Confirm with the institution.
  • Asking in advance about transfer fees, processing time, and any trading restrictions during the transfer reduces the chance of an unexpected gap.

9. Eight Common Misconceptions

  1. Contributing always produces a refund - No. The credit is subtracted from computed tax, so a small determined tax for the year means the full amount may not be received even if the ceiling is filled.
  2. KRW 9 million into pension savings yields KRW 1.48 million - No. The standalone ceiling for pension savings is KRW 6 million. Filling the KRW 9 million ceiling requires the remainder to go into a retirement pension account.
  3. Principal is safe in a pension savings fund account - No. It is performance-based and outside deposit insurance. Capital loss is possible.
  4. At 55 you can take everything out - No. Two more conditions apply: five years since opening, and the pension withdrawal ceiling. Amounts above the ceiling attract other income tax.
  5. Receiving it as a pension means no tax - No. Pension income tax is withheld, at a low rate. The tax reduced on the way in is settled at a lower rate on the way out.
  6. Above KRW 15 million everything is aggregated - No. The taxpayer may elect between aggregate taxation and separate taxation at 15%, or 16.5% with the local surcharge.
  7. Early withdrawal always costs 16.5% - No. Under the ordering rule, contributions that never received a credit come out first and are not taxed. Unavoidable causes also attract the pension income tax rate instead.
  8. Switching providers requires closing the account - No. A contract transfer preserves the tax treatment. Closing should be the last resort.

10. Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Can I open a pension savings fund account with no income?

The pension savings account itself is described as having no income requirement for opening. But a tax credit only matters if there is tax to pay, so a year with no income and a determined tax of zero produces no credit benefit. Tax deferral and the low pension income tax rate still apply, though the long lock-up should be weighed alongside them.

Q. Does contributing everything in December change the credit?

The credit is based on the annual contribution, so timing within the year generally does not change the credit amount. In a performance-based account, however, the purchase date does affect investment results, and a single December contribution concentrates the outcome on one timing decision. Whether to spread purchases is a personal judgment and neither approach can be declared better.

Q. Do I need both a pension savings fund account and an IRP?

Not necessarily. Filling the combined KRW 9 million ceiling requires both, but if the plan is to contribute up to KRW 6 million, one pension savings account suffices. Conversely, receiving severance pay as a pension requires an IRP. It is a question of purpose.

Q. Can I see all my pensions and their values in one place?

The Financial Supervisory Service integrated pension portal lets residents view public and private pensions together, with projected payouts and comparative disclosure of returns and fee ratios by institution. It is a public channel that can be checked independently of any provider's sales material.

Q. Is contributing above the credit ceiling a waste?

It is inefficient in the sense that it produces no credit that year. But that amount becomes a non-taxable balance that comes out first and untaxed on a later withdrawal, acting as a liquidity buffer. The money is still locked in the account, so the decision belongs within genuinely spare funds.

Conclusion: You Get Back Only as Much as You Understand

A pension savings fund account has two faces. One is the tax credit at the moment of contribution; the other is long-run tax deferral running all the way to payout. Most guidance covers only the first, yet the second is what actually decides the outcome. The ceiling applies in two layers, KRW 6 million and KRW 9 million. The credit rate splits on the single line of KRW 55 million in total wages. Age 55, five years, and the withdrawal ceiling must all be satisfied for a withdrawal to count as a pension. And breaking the account costs 16.5% regardless of income. Knowing just those four points prevents most mistakes.

In order of execution: first, check your expected determined tax, because no tax owed means no credit. Second, map five to ten years of cash needs and size the contribution inside that. Early closure is a net loss in the 13.2% band. Third, decide the split between pension savings and IRP on three criteria: liquidity, risk asset weight, and whether severance pay needs a home. Fourth, do not defer payout design; stretching the period and starting later both lower the average rate. Fifth, when an account disappoints, use a contract transfer rather than closing it.
