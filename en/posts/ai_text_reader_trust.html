Introduction: the moment you close the tab without finishing

You open a document, get three paragraphs in, and the scrolling speeds up. Your eyes bounce across the subheadings and the tab is gone before the end. What just happened was not a judgment that the content was wrong. It was a judgment that there is nothing here worth the reading time, and it came from impression rather than from whether any sentence was true.

Lumping that reaction together as dislike of AI writing leaves nothing usable. What actually happens is far more specific. A reader picks up a few surface signals, uses them to estimate how much effort went into this page, and allocates the remaining attention against that estimate. The call takes half a second and is usually not conscious at all.

Here is the conclusion up front. Style has a poor signal-to-noise ratio as evidence. It is often right. The trouble is that when it is wrong, the cost lands on particular groups, and the person doing the judging has almost no way to find out they were wrong. Text you decided not to read does not write back.

This article separates the signals readers genuinely react to, points at where those same signals appear in human writing, puts numbers on the cost of misjudgment, and finishes with a standard you can use instead of style. Which phrases get flagged as tells most often is covered in the catalogue of AI writing tells, and the engineering side of embedding statistical marks in text is in how text watermarking works and where it breaks. What is on the table here is not how to detect, but what detecting is worth and what it costs.

1. Banner blindness has moved into prose

1.1 What the original piece says

The discussion was pulled back up by a short essay on a personal blog. The author describes running into low-quality generated documents at work often enough that his brain started filtering them out automatically — the same adaptation that makes eyes skip advertising banners. In his own phrasing, all those generated posts in his feed had left him pre-trained, and the piece closes on a paradox: the tool that was supposed to make him more productive is now what slows him down.

One thing deserves to be said plainly. This is a first-person account, not an experiment. There is no sample, no measurement, no control, and no indication of how many documents the impression rests on. It resonated widely not because the claim is strong but because the phenomenon described is common. That is the only standing it is cited with here: some people report this adaptation.

1.2 Why ignoring becomes automatic — attention is a budget

Banner blindness needs three conditions. The form must be repeatably identifiable, the correlation between that form and low value must have been actually learned, and the cost of judging must be very low. If it looked different every time no rule would form; the correlation only sticks after being burned a few times; and if you had to read it to know, the whole thing could never be automated.

Generated text satisfying all three at once is the change of the last few years. Widely shared tools made the form uniform, sheer volume trained the association with low value, and the first screen of a document is enough to reach a verdict.

What matters here is not the outcome but the structure. Automated dismissal has no feedback loop. Nobody who skipped a banner goes back to check whether it held something they needed, and nobody who skipped an article checks whether it was actually good. That makes it a kind of judgment whose false positives never enter the statistics. Everyone's confidence in their own accuracy therefore climbs over time while the actual accuracy stays unknown.

2. The signals readers actually react to

The phrase this reads like AI covers several different things. Pulled apart, there are four.

2.1 Vocabulary — the density of stock phrases

Certain connectives and modifiers come back several times on one page. Not only that, in conclusion, in today's fast-changing landscape, plays a crucial role in. No individual phrase is wrong. What catches is density. A person rewriting their own draft gets bored of a repeated phrase and changes it; text where that boredom never fired keeps crossing the same gap with the same words.

2.2 Structure — paragraphs with the rhythm flattened out

Sentences run to similar lengths, paragraphs to similar sizes, subheadings at ruler-measured intervals. It looks easy to read and does the opposite. Without rhythm there is no emphasis. Nothing marks what matters, so the eye moves at one constant speed, and that speed is skimming.

2.3 Over-listing — bullets padded out to three

A set that naturally has two items, or five, arrives with three. Bullets are trimmed into symmetry, every subheading gets its summary line, a table appears where no table was needed. Form has value when it carries information; when the form is filled and the information is not there, the reader reads it as volume manufactured out of formatting.

2.4 Unsourced assertion and both-sidesism

This is the substantive one. A number turns up with no origin, or every sentence hedges both ways until nothing is claimed at all. The first three are matters of impression, but this is where a reader takes an actual loss. A figure that cannot be checked is worse than no figure, and balance without a judgment spends the reader's time for nothing.

3. The same signals show up in human writing

That last column is the spine of this article. None of the five belongs to machines alone.

  • People writing in a second language. You use only what you are sure of, so vocabulary narrows and sentence patterns repeat
  • People still learning. At the imitation stage the form arrives first and the content gets filled in later
  • Organizations with fixed boilerplate. When tone and wording are prescribed, erasing individual variation is the entire point
  • Translated writing. The syntax of the source language survives and produces an odd uniformity
  • Heavily reviewed writing. When several people's edits stack up, the sharp sentences are the first to be sanded off

One judgment follows. These signals point not at «a machine wrote this» but at «this sits close to the average». And there are plenty of non-machine reasons to end up writing close to the average.

Where that uniformity comes from at the sentence level in Korean, and which constructions collect translationese and hedging, is taken apart in the piece on Korean style conventions. Its conclusion is the same: what surface strings catch is a tendency, not an author.

4. The cost of a wrong call — who gets hurt first

This is not a problem that has to stay abstract. There are numbers.

A 2023 study in the journal Patterns ran seven commercial GPT detectors over two bodies of human writing: 91 TOEFL essays by non-native English speakers, and 88 essays by US eighth-graders. The results looked like this.

CorpusResultFigure
TOEFL essays (non-native)Misclassified as AI-generated (average)61.22%
TOEFL essays (non-native)Flagged by all seven detectors18 of 91 (19.78%)
TOEFL essays (non-native)Flagged by at least one detector89 of 91 (97.80%)
US eighth-grade essaysCorrectly classified as human-writtenover 90%

The study also names the mechanism. The unanimously flagged essays had significantly lower perplexity than the rest. Translated out of the jargon: the simpler the vocabulary and the more predictable the next word, the more machine-written the text looks. The clinching detail is that when the researchers rewrote the same essays with richer vocabulary, the verdicts flipped to human. What the detectors measure is lexical flourish, not authorship.

From that point on, who pays is decided automatically.

  • People writing in a second language. The habit of choosing safe words is itself the grounds for being flagged
  • Students. Learners' sentences are structurally predictable, and school is one of the few places where a verdict comes attached to a consequence
  • Researchers and applicants outside the English-speaking world. Abstracts, applications and personal statements are exactly the documents where a single call is hard to undo
  • Staff writing to a house style. Rules designed to erase individual variation work against them at judgment time

What makes false positives especially hard to manage is the asymmetry. The judgment is usually private, and the person judged often does not know a judgment occurred. With nowhere to object, nothing gets corrected. This is precisely where the feedback-free verdict described earlier turns into real damage to an individual.

5. What detectors report and what they cannot say

The point is not to recommend a product. What matters more is what these tools structurally cannot do.

  • A score is a probability, not a verdict. The output estimates how close a text sits to the distribution a model predicts well. It is not an instrument for measuring who wrote it
  • The baseline is not fixed. What you treat as the human reference changes the score on identical prose. The two opposite results in the study above are the demonstration
  • It falls apart on short text. Statistical judgment with a small sample means wide confidence intervals. A score attached to a single paragraph carries almost no information
  • Editing and translation defeat it. Human-edited generation and machine-polished human writing blur into the same middle band, and that band is where most real documents live
  • Verifiable error rates are not published. Internal metrics for commercial products are generally closed, and the figures that do appear are often self-assessed

Vendors have conceded the limits too. OpenAI withdrew its own text classifier as of July 20, 2023, and the reason given in the notice was its low rate of accuracy. That the party best positioned to attempt detection reached that conclusion is better read as a signal about the nature of the task than about one product's polish.

In short. Detection scores have value as one input. But the moment they alone drive a sanction, a rejection or a grade, the false positive rate from the previous section is passed straight through to an individual. Using a score as evidence requires an appeal path and a way to check.

6. Why writing acquires an AI tell — convergence on the average

Why the signals take that particular shape has an explanation at the level of mechanism. It is not a quirk of a given product but a property that follows from producing text this way.

  • It maximizes the plausibility of the next word. Maximum plausibility is by definition the most common choice. String common choices together and you get average prose
  • It is tuned away from risk. Hedging is safer than asserting, and presenting both sides is safer than picking one. The whole thing tilts slightly toward blurred conclusions
  • It answers format requests reliably. Ask for a list, a table or subheadings and you always get good ones — whether or not the content needed that shape
  • It carries conversational habits into documents. Restating the request, confirming understanding, offering next steps: natural in dialogue, dead weight in a document

Something follows immediately. Put a person under the same pressures and you get the same prose. Requirements to avoid mistakes, to fit a fixed template, and to offend nobody reproduce all four in ordinary organizational writing. A good part of the AI tell is therefore a fingerprint of conditions rather than of tools.

This convergence is not confined to text, either. How the same property surfaces in visual elements and how readers read it is set out in the piece on how AI images erode trust.

7. What platforms and search actually look at

Setting individual verdicts aside, the parties handling documents at scale define the problem differently. In the published guidance the axis is value, not production method.

  • Google's search spam policies define scaled content abuse as generating many pages primarily to manipulate search rankings rather than to help users, and state that this applies no matter how the content is created
  • The documentation on generative AI content frames the violation as mass page production that adds no value for users, not as tool use in itself
  • The helpful content documentation asks creators to examine who, how and why, and includes a question on whether the use of automation, including AI generation, is self-evident to visitors through disclosures
  • The same document also asks whether the content provides original information, reporting, research or analysis, and whether it adds substantial value rather than merely copying or rewriting other sources

Read together, the guidance cares about three things: originality, added value, and context. Style is not on the list. Guidance is of course not a specification of how ranking systems behave, and how those systems treat style signals is not public. But where the published criteria place the axis is unambiguous.

8. From judging style to checking verifiability

At this point an alternative is needed. If style is off the table, what goes in its place? Whatever can be checked.

AxisStyle signalVerification signal
What it measuresWhether the wording is commonWhether the claim holds up
Cost of judgingNear zeroThe time it takes to open one link
When it is wrongYou never find outIt surfaces the moment you check
Who it disadvantagesWriters close to the averageWriters who wrote without grounds
Relation to tool useCorrelated, but not causalApplies regardless

Five things are worth looking at.

  • Do the figures and quotations carry sources — and does the link actually open? A dead link is the same as no link
  • Is the source primary? An original from the issuing body, or somebody's summary passed along again. A citation of a citation usually loses a digit somewhere
  • Does the claim narrow itself? A sentence pinned to conditions and a date can be proven wrong. A sentence that covers every case was never checkable to begin with
  • Are the gaps left as gaps? Whether unknowns were papered over with plausible filler shows up in empty table cells and explicit reservations
  • Is there a record of corrections? Change logs, update dates and marked revisions are the only physical evidence that someone is still standing behind the text

What these five share is that they apply regardless of whether a tool was used. An unsourced sentence is unsupported no matter who typed it, and a sourced sentence checks out however it was produced. Moving from verdict to verification does not mean working harder. It means spending the same minute where it leaves a result.

9. What stays with the writer — responsibility does not transfer

Changing the reader's standard clears something up on the writing side too. However a text was produced, responsibility for its facts stays with whoever put their name on it. Tools do not take a share. This is not a normative claim but a structural one: think about who writes the correction notice when a wrong number goes out, and the answer settles itself.

So what a writer actually has to do is not adjust their prose style but this.

  • Attach a source to every figure. A number whose origin you cannot state comes out of the sentence
  • Open the links you cite. A URL existing and its contents actually supporting the claim are two different facts
  • Leave the unconfirmed blank. Keep the empty cell and add one line on why it is empty. Estimates dropped into gaps come back later as citations
  • Narrow the scope. Conditions and dates instead of generally. A narrow sentence is also easier to fix when it turns out wrong
  • Build a correction path. Update dates and a note of what changed give the reader something to adjust their trust against
  • Decide disclosure by audience. In the spirit of Google's question about whether automation is self-evident to visitors, decide what and how much to state on the basis of what the reader can do with the information

The inverse is just as clear: work that is not worth doing. Deliberately contorting vocabulary to slip past a detector buys nothing. Section 4 shows exactly why — rewrite with fancier words and the verdict flips. What that effort produces is evasion of a verdict, not quality, and it usually makes the text harder to read.

10. A checklist for the reader

Split by time budget. The order is check, not judge.

10.1 Thirty seconds — the skim

  • Does any source appear at all in the paragraphs that contain numbers?
  • Do the claims carry conditions and dates, or is it all general truths?
  • Is there somewhere that says what could not be confirmed?
  • Do the lists and tables carry information, or only fill a shape?

10.2 Three minutes — deciding whether to use this at all

  • Pick one key figure and open its link. Does it open, and does that document actually state that number?
  • Check whether the source is primary or a re-citation. If it is a re-citation, climb one level up
  • Check whether the piece makes a claim of its own. If it only lists both sides, there is no judgment here to take away
  • Cross-check the same claim against one other source

10.3 Thirty minutes — using it as evidence

  • Look at the sample and method behind the cited work. What exactly was measured, and on whom?
  • Work out how far it transfers to your situation. Different population, and the number does not carry over
  • Check the publication and update dates. For rules, prices and policies, the date is the validity
  • Deliberately look for material that reached the opposite conclusion

Compressed to one line. Instead of asking did AI write this, ask can I check one of these sentences right now. The first question has no answer; the second has one in thirty seconds.

What could not be confirmed

  • A controlled experiment measuring reader trust for text alone. Studies exist for images and for news contexts, but no experiment presenting identical content with only the style varied was located. The signal-by-signal reactions in section 2 are an observational account, not measurements
  • Detector false positive rates on Korean, Japanese and Chinese text. The figures in section 4 come from a study of English writing. Whether the same magnitude of bias appears in other languages could not be confirmed
  • Current detector performance. The study cited evaluated commercial tools as of 2023. How those products have changed since is outside what can be checked, because internal metrics are not published
  • Whether ranking systems use style signals directly. Section 7 stays inside what the published guidance documents say. The behaviour of the actual systems is not public
  • The scale behind the original author's observation. The essay cited in section 1 contains no sample and no measurement. Only the existence of the phenomenon is cited, not its frequency or size

Conclusion: stop judging, start checking

The reaction itself is not the problem. Attention is finite, and skimming is a normal way to allocate a finite budget. The trouble starts when the reaction hardens into a conclusion.

Three things to keep.

One. Style does not point at authorship. Seven commercial detectors misclassified 61% of non-native essays as AI-generated, and rewriting the same essays with richer vocabulary flipped the verdict. What is being measured is lexical flourish, not who wrote it.

Two. The cost of misjudgment is not evenly distributed. It lands on people who choose safe words, people who are still learning, and people writing in a second language. And because the verdict has no feedback loop, whoever made it accumulates confidence while never learning their own accuracy.

Three. Trust rests on verifiability, not on style. Is there a source, does the link open, is the scope narrow, are the gaps left as gaps, are errors corrected. All five can be settled by the reader personally, and all five apply the same way to any text however it was made.

One last inversion that this subject invites. Given two pieces carrying the same signals, one checks out and one does not. That difference never emerges from staring at the prose, however long — it emerges in the thirty seconds it takes to open a link. Delay the verdict by thirty seconds and most of the time you no longer need one.
