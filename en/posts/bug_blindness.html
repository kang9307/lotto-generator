How a Defect Becomes the Way It Has Always Been - Why Familiar Bugs Stop Registering, and How to Make Them Visible Again

Once the workaround becomes a habit, the reports stop. What to write down while a new person's eyes are still open

Sep 1, 2026 Dev & Infra

There are two kinds of team with a short bug list. One genuinely has few bugs. The other has stopped filing bugs as bugs. From the outside the numbers look almost identical: open issue count, weekly new tickets, the length of the reproduce-me queue. What separates them is not the list but the eyes that produce the list.

This piece is not about how to find and fix bugs. Narrowing and reproducing is a craft I have written about separately. What is in view here is the step before that. How does the moment arrive in which something is perceived as a defect, and why does that moment never arrive for certain defects at all?

This is a general treatment of how software quality is perceived, not an assessment of any particular product, service or company. The studies cited come from different fields with different methods, so none of them transfers wholesale to an individual engineering organisation.

The trigger - people who count bugs and people who do not

In late August 2026 a piece that takes this subject head-on circulated in a Korean developer community and drew a long discussion. Its author, Dan Luu, wrote that he runs into hundreds to thousands of bugs a week. That is not because he uses computers in some unusual way. Most people are hitting the same things, and his diagnosis is that the fact of having been hit never reaches consciousness.

His best example is a childhood mechanical mouse. Dust gets into the ball and the cursor jumps. But before any judgement formed that the thing needed cleaning, a habit of jerking his hand oddly formed first. For as long as that habit was in place, the perception that the mouse was broken never got constructed. The problem was not solved, it was covered, and covered problems are not observed.

The same piece lists a string of others: waiting a few seconds before typing a document title because typing immediately gets the text overwritten, toggling wireless off and on before logging into a laptop. The author argues that accumulating habits like these is a large part of what gets called being good with computers. That sentence is uncomfortable because of what it implies: competence and blindness are built out of the same material. The longer someone has used a system, the more they route around, and the more they route around, the less they see.

Not a problem of fixing, a problem of seeing

Handling a defect usually goes in four steps: perceive, reproduce, narrow, fix. The last three have accumulated technique and tooling around them. Bisecting to the offending commit, building a minimal reproduction, adding instrumentation are all well-mapped territory. But when the first step is empty, the other three never start.

What makes this worse is that management metrics cannot see the distinction. A defect that is not in the backlog is not a low-priority defect. From the organisation's point of view it is a defect that does not exist. Low priority eventually surfaces; non-existent has no surface to rise to.

It is also a different animal from a bug that lay dormant for years. The defect that took sixteen years to surface stayed hidden because its trigger conditions were rare. The case here is the opposite. The conditions are met daily, the defect fires daily, and somebody hits it every day. It is just that nobody calls it a defect.

  • Rare defect — the firing itself is uncommon. It does show up in crash reports. You are not blind to it, you simply do not meet it
  • Unperceived defect — it fires daily and is in the logs. What is missing is anyone reading those logs as evidence of a defect
  • The responses differ — the first needs wider observation; the second needs existing observation read differently
  • So do the costs — the first takes equipment and time. The second usually starts with one meeting

The three stages in which familiarity rewrites perception

Getting used to something is a lumpy phrase. In practice there are distinguishable stages, and each leaves a different trace that can be observed from outside.

The bottom layer is habituation: a decrease in response to repeated stimulation, specifically one that is not explained by sensory adaptation or motor fatigue. That is the definition Rankin and colleagues set out in Neurobiology of Learning and Memory volume 92 in 2009. One of its characteristics connects straight to practice. Withhold the stimulus for a while and the response recovers at least partially. That is the first day back from a long holiday, when a loading time nobody minded last month is suddenly irritating. The irritation is not you being oversensitive; it is a sense that had faded briefly returning. Which means the first day back is an observation opportunity, not a settling-in period.

Habituation, though, is an individual-level phenomenon, and specifically one at the level of stimulus and response. For a whole team to stop calling a defect a defect, two more layers stack on top.

  • The reporting curve bends at stage two. Not because there are fewer defects but because reporting stopped paying
  • Stage three leaves no record, so no metric catches it. If anything the metrics improve
  • So the sentence we hardly get any tickets about that area now has two readings. It was fixed, or it reached stage three
  • There is one way to tell them apart. Hand that area to someone touching it for the first time and the answer arrives immediately

Once a workaround exists, the reports stop

There is a far thicker body of research on workarounds in healthcare than in software. The scoping review Debono and colleagues published as BMC Health Services Research volume 13, article 175, in 2013 pulled together 58 empirical studies of nurses' workarounds in acute care. The definition it works from is precise. A workaround is an observed or described behaviour that may differ from organisationally prescribed or intended procedures, and that circumvents or temporarily fixes an evident or perceived workflow hindrance in order to meet a goal.

Two of the review's findings apply to software teams without modification. One is that workarounds are shared informally and become part of group behaviour. The other is that the effect of this is to hide problems and practices occurring in real time, which prevents organisational learning and improvement. A behaviour that is plainly rational for the individual erodes the organisation's ability to observe itself.

Why reporting stops is a matter of arithmetic. The workaround costs two seconds. Reporting does not. You write reproduction steps, attach a screenshot, explain again when the assignee cannot reproduce it, and have the conversation once more when it is closed as intended behaviour. For the individual doing the reporting, that calculation loses every time. And the organisation only ever sees the output of that calculation.

  • Cost asymmetry — workarounds are measured in seconds, reports in minutes to hours. On individual terms the workaround wins every round
  • No reward — the workaround pays immediately. The report pays in a few months, or never
  • Social friction — a report easily reads as criticism of someone's work. A workaround collides with nobody
  • Learning happens by area — two or three closures as by design and it is not just that item that stops being reported but the entire area around it
  • What gets transmitted — what passes to new people is the workaround, not the defect. Two generations on, the trick survives and the reason is gone

Where it has always been like that gets manufactured

The process by which an organisation absorbs deviation as normal has a name. It is the normalization Diane Vaughan set out in The Challenger Launch Decision, published by University of Chicago Press in 1996. When departures from a norm repeat and nothing goes wrong, the departure gets absorbed into the culture until it no longer reads as a departure. Each repetition shifts the baseline a little.

A line has to be drawn here. What Vaughan analysed was safety norms and the outcome was fatal. Discussing an editor that overwrites a title with that weight is inflation, and inflation weakens the argument rather than strengthening it. What transfers is not the conclusion but one mechanism. Repetition without consequence moves the baseline. And a baseline that has moved leaves no record of having moved.

The sentence it has always been like that appears in one specific place: when a new person asks. It is not an answer to their question. It is a signal that an undocumented workaround exists inside the team. The person answering knows the trick and has already forgotten what the trick is covering.

  • When you hear it, get what exactly has always been like that written down in one line on the spot. Unwritten, it is gone by next week
  • The person who said it is not at fault. They are probably the longest-serving person on the team, and by the same token the best at routing around things
  • Conversely, a team that never hears the sentence is in one of two states. Genuinely clean, or without any new people
  • Several other phrases carry the same value. Just click it again, you do not really need that screen, personally I go in through this side

The window that opens only for newcomers and outsiders

A new arrival has no workaround habits. So they enter through the designed path and take the friction that was always there full in the face. As an instrument this state is extraordinarily expensive. It cannot be bought, and it cannot be used twice on the same person.

The window closes after roughly the first few weeks, and why it closes matters. Not because they got desensitised but because they learned the workarounds. Which means the thing that closes the window is onboarding itself, and the better the onboarding the faster it shuts. That is the nastiest part of the whole problem.

The most common failure comes from kindness. A new person says this seems a bit off, and the person beside them says that is because of such and such, here is what you do. The explanation is correct and necessary. It just has to come after the writing down. Once the explanation is out, that observation is not recoverable.

It is tempting to reach for the attention literature here, but the target is different. In the experiment Simons and Chabris published in Perception volume 28 in 1999, close to half of observers counting basketball passes failed to see a person in a gorilla suit cross the screen for nine seconds. In the condition counting the white team 54 percent missed it, in the black-team condition 42 percent, and in the condition where they had been told in advance, nearly everyone saw it. That is a failure to catch an unexpected stimulus once, which is a different mechanism from ceasing to register something encountered repeatedly. Exactly one thing transfers: specify what to look for and the detection rate changes.

  • Which is why asking a new person to please find bugs produces almost nothing. They do not yet have a standard for what counts as one
  • Specify instead. Points where you repeated the same action twice or more, points where you stared at a screen for three seconds, points where you asked the person next to you
  • Change sits at a similar distance. In the field study Simons and Levin published in Psychonomic Bulletin & Review volume 5 in 1998, a conversation partner was swapped for a different person mid-conversation and only half of pedestrians noticed. What transfers is one direction: gradual change is especially invisible
  • Slow a response by 100 milliseconds every release and nobody reports it. Ten releases is a full second. Add that same second in one go and the report arrives that day

What to write down before the window closes

With a first-week log, the form is half the work. Leave it free-form and you get impressions, and impressions cannot be processed. Specify the fields and narrow them, and observations come back instead.

  • Where you got stuck and the clock time — you need the time to line it up against logs later
  • What you were trying to do at that moment — write the goal, not the screen name. Without the goal there is no reproduction
  • How many times you actually did something — times you went back, times you clicked again, times you opened a new tab
  • Who you asked and what answer you got — this is the field where it has always been like that gets captured
  • The thing that felt like your fault but you are not sure about — the most valuable field. A failure someone blames on themselves never reaches any list by any route
  • What you looked for in the docs and could not find — documentation defects and product defects separate only here

Three rules should be attached for the person writing. All three exist to lower their burden.

  • Do not judge. Whether this is a bug is not the writer's job. Demand a judgement and half of it goes unwritten
  • Do not guess at causes. Attach a guess and the reader spends their attention checking the guess and lets the observation slip past
  • Do not ask for a fix. The moment it becomes a request, the list becomes a negotiation, and negotiations get trimmed

Then there is what happens to it afterwards. Turning the list straight into tickets is the most common mistake. Half get closed as intended behaviour and the rest sink below the priority line. From the writer's side nothing happened, and the next person's notes never come. This list is observation data, not a backlog. When three people's notes are stacked and the same item shows up three times, what should open is a design discussion, not a ticket.

Specification or evasion

That is not a bug, that is by design is frequently legitimate. There are intended constraints, things given up to preserve a more important guarantee, and cases where two requirements collided and one was chosen. Calling rulings like these evasions makes design itself impossible.

The dividing line is not tone but when the reasoning existed. Was the basis for calling it a specification already present at decision time, or was the explanation constructed retroactively because the situation is awkward now? Put in a table it is usable in the meeting itself.

What to askAnswer if it is a specificationAnswer if it is an evasion
Is there a record from decision time of deciding it this wayYes, including the alternatives considered and why they were rejectedNo. The explanation is being written now
Are users told in advanceIt is in the docs and on the screenThey find out after they hit it
Where does the workaround liveIn official documentationIt is passed on by word of mouth only
Designing it fresh today, would you build the same thingYes. The same choice againNo. But the cost of changing it is large
Who gets relief from this rulingThe user. A more important guarantee is preservedThe team. This quarter's list gets shorter
  • The last two rows are the working discriminant. A no on the fourth row means this is not a specification but a deferred defect
  • Renamed a deferred defect, it stays on the list. Called a specification, it disappears on the spot. Not fixing it this quarter is the same either way, but the record is different
  • The cost of changing it is large is an entirely legitimate reason. The only illegitimate move is dressing that reason up as design intent

Four devices that make it visible again

What remains is something you can actually run. The four below have good returns for their cost, and none of them requires buying a new tool.

A new person's first-week log

Use the form from the previous section as-is. Operationally, the thing that matters is naming the recipient in advance. With no one to receive it, the log ends as a private note. And it is better to separate the recipient from whoever runs onboarding. When they are the same person, the urge to explain is hard to resist, and explaining closes the window.

Inventorying the workarounds

This one is aimed at your own team, not at new arrivals. Pick an hour, and during that hour write one line every time you do something that is not the intended path. Refreshing, going back, double-clicking, operating something only in a particular sequence, moving to a different tool to get a step done. You could screen-record and review it later, but self-reporting is far cheaper, and more importantly the act of writing briefly undoes the habituation.

Reclassifying support contacts

Support contacts are already piled up, and they are usually classified only by whether they were resolved. Change the axis and regroup them: same screen, same step, same sentence. A good share of what closed as a usage question was in fact a failure to find something. When a user's inability to locate a feature is processed as a question and closed with a helpful answer, that screen's defect lives on forever as a support statistic.

Finding repeated retries in the logs

Same user, same endpoint, short interval, repeated. What you are looking at here is not failure responses. Failures are already monitored. The signal is repeated success responses. Succeeding and then doing it again means the result did not match the expectation, which means the screen failed to show the result properly. A user pressing save three times believes the save did not go through.

  • The four look at different layers. Run only one and only the layer that one looks at gets clean
  • If an order is needed, support reclassification goes first. The material already exists and you do not have to wait for a new hire
  • Three of the four depend on people writing. Time spent narrowing the format matters more than time spent collecting
  • None of the four should be evaluated by how many fixes came out. Make fix count the criterion and the item count drops from the next round on

The same thing happens on the user's side

Everything so far has been about the building side. The same thing happens on the using side, and it is much quieter there. A user who has adapted to an inconvenience does not complain. They adapt or they leave, and when they leave they do not leave a reason.

One cause is the one the original piece names: people are poor at perceiving flaws in things they chose or like. The other matters more. Users blame failures on themselves. Did I click the wrong thing, am I just not finding it, is it supposed to be this hard? A failure charged to oneself never arrives as a support contact either.

This is why satisfaction surveys cannot reach this layer. It is not unusual for someone who answered no to is there anything inconvenient to be spending an extra three seconds on the same screen every day. For them those three seconds long ago stopped being an inconvenience and became procedure.

  • Path length beats churn rate — the danger signal is a stretch where completion holds steady while the number of steps to the goal grows
  • No contacts is not evidence — a screen nobody contacts you about and a screen that works are indistinguishable in the metrics. Separating them requires sitting someone down in front of it
  • User workarounds are more inventive than the team's — browser extensions, a separate spreadsheet, screenshotting and sending it over chat. These traces live outside the product, which is why they are invisible
  • A cheap way to find the outside traces — look at what words appear alongside your feature names in user communities. The tricks are usually written down there
  • Departed users are not in the sample — only the ones still here answer. The brighter the survey result, the larger this bias

Where these methods fall over

That was the version where it works. In practice there are a few specific places it falls over, and knowing them in advance is most of avoiding them.

  • Write everything down and nothing survives. A 200-line first-week log does not get read, and an unread list does not produce a second round. That is the reason for narrowing the fields
  • A good share of what a new person flags is genuinely something they do not know yet. Sorting that costs effort. But sort in a hurry and the real items go out with it. Better to stack three people's notes and start from what overlaps
  • Treat all friction as defect and you strip out intended friction too. A confirmation step in front of an irreversible action is friction but not a defect
  • The person running this gets used to it in turn. Whoever maintains the workaround inventory habituates to the inventory. If item counts start dropping from the third round, suspect dulled perception before improvement
  • There are diminishing returns. Run the same device repeatedly and the yield falls. How many rounds before you stop is the same class of judgement as in review, and here too it is better to fix the stopping rule in advance

What you can do this week

No large programme is needed. None of the four above requires tool adoption or budget approval. Keeping the scope small and completing one full lap is the goal of a first round.

  • Ask anyone who joined in the last three months what was inconvenient and is now familiar. Thirty minutes, no prior records needed. This question catches items that have just dropped into stage three
  • Regroup the most recent 50 support contacts by screen name. If three screens come out on top, that is the draft of next quarter's list
  • Set aside one hour as a team and have everyone count the moves that are not the intended path. Even with no detail, the raw counts show which areas are heavy
  • Pull one query for points where the same user got two or more success responses within three minutes. The top of that list is your screen-defect shortlist
  • Open a channel for one-line notes every time it has always been like that is said. Do not process anything, just accumulate. A month of it is enough to judge from
  • Check the date the next new hire starts and name the recipient now. Deciding on the day itself is already too late

The list has one thing in common: none of it decides yet what to fix. What the perception step needs is not judgement but a record, and judgement loses nothing by waiting until a record has repeated three times. Getting used to things cannot be prevented. What can be prevented is failing to write it down before you get used to it.

What I could not verify

  • The originating piece carries no publication date, so its date of writing could not be established. Nothing in the body assumes a point in time on that basis
  • The specific product and company cases in the original, and the survey figures quoted there, are not reproduced here because the primary sources could not be checked directly
  • For habituation, inattentional blindness and change blindness, only the definitions and figures confirmed in each experimental source are used. No study explaining organisation-level failure to perceive defects through these concepts could be located, which is why all three appear here as boundary lines rather than as explanations
  • The workaround research is on acute-care nursing. Evidence that it transfers to software organisations at the same rate and in the same form could not be found, so only the definition and the mechanism were taken
  • No controlled study measuring the effect of devices like first-week logs or workaround inventories could be located. What is described here is an observation method, not a validated intervention
