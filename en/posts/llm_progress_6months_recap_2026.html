LLM Progress Recap H1 2026 - Every Major Change in 6 Months at a Glance

From November 2025 to May 2026 - the six months that reshaped AI, distilled into one practitioner's matrix

May 21, 2026 Tech & IT

Why a six-month recap is the right granularity in 2026

Six months ago, in November 2025, the frontier of language models looked stable. GPT-4.5o was still the default, Claude 4.5 Sonnet had just shipped, and Gemini 2.0 Flash was the budget workhorse. The narrative was that scaling laws were softening and the curve had flattened. Six months later, almost every assumption from that paragraph is wrong. The November-to-May window is the busiest LLM release cycle since the original GPT-4 launch, and the speed has caught even full-time AI builders off guard. Most engineers I talk to are running 2025-era model assumptions in their stack and quietly leaving 30 to 50 percent of available quality, cost, or speed on the table.

This article is the recap I needed for my own work. It walks through every major lab and open-weight family, compresses the changes into matrices and tables, and ends with five concrete takeaways and a forward look at H2 2026. The shape is deliberate: one section per major actor, then trends, then benchmarks, then advice. If you only have time for the matrices, scroll to the two large tables. If you want the underlying story, read straight through. Throughout, my goal is to keep this grounded in what changes real builders' decisions this quarter, not in marketing language any lab would object to.

Three framing notes before the actor-by-actor recap. First, model numbers are not progress measurements; some labs ship a "0.1" that is a bigger jump than another lab's "2.0". I will say what actually changed, not just the version. Second, closed-weight and open-weight progress are now running on parallel curves, with the gap measured in weeks rather than months on some axes. Third, the most consequential change is not any single model release - it is the surrounding stack: agentic harnesses, coding agents, memory primitives, and the Model Context Protocol becoming a de facto standard. I will keep returning to that point.

OpenAI: GPT-5, Goals, and the Operator track

OpenAI's H1 2026 is dominated by two moves. The first is the GPT-5 release at the end of January, which pulled together the reasoning track previously fragmented across the o-series. GPT-5 is a single product with adaptive thinking budget rather than a separate "reasoning model" toggle, and that simplification alone removed an enormous source of confusion for developers. The second move is Codex Goals, released in stages from late February through April, which turned the Codex sub-product from a chat completion into something closer to an autonomous coding agent that takes a high-level objective and works against a repository for hours.

The quieter changes matter for production. The Operator stack went generally available in March, with a stable computer-use API and noticeably better recovery from broken page states. Realtime API gained native tool use, which closed the gap with Anthropic for voice-driven workflows. Memory persistence moved from an experimental feature to a documented surface with explicit storage, retrieval, and forgetting primitives, which made building "the assistant remembers me across sessions" a one-week project rather than a one-quarter one. None of this dominated the press cycle, but it is what the production stack is actually built on.

Pricing-wise, GPT-5 launched with input cost roughly flat against the late-2025 GPT-4.5o and output meaningfully cheaper thanks to longer prompt caches. The 4o family is being deprecated on a documented timeline that ends in Q3 2026, and developers still on 4o today should plan a migration window now. For background on the Codex Goals shift specifically and how to wire it into your daily loop, our prior piece OpenAI Codex Goals Practical Guide 2026 goes deeper.

Anthropic: Claude 4.5, 4.6, 4.7, and the year of Claude Code

Anthropic ran three significant releases in six months. Claude 4.6 in late December 2025 tightened tool use and added native long-document handling. Claude 4.7 Sonnet in March 2026 brought computer use into the default product and pushed coding benchmarks decisively above the rest of the field. Claude 4.7 Opus in May 2026 is the current frontier model on most agentic and long-context tasks, and the one I personally use for multi-hour autonomous coding sessions. Each release was incremental in name, but cumulatively they widened Anthropic's lead in the "send the agent off for two hours and trust it" category.

Around the models, Claude Code matured into a full product. Skills, hooks, sub-agents, and a stable settings.json schema turned the CLI from a power-user toy into something teams actually deploy across a codebase. The Model Context Protocol that Anthropic kicked off in late 2024 became a de facto industry standard in H1 2026; OpenAI, Google, and most major IDE vendors now ship MCP support natively. This is probably the single most consequential standards shift of the period - by quietly winning the protocol layer, Anthropic put itself at the center of the developer-tools stack regardless of which model anyone happens to be calling.

The other notable Anthropic story is people, not products. Andrej Karpathy's move to Anthropic in February was the most-discussed AI hire of the year. Whatever the long-term impact on research, the short-term effect on the company's pull with senior researchers across the industry was immediate and visible. For practical day-to-day use of Claude Code itself, our standing guide Claude Code Skills Complete Guide covers the patterns that survived through the 4.6 and 4.7 transitions.

Google: Gemini 3.0, 3.5 Flash, and the pricing war

Google's strategy in H1 2026 was less about peak quality and more about radical price-performance pressure. Gemini 2.5 in December was a quiet quality lift over 2.0. Gemini 3.0 in February was the first release that put Google clearly back in the frontier conversation, with strong multimodal performance and a usable 2-million-token context window. The headline release of the period, though, was Gemini 3.5 Flash in April, which cut output cost by about 60 percent versus 2.0 Flash while improving quality enough to make it the obvious default for high-volume, low-stakes traffic.

AI Overviews in Google Search moved from "people argued whether it was a good idea" to "a permanent fixture of how a billion-plus people search." This is consequential beyond the model itself: AI-generated answers are now the layer between users and most of the open web, and content strategy in 2026 has to be written for that layer first. Inside Google's developer surface, the I/O 2026 announcements in mid-May previewed deeper Gemini integration into Workspace, Android, and the new Code Assist agent tier. None of this is shocking individually; the cumulative effect is that Google is back to being a default rather than a secondary option.

The price point matters most for teams running heavy automation. If you are building anything that calls an LLM more than a million times a month, the math has changed in Gemini's favor for the bottom 70 percent of your traffic. Reserve the frontier models for the top 30 percent where the marginal quality lift is worth it. That two-tier pattern is now the default architecture for serious LLM products, and we will return to it in the trends section.

Meta: Llama 4 series and the open strategy at scale

Meta's H1 2026 was the Llama 4 family. The Llama 4 7B, 30B, and 200B releases between December and April were not individually frontier-shifting, but as a coordinated drop they pushed open-weight models meaningfully forward on three axes: longer context (now solid out to 256K), tool use parity with closed models at the 30B size, and quality lift at the 200B size that put it in the same conversation as Claude 4.5 Sonnet for many tasks. Meta also opened a clearer licensing regime that satisfies more enterprise legal teams than the previous Llama 3.x terms did.

What Meta did not do is also relevant. There was no Llama 4 release that targets frontier closed models head-on at the 500B-plus scale, and the company's public messaging in early 2026 framed that as a deliberate strategic choice rather than an inability. The bet is that "good enough" open weights at common sizes, combined with the cost of self-hosting falling, eventually pulls a much larger share of inference workload than chasing the absolute frontier ever would. Whether that bet pays out is a six-quarter question, not a six-month one, but the early signal from enterprise self-hosted deployments is favorable.

For practitioners, the actionable lesson is that Llama 4 30B is the size to know. It runs comfortably on a single H100 or a Blackwell B200 at production speeds, handles tool use well, and gives you a hosted-API-class experience with zero vendor lock-in. Combined with vLLM 0.7's batching improvements, the cost-per-token for self-hosted Llama 4 30B is now competitive with Gemini 3.5 Flash on raw inference, before counting the strategic value of owning the stack.

China's Big Three: DeepSeek, Qwen, Doubao

The China story in H1 2026 is the most surprising one for outside observers. DeepSeek V3 in late November 2025 was already strong; V4 in February 2026 is genuinely competitive with frontier closed models on coding and math. The 671B MoE architecture activates roughly 37B parameters per token, which keeps inference cost reasonable while pushing quality into the top tier on a per-task basis. Open weights, MIT-style licensing, and a paper that documents the training recipe in unusual detail made DeepSeek V4 the single most-deployed open model of Q1 2026.

Alibaba's Qwen 3 family, released in March 2026, pushed coding hard. The Qwen 3 Coder 32B variant is, on my own benchmark runs, the strongest small open coder available right now. Together with Qwen 3 235B for general use, Alibaba is now serving more open-weight inference globally than any other source by some estimates. ByteDance's Doubao 1.6 in April 2026 is less well known outside China but matters in the Chinese-speaking market because it ships with native cross-modal capabilities and tight TikTok integration. Whether you use these models or not depends heavily on your data-handling stance, but pretending they do not exist is no longer a defensible position. The full landscape is covered in our standing comparison DeepSeek vs Qwen vs Doubao - China LLM Comparison 2026.

One technical aside worth flagging: the recent work on steering vectors in DeepSeek V4 opened a small but meaningful interpretability door, and external teams have already shown reproducible behavior shifts on coding evaluations. That is one of the more interesting research stories of the half-year, and our LLM Steering Vectors and DeepSeek V4 piece dives into the practical implications.

xAI: Grok 3 to Grok 4, the underdog narrative

xAI quietly stopped being a meme. Grok 3 in late November 2025 was the first version that landed inside frontier range on reasoning benchmarks; Grok 4 in April 2026 narrowed the remaining gap. The model is still primarily distributed through X and the standalone consumer app, and that distribution gap keeps it from being a default developer choice. The technical work, however, is real - Colossus, the company's training cluster, scaled to a level that few competitors can match short of a new Microsoft-OpenAI-style deal, and that hardware base is now reflected in the model.

For practitioners, Grok 4 is a viable backup model and an unusually strong choice when you need real-time web grounding without building your own retrieval layer. The pricing is mid-tier and the API has stabilized. The constraint is mostly cultural: many enterprises still treat xAI as too tied to X for procurement reasons, and that perception is taking longer to shift than the underlying technical position justifies. If your stack already calls a router that load-balances across providers, adding Grok 4 as an option costs a few hours and gives you measurable resilience against the next provider outage.

Mistral, Cohere, and the second-tier consolidation

The second tier of frontier labs went through real consolidation in H1 2026. Mistral Large 3 in January reasserted Mistral's position as the strongest European frontier option, with EU-resident infrastructure and licensing terms that satisfy regulators in ways US-labs still struggle with. Mistral's smaller open releases (Codestral 2, Pixtral 2) kept their developer mindshare alive. Cohere's Command R-Plus 2026 update doubled down on enterprise RAG workflows and continues to outperform the headline frontier models on grounded retrieval tasks specifically.

Several smaller actors that were still in the conversation in late 2025 effectively dropped out. AI21's Jamba family is now a niche choice for very long context only. Inflection has pivoted away from frontier consumer models entirely. Reka and a handful of others continue to ship technically interesting work but no longer compete at the top of any benchmark anyone cares about. The market is consolidating to roughly seven serious LLM providers globally - OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta (open), DeepSeek, Alibaba/Qwen, and xAI - with Mistral and Cohere as strong regional or vertical options. That's a meaningful narrowing from twelve-plus at the end of 2024.

Local LLM: Llama.cpp, Ollama, vLLM, MLX

The local and self-hosted ecosystem moved fast. Llama.cpp added stable Vulkan and ROCm paths, which broke the NVIDIA-only assumption that was true a year ago. Strix Halo PCs (AMD's high-memory APU laptop platform) running 70B models at usable speeds became a real product category in Q1 2026. Ollama crossed into mainstream developer tooling with stable model registries, predictable update channels, and tight integration with VS Code and JetBrains IDEs. vLLM 0.7 and 0.8 cut inference cost in production deployments through paged attention v3 and speculative decoding improvements. Apple's MLX 0.20 release made M-series Macs the highest-memory-per-dollar local inference option for the 30B-to-70B parameter band, and M5 Max devices ship today with enough unified memory to host Llama 4 30B and Qwen 3 32B without compromise.

The combined effect is that a real share of low-stakes LLM traffic is leaving the API. Personal note-taking assistants, IDE autocompletion, summarization of internal documents - any task where data sensitivity, latency, or cost matters more than the absolute frontier - is migrating to local inference. AI coding tools: Cursor, Claude Code, Codex, Aider, Cline

AI coding tools were the single fastest-moving sub-category of the half-year. Cursor's agent mode matured through November and December into something that handles multi-file refactors reliably, and the company's pricing held even as competition intensified. Claude Code became the dominant CLI tool for serious autonomous coding workflows; if you are running tasks that take 30 minutes or more, Claude Code through Claude 4.7 Opus is the current default. Codex Goals via the OpenAI Codex sub-product is the strongest one-shot generation surface; for tight iteration loops where you want to see the model's output and tweak quickly, it is hard to beat.

Aider stayed strong as the open-source baseline, and Cline filled the VS Code-integrated agent niche for developers who want an in-editor experience without leaving the IDE. The honest assessment after three months of mixing them is that no single tool dominates every workflow. Cursor for in-editor pair programming, Claude Code for long autonomous tasks, Codex for one-shot generation, and Aider as the open fallback when you want a tool you can fully inspect. Six-month core trends

Stepping back from individual labs, four trends define the period.

Agentic AI moved from demo to production. The defining feature of every frontier model release this half-year was longer, more reliable autonomous chains. Two-hour autonomous coding sessions are now realistic with Claude 4.7 Opus. Multi-step research, where the model proposes, browses, summarizes, and iterates on its own plan, works with GPT-5 and Gemini 3.0. The remaining failure modes are real but no longer disqualifying for production use.

Reasoning models stopped being a separate product category. The o-series became part of GPT-5's adaptive thinking budget. Claude's extended thinking is on by default. Gemini 3.0 uses internal chains transparently. The "thinking model" toggle that confused users for the past year is largely gone, and that simplification is itself a quality improvement.

Multimodal became table stakes. Vision works in all frontier models. Audio in and out is standard. Video understanding is competent across the top three labs. The differentiator is no longer "do you have multimodal" but "how good is your tool use stitched together with your multimodal inputs."

Smaller-better is real at the 30B class. The single biggest gainer of the half-year by frequency of deployment is the 30B parameter band. Qwen 3 32B Coder, Llama 4 30B, and the smaller DeepSeek distills all sit in this range, and they handle the 70 to 80 percent of tasks where you do not actually need frontier IQ.

Benchmark shifts: MMLU saturated, SWE-bench surging

The benchmark landscape changed materially. MMLU is effectively saturated; every frontier model sits between 88 and 91, and the noise floor of the benchmark itself is in that range. SWE-bench saw a roughly doubled top score over six months, from 49 percent in November 2025 to above 90 percent in May 2026 on the Verified subset, which is the most consequential single benchmark shift of the period. GPQA top scores moved from low-60s to high-70s. ARC-AGI v2 remains the hardest standing public benchmark and is where the labs are now most visibly competing.

The lesson for engineers reading benchmarks: SWE-bench is now the most useful single number for "is this model good at coding", and the gap between MMLU performance and real-world utility is wider than ever. A model can MMLU at 90 and still fail on production debugging that Claude 4.7 handles in a single pass. Always pair benchmark numbers with task-specific evaluations on your own workload.

Five takeaways every developer should internalize

Compressed advice from the six-month picture.

  1. Rotate at least two coding tools. Claude Code plus Codex, or Claude Code plus Cursor, is the most resilient pattern. Their failure modes are different, and you will keep working when one provider has an outage.
  2. Set up a two-tier routing layer. Send 60 to 80 percent of traffic to Gemini 3.5 Flash or Claude Haiku class. Reserve frontier models for the calls that actually need them. The cost savings pay for the engineering in a week.
  3. Run at least one model locally. A 30B model on an M-series Mac or a workstation with a Blackwell card covers offline work, sensitive data, and gives you ground truth for "is this task even possible without the API." Builders without local experience are increasingly visible by the questions they ask.
  4. Treat MCP as default. The Model Context Protocol is the new "REST for AI." Building your own bespoke integration to ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini separately is now wasted work; one MCP server connects to all three.
  5. Re-evaluate your benchmarks quarterly. The model that won your eval in November is probably second-tier today. Run your eval suite every three months at minimum, and pick the new default with eyes open.

What to expect in H2 2026

Forecasting is hard and most predictions age badly, but the trajectory of the past six months supports three calls with reasonable confidence.

First, agentic systems become the default UI for power users, not a niche. The user interface evolution is the same one chatbots went through in 2023: experimental in Q1, mainstream in Q4. Expect every major lab to ship an agentic surface as part of the default product by year-end, not as an opt-in beta.

Second, model differentiation moves from raw IQ to memory, tool use, and price-per-task. The pure benchmark race is plateauing for closed models, and competition shifts to the surrounding stack. Whichever lab gets memory right first (the model remembers you across sessions in a way that feels useful rather than creepy) likely takes a meaningful share of consumer mindshare.

Third, on-device and laptop-class inference quietly takes over a large share of low-stakes traffic. M-series Macs and Strix Halo PCs running 30 to 70 billion parameter models at usable speeds shift a substantial share of consumer usage off the API entirely. This is bad for API revenue in the budget tier and very good for user privacy.

If you are planning a roadmap for H2 2026, build for those three. Skip the "wait for the next model" trap. The capabilities you need are already in the stack you can run today.

Conclusion: six months of structural shifts

The November-to-May window was not just another upgrade cycle. It was the period when agentic systems became real, when open weights closed the frontier gap on enough axes to matter, when prices dropped enough to change architecture patterns, and when MCP locked in as a standards layer that defines how the next two years of tools get built. Any one of those is a normal year's worth of progress. All four happening together is the structural shift.

The practical posture for builders is unchanged from earlier in the cycle: stay close to the tools, rotate at least two of them, evaluate your stack every quarter, and resist the temptation to wait for a perfect default to emerge. The frontier moves too fast for that to ever happen. Your competitive advantage is in how quickly you compose the available pieces into something that solves a real problem, not in which lab's logo is on the API call. Read the matrix, run the math, and ship the next thing.
