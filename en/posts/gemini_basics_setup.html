Getting Started with Gemini — Deciding What to Delegate and What to Verify

Account rules, the capability line, what not to paste, and what to cross-check

August 16, 2026 Tech & IT

What trips you up is not the interface, it is the boundaries

You sign in with a Google account and get a single input box. The invitation to ask anything is friendly, but the places where new users actually get stuck arrive right after it. The answer comes out fluent and you have no way to judge whether it is correct. It contradicts something you read in search results yesterday and you cannot tell which side to trust. Nobody tells you whether pasting a company file is acceptable. Some features work and others say your account cannot use them, and the rule behind that is invisible.

This is not an article about where the buttons live. Interfaces get redesigned within months, and people who memorised the layout still hit all four of those walls. What separates people who use an AI chatbot comfortably from people who keep struggling is not the number of features they know but whether they know the boundaries. Which tasks are safe to hand over and which must be cross-checked, why the split falls where it does, what should never go into a conversation, and what to confirm before you use an answer. Those four boundaries barely move even when the interface does.

The age criteria and data-handling figures below reflect August 2026, drawn from Google's Gemini Apps help pages and privacy hub. Google adjusts these, so any decision involving payment or internal policy should be checked against the current official pages.

Decide first: which account you sign in with changes the rules

Gemini changes age thresholds, available features and how your conversations are handled depending on the account you signed in with. Skip this and you find out the rules were different only after you have already pasted a company document. Google's guidance recognises three account types: a personal Google Account, a work Google Account on a qualifying Workspace edition, and a school Google Account.

  • Personal and school accounts start at 13. Countries set their own digital consent ages, so where a different age applies locally, that one governs.
  • Work accounts require 18 or over. The same person faces a different threshold depending on which account they signed in with.
  • Children under 13 are controlled by a parent. Access is turned on and off through Family Link. Gemini Apps are not available for supervised accounts in the European Economic Area, Switzerland and the United Kingdom, and some features are not yet open to supervised users.
  • Some regions add further limits. In the EEA, Switzerland and the UK you must be 18 or over to use Gemini Apps on a Google AI plan. Local guidance takes precedence, so do not assume what applies here applies where you live.
  • You can use some web features without signing in. Signing in is required for additional features and for saving your activity. If you only want to see how it performs, you can try before creating anything.
  • Plan pricing is not settled here. There are free and paid tiers, and names, limits and prices move often. The checkout screen is the final authority. To compare plans and models across services, the plan and performance comparison is the piece built for that.

Anyone signed into both a personal and a work account in the same browser should be particularly careful. Whichever account you are chatting from determines how that conversation is handled. If company material is going anywhere near the box, confirm this first.

What it does well and what it does not

This distinction carries most of the value. The principle compresses to one line. Processing material you already have is safe to delegate; retrieving facts that exist out in the world is not. Summarising, translating, drafting and reformatting sit on the first side. Current information, arithmetic and source verification sit on the second.

Safe to delegate Must be cross-checked
Summarising and restructuring text you pasted Today's facts, prices, schedules, latest news
Translation, tone changes, line editing Arithmetic, unit conversion, totals and ratios
Drafts, outlines, agendas, meeting notes Statute and contract clause numbers and wording
Table cleanup, format conversion, categorisation The real source and exact wording of a quotation
Naming, idea lists, generating counterarguments Specific facts about a named person, firm or product
Explaining code, decoding error messages, regex Execution results, function signatures, library versions

Each column has something in common, and once you see it you can classify tasks that are not in the table.

  • The left column has no single right answer. Five people summarising the same page produce five versions and all of them can be usable. There is very little room to be wrong.
  • The left column keeps its material inside the conversation. The pasted text is the evidence, so nothing has to be recalled — the model works on what is in front of it.
  • On the left, you notice errors immediately. If a summary of your own document contains something that was not in it, you see that on the first read. This is the decisive property.
  • The right column has exactly one right answer. Last year's revenue, a clause number, a calculated result. Being close is worth nothing.
  • The right column keeps its material outside the conversation. Unless you paste the source, the model has to reproduce the fact, and that is probability rather than memory.
  • On the right, you cannot tell when it is wrong. This is the real danger. An invented case number and a slightly off statistic both read as perfectly plausible prose.

So the working test is not good-at versus bad-at. It is this: is this a task where I would recognise a wrong answer? If yes, delegate. If no, cross-check or do not delegate at all. It is also worth remembering that right-column items move to the left the moment you paste the source into the conversation. That single move changes accuracy more than anything else available to you.

Why the split falls there — a machine that picks the next word

One paragraph of mechanism makes the boundary obvious. A generative model is trained on enormous quantities of text to estimate the probability of what comes next. When you ask a question it does not query a database; it selects plausible continuations piece by piece. The reason the prose flows naturally and the reason it states non-existent facts with confidence come from the same single property.

  • Plausible and true are separate axes, and the model directly optimises the first one. Smooth prose means the form fits, not that the content is right. Readers collapse those two together, and that is where the misunderstanding starts.
  • It is strong where the transformation is well defined. Summarising, translating and classifying have clear rules from input to output. Recovering one specific fact has no such rule.
  • Fabrication has a cause. A non-existent paper title, a plausible author, a link that will not open — these are not lies but the most natural-looking completion of a pattern. That is why the model does not catch them itself until you point them out.
  • Arithmetic can be handled the same way. Predicting the next digit instead of computing it fails more as the digits multiply. Calculation tools are often attached now, but you generally cannot tell whether the answer in front of you was computed or predicted. Hence checking the maths is the default.
  • Google states the limit on screen. The note that Gemini will make mistakes and can give inaccurate information is not marketing modesty; it is a summary of how the thing works.

Why search results and chatbot answers differ

When the same question produces different answers in Google Search and in Gemini, it feels like one of them is broken. Neither is. The two tools do different jobs. Search locates documents that already exist and returns a list. It does not manufacture missing documents, and when there is nothing it says so. A chatbot produces sentences, and a sentence appears even when no underlying source exists.

  • The output has a different nature. A search link is the source itself. A chatbot answer is a paraphrase, which means it is not a citation until you have opened the original.
  • Recency works differently. A model has a training cutoff and by default knows nothing after it. Search grounding can fill the gap, but it is not always visible whether the answer you just received came from search or from training.
  • Reproducibility differs. Ask the same question twice and the wording changes. Whether only the wording changed or the substance did too is something you have to read for. For an answer that matters, asking twice on purpose is a cheap check.
  • They respond differently to not knowing. Search shows zero results; a chatbot tends to fill the blank. Questions with no real answer therefore attract unusually smooth responses.
  • Source selection is its own mechanism. Which documents a chatbot leans on is not the same as search ranking. That subject is handled separately in the analysis of how AI picks its sources.

Gemini has a double-check feature that uses Google Search to flag statements as likely similar to, or likely different from, content it finds. Useful, but not decisive. Google's own help page states that the feature can be wrong: it may report similar content that in fact contradicts the answer, and the web page it found may itself be inaccurate. It does not apply to responses generated with apps, and it skips code and markdown tables. Treat it as a first filter, not a verdict.

Personal and company material — where to check before pasting

Specific policy wording keeps changing. What is worth memorising is therefore not the policy but the place you check it. On a personal account, one activity setting governs how your conversations are used. There are three places to look: the Gemini Apps activity page, the Gemini Apps privacy guidance, and — if you are on a work account — your organisation's administrator policy.

Wherever you look, the items to check are the same. The on-screen labels change; these five do not.

  • Are chats saved, and what is the auto-delete period? The toggle and the retention choice usually sit together.
  • Do humans review, and how long are reviewed chats kept? This is stated separately from the auto-delete period, so you have to look for it specifically.
  • What is collected alongside the conversation? Location and device information and usage logs belong here.
  • Are connections to other services switched on? Linking mail, storage or photos makes things easier and widens the range of information entering the conversation.
  • Is there a mode that does not save? Check whether a temporary-chat option exists that stays out of your history.

As of August 2026, here is what holds on a personal account. With the activity setting on, chats are saved, can be used to improve the models, and auto-delete after 18 months by default. With it off, future chats stay out of your activity and are not used for training, but are retained for 72 hours so responses can be generated. Temporary chats fall under the same 72 hours.

The most important line sits elsewhere. A subset of chats is read by human reviewers, reviewed chats are retained for up to three years, and deleting your activity does not remove them. That single fact changes practical judgement, because it means there is a window in which the assumption that you can clean up later simply does not hold. For the same reason Google's guidance explicitly says not to enter confidential information you would not want a reviewer to see.

So the usable rule on a personal account reduces to one question. Would it be a problem if this exact text were quoted outside? If yes, it does not go in. When the material is genuinely needed, strip identifiers — names, national ID numbers, account numbers, addresses, employee IDs — and keep only the structure. Contract review works fine with the parties renamed, and log analysis keeps its patterns with the real addresses masked.

Work accounts run on different rules

If your account came from an employer or a school, much of the previous section changes. Generative AI in Workspace carries separate data-handling commitments that Google states explicitly. The core of it is that customer content is not human reviewed or used for generative AI model training outside your domain without permission, and that interactions stay inside your organization.

Aspect Personal account Work or school account
Minimum age 13 or over, local age takes precedence 18 or over for a work account
Use for model training Possible depending on your settings Not used outside your domain without permission
Human review A subset of chats is reviewed Not reviewed without permission
Who switches it on or off You Your organisation's administrator
Who sets retention You choose the auto-delete period The administrator sets the history policy
Additional controls None Existing data-loss-prevention and rights controls apply

There is one easy way to misread that table. It does not say a work account is safe; it says that on a work account the rule-maker is your employer. Administrators can enable or disable features per service, set how long conversation history is kept, and govern which internal data is reachable. The final authority on whether company material may be pasted is therefore your internal policy and your administrator's configuration, not the vendor's document. If your organisation has no policy, that is not permission — it is an unanswered question.

Request patterns that actually pay off

Changing the request changes the result. But three plain moves do far more work than any magic phrasing: move the context inside the conversation, fix the format up front, and ask for one thing at a time. Five worked examples show the difference faster than an explanation.

Request that underperforms Reworked request What changed
Summarise this article Summarise the text below into five lines for a team announcement. Each line under 90 characters, carry numbers over exactly as written (text pasted) Material, purpose, format and constraint supplied
Write me some marketing copy Landing-page headline for a lunchbox subscription aimed at office workers in their thirties and forties. Three options under 40 characters, each with one line naming the angle it plays Audience, product, placement, count, rationale
Fix this code The function below fails on an empty list. Here is the error message. Explain the cause only, and hold the fix until I confirm Symptom and evidence given, steps separated
Write the report Step one, the outline only. Once I approve it, step two is section 2 alone. Tone alignment comes last A long job broken into stages
Is this true? Find primary sources for this claim and attach them as links. Mark any item you could not find as not found Permission to leave a blank empty
  • Put the context inside, not in front. Do not describe the document, paste it. At that moment a fact out in the world becomes your material, and right-column items from the earlier table move to the left.
  • Fix the format first. How many lines, which table columns, in what order. Without a format the output only gets longer. Length limits are especially well obeyed.
  • Ask for one thing at a time. Demand outline, body and tone in one go and the front gets served while the back goes vague. Staging also lets you change direction midway.
  • Constraints beat role-play. Under 90 characters, numbers carried over exactly beats you are an expert. The more checkable the constraint, the better it is followed.
  • Say explicitly that not knowing is allowed. The tendency is to fill blanks, so blanks stay empty only when you permit them. This one sentence sharply reduces invented sources.
  • When it is wrong, say what is wrong rather than try again. Try again reproduces the same mistake in different words. Point at the line and the error and only that part changes.

To work on request design more systematically, the fundamentals of writing prompts is the better read. What is here was picked for showing results within the first few days.

What to confirm before you trust an answer

You cannot verify everything, and you do not need to. The places where things actually go wrong are narrow and repetitive. Numbers, dates, quotations. Cross-checking those three against the source catches most of it.

  • Check numbers with their units and basis. The value is often right while the unit is wrong, the base year differs, or the population is not the same. Anything that involved arithmetic gets redone in a calculator.
  • Ask what date the answer is based on. For rules, rates and prices that get amended, the effective date is the whole story. Describing a superseded version in the present tense is the classic failure mode.
  • Check quotations in three steps. Does the document exist, does the link open, is that sentence actually in it. The third step catches more than you would expect.
  • Confirm that proper nouns exist at all. People, companies, products and clause numbers are the easiest shapes to fabricate convincingly.
  • Be suspicious when you specified a count. Ask for five and you will get five, padded with items that do not exist. Asking for however many actually exist is safer.

Some domains carry more risk than others: medicine, law, tax, investment and safety. In these five a plausible answer is more dangerous than an obviously wrong one, and the cost of being wrong far exceeds the cost of checking. Here the chatbot answer should be used as a table of contents for what to look up, never as the conclusion, with the original text and a qualified professional closing the question.

One last test worth keeping. If verifying takes longer than doing it yourself, the task should not have been delegated in the first place. Once that calculation is available to you, you can decide for yourself what to hand over, and you stop needing anyone else's list of use cases.

What this article does not settle

The following are deliberately not stated as fact here. They vary by date, region and account, or are only fixed on the official page and the checkout screen.

  • Model names and per-version performance are not settled here. Line-ups shift and the same name gets updated underneath. For that axis, the Gemini model line-up piece and the AI model comparison hub are the entries that get refreshed.
  • Plan names, prices and usage limits are not settled here. Currency display and tax handling are only confirmed at checkout.
  • The list of supported countries and languages is not settled here. Availability lands at different times by region and splits by account type within the same country. Google's supported languages and countries page is the authority.
  • Where any given feature sits on screen is not settled here. Web and mobile differ and redesigns are frequent. That is why the text above says only in settings.
  • Which features are open in any particular country is not settled here. Features arrive on separate schedules, so a single snapshot goes stale quickly.

Frequently asked questions

Q1. How old do you have to be to use Gemini?

Personal and school accounts start at 13, work accounts at 18. Where a country defines a different age, that one governs. Under-13s are controlled by a parent through Family Link, and Gemini Apps are not available for supervised accounts in the EEA, Switzerland and the UK. In those regions a Google AI plan requires 18 or over. Figures reflect August 2026.

Q2. Can I paste company documents or personal data?

It depends on the account. On a personal account a subset of chats can be read by human reviewers, and Google says not to enter confidential information you would not want a reviewer to see. For work and school accounts, Google states Workspace content is not used for training outside your domain or human reviewed without permission. The final authority is still your internal policy and administrator settings.

Q3. If I turn chat history off, is nothing saved?

No. With it off, future chats stay out of your activity and out of training but are retained for 72 hours. With it on, activity auto-deletes after 18 months by default and the period is adjustable. And chats already read by human reviewers are kept for up to three years and are not removed when you delete your activity. Cleaning up later is not a defence.

Q4. Search and the chatbot disagree. Which is right?

They do different jobs. Search finds documents that exist, so the link is the source; a chatbot computes the next words, so an answer appears with or without a source behind it. When a fact is at stake, the evidence is the source link, not the sentence. Use the answer to decide what to look up and close the question in the original.

Q5. How do I check whether an answer is correct?

Cross-check numbers, dates and quotations — that is the highest-yield move. Numbers down to units and base year, dates down to effective and amended dates, quotations down to whether the sentence is really in the document. The double-check feature helps, but Google's own page says it can be wrong, so use it as a first filter only.

Closing

The first few days need very little. Which account you are signed into, whether this is the kind of task where you would spot a wrong answer, whether this text is safe to paste, and which numbers, dates and quotations in this answer need cross-checking. Spend thirty seconds on those four each time and the rest is safe to learn by clicking around.

Two things are worth doing in the first week. One is opening the activity setting once and deciding retention deliberately rather than by default. The other is the habit of pasting material instead of describing it. The first reduces what you regret later; the second does more for accuracy than anything else. Long-run regret with these tools almost always traces back to something that should not have been pasted, or something copied out without being checked.
