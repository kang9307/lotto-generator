Vacuum Cleaner Buying Guide: Cordless vs Corded, Suction Power, and Mop Combos

From a type comparison table through AW/Pa suction ratings, HEPA filters, auto-empty stations, and pet hair pickup - everything to check before you buy

Jul 24, 2026 Life & Practical

Introduction: What to Check Before the Spec Sheet Talks You Into Anything

Vacuum cleaner listings are full of unfamiliar terms - AW, Pa, HEPA grade, auto-empty station. Even within cordless stick vacuums alone, every brand labels things differently, and once you add mop-combo robot vacuums and bed vacuums to the mix, the decision gets a lot more complicated. A vacuum is an appliance you use daily, and it also comes with ongoing consumable costs - batteries, filters, dust bags - so if you don't set a clear standard before buying, you can easily end up with something that gathers dust in a closet.

This guide, current as of July 2026, walks through what to check in order: a type comparison table for cordless stick, corded canister, mop-combo, and bed vacuums; what the suction-power units (AW, Pa) actually mean; the battery, dust bin, HEPA filter, and auto-empty station considerations specific to cordless sticks; heads for different floors and situations; filter and dust-bin maintenance and allergy considerations; pet hair pickup; weight, noise, and storage; and finally, selection criteria and a checklist by housing type and lifestyle. It doesn't recommend any specific brand or model - the goal is a set of standards you can apply to any product you're considering.

1. A Full Type Comparison - Cordless Stick, Corded Canister, Mop-Combo, and Bed Vacuums

1.1 What Sets Each Type Apart

Cordless stick vacuums run on a battery, so there's no cord to manage, they're easy to move around, and they store upright on a dock. The trade-off is limited battery capacity, which caps continuous run time - especially on the highest power setting, where real-world run time drops sharply. Corded canister vacuums are the traditional design, with the body connected to the head by a cord; as long as it's plugged in, suction stays consistent with no time limit, which is their biggest strength. The downside is the hassle of plugging and unplugging as you move room to room, and the body's weight takes up more storage space.

Mop-combo models handle both suction and mopping in a single device - either a robot vacuum that mops on its own, or a cordless stick model with a vibrating mop pad activated at the press of a button. The convenience is real, but it comes with extra upkeep (washing and drying the mop pad), and suction is often rated lower than on suction-only models. Bed vacuums are a separate category built specifically for mattresses and bedding that a regular head can't reach properly, focusing strong suction and a vibrating "tapping" action on pulling up dust and dust-mite debris.

2. Understanding Suction Power - AW, Pa, W, and Real Cleaning Performance

2.1 What Each Unit Actually Shows

Vacuum spec sheets mix W (watts), AW (air watts), and Pa (pascals). Watts is just how much power the motor draws - it has no direct relationship to cleaning ability. AW reflects both airflow and suction pressure together, making it the metric that comes closest to real-world cleaning performance. Large corded vacuums typically fall in the 100-300 AW range, while cordless sticks run roughly 50-150 AW, and cordless models under about 150 AW are sometimes noted to struggle on carpet. Pa measures suction pressure alone - how hard the unit pulls - without accounting for airflow volume. Cordless vacuums are commonly rated somewhere around 10,000-28,000 Pa.

2.2 Only Comparable Under the Same Conditions

The catch is that manufacturers use different test conditions and labeling units, so numbers expressed in different units aren't directly comparable. A high Pa number alone doesn't guarantee better real-world pickup - airflow has to back it up for dust and debris on the floor to actually travel into the head. So compare on an AW basis where possible, and if that's not available, only compare figures measured under the same test conditions. Worth noting: at a similar price point, suction-only robot vacuums are often rated with roughly 5-20% higher suction than mop-combo robot vacuums - a useful data point if you're weighing a combo unit.

2.3 The Motor/Battery Trade-Off With Run Time

On a cordless stick vacuum, battery drain accelerates sharply as suction increases. Even a model rated for 30-plus minutes on standard mode commonly sees its drain rate jump 2-3x on max power, cutting real-world run time down to roughly 10-15 minutes. In other words, the "maximum run time" on a spec sheet is usually based on the lowest power setting, so you need to factor in how often you'd actually run max power when estimating real usable time. Lithium-ion batteries are generally known to start degrading after around 400-500 charge cycles, roughly 2-3 years in - worth keeping in mind if you plan to keep the vacuum a long time.

3. Key Checkpoints for Cordless Stick Vacuums

3.1 Battery - Removable vs Built-In

With a removable battery, once capacity fades you simply buy a new battery pack and swap it in, which keeps long-term upkeep simple. A built-in (non-removable) battery often means sending the unit to a service center just to replace it, and repair costs can add up once the warranty expires. If you plan to keep the vacuum for years, check ahead of time whether it supports a removable battery and what a replacement costs.

3.2 Charging Dock and Dust Bin Capacity

A wall-mounted charging dock is convenient - just set the vacuum in place after use and it charges automatically, without taking up separate storage space. A smaller dust bin needs emptying more often, so households with pets or ones that clean a large area in a single pass will find a more generous bin capacity more convenient.

3.3 HEPA Filter

The HEPA filter is the last thing air passes through before being exhausted back into the room. A lower grade means some fine dust can be released back indoors, so households with allergies especially need to pay attention to this rating. The grade differences are covered in more detail in the next section.

3.4 Auto-Empty Dust Station

This feature automatically transfers the contents of the dust bin into a bag inside the docking station whenever you set the vacuum down - no more opening and emptying the bin by hand. More importantly, because dust moves straight into a sealed bag instead of becoming airborne, it's hygienically better for users with allergies. A single station can also handle several cleaning sessions' worth of dust before it needs emptying, which is another convenience. That said, models with a station typically cost about $75-150 (roughly 100,000-200,000 KRW) more, plus an additional $15-25 (roughly 20,000-30,000 KRW) a year for replacement bags. Leaving a bag unchanged for too long can also invite pests like dust mites, so it needs to be swapped on a regular schedule.

4. Choosing Heads for Different Floors and Situations

Even the same vacuum performs very differently depending on which head you attach.

4.1 Carpet - Motorized (Power) Brush Head

Since dust gets embedded deep in carpet and rug fibers, a motorized (power) brush head, where a rotating brush inside the head lifts dust out, is the better choice. Suction alone struggles to pull dust out of deep carpet pile - the brush's rotation is what actually gets the job done.

4.2 Hardwood - Soft Roller Head

For hardwood or tile floors, a soft head with a soft roller or velvet-like material is the better fit. A stiff brush risks scratching hardwood, while a soft head is better at hugging the floor to lift fine dust and hair.

4.3 Crevices - Crevice Tool

Tight spots like the gap between furniture, sofa cushions, or window tracks call for a narrow crevice tool. Most vacuums include one as a standard accessory, so it's worth checking the included accessory list before buying.

4.4 Cars - Mini Motorized Brush

Car seats and mats have a lot of tight, curved surfaces, so a small, lightweight mini motorized brush head is handy. Cordless stick vacuums, thanks to their battery design, are easy to bring out to the car, and a smaller head makes it easier to reach into seat gaps or under floor mats.

5. Filter and Dust-Bin Maintenance, and Allergy Considerations

5.1 HEPA Grade and Sealed System Design

Filter grade isn't the only thing that matters for allergy management. An H13-grade HEPA filter is known to remove 99.95% or more of particles at 0.3 microns, making it effective against allergy triggers like pollen, household dust, and pet dander. But no matter how high the filter grade, if the seam between the body and filter isn't sealed and air can leak around the filter, real-world effectiveness drops significantly. In other words, don't just look for a "HEPA filter" label - checking whether the system is actually a sealed design matters more for allergy management.

5.2 Washing and Odor Control

The dust bin and pre-filter are usually designed to be washed with water, but reinstalling them before they're fully dry can lead to a musty smell and mold. Check the manual for the recommended washing interval and drying time (often 24 hours or more), and note that the HEPA filter itself often can't be washed - confirm washability before you buy.

5.3 Allergy Checklist

  • Is it rated H13 HEPA or higher?
  • Is the seal between body and filter actually sealed?
  • When you empty the bin, does dust avoid blowing back toward your face - or would an auto-empty station be a better fit?
  • Can you realistically keep up with the filter's washing interval and full-drying time?

6. Handling Pet Hair

If you live with pets, look at suction power and head design together. A brush spinning at 2,000+ RPM is reported to be effective at lifting hair embedded in carpets and rugs, and an anti-tangle comb built into the head automatically frees hair wrapped around the brush roll during cleaning, cutting down on how often you have to pull hair out by hand. A rubber-bristle brush roll also tends to have less hair wrap-around than a bristle brush. In a pet household, it's more sensible to prioritize suction and brush-head performance over the mopping feature, and since pet hair and dander scatter easily, an auto-empty station helps keep allergens from spreading when you'd otherwise open the bin by hand.

7. Weight, Noise, and Storage

Weight matters especially for cordless sticks. When you're cleaning overhead - ceilings, curtains, the top of cabinets - even a 1kg difference is noticeable, so it's worth picking one up in a store to check whether the weight distribution puts strain on your wrist. A corded canister's body is heavier, but since it stays on the floor and you just pull it along, the strain on your arm is comparatively lower. Noise varies a lot by mode - if you'll be vacuuming early morning or late at night in an apartment, check the decibel (dB) spec at the low-noise setting, not the number quoted for maximum output. For storage, cordless sticks take up little space since they stand upright or hang on a wall-mounted dock, while corded canisters and units with an auto-empty station need dedicated storage space - worth planning for ahead of time.

8. Selection Criteria by Housing Type and Lifestyle, Plus a Checklist

8.1 Choosing by Living Situation

If you live alone in a studio or small space, or move often, a light, easy-to-store cordless stick is frequently all you need. If your living room has carpets or rugs, or you do a deep clean often, a corded canister - or a high-power cordless stick paired with a dedicated carpet head - works better. For a smaller, hardwood-heavy home where you want cleaning automated, a mop-combo robot vacuum paired with an auto-empty station is worth considering. If someone in the household has allergies or rhinitis, prioritize an H13+ HEPA sealed-system filter; if you have pets, prioritize suction and an anti-tangle brush head over the mopping feature. If bedding hygiene is a particular concern, consider a bed vacuum separately, keeping in mind that its UV sterilization claims are limited in practice - suction power and filtration performance matter more.

8.2 Final Checklist Before You Buy

  • Have you identified your housing type and floor mix (hardwood vs carpet share)?
  • Have you compared suction on an AW basis where possible, or figures measured under the same conditions otherwise?
  • For a cordless model, have you checked real-world run time on max power and whether the battery is removable?
  • Have you confirmed the HEPA grade (H13+) and whether the system is sealed?
  • Can you absorb the added cost of an auto-empty station (unit price plus annual bag cost)?
  • Are the heads you need - carpet, hardwood, crevice, car - included as standard accessories?
  • If you have pets, have you checked for an anti-tangle brush design?
  • Have you checked weight and noise (dB) specs against how you'll actually use it (apartment living, overhead cleaning)?
  • Have you settled on storage space and where the charging dock will go?

9. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Should I choose a cordless or a corded vacuum?

It depends on your home size and cleaning habits. A cordless vacuum tends to suit small homes cleaned briefly every day, while a corded vacuum tends to suit larger homes with a lot of carpet that get a deep clean once a week. Cordless models have gotten much closer to corded-level suction recently, but battery limits still cap continuous run time.

Q2. When comparing suction, should I look at AW or Pa?

AW (air watts) reflects both airflow and suction pressure together, making it the metric closer to real cleaning performance. Pa reflects pressure alone, so a high Pa number alone doesn't guarantee better cleaning. Since manufacturers test under different conditions, only compare figures measured under the same conditions.

Q3. Can a mop-combo robot vacuum fully replace mopping?

To some extent, but with real limits. Effective mopping depends on downward pressure on the floor, and suction+mop combo units are often built in a way that can't apply as much pressure as a dedicated mop unit. For heavily set-in stains, a combo robot alone may not be enough, so hand-mopping the worst spots is the practical approach.

Q4. Does a HEPA filter really help with allergies?

An H13-grade or higher HEPA filter is known to remove 99.95%+ of 0.3-micron particles, making it effective against allergens like pollen, household dust, and pet dander. But if the seal between the body and filter isn't airtight and air leaks around it, effectiveness drops even with a high-grade filter - so check for a sealed design alongside the grade.

Q5. Does the UV sterilization feature on a bed vacuum actually work?

Don't overestimate it. Some tests have found that UV alone barely eliminated dust mites, and UV's sterilizing effect itself is described as limited. When choosing a bed vacuum, it's safer to prioritize suction and filtration performance (whether it releases collected dust and mites back out) over the UV feature.

Conclusion: The Combination Matters More Than Any Single Spec

A vacuum can't be chosen on a single suction number, or on the simple binary of cordless versus corded. You get a product you're happy with for years only by first picking a type that matches your real living conditions - housing type, floor mix, allergies, pets - and then checking suction units, battery, HEPA grade, head selection, weight, and noise one by one within that type. The surest way to apply all of that is to use the comparison table and checklist in this guide to work through an actual product's spec sheet, item by item.

Specific specs, pricing, and bundled accessories vary by brand and over time, so check the manufacturer's official materials and current reviews before you buy.
