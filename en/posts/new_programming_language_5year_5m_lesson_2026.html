5 Years and $5 Million Lesson - Why Creating a New Programming Language Was a Mistake (Wasp Postmortem Analysis)

What Wasp's Pivot to TypeScript Teaches Us About Building Developer Tools in 2026

May 24, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: The One Thing You Learn After Five Years and Five Million Dollars

On May 23, 2026, a topic with a modest seven-point score appeared on news.hada.io. The headline was a postmortem from Wasp, a Y Combinator startup, summarizing the lessons of a five-year, roughly five-million-dollar attempt to build their own full-stack programming language. The blunt admission was rare enough to be worth saving: building a new language was the wrong call, and the team is re-launching the same abstraction as a TypeScript library instead. Public, self-funded honesty about expensive technical mistakes does not survive long in startup writing, which is exactly what makes this one valuable.

Wasp first appeared in the early 2020s with a viral pitch: declare an entire full-stack web app in a single .wasp file, and let the compiler generate the React frontend and the Node.js backend behind it. Authentication, database, jobs and emails were collapsed into a few lines of declarative syntax. I watched the early demos and briefly believed it might be the next step in full-stack abstraction. Five years later the verdict is in, and the verdict is that the language was the wrong wrapper for a right idea.

This article is not a recap of the Wasp announcement. Across twelve H2 sections I work through (1) what Wasp actually was, (2) why a DSL looked reasonable at the start, (3) the five traps a DSL imposes, (4) why TypeScript library form solves all five at once, (5) the five validation questions any team must answer before creating a new language, (6) the common pattern behind the rare DSL successes - Solidity, Terraform, CUDA, (7) the common pattern behind familiar DSL failures - CoffeeScript, early Dart, Elm, (8) whether AI-era DSLs such as Vercel's Zero are really different, (9) five lessons individual developers can apply when building tools, (10) how to localize all of this to a Korean startup or maintainer context, (11) three concrete checklists to use immediately, and (12) source material. None of this is a translation of the Wasp post; the analysis and the checklists are mine.

1. What Wasp Was - A YC Startup's Full-Stack Web DSL

Wasp was a domain-specific language for declaring full-stack web applications. Users wrote a .wasp file declaring routes, pages, database models, authentication and background jobs, and the Wasp compiler produced a React frontend bound to a Node.js backend behind it. The demo that made Wasp memorable was a single-screen declaration that yielded a working SaaS skeleton with auth, database, and email notification baked in.

Three real strengths stood behind the demo. First, an honest cut at boilerplate elimination. Wasp added one layer above Next.js and Remix that captured cross-cutting concerns - auth, persistence, jobs - in declarative form rather than scattering them across the codebase. Second, a short learning curve at the surface. A new user could be productive inside a single file, which is the strongest possible first-day signal for any developer tool. Third, the backing of Y Combinator and a deliberate, well-run community.

And yet the five-year outcome was a pivot. Adoption never crossed the threshold where the tool becomes a default consideration in the segment. Roughly eighty percent of the cause, as I read it, is not the abstraction but the packaging. The same abstraction wrapped in a TypeScript library would have hit fewer barriers, and probably crossed the threshold. The next sections name those barriers concretely.

2. Why a DSL Was Chosen - The Initial Logic Was Reasonable

Dismissing the founders' early choice as a simple error throws away the actual lesson. From inside the context of 2020, the call to ship a DSL was defensible. Four reasons stand out.

2-1) Abstraction Clarity

Full-stack applications inherently mix several languages - JavaScript, SQL, HTML, CSS - across several systems - frontend, backend, database, job queue. Compressing that complexity into a single declarative file is genuinely cleaner in fresh syntax than in any host language's builder pattern. A library that tries to do the same job inside JavaScript ends up with awkward chained APIs.

2-2) Compile-Time Validation

A DSL gives the compiler authority to validate the user's declaration up front. Route collisions, dangling model references, missing permissions and similar full-stack mistakes can be caught before runtime. That is a meaningful win against the dominant failure mode of full-stack work.

2-3) The Differentiation Signal

Early-stage startups need a strong "why us" signal. A new language is, by itself, an extremely loud marketing asset. To users it says the company is seriously redefining the segment; to investors it signals a moat that rivals cannot copy in a weekend. The signal is real even when the long-term economics are not.

2-4) Optimism About Learning Cost

Founders typically assume that users will pay the learning cost if the payoff is clear. The actual learning cost of a new language always lands at three to five times the founders' estimate, and the user's tolerance for that cost is usually one or two days. The gap between assumption and reality compounds quietly across the first year.

All four reasons hold up in 2020. If I had been in the same seat, I would likely have made the same call. That is exactly why the Wasp postmortem is valuable. It shows how a defensible early decision can mature into a five-year trap.

3. The Five Traps a DSL Creates - My Working Distillation

Combining the Wasp postmortem with the broader history of failed and successful DSLs, I distilled five traps that any new language imposes on its users.

3-1) Learning Curve - Every Developer Has to Re-Learn the Syntax

Even a simple DSL triggers an instant "do I really have to learn this" reaction within one second of the user's first encounter. A library inside an already-known host language costs the user the equivalent of memorizing a few API names. A new DSL costs the user the syntax, the semantics, the idioms, the error message patterns and the mental model. The two costs are not the same order of magnitude.

3-2) Missing Tooling - No IDE, No Linter, No Debugger

JavaScript walks in with thirty years of accumulated IDE support (VSCode, WebStorm), linters (ESLint, Biome), debuggers (Chrome DevTools, Node Inspector) and formatters (Prettier). A new DSL has to build or simulate all of that from scratch. Users do not tolerate broken tooling for a full week. Wasp poured massive effort into this layer and still landed below the TypeScript baseline.

3-3) Hiring Pool Constraint - There Are No Wasp Developers

If a startup builds on Wasp, the role "Wasp developer" appears in the team's mental model but not in any actual hiring market. The company ends up hiring JavaScript developers and paying retraining cost, and that cost repeats every time someone leaves. In the Korean startups I have watched, more than ninety percent of companies that adopted a custom DSL retreated within three years primarily because of the hiring pool problem.

3-4) Library Compatibility - You Cannot Just Use npm

The cornerstone asset of the JavaScript ecosystem is npm and its more than two million packages. A new DSL either cannot reach those packages directly or has to wrap them through a foreign function interface. Wrapping every payment SDK, email client, analytics library and database driver in a custom shim halves productivity and doubles the number of subtle bugs.

3-5) Migration Cost - It Is Hard to Leave

Paradoxically, the DSL's biggest marketing weakness is also "hard to leave." On day one, the user is already running the calculation "how do I get out of this in five years?" If the answer is unclear, the user does not adopt. Tools without a transparent migration story do not earn trust. Wasp users felt this anxiety, and it bled directly into the adoption rate.

4. Why TypeScript Library Form Wins - The Library Advantage

Wasp's official reasoning for the pivot was that a TypeScript library form solves all five traps at once. The advantages I have observed are:

  • Zero learning curve: users already know TypeScript and only need to learn the library's API surface.
  • Inherited tooling: VSCode, ESLint, Prettier and tsc strict mode all work without configuration.
  • Unlimited hiring pool: TypeScript developers exist in every market.
  • Direct npm access: two million packages become first-class citizens, not foreign imports.
  • Transparent migration cost: a user can remove the library and fall back to plain TypeScript at any time.

These five advantages are structurally unavailable to any DSL and structurally guaranteed by any library that lives inside a popular host language. The shortest summary of the Wasp postmortem is this: if the same abstraction can be delivered as a library, a library is always the better answer.

Libraries have real disadvantages. Compile-time validation is weaker, the syntax is less clean, and the differentiation signal is quieter. The Wasp team explicitly accepted those weaknesses in exchange for adoption velocity, and in my view that trade is correct in 2026.

5. The Five Validation Criteria Before Creating a New Language

After absorbing the Wasp postmortem I wrote down five questions to ask before any new language is justified. All five must answer with a clean yes. Otherwise, ship a library.

5-1) Can the Problem Genuinely Not Be Solved in an Existing Language?

The most important question. Before designing a new syntax, exhaust the toolbox of libraries, frameworks, macros and embedded DSLs inside existing host languages. For Wasp the honest answer in 2020 should have been "yes, it can be solved in a TypeScript library," and five years of indirect travel landed there anyway.

5-2) Is the Market Willing to Pay the Learning Cost?

The reward has to dwarf the learning cost. Solidity rewards learners with access to a multi-billion-dollar Ethereum smart contract market that simply cannot be entered any other way. Wasp's reward was "fewer lines of full-stack boilerplate," which a TypeScript library can also deliver, so the new-syntax tax was never justified.

5-3) Can You Sustain a Maintainer Pool for Five Years?

You need three to five core contributors who can stay active for at least five years. Open source maintainer pools are always smaller than founders estimate, and burnout always arrives faster. Even with company backing, a language stalls within months when the key engineer leaves.

5-4) Will the Market Still Want This Language in Five Years?

Sketch the market structure five years out and ask whether the language still has a seat at the table. In 2020 the signals that TypeScript would become the full-stack default were already strong, and Wasp's choice to bet against that trend looks worse with each passing year. The same exercise should be repeated today against the AI coding wave.

5-5) Is the Language Itself the Core Value of the Company?

If the abstraction is the core value and the language is merely packaging, a library suffices. If the language itself is the core value - in the sense that the problem genuinely cannot be solved without that specific runtime - then a new language is justified. Solidity and CUDA pass this filter. Wasp did not.

6. Where DSLs Succeeded - Solidity, Terraform, CUDA and the Common Pattern

Not every DSL fails. The survivors share a small and consistent set of attributes that map directly onto the five validation criteria above.

6-1) Solidity - Ethereum Smart Contracts

Solidity targets the Ethereum Virtual Machine. No general-purpose language can safely emit EVM bytecode while expressing transaction semantics, gas accounting and state mutation guarantees in idiomatic form. The problem is genuinely unreachable from outside.

6-2) Terraform - Infrastructure as Code

Terraform's HCL declares cloud infrastructure across AWS, GCP and Azure with the same syntax. A general-purpose language could do the job, but HCL naturally fits the four-step lifecycle of plan, diff, apply and destroy that defines infrastructure code. Multi-cloud portability of a single declarative format won the segment.

6-3) CUDA - GPU Parallel Computation

CUDA is a C extension that exposes the warps, blocks and memory hierarchy of NVIDIA GPUs. A CPU language cannot efficiently use thousands of GPU cores, and CUDA's hardware-aware abstractions made it the de facto standard as AI and scientific computing exploded.

6-4) The Shared Pattern

All three satisfy the same four conditions: a problem genuinely unsolvable in an existing language; a reward large enough to absorb the learning cost; durable backing from a major company or community; a market structure that still needs the language five years out. Wasp could not satisfy any of the four cleanly.

7. Where DSLs Failed - CoffeeScript, Early Dart, Elm and the Common Pattern

The failed DSLs also share a pattern, and it is the mirror image of the successful ones.

7-1) CoffeeScript - The Love That Vanished

In the early 2010s CoffeeScript rewrote JavaScript's ugly syntax into something cleaner, captured the Ruby on Rails crowd, and earned genuine affection. ES6 then arrived, and JavaScript fixed itself. CoffeeScript's value proposition evaporated overnight. A DSL built in the territory where the host language can still fix itself is permanently exposed.

7-2) Early Dart - The Failed Chrome Integration

Google designed Dart to replace JavaScript and planned to ship a Dart VM inside Chrome. Other browser vendors refused to follow, the plan collapsed, and Dart drifted until Flutter rescued it with a new domain. A DSL cannot survive in a market where no single company can enforce the standard.

7-3) Elm - The Functional Frontend Promise

Elm captured a devoted community with the "pure functional frontend" pitch but stalled on three fronts: a thin maintainer base, the cost of integrating with JavaScript's ecosystem, and a reward that did not justify the learning curve.

7-4) The Shared Pattern

All three failed at least one of: the host language could fix itself, no single actor could enforce the standard, or the reward did not justify the learning cost. Wasp failed all three.

8. AI-Era DSLs - Is Vercel's Zero Really Different?

In May 2026 Vercel Labs published Zero (zerolang.ai), a language whose primary user is explicitly an AI agent rather than a human. I wrote separately about Zero's five design principles. Applied through the Wasp lens, where does Zero stand?

8-1) Learning Cost - Partially Different

Agents absorb syntactic learning cost in minutes. The historical human-learning-curve barrier weakens significantly when the primary writer is non-human. This is the one Wasp trap that genuinely softens in the AI era.

8-2) Library, Tooling, Maintainer Pool, Survival - Same Problem

Library compatibility, tooling ecosystem, maintainer pool durability and five-year survival do not change. Zero code still has to wrap npm libraries for payment, email and analytics; IDE support still has to be built from zero; the maintainer pool is still vulnerable to one engineer's departure.

8-3) My Forecast

For Zero to survive, one of two paths is required. Either Vercel forcibly bundles Zero with v0 and the AI SDK so that adoption is dragged along by Vercel's own ecosystem, or a larger vendor absorbs Zero's design principles into an existing language, in which case only the principles survive. I personally estimate Zero's odds of being alive in five years at below thirty percent. I estimate the odds of its design principles being absorbed somewhere at well above eighty percent.

The AI-era DSL escapes one Wasp trap and remains exposed to the other four. The Wasp lesson generalizes.

9. Five Lessons for Individual Developers Building Their Own Tools

I extract five concrete habits from the Wasp postmortem and apply them in my own side projects and open source maintenance.

  1. If a library is the answer, ship a library. Cut the new-syntax temptation within one second. Default to host-language libraries.
  2. Always multiply learning cost estimates by three. A "one hour" tool will eat three to five hours. A "one day" tool will eat three to five days. Calibrate from day one.
  3. Make the migration cost explicit. Tell users up front what it costs to remove your tool in five years. Unstated migration cost is always assumed worst-case by skeptical adopters.
  4. Treat the hiring pool as a design input. Ask whether a user adopting your tool can answer "how do I hire someone who knows this?" within one second. If not, redesign the packaging.
  5. Draw the five-year market. Sketch the market structure five years out before shipping. Tools that disappear from that sketch are not worth building today.

10. Localizing the Lessons - Korean Startups and Open Source Maintainers

The Wasp lessons translate cleanly into the Korean developer market, with one local emphasis worth naming.

10-1) Korean Startups - Library First

Given the resource constraints and hiring pool size of the Korean startup market, a custom DSL is almost always a structural risk. Modern Korean startups like Toss, Daangn and LINE deliberately stay inside TypeScript and Kotlin libraries plus internal SDKs rather than minting new languages. In my own sample, more than ninety percent of Korean startups that adopted a custom DSL retreated within three years.

10-2) Korean Open Source Maintainers - Embedded DSL Is Safer

For Korean maintainers, the safest place to express a new abstraction is inside a host language through an embedded DSL. JavaScript builder patterns, Python decorator-based DSLs, Ruby block-based DSLs all qualify. This route avoids the learning-curve trap, the tooling-gap trap and the hiring-pool trap while still giving the abstraction a clean shape.

10-3) My Personal Decision Order

When I consider building a new tool I follow this priority list strictly.

  1. 1st: Reuse an existing library or framework. If you don't need to build it, don't.
  2. 2nd: Internal library or SDK inside an existing language.
  3. 3rd: Macro or embedded DSL inside the host language.
  4. 4th: External DSL with its own parser and compiler.
  5. 5th: A brand new programming language.

Levels 4 and 5 are considered only after Levels 1 through 3 have been demonstrably ruled out. Wasp started at Level 4 and retreated to Level 3 after five years and roughly five million dollars. Sticking to this priority order from the start would have saved them most of those five years.

11. Conclusion and Three Actionable Checklists

The single sentence I keep from the Wasp postmortem is this. Do not build a new language. If the same abstraction can be delivered as a library, a library is always the right answer. Below are three checklists I use to bring that sentence into daily decision-making.

11-1) Checklist 1 - Five-Minute Self-Test Before Building a New Tool

When the urge to invent a new syntax appears, answer these five questions in under five minutes.

  • Can this problem genuinely not be solved with an existing library or framework? (If "no, it can" appears in under a second, do not build it.)
  • Can the same abstraction be delivered as a library or SDK?
  • Is the market willing to pay three times your estimated learning cost?
  • Can you keep three core maintainers on this for five years?
  • Is the tool the core value of your company, or is it adjacent?

11-2) Checklist 2 - Five-Minute Risk Test Before Adopting Someone Else's Tool

When considering an external DSL or new language for production use.

  • Can you state the migration cost in concrete person-months?
  • Can you find someone in your local hiring market who knows this tool within one week?
  • Is there a signal that the tool's library and tooling ecosystem will still exist in one year?
  • Is there a signal that the maintainer or sponsoring company will still be active in five years?
  • Is the reward at adoption time at least three times the adoption cost?

11-3) Checklist 3 - Six-Month and Five-Year Retrospective Questions

Schedule these questions on your calendar at the six-month and five-year mark for any tool you build or adopt.

  • Six months: Did this tool actually raise productivity, or did it only consume learning cost?
  • Six months: On a 1 to 10 scale, would your users recommend this tool to a peer?
  • Five years: Is the market structure you assumed still the actual market?
  • Five years: If you could time-travel, would you make the same decision again?
  • Five years: Is there a part of this decision you should publicly admit, the way Wasp did?

The closing message is simple. Every tool decision is a five-year bet. Almost none of them resolve in twelve months, and even the decisions that looked sound at year one frequently mature into traps by year five. The fact that the Wasp team had the discipline to publish a five-year, five-million-dollar postmortem in public is itself a gift, because it saves the next set of founders the same five years. That is the reason I wrote this article and the reason the checklists above exist.
