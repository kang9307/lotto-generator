At the time it was annoyance; written down it becomes material

You have probably tried to cancel a subscription and found yourself six screens deep. Signing up was one button. Cancelling ran through a survey, two declined discount offers, and only then the final confirmation. In the moment it is just annoyance. A few days later it is forgotten. Because it is forgotten it is recorded nowhere, and because it is recorded nowhere the next person meets it as though it were new.

Over the past few years there has been a visible push to break that loop - to treat the annoyance not as personal bad luck but as the output of a design, and to gather scattered experience in one place. One such effort is an open wiki collecting anti-consumer practices. It can be edited without an account, and as of August 2026 the site displays 1,428 articles and 174 active contributors. What can safely be said here stops at the fact that such collecting efforts exist. Whether the individual entries are accurate is an entirely separate question, and half of this article is about that separate question.

So this is not about which company did what. It is about the form of the record itself: what can be grouped into patterns, where inconvenience parts ways with deception, what missing piece drops a record back down to a complaint, and who pays when the record is wrong.

This article is general information and not legal advice. Whether a given practice breaches any rule depends on the facts, and that assessment belongs to the competent authority and to qualified professionals.

The design patterns that put consumers at a disadvantage

Grouping into patterns rather than listing incidents shows two things: the same structures repeat regardless of industry, and those structures operate on different layers. A problem of screen layout, a problem of notice timing, and a problem of exit cost look alike but call for different responses.

PatternHow it appears on screenWhat the consumer loses
Asymmetric exit pathSignup on one screen; cancellation across several, or via another channelTime and resolve. Stop midway and the contract stands
Default settingsAdd-ons or data-use options arrive already selectedBeing treated as having chosen what was never chosen
When the price appearsPart of the amount up front; the total only just before paymentThe chance to compare, after all details are entered
Visual hierarchyAccept and decline buttons differ markedly in size, shape, colourAttention. Move fast and you press the other one
Repeat promptingAn already declined choice raised again by pop-up, and againPatience. Saying yes becomes the way out of the screen
Renewal noticeWeak notice at the moment of paid conversion or a price risePredictability. You learn from the statement
Export limitsNo format in which to take accumulated records awayFreedom to move. The cost of leaving is locked inside

None of this is newly discovered. A public catalogue has been organising the same material for years and lists seventeen patterns, including fake scarcity, fake social proof, disguised ads and comparison prevention, which the table above leaves out. What matters is that the naming work is largely done. What is needed now is not another label but a line drawn between the things already labelled.

Inconvenient design and deceptive design are not the same

The phrase dark pattern has spread very wide in a few years, and it has blunted as it spread. A slow page, a deep menu, a small-print agreement all get called one. The phrase then trends while judgment becomes impossible.

There is a signal worth noting. The catalogue site that did most to spread the term renamed itself. Its address is now deceptive.design, and a line near the foot of the page records that it was formerly darkpatterns.org. Dark is an appraisal: it tints the target morally without saying what the problem is. Deceptive names the character of the act, which makes it something that can be checked.

Put simply, inconvenience is an outcome and deception is a method. Many steps, slow responses, things that are hard to find - all inconvenience. That may mean the design is clumsy or was deprioritised; it does not mean anyone was misled. Deception arises where what the screen shows and what actually happens come apart.

Losing this distinction costs in both directions. One loss is that real deception gets buried: once everything is a dark pattern the list grows longer and lighter. The other runs the opposite way. Once every interface grievance is elevated to a moral claim, the claim becomes unfalsifiable. An unfalsifiable claim looks strong, but it cannot be tested either, and so it changes nothing.

Where the line falls - four questions

There are not many useful questions here. Four are where things actually part.

QuestionCloser to inconvenienceCloser to deception
Is the choice visible on screen?Visible, but tedious to findNot shown, or shown as something else
Does the result match the display?It matches; understanding it takes effortIt differs in amount, term, or cancellation status
Is it reversible, and was the cost disclosed?Reversible, with conditions given in advanceIrreversible, or the terms surface only afterwards
Does the asymmetry run one way?Both sides carry some frictionEvery mistake lands in the same party's favour

The four do not weigh the same. The second is decisive. A mismatch between display and result is a question of fact, not taste, so placing the screen beside the statement ends the inquiry. The fourth is weak on its own: designs that favour the seller are countless, and that alone is not deception.

The fourth question earns its keep in combination. Where two or more of the first three are triggered and the direction of error also runs one way, coincidence becomes a hard explanation to sustain. Grounding a judgment in the direction across several screens rather than in a single screen is far sturdier.

What should count as evidence

Three things have to survive for a record to hold: when, what state something was in, and whether another person can walk the same route. Lose one and there is no way to confirm it later.

  • Screen recording - an unbroken capture from start to finish. The timestamp written into the file matters as much as the footage
  • Versions of terms and policies - since the point is which sentence changed and when, the before and after versions only mean something together
  • Payments and statements - the amount and the billing moment; the most direct material when display and result diverge
  • Notices received - proving something never arrived is hard. Rather than contest an absence, keep the wording and arrival time of the notices that did come
  • Path description - an ordered sentence naming where you started, what you pressed, and what came next

One sentence carries this section. Without verifiability it is not a record but a complaint. Cancelling is hard is an impression. This path runs six screens to completed cancellation, one of which hands off to another channel, checked 24 August 2026 is a record. The difference is not force but falsifiability. The second can be shown to be wrong, which is exactly why it carries weight when it is right.

Timestamps matter for one more reason. Interfaces change often, and the same service may run experiments serving different screens to different users on the same day. A record without a date and conditions ends with a single reply: we never used that screen.

The cost of a record that is wrong

Collective formats have a real strength. One person's experience is dismissed as chance; a hundred identical accounts read as structure. The same structure, though, brings its own weaknesses.

  • Verification arrives late - the edit is live immediately and the checking comes afterwards. In between, the entry is read and cited as written
  • Correction is asymmetric - the first entry costs one edit, while a correction only starts once the party with contrary evidence knows the record exists
  • Time is not pinned down - a practice already fixed sits there in the present tense, and the record keeps being cited after it stopped being true
  • The index persists - amend the page and the search cache and the reposts keep the old version. Fixing the source does not catch the spread

The subject is not the only one who pays. One visibly wrong entry does not lose trust alone; it drags the whole list down with it. Entries researched carefully and written accurately get folded into the same shrug. The reason a documentation project has to be fussy about accuracy is self-interest, not virtue.

The fixes are not technical; they are a matter of the entry form. Four fields cover most of it.

  • Make a checked-on date mandatory. An entry without one is incomplete
  • Attach a primary source link to each claim - a recording, a saved version of the terms, an official notice
  • Add a status field distinguishing current, resolved, and under review, so old records stop reading as present tense
  • Publish a correction route. If it is not visible where contrary evidence should go, correction is not practically possible

What the rules already cover

Documentation projects often start from an assumption worth checking: that this area is a regulatory blank. The table below is Korean, drawn from the current provisions on the national statute portal (in force 21 July 2026), and is offered as one worked example rather than as a global standard.

AreaBasis (Korean law)What the provision addresses
Online interface designAct on Consumer Protection in Electronic Commerce, Article 21-2Luring with only part of the total shown, consent pre-marked on an option, choices displayed in markedly different form, complicating cancellation or withdrawal without justifiable grounds, repeated change demands via pop-ups
Recurring charges and conversionsAmended e-commerce rules (in force 14 February 2025)Consent within 30 days before a price rise or a free-to-paid switch, plus notice of the conditions and method of cancellation
Labelling and advertisingAct on Fair Labelling and Advertising, Article 3(1)False or exaggerated, deceptive, unfairly comparative, and disparaging labelling or advertising
Withdrawal of offerE-commerce Act, Article 17Seven days as a rule. Where performance differs from the advertising or the contract, three months from delivery or 30 days from learning of it. Exceptions are also set out
Instalment defenceInstalment Transactions Act, Article 16Refusing instalment payment on stated grounds. In indirect instalment contracts, notice to the credit provider where the price meets a threshold set by decree

What can be said here stops at the existence of a regulatory area. Which screen falls under which clause, and whether justifiable grounds existed, belong to an assessment built on facts. That a provision exists and that a given case breaches it are entirely different propositions.

This is why writing unlawful into a record weakens it. The verdict is not the recorder's to give, and a sentence that borrows one hands the subject a handle for rebuttal. A practice is not automatically unlawful, and design can be bad without being unlawful. The value of a record is not in the label. It is in leaving facts that someone can later judge. The parallel problem of the product itself quietly shrinking is covered separately in shrinkflation and skimpflation.

What an individual can keep

No elaborate preparation is required. Almost all of the difference lies in whether it is kept in a form that is usable later.

  • Capture the pre-payment screen and the final charge at the same moment. Separated, they cannot be compared
  • Record the cancellation path in one continuous take. Screenshots taken along the way cannot show how many steps there were
  • Save terms and policies before they change. Retrieving an earlier version afterwards is mostly impossible for an individual
  • Do not delete notice emails or app alerts. The wording and the send time have to survive together
  • If a data download exists, take it before leaving. After an account closes there is usually no route back

Places to ask also exist. In Korea a single nationwide consumer counselling line operates under the number 1372, with online counselling at ccn.go.kr, linking consumer groups, the Korea Consumer Agency and local consumer centres. Article 60 of the Framework Act on Consumers establishes the Consumer Dispute Settlement Commission within that agency. For instalment purchases, Article 16 of the Instalment Transactions Act provides a right of defence, and the provision states that a written defence takes effect from the day it is sent.

The above goes only as far as noting that such channels exist. Which one handles a given matter, whether the requirements and deadlines are met, and what procedure applies are for the relevant body to advise and assess. Nothing here recommends or rules out taking a matter forward.

Step-by-step handling once a dispute has begun is covered in the consumer dispute resolution guide. This article is concerned with the stage before that - making sure there is something left to argue with.

Seen from the company side

Not every inconvenience is malice. Leave that sentence out and the record is half-built. Bad cancellation flows have explanations other than intent.

  • Legacy - signup on the new system, cancellation still on the old one. Merging them creates no new revenue, so it keeps slipping down the list
  • Verification and settlement - identity checks, settling the remainder of a term, unwinding bundled products genuinely add steps
  • Screens with no owner - a growth team owns the signup screen; the cancellation screen is nobody's result
  • Measurement bias - conversion appears on the dashboard daily and the quality of a cancellation never does. Only what is visible improves

None of that excuses the outcome. Intent is a mitigating circumstance, not a refutation of the result. But this section carries a practical implication for anyone keeping records. A record that asserts intent gets weaker. They built it that way on purpose is hard to prove and easy to deny. This path runs six screens, signup runs one needs no proving at all. A record is strongest when it holds to what was observed.

What could not be confirmed

The article and contributor counts for the wiki project are the figures the site itself displays. The accuracy of its entries, whether a review process exists, and how correction requests are handled were not confirmed. That is why this article vouches for none of it.

No authoritative survey measuring how widespread these designs are in any given market was confirmed here. Nor were statistics on the scale of enforcement since the February 2025 rules took effect. On whether the regime is working, this article says nothing.

The threshold the Instalment Transactions Act sets for indirect contracts is delegated to a decree, so the figure is not given here. Interpretation and application of the provisions are left out for the same reason. That is not an omission but outside the scope of this article.

Frequently asked questions

What separates deceptive design from a merely inconvenient screen?

Inconvenience is an outcome; deception is a method. Many steps, slow pages, buried menus - that is inconvenience, which says the design is clumsy rather than that anyone was misled. Deception begins where what the screen shows and what actually happens come apart: the first screen quotes one figure and the charge is another, or an option nobody selected carries forward as selected. Call every annoyance deception and real deception gets buried in the pile; and inconvenience varies from person to person, while a gap between display and outcome is a question of fact that can be checked.

What does a personal record need in order to be useful later?

A timestamp and a reproducible path. Interfaces change often and two people can be served different screens on the same day, so a record without a date cannot be confirmed afterwards. The reproducible path is the ordered account of where you started, what you pressed, and what appeared next. In practice the single largest improvement is screen recording: when the number of steps to cancel is the point in dispute, a handful of screenshots cannot establish how many steps there were.

What goes wrong when a collectively built record is inaccurate?

Correction costs far more than the original entry. In an openly editable format the first entry takes one edit, while a correction only begins once the party holding contrary evidence learns the record exists. A timing problem compounds this: a practice already fixed keeps sitting in the present tense, so the record continues to be cited while no longer being true, and even after the page is amended the search cache keeps the old version alive. One visibly wrong entry drains trust from the whole list, and the accurate entries lose force alongside it.

Do consumer rules already address this kind of design?

There is an existing regulatory area. Under Korean law, Article 21-2 of the Act on Consumer Protection in Electronic Commerce lists prohibited conduct in operating an online interface: luring with only part of the total shown, consent pre-marked on an option, choices displayed in markedly different form, complicating cancellation or termination without justifiable grounds, and repeated change demands through pop-ups. Rules requiring prior consent and notice before a recurring charge rises or a free plan turns paid took effect on 14 February 2025. Whether a specific screen falls under those clauses is a separate question belonging to the competent authority.

If cancelling feels hard, is that a problem in itself?

Feeling alone does not settle it. Extra steps sometimes have explainable causes such as identity verification or settling the remainder of a contract, and sometimes signup lives in a new system while cancellation is stranded in an old one. So the axis of judgment is asymmetry rather than difficulty: how wide the gap is between entering and leaving the agreement, and whether that gap is explained anywhere. What an individual can usefully do is record the gap instead of the feeling. Where a case belongs is for a consumer help line to advise and the competent authority to assess.

Closing - the conditions under which a record has force

Attempts to document unfavourable practices collectively will keep growing. Individual experience is forgotten while a list persists, and a list spares the next person the whole discovery. The trouble is that a list on its own changes nothing. What changes things is not its length but the character of each entry.

Four conditions decide whether a record has force. It carries a date. It names a path someone else can follow. It is updated to resolved when the practice is fixed. It publishes where contrary evidence should be sent. All four concern accuracy, not intensity. The entry that survives is not the most forcefully written but the easiest to verify.

Aim a project at naming bad companies and the list grows fast and is dismissed fast. Aim it at leaving facts that can be judged later and growth is slow but citations carry weight. Which of the two you get is already decided by the entry form you write the first item into.
