Redesigning Your Expertise in the AI Age — The 5 Stages of AX and the J-Curve

While organizations go through AI transformation, what capabilities should individuals bet on? The era of judgment, context, and taste beyond the tool

2026-06-26 Trends & Culture

Introduction: The Transformation Era — Where Are You?

As of June 2026, AI has moved well past the stage of debating whether to adopt it. The question now is singular: as AI rapidly reshapes organizations and individuals, how do I redesign my expertise? The AX (AI Transformation) journey and J-curve framework presented by Ha Yong-ho of DataOven in a 165-page deck offers a powerful map for answering this question. Rather than reproducing the presentation, this analysis layers personal observations and original interpretation on top of that frame.

To be clear: this article does not teach you how to use AI tools. It does not cover which prompts work best or which model writes better code. Instead, it tackles a more fundamental question. When AI increasingly does more things well, what about me becomes scarce?

1. The 5 Stages of AX Transformation — Companies and Individuals Follow the Same Curve

Most organizations that adopt AI go through a recognizable pattern. First comes enthusiasm. A report draft in five minutes, code generated ten times faster, translation costs gone. Leadership is excited, employees start experimenting, and the general mood is that something big has arrived.

Then a few months in, the plateau hits. Prompts produce wrong answers. Hallucinations creep into documents. The gap between expectation and reality becomes visible. Quiet skepticism grows — "this isn't as impressive as I thought." This is the bottom of the J-curve.

Organizations that endure the plateau enter a phase of re-excitement. Rather than simply using AI, they redesign workflows around it and discover entirely new productivity levels. But even here, skepticism follows. Is the ROI actually measurable? What are the costs? Who is responsible when AI gets it wrong? Internal friction builds between adoption advocates and resisters.

Then comes the final crossroads. At this juncture, organizations split into two paths: fully redesign for an AI-native operating model, or revert to previous methods. Those who revert get outpaced by competitors who internalized AI into their core operations. The same applies to individuals. Knowing which of these five stages you are currently in is where redesign begins.

2. The AI J-Curve — The Discomfort at the Bottom Is the Condition for Growth

The J-curve originated in economics to describe how policy changes initially worsen outcomes before improvements materialize. AI adoption exhibits the same shape. When learning new tools, you are slower and more awkward than with familiar methods. Criteria for quality become unclear. The line between "what I produced" and "what AI produced" blurs.

According to PwC's 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer, companies that adopted AI experienced an average productivity decline of 1.33 percentage points in their first one to two years. However, firms that pushed through the bottom and reached the upswing recorded meaningful productivity advantages over competitors. The decisive differentiator was one thing: whether they invested simultaneously in complementary capabilities.

The same holds at the individual level. Those who endured two or three awkward months with AI tools, and those who quit that discomfort, look dramatically different six months later. A consistent pattern in my own observation: people who exit at the plateau usually conclude "AI isn't ready yet" and return to previous methods. But after that conclusion, AI does not stop advancing.

What matters in the J-curve is not how deep the dip is, but recognizing that you are in the dip. Professionals with the metacognitive awareness that "this discomfort is a normal part of transition" persist. Those without it exit early.

3. Replaceable vs. Irreplaceable Expertise — What Is the Difference?

The domains AI replaces rapidly are clear. Repetitive document production, standardized data analysis, translation and summarization, rule-bound coding, pattern-based classification. These are already being performed faster and more accurately by AI, or rapidly approaching that threshold.

What remains irreplaceable? Three categories, in my analysis.

3.1 Judgment — Decisions Under Uncertainty

AI excels when data is abundant. But most consequential real-world decisions are made under incomplete information, conflicting interests, and unprecedented circumstances. Which contract clause to accept, how to navigate a personnel situation, which of two strategies fits this particular organization at this particular moment — these are questions of context and judgment, not data. AI can enumerate options. The choice, and its responsibility, remains human.

3.2 Context — The History of Organizations and Relationships

You cannot ask a newly onboarded AI "what does this document mean in our company's context." The memory of the same mistake made ten years ago, why that manager is sensitive to a particular phrase, what that client said two months ago — context is the accumulated residue of lived experience. No matter how quickly AI processes documents, the humans who assign meaning to those documents based on lived organizational history remain indispensable.

3.3 Taste — Knowing What Good Looks Like

As of 2026, "taste is the new bottleneck" circulates widely in design and strategy communities. AI produces the average. The ability to identify what stands above that average is taste. The tone of a piece of writing, the direction of a product, the nuance of a message — in a flood of AI-generated output, the professionals who can distinguish excellent from merely adequate become scarce. Taste is not simply aesthetic preference. It is a standard of judgment built from deep immersion in a domain, from seeing vast quantities of good and bad work, from time spent developing a refined sense of what matters.

4. Practical Expertise Redesign — What to Change and How

Theory is sufficient. What does the redesign actually look like? Three axes.

4.1 Shift the Center of Gravity from Execution to Direction

If your value has resided in "producing things well," the AI-era shift moves it toward "deciding what should be produced." AI writes the report. A human decides what the report needs to say, for whom, and to what end. AI writes the code. A human judges what system to build and why, right now. This transition is uncomfortable. Moving from evaluation based on execution skill to evaluation based on directional clarity requires a different kind of muscle. Without deliberately building that muscle, AI takes execution while you also lose direction.

4.2 Do Not Abandon Domain Depth

A common claim holds that generalists who know a bit of everything thrive in the AI age. This is half right. What actually matters is AI augmentation built on top of domain depth. AI can summarize a legal document, but knowing where the risk actually lives in that contract requires someone who understands law deeply. AI can generate code, but knowing what architectural decision will create problems in five years requires an engineer with hard-won experience.

The real risk is relying on AI-provided easy answers while failing to build domain knowledge. Microsoft's 2026 Future of Work Report names this "Cognitive Laziness." When AI always produces a quick answer, humans risk losing the muscle of thinking independently and verifying rigorously.

4.3 Design a Workflow That Collaborates With AI

Knowing how to use AI and collaborating with AI are different things. The former is tool proficiency; the latter is workflow redesign. Concretely: you set direction and criteria, AI executes. You review AI output through the lens of your context and taste, and refine it. In the process of refining, your own criteria become sharper. Repeat this cycle, and you and AI form an increasingly effective team over time.

PwC's research found that companies combining AI adoption with deliberate reskilling and workflow redesign recorded clear productivity advantages over those that did neither. The same applies individually. Do not stop at using AI tools — simultaneously upgrade the frameworks and standards you use to evaluate what those tools produce.

5. Where to Invest Right Now

As of June 2026, with AI adoption now routine, investment priorities are clear in my view.

First, invest in opportunities that build judgment. Instead of outsourcing hard decisions to AI, form your own judgment first, then use AI's perspective as a reference point for comparison. That repeated practice builds judgment. Second, maintain and deepen domain knowledge. Stay deep enough in your field to notice where AI gets it wrong and what context AI misses. That is the only way to evaluate the quality of AI output. Third, develop taste deliberately. Expose yourself to excellent work in your domain and practice articulating why it is excellent. In a sea of AI-produced averages, taste becomes the compass that distinguishes you.

The PwC 2026 survey found that employees with advanced AI capabilities earn an average of 56% more than peers in identical roles without those capabilities. But the gap between those who merely use AI tools and those who layer judgment, context, and taste on top of AI fluency is widening even faster. Tools are distributed equally. What lies beyond the tool becomes scarce.

6. Conclusion — Bet on What Lies Beyond the Tool

The 5 stages of AX and the J-curve are a story about organizations — but they are equally a story about individuals. Which stage am I in right now? Will I quit at the J-curve bottom, or persist through it? After the rebound, what will I create value with?

Handing to AI what AI does well is wisdom. But watching AI do what it does well while neglecting your own expertise is dangerous. Judgment, context, taste — these are the domains AI finds it hardest to close in on quickly. And paradoxically, the more AI advances, the scarcer these three become. The more consistently AI produces the average, the more valuable the ability to recognize what surpasses that average becomes.

Redesigning expertise is not about learning new tools. It is knowing what becomes scarce in the AI age, and investing there deliberately.
