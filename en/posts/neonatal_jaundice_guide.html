Newborn Jaundice - What Is Normal, and When to Go In

The clock is the backbone of the diagnosis. Separating what parents can see at home from what they cannot

2026-09-01 Health & Wellness

Being told that the baby looks yellow is not a rare event. Korea's national health information portal, run by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency, states that jaundice appears in the first week of life in roughly 60 percent of term infants and 80 percent of preterm infants. At that rate, saying almost everyone goes through it is not an exaggeration.

The trouble starts after that sentence. Common does not mean safe. The same yellow is, in most cases, a physiologic change that fades on its own within days, and in rare cases the road to irreversible neurological injury. There is no way to separate those two by eye. What does separate them is a single axis: time. When it appeared, which day it is now, and whether it is fading or deepening — that is nearly the whole of this problem.

This article is general information. Diagnosis and treatment follow the judgement of a clinician who has examined the baby. It deliberately contains no bilirubin thresholds and no treatment tables, because those values only read correctly when gestational age, hours of life and risk factors are entered together, and they are not something a parent can apply alone. A newborn's condition can change quickly. If something seems wrong, being seen straight away is the right call.

First - the signs that need care without delay

This section is out of order on purpose, at the front, because there are situations where nobody has time to read to the bottom. If any one of the items below applies, treat it as a reason to be seen now rather than something to watch. Night or weekend does not change that.

Signs that need a decision immediately

  • Jaundice visible within the first 24 hours of life. Jaundice at this point is not explained by the usual course of physiologic jaundice. It is not an item to observe over time
  • The baby is limp and hard to rouse. Does not wake properly, feels floppy when held, refuses to feed
  • A high-pitched, piercing cry unlike the usual one, going on and on
  • Arching backwards repeatedly, or muscles becoming stiff
  • Fever. In a newborn, fever is a separate emergency on its own
  • Pauses in breathing, or breathing that has become irregular
  • Movements that look like a seizure

Signs that need checking the same day

  • Stools that are chalky white, grey or pale cream. This can mean bile is not reaching the gut
  • Urine dark enough to stain the nappy. More so when it comes with the item above
  • Yellow reaching the palms and soles. Jaundice generally starts at the face and moves down, so visible colour at the feet is treated as a sign of how far it has gone
  • Jaundice still present after two weeks. For preterm babies the marker is three weeks
  • Jaundice that faded and is now deepening again
  • Feeding has dropped noticeably and wet and dirty nappies have dropped with it
If any one of the above applies, get medical care without delay. At night or on a holiday, the emergency department is the right choice. This list is not there to tell you the cause. It is there to mark the point at which the judgement has to be handed to a clinician.

Why almost every newborn gets it

Bilirubin is the pigment released when red blood cells reach the end of their life and break down. Adults make it and clear it every day. The reason that balance tips for a few days in a newborn comes down to three things.

First, the production side is fast. A baby starts life with a high haemoglobin concentration, and fetal red cells have a shorter lifespan than adult ones, so they turn over quickly. That means plenty of raw material.

Second, the clearing side is slow. Converting bilirubin into a water-soluble form so it can leave in bile requires the liver enzyme UGT1A1, and in a newborn that enzyme is running at about 1 percent of adult activity. For a few days, production is faster than an adult's and processing is a hundredth of an adult's.

Third, what was sent out comes back. A newborn's gut has not yet established its bacterial flora, and beta-glucuronidase in the intestine unpacks bilirubin that has already been processed. What is unpacked is reabsorbed. This return path is called enterohepatic circulation, and it grows larger the less the baby feeds and the less the gut empties.

The few days when those three overlap are physiologic jaundice. It is not a sign of illness but of a transition, and it usually passes without intervention. Pathologic jaundice, however, shows up as exactly the same yellow. The fact that physiologic jaundice is common is not grounds for reassurance; it is the reason checking is so easy to skip.

The clock is the backbone of the diagnosis

The same shade of yellow reads completely differently depending on how many hours old the baby was when it appeared. That is why the first question in the clinic is about timing rather than intensity. The table below sets out what is read differently at each stage. Sources describe the usual course of physiologic jaundice slightly differently. The Korean agency writes that it can last from day 2 to day 8 in term infants and up to day 14 in preterm infants. Seoul National University Hospital's medical information describes it as appearing on day 2 to 3 and clearing by day 5 to 7. The US StatPearls reference states that it appears after the first 24 hours, peaks at 48 to 96 hours, and resolves over two to three weeks. The spread exists because the real course varies with gestational age and feeding, and every source agrees on the same point: the direction matters more than the exact hour of the peak.

Jaundice within the first 24 hours

This one line is the most important sentence in the article. Jaundice visible within the first 24 hours of life is not explained by physiologic jaundice. The three mechanisms in the previous section do not push bilirubin high enough to be visible in a single day. To reach that speed, there is usually one more process at work.

The one found most often is haemolysis. This covers cases where the mother's and baby's blood types are incompatible and the baby's red cells break down rapidly. Rh and ABO incompatibility are the classic examples, and conditions where the red cells themselves are fragile, such as G6PD deficiency, produce the same result. Infection and sepsis are also part of the background to early jaundice.

Clinically, the rate of rise is watched alongside the value. According to the American Academy of Family Physicians summary of the 2022 AAP guideline, a rise of 0.3 mg/dL per hour or more within the first 24 hours, and 0.2 mg/dL per hour or more thereafter, is used as an indicator suggesting haemolysis. Those figures are here not so a parent can calculate anything. They are here to show that what is watched is the slope between two values, not one value — and that a repeat test time you have been given was not chosen arbitrarily.

On day one, a parent's job is not to guess the cause. Telling the maternity unit or the paediatrician that the baby looks yellow now is enough, and testing answers the rest.

Breastfeeding jaundice and breast milk jaundice are not the same

This is the classic place where two things get merged because the names sound alike. In practice the timing, the mechanism and the response all differ. And neither of them leads straight to stopping breastfeeding.

AspectEarly type - suboptimal intake jaundiceLate type - breast milk jaundice
When it appearsFirst week of life, usually peaking on day 3 to 5Starts after day 5 to 7, peaks around two weeks. Can carry on for one to two months
MechanismIntake is low, so output is delayed and enterohepatic circulation growsExplained by components of breast milk affecting bilirubin processing and reabsorption
The baby's stateOften comes with weight loss and fewer wet and dirty nappiesThe baby usually feeds well and gains weight steadily
Direction of the responseSupport feeding frequency and latch to raise intakeIf it persists, other causes are ruled out
NamingThe 2022 AAP guideline renamed this suboptimal intake jaundiceThe name breast milk jaundice stays

The renaming of the early type is not a tidying-up of vocabulary. It is closer to a deliberate break with the idea that milk is the cause. The cause is not what is in the milk but how much went in, so the direction of the fix is not stopping but helping the feeding. The AAP guideline recommends starting lactation support within the first hour after birth and feeding at least eight times in 24 hours. The Korean agency's material mentions ten or more breastfeeds a day in the context of early breast milk jaundice.

For the late type, if the baby is feeding well and gaining weight, that alone is not a reason to stop. What matters is that breast milk jaundice is a label applied after other causes have been excluded. Deciding in advance that jaundice persisting past two weeks is breast milk jaundice opens the path to missing cholestasis. How to feed is settled with the clinician who has seen the baby; it is not a matter for a parent to stop or continue on their own reading.

What raises the odds

Risk factors are not a diagnosis; they are probability. Having one does not mean the jaundice will worsen, and having none does not mean safety. What they change is how tightly the checking is spaced. The list used by the 2022 AAP guideline and by the clinical pathways that follow it looks like this.

  • Lower gestational age. Handled so that risk rises with each week further from 40 weeks
  • Jaundice that appeared within the first 24 hours
  • A bilirubin value at discharge close to the phototherapy threshold
  • Haemolysis, whether from blood group incompatibility or another cause
  • Phototherapy already given before discharge
  • A parent or sibling who needed phototherapy or an exchange transfusion
  • A family history suggesting an inherited red cell disorder, including G6PD deficiency
  • Exclusive breastfeeding where intake is not adequate
  • A scalp haematoma or significant bruising. Pooled blood breaking down adds raw material for bilirubin
  • Down syndrome
  • A macrosomic infant of a mother with diabetes

Separately from that list, the guideline keeps a second one for neurotoxicity risk factors. At the same bilirubin value, these raise the risk to the brain, so the threshold for intervening comes down.

  • Gestational age under 38 weeks
  • Serum albumin below 3.0 g/dL — less capacity to hold bilirubin bound
  • Isoimmune haemolytic disease, G6PD deficiency, or other haemolytic conditions
  • Sepsis
  • Significant clinical instability in the previous 24 hours

The fact that these are two separate lists matters in practice. The first decides how often to check; the second decides at what value to act. Parents do not need to memorise either, but it is worth knowing that this is why the baby in the next cot can get a different decision at the same number.

Race came off the list in 2022

Older writing on newborn jaundice commonly lists East Asian race as a risk factor. That was not invented. The 2004 AAP guideline did list East Asian race among the major risk factors for severe hyperbilirubinaemia.

In the 2022 revision, that item is gone. The Academy removed race and ethnicity as independent variables used in assessing the risk of developing severe hyperbilirubinaemia, in line with its own policy on eliminating race-based medicine. The 2004 text that framed Black race as protective also collapsed. The Academy's own question-and-answer material points instead to research showing a higher risk of hazardous hyperbilirubinaemia in Black infants.

What went in its place is the likelihood of G6PD deficiency based on genetic ancestry. It remains true that G6PD deficiency is more frequent in populations with ancestry from sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East, the Mediterranean, the Arabian Peninsula and Southeast Asia, and the guideline now asks for that specific information rather than a racial category. It drops the broad box and asks directly about the genetic predisposition the box was standing in for.

That said, differences by population have not been erased wholesale. The Merck Manual Professional Edition places the bilirubin peak at day 3 to 4 in general, and describes it as later, around day 7, in East Asian infants. In other words, descriptions of the shape of the course remain, while the race entry in the risk-factor list is what was removed. These are two different statements, and the distinction is worth holding onto when reading older articles.

How it is measured - and the limits of the eye

There are effectively two ways to measure jaundice: a transcutaneous bilirubinometer held against the skin, and a serum bilirubin test from a blood sample. The transcutaneous reading is painless and immediate, but it is closer to a screening tool; once the value passes a certain level or comes close to the treatment threshold, it is confirmed with a blood test.

The transcutaneous route has its own limits. Readings tend to come out lower as skin pigmentation increases, and that bias grows as bilirubin rises. Areas with a scalp haematoma or extensive bruising are excluded from measurement, and under 24 hours of age the device is not used at all, with serum measurement taking its place. Even the machine has conditions it cannot see through.

So what about the eye? The NICE sentence is short and unambiguous: do not rely on visual inspection alone to estimate the bilirubin level in a baby with suspected jaundice. The AAP guideline summary likewise states that after the first 24 hours visual estimation is unreliable and can differ substantially from the actual value. There are several reasons.

  • Lighting changes the colour. The same baby looks different under fluorescent light and in daylight
  • Skin tone and constitution interfere. The same value shows up to different degrees
  • The order of spread is only a guide. Starting at the face and moving toward the feet is a rough tendency, not a scale
  • During phototherapy the gap widens. The visible yellow can fade while blood bilirubin remains high
  • Parents see the baby every day. A change that deepens slowly is seen last by the person who sees it constantly

So looking at home is not for estimating a number. It is for deciding whether to call, and the basis for that decision lies in the red-flag section above rather than in the depth of the colour. If you are going to look at colour, it is better done in bright natural light, pressing the skin briefly and releasing, and checking the whites of the eyes and the gums as well. Even then, you cannot know the number.

Treatment - what phototherapy actually does

Phototherapy does not burn bilirubin off or destroy it. It shines a narrow band of blue-green light that bilirubin absorbs well and changes the shape of the molecule. Bilirubin in that altered form can leave in bile and urine without passing through the liver's processing step. Since that processing capacity is exactly what a newborn lacks, the treatment routes around it.

Because the effect takes time to appear, the starting threshold is set well below the exchange transfusion threshold. When to start is decided by putting gestational age, hours of life and neurotoxicity risk factors together, which is why the same number leads to different conclusions in different babies. That is the reason this article carries no threshold table.

An exchange transfusion removes the baby's blood in stages and replaces it with an equal volume, taking out the antibodies driving haemolysis along with the bilirubin. It is considered when phototherapy is not controlling the level or when signs of acute bilirubin encephalopathy appear, and the range in between is covered by staged measures such as intensive phototherapy, intravenous fluids and immune globulin. The 2022 AAP guideline sets this middle band out as its own escalation pathway, and at that stage bilirubin is re-measured at much shorter intervals.

Starting treatment at a lower threshold is not unconditionally safer either. One of the drivers of the 2022 revision was the cost of overtreatment: unnecessary admissions, time spent with parent and baby separated, and disruption to the feeding rhythm are themselves costs. The modest raising of thresholds in the revision reflects accumulated safety evidence, not a lighter view of the risk.

Kernicterus - why the checking has a deadline

This section is not here to frighten anyone. It is here to explain why all the checking above exists at all.

Most bilirubin travels bound to albumin in the blood. When the unbound share grows, some of it crosses into the brain and deposits in the basal ganglia and particular brainstem nuclei. The acute state is called acute bilirubin encephalopathy, and the state left behind once the damage is fixed is called kernicterus.

The acute phase is generally described in three stages. Early on there is lethargy, floppiness and poor feeding — appearances that are easy to miss. In the intermediate stage muscle tone rises instead, and arching backwards appears. As it progresses it turns back toward floppiness. That the early signs are non-specific is the hard part of this condition, and it is why the red-flag list above is shaped the way it is.

The sequelae that remain cannot be reversed. Hearing loss, athetoid cerebral palsy, eye movement abnormalities including upward gaze palsy, and enamel defects of the baby teeth are the classic combination described.

Fortunately it is rare. But a good part of why it is rare is the prevention system itself. StatPearls reports that in kernicterus registry data, G6PD deficiency accounted for 21.3 percent, severe haemolysis for 20.5 percent and birth trauma for 15 percent. In other words, identifiable conditions account for a large share. That is why the inconvenient routine of pre-discharge measurement and a follow-up visit is kept in place.

What can and cannot be done at home

There is no way to measure bilirubin at home. What can be watched are proxies. The table below sets out what to observe and why. Do not score them; read them as a trend.

What to watchWhat you are looking atWhy it matters
Feed countHow many feeds in 24 hoursThe AAP guideline recommends at least eight in 24 hours. More intake means the gut empties and the return loop shrinks
UrineHow often the nappy is wet, and the colourA proxy for intake. Dark urine staining the nappy also reads as a cholestasis signal
StoolColour and frequencyChalky white, grey or pale cream is a same-day reason to be seen, not something to wait on
WeightThe trend against birth weightThe most practical clue to suboptimal intake jaundice
The baby's stateAlertness, muscle tone, the quality of the cryFloppiness or a high-pitched piercing cry matters far more than colour

There is also a list of things not to do. Giving water or sugar water to flush bilirubin out is not recommended. The AAFP summary of the AAP guideline states that oral supplementation with dextrose solution or water to prevent hyperbilirubinaemia in breastfed infants is not recommended. NICE likewise says not to give additional fluids to breastfed babies during phototherapy. The reason is simple: bilirubin is not washed out in urine; most of it leaves in stool. If water goes in and milk goes in less, the effect runs the other way.

Few of the methods circulating online have verifiable support. One thing is certain: feed enough, do not miss the red flags, and keep the follow-up appointment. That is where the home role ends, and it is not a small one.

The sunlight advice

This gets its own section because it is the most widespread piece of folk advice. The guidelines reject it explicitly.

The NICE sentence is one line: do not use sunlight as treatment for hyperbilirubinaemia. The 2022 AAP guideline gives the reasoning. Undressing an infant and exposing them to natural sunlight at home cannot be recommended given the risks of hypothermia, hyperthermia, sunburn and long-term skin malignancy, and because natural unfiltered sunlight cannot be applied at a prescribed dose. That direct sunlight lowers total bilirubin has been observed; it does not follow that it works as a treatment anyone can deliver.

It is worth naming the difference from hospital phototherapy here. Treatment equipment emits only the narrow band bilirubin absorbs, contains no ultraviolet, has a set intensity, duration and distance, and runs with the baby's temperature monitored throughout. Sunlight at a window has none of those four. They are not the same thing under two names.

Why they ask you to come back after discharge

As postnatal stays have shortened, the hour at which bilirubin peaks now often falls after discharge. Given that the physiologic peak sits around the third day of life, the reason is obvious. A low value at discharge is not a guarantee of a low value two days later.

So the 2022 revision sets the follow-up time by distance. How far the discharge value sits below the phototherapy threshold determines whether the next measurement is in a few hours, the next day or three days later. The closer to the threshold, the shorter the interval. The advantage is that not every baby has to be called back on the same schedule; the price is that the reasoning behind the appointment time a parent is handed is invisible from the outside.

  • The follow-up time given at discharge was not chosen arbitrarily. It comes out of the distance between the value and the threshold
  • If the baby went home after phototherapy, a repeat measurement the day after stopping is standard. If treatment began within the first 48 hours of life or there was haemolysis, the check comes sooner
  • Between visits, a red flag means not waiting for the appointment
  • Home phototherapy is an option with conditions attached: 38 weeks or more, at least 48 hours old, no neurotoxicity risk factors, and daily measurement available, among others

Jaundice past two weeks

From here the kind of question changes. Up to now it has been how high. At this point it becomes which bilirubin.

Bilirubin is divided into the indirect form, before the liver processes it, and the direct form, after. Physiologic jaundice and breast milk jaundice are states in which the indirect form is elevated. An elevated direct form is a different story entirely, because it means bile is being made but is not reaching the gut. That is why the 2022 AAP guideline recommends measuring total and direct bilirubin separately at two weeks in formula-fed infants and at three to four weeks in breastfed infants.

NICE marks the same spot from the side a parent can see. When jaundice persists beyond 14 days in a term baby or 21 days in a preterm baby, look for pale chalky stools and dark urine that stains the nappy. That combination matters because of biliary atresia.

Biliary atresia is not common. It is, however, reported more often in East Asia, including Korea, than in the West. One review puts the incidence in Western countries at 0.52 to 0.71 per 10,000 live births, Japan and Taiwan at 1.1 to 1.48, China at 2, and Korea at 1.06. And in this condition the timing of surgery strongly shapes the outcome. Canadian data cited in the same review showed four-year native liver survival of 49 percent when the Kasai procedure was done at 60 days or less, against 23 percent when it was done after 90 days. A Japanese stool colour card screening programme covering more than 300,000 newborns reported 76.5 percent sensitivity and 99.9 percent specificity.

These numbers say one thing. Looking at stool colour in a baby still jaundiced after two weeks is not a trivial step. Beyond biliary atresia, causes such as congenital hypothyroidism and galactosaemia are reviewed at the same point, and re-checking the newborn screening result is part of this stage.

In short

Most babies go through newborn jaundice and in most it fades within days. Both are true, but stopping there leads wrongly to the conclusion that the checking can be skipped. Most cases pass safely because the checking is not skipped, not because it was safe to begin with.

There is one axis to remember: time. Appeared on day one, be seen now. Appeared on day 2 to 4 and fading from day 5 to 7, the usual course. Still there past two weeks, a different question. To that, add a handful of signs that hand the judgement over regardless of colour: floppiness, refusing to feed, a high-pitched piercing cry, fever, pauses in breathing, chalky pale stools and dark urine.

What can be done at home is to feed enough, watch the nappies and the baby's state, and keep the scheduled follow-up. What cannot be done is estimating the number. A newborn's condition changes quickly. Not being sure is itself a reason to be seen.

To repeat: this article is general information and does not replace diagnosis or treatment. Treatment thresholds and protocols are deliberately absent because they are values that only read correctly with gestational age, hours of life and risk factors entered together. Judgement about the baby's condition belongs to the clinician who has examined the baby, and because a newborn's condition changes quickly, anything suspected should be seen straight away.
