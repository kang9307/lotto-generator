100 Diagrams for Korea's Institutions: AI Literacy, Turned Around

Not 'learning AI' but 'learning through AI' - between access and hallucination risk

Jul 12, 2026 Trends & Culture

Intro: AI Literacy Is Changing Who - and What - Gets Learned

Ask someone what "AI literacy" means and they will usually describe learning to write better prompts or understanding a chatbot's limits. A project posted on the developer community GeekNews (news.hada.io) quietly bends that assumption. A South Korean civil servant used AI to turn 100 of the country's major institutions and systems into one-page diagrams, and the community's reaction was overwhelmingly enthusiastic.

What caught my attention is not how polished the site is, but the approach itself. Instead of a person learning how to use AI, this project uses AI as a tool to raise understanding of a domain people already found difficult - complex social institutions. It is a case where the center of gravity shifts from "learning AI" to "learning through AI". Below, I unpack why that shift matters, and what should make us cautious.

1. The Inversion: From "Learning AI" to "Learning Through AI"

1.1 The Conventional View of AI Literacy

Most AI-literacy discussions run in one direction. As the World Economic Forum argued in a piece published in 2026, AI literacy is now treated as a foundational skill on par with reading, writing, and digital competence. Education circles echo the same framing: several guides published in early 2026 tell teachers and students they need to learn how AI works and how to use it responsibly. In short, the conversation has almost always centered on how a person handles AI.

1.2 What This Project Turns Upside Down

The Korea100 project bends that frame. What its creator newly learned was not how to use AI - that part was likely already familiar - but how to use AI as leverage to read the statutes behind 100 institutions that were unfamiliar even to them, and turn that reading into structure. AI was not the end goal of learning; it was the lever that sped up a person's path to difficult domain knowledge. In this framing, the thing that needs literacy is not AI but the institutions themselves, and AI becomes the assistive tool for acquiring that literacy. The inversion looks small, but it is practically significant: nobody has to become an AI expert first. Anyone can use AI as leverage to individually raise their own literacy in whatever domain they need.

2. Compressing Complex Institutions Into One Page: The Value of Visualization

2.1 Information Asymmetry and Access

Korean statutes and institutions are scattered across a maze of ministries, clauses, and enforcement decrees. Official channels like the National Law Information Center or the Open Data Portal exist, but reading the raw clauses and reconstructing how they apply to your own situation is a separate skill. Researchers have long noted that even when civil society wants to discuss reasonable institutional reform, it lacks the underlying knowledge and information to do so. Being "disclosed" and being "understandable" are two different problems.

2.2 Why "One Page" - Cognitive Load and Comprehension

Research on information visualization explains that compressing a complex system into the relationships between its elements turns information into a far more manageable form. Compress clauses spread across many pages into a single diagram, and a person can scan the whole structure at once instead of following each clause in sequence. The same research warns, though, that as a network grows more complex, the diagram itself can become hard to read again. The "one page" constraint is itself an editorial act that filters information down to an understandable level - and who did that filtering, on what criteria, becomes the real question of trust.

3. How AI Narrows the Knowledge Gap: The Democratization of Expertise

3.1 Unbundling Domain Expertise

Several analyses of AI's spread call this the "unbundling of expertise". In the past, the ability to read statutes and turn them into diagrams was concentrated among a trained few. With generative AI, much of that ability - summarizing source text, organizing relationships, turning a draft into a visual - can now be approximated by non-experts, much faster than before. As a piece in Humanities and Social Sciences Communications puts it, this trend creates a situation where "anyone can look like an expert", while also raising a new question: then who do we trust?

3.2 The Korean Context: Why Administrative Information Is Different

Korea's administrative system is split finely across ministries, and often you have to trace all the way down to enforcement decrees and enforcement rules to know how something actually applies. In that environment, the time and ability to read statutes has effectively been the bottleneck to information access. If AI lowers the front end of that bottleneck - reading and structuring - the effect is not a mere convenience feature. It can meaningfully expand civic literacy: the ability of citizens to work out their own rights, obligations, and benefits for themselves.

4. But: Accuracy, Hallucination, and Verification

4.1 Institutions Keep Changing

The biggest weakness of this approach is accuracy. Institutions and statutes change constantly through legislative and administrative amendments. If a user doesn't realize a one-page diagram is only a snapshot from the moment it was made, they risk mistaking stale information for current information. To its credit, the Korea100 project marks a reference date for the statutes on each institution's page and states clearly that the material is informational, not legal advice. That safeguard itself is evidence that the creator takes the accuracy problem seriously.

4.2 Why Hallucination Is Dangerous Here

AI hallucination - generating plausible but false content - is especially costly in the domain of institutions and law. A 2026 survey of legal AI tools reported factual error rates ranging from the mid-teens to over 30% depending on the tool, and hallucination on citation tasks alone averaged in the low teens. Related coverage found that U.S. courts alone saw AI-hallucination-related cases cross 700 in 2026, with related sanctions exceeding $145,000 in the first quarter alone. As I noted in my earlier pieces on Korean lawyers and hallucination prevention in the Claude AI Opus era and MiniLex, a Korean legal RAG demo, legal and institutional information is exactly the domain where the gap between "plausible" and "accurate" carries the highest cost.

4.3 This Project's Safeguards and Remaining Limits

The project has built in reasonable safeguards for an individual effort: a way to request verification or report errors, and an explicit statement that it is not legal advice. That is a fair level of responsibility for a personal project to take on. But structural limits remain. Whether one person can sustainably track update status across 100 institutions over time is a separate question, and if users never actually check the "as-of" date or cross-reference the source text, the safeguard doesn't really function. In the end, the tool's safeguards and the user's verification habit have to work together.

5. What It Means for Citizens - and for Public Institutions

5.1 A New Axis of Civic Information Literacy

The message this project sends to readers is not "memorize these 100 institutions." It is closer to a signal that how we access information is changing. If civic information literacy used to be mostly about "where do I find the official information", it now gains a second layer: "how do I verify information that has been organized by AI." Treating a visualized diagram as a reference point - checking its as-of date and cross-checking the source when it matters - becomes a new basic skill.

5.2 A Lesson for the Public Sector

At the same time, this case is a challenge to public institutions. That a single citizen's volunteer effort drew such an enthusiastic community response is, ironically, also a sign that official channels were that hard to access. If government agencies borrowed this style of visualization for official information, accessibility could improve substantially. But an official channel would need far stricter safeguards than a personal project - update cycles, clear accountability for verification, and a defined correction process.

Conclusion: The Direction of the Tool Is Shifting

What this project shows is not a finished answer but a direction. The center of gravity in AI literacy is shifting from "learning how to use AI" to "using AI as leverage to raise literacy in a specialist domain" - and that shift comes bundled with a cost in accuracy, hallucination risk, and verification, proportional to its benefit.

My own conclusion is straightforward. Tools like this are excellent as a first entry point into information, but they cannot be the last checkpoint. Use the visualized diagram as a doorway, but when an actual decision is on the line, always confirm the source text and the latest amendment status. As of July 2026, that is the minimum habit the new public-information literacy of the AI era demands.
