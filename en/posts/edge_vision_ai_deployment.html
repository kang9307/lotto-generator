Putting Vision AI on Devices in the Field - What That Decision Already Settles

Cost, latency or residency: which one drives it changes the design. And the runtime gets decided before accuracy does

2026-09-01 Tech & IT

Vision projects rarely come apart at the model. Accuracy was settled back in the office and the demo ran fine. The trouble arrives afterwards, when twelve cameras become sixty, when the installation moves from a meeting room to a factory aisle and an outdoor gate, and when the network changes from office fibre to a field uplink. The question that reopens at that point is not how to shrink the model. It is whether this workload belongs in the field at all.

And once that call is made it quietly settles most of what follows. Which accelerators are available to you, which model families can even be candidates, what is allowed to reach the server, and how anything gets updated after installation all branch from here. This piece walks through how to make that call and, more usefully, what the call decides on your behalf.

This is a general account of how field vision systems are structured, not an evaluation or endorsement of any product or vendor. License and documentation details are as published in early September 2026 and can change, so verify the primary sources before committing. The statutory section describes the text of the provisions and is not legal advice on any specific installation.

Where the assumption of uploading everything breaks

Most data is produced by events. An order arrives and one order record exists; someone signs in and one log line exists. Video does not work that way. It keeps arriving at the same rate even when nothing at all is happening. That is the first reason a vision deployment is a different kind of decision.

Put numbers on it and the shape becomes clear. One 1080p stream at 4Mbps running for twenty-four hours is roughly 43GB. Thirty cameras make that 1.3TB a day, and close to 40TB across a month. What matters is that this figure is a floor rather than an average. Busy scenes and night-time sensor noise push variable bitrate upward, and a quiet stretch at 4am does not halve it.

Then the asymmetry of the uplink lands on top. Connections that reach industrial sites are usually generous downstream and thin upstream. When a setup that ran fine in the office falls over on site, the cause is far more often a narrower pipe out than a heavier model. Storage grows along the same axis: adding a few days of retention and adding a few cameras land on the same cost line.

The three reasons you cannot upload demand different designs

The sentence «we cannot send it all to the cloud» hides three different problems. The bill for bandwidth and storage, the time from frame to action, and a regulation or contract saying the footage must not leave the premises. They arrive at the same conclusion but they ask for entirely different systems, which is why the reason has to be narrowed to one early.

Blur them together and a familiar outcome follows. The real constraint was cost, so an expensive accelerator gets bought to cut latency and the monthly bill stays exactly where it was. Or the footage must not leave, inference is moved on-device, and debug thumbnails and crash reports keep flowing outward anyway. The boundary is not drawn around inference alone.

Why it cannot go upWhat it actually demandsWhat misreading it looks like
Bandwidth and storage costFewer bytes on the uplink. The verdict can often arrive several seconds late without harmBuying a faster accelerator to cut latency while the recurring bill stays untouched
Response latencyA ceiling on the time from frame arrival to action output, measured at the worst case rather than the meanMeasuring only average latency and passing. What people notice is the occasional spike
Data must not leaveA boundary drawn physically, covering logs, thumbnails, crash dumps and the model update path as well as inferenceMoving inference on-device while footage keeps leaking through incidental channels
  • If cost drives it, the optimisation target is uplink bytes, not the model. Halving the model changes nothing while raw video still goes up
  • If latency drives it, write the acceptance criterion as a percentile. An average of 40 milliseconds is not something two teams can agree on
  • If regulation drives it, the first drawing is not an architecture diagram but every line the data travels along. Remote support sessions and log collectors are the ones most often forgotten
  • Two of the three genuinely overlap sometimes. Even then one of them has to rank first or no trade-off can be settled

Ship events, not footage

One shift in framing is what makes the whole decision executable. What lives in the field is not a model but the conversion of video into events. A camera produces an endless stream of pixels; the system downstream usually needs a handful of sentences. Something like: at gate three, at 14:22:07, a forklift crossed the stop line.

Rerun the earlier arithmetic and the gap shows. Say an event carrying a timestamp, a class, coordinates, a confidence value and one small thumbnail comes to 20KB, and one camera produces 200 of them a day. Thirty cameras then generate about 120MB a day, against 1.3TB for raw upload — four orders of magnitude apart. These are calculations on assumptions rather than measurements, but moving the assumptions by a factor of two or three does not flip the order of magnitude.

  • The moment video becomes events, everything downstream turns into an ordinary data pipeline. No video-specific infrastructure is needed
  • Something is given up in exchange. A scene that produced no event effectively never happened. You cannot go back and look
  • So the safe order is to keep deliberately more during the first few months and tighten only once the decision thresholds have settled
  • Retention policy is not an engineering decision but one made together with operations and legal. Bolted on later, the data already accumulated becomes the problem

Where to draw the line

Raw versus events is not a binary but a continuous dial. Where the dial sits fixes bandwidth cost, retraining potential and privacy exposure all at once. That single line is effectively the system design document.

Five positions cover most real deployments. The further up the list, the more information survives and the more cost and risk come with it.

Where the line sitsWhat reaches the serverWhat becomes unrecoverableWhen it fits
All raw footageEvery frameNothingFew cameras, bandwidth to spare, and an urgent need for retraining data early on
Clips around eventsShort segments containing the moment of the callScenes in the stretches that produced no callDecisions a person has to confirm before they count
Event plus thumbnailMetadata and one low-resolution imageEvidence visible only at full qualityIndustrial inspection and counting, where the call is unambiguous
Event metadata onlyTimestamp, class, coordinates, confidenceThe scene itself. A wrong call cannot be checked by eyeAreas where the footage itself must not be retained
Aggregates onlyStatistics such as counts per hourTraceability of individual incidentsRemote sites on extremely narrow links
  • The line can differ per camera. In practice applying one policy to an entrance area and a production line is the stranger choice
  • Lowering the line is easy and raising it is hard. Frames already discarded do not come back
  • So record the cost of reversal alongside the decision. Collecting the data again usually costs more than storing it did

What a field device forces on you

Choosing the field means choosing a machine that is nothing like a rack server. It goes into a sealed enclosure with no fan, it draws from whatever distribution board already exists, and in summer the ambient temperature climbs. The thermal limit is reached before the performance limit. Benchmark throughput is a short-window figure; production is months of continuous operation.

Memory behaves differently too. On a server you add more when you run short; a field device usually ships with a fixed amount and no expansion slot. It is not one model in there but the decoder, preprocessing buffers, tracker state and the operating system all sharing the same pool. Add cameras and this is what binds first.

  • Sustained throughput and peak throughput are different numbers. Where clocks drop as temperature rises, the value after thirty minutes is the real one
  • The power budget is set by the site, not the enclosure. If it does not fit the existing distribution capacity, electrical work joins the project
  • Physical access is expensive. When one reboot requires someone to drive out, stability stops being a nicety and becomes a line item
  • Supply horizon starts to matter. Development boards are easy to buy today; whether the same module exists in three years is a separate question
  • Unattended recovery after boot is a baseline requirement. Power cuts do not arrive on schedule and sites are usually unstaffed

Where the runtime gets decided before accuracy

This is the step whose order gets reversed most often. The habit is to read an accuracy table, choose a model, then attach hardware. Field deployment runs the other way. An accelerator does not execute a model. It executes operators. If the model contains an operation the target runtime does not support, that part has to run somewhere else.

The ONNX Runtime documentation states the mechanism plainly. An execution provider reports the nodes and sub-graphs it can take through the GetCapability() interface, and the runtime partitions the graph accordingly. The provider list is ordered by priority: give it ['CUDAExecutionProvider', 'CPUExecutionProvider'] and capable nodes run on CUDA while everything else runs on CPU.

Thanks to that design, outright conversion failure is rare. The problem is precisely that it does not fail. The model runs, the outputs are correct, and a handful of nodes in the middle of the graph have quietly dropped to CPU with a memory round trip at every seam. Nothing anywhere is logged as an error to explain the missing throughput.

  • When narrowing candidates, put «does this offload whole on the target runtime» in the column next to accuracy
  • The way to find out is execution, not documentation. Converting once on the target device beats reading a support matrix
  • If the postprocessing contains an unusual operation, lifting just that part out of the model is often the better move
  • Some families are designed to finish without NMS at all. Ultralytics documents YOLO26, released in January 2026, as using a one-to-one head by default so that it produces end-to-end predictions with no NMS required

What changes quietly during conversion

Conversion is not a change of file format. The moment precision drops, results move with it. The ONNX Runtime documentation puts it without hedging: quantization is not a loss-less transformation and it may negatively affect a model's accuracy. The same page therefore points to debugging tools that compare the weight and activation tensors of the original graph against the quantized one.

The selection guidance is written down too. Dynamic quantization is recommended for RNNs and transformer-based models, static quantization for CNNs. Static runs a set of calibration inputs first and writes the resulting parameters in as constants, which means any gap between the calibration data and the actual scenes on site lands directly on accuracy. Where post-training methods miss the target, the documentation's next recommendation is to retrain with quantization-aware training.

How the shrinking techniques work and what they cost first is not this article's subject. The pattern where average accuracy holds while rare cases collapse first is covered separately in What Shrinking a Model Actually Costs. One thing is worth carrying forward: if you do not compare before and after on the same data, you ship without knowing the accuracy moved at all.

  • Set the acceptance bar against the converted model running on the target device, never the pre-conversion one
  • Calibration data is only meaningful when it comes from frames pulled from the site being installed, not from the training set
  • Put the hard stretches — night, backlight, dust — into the evaluation set deliberately. Averages hide exactly these
  • Fix the conversion pipeline in a script rather than doing it by hand. A conversion you cannot reproduce is one you cannot diagnose later

Read the license before you deploy

This item surfaces late for a simple reason: it causes no trouble at all during training and evaluation. It starts mattering the moment that model ships inside equipment that is sold, and by then the whole pipeline has been shaped around it. It belongs in the same column as accuracy while the candidate list is still being narrowed.

  • An internal-only system is not automatically safe ground. AGPL-family terms extend to users reached across a network
  • The cost of switching model families is only realistic once postprocessing and evaluation code are counted in

The bottleneck is usually not inference

Shaving milliseconds off inference is visible work and it reports well. Look at the whole path from camera input to event, though, and those milliseconds are often a small slice. Unpacking the compressed stream, reshaping pixels into the size and layout the model expects, cleaning up the resulting boxes and stitching the same object across frames all consume time.

Camera input → Decode → Preprocess → Inference → Postprocess → Track/decide → Event

[Handled by the accelerator]  Inference
[Landing on the CPU]          Preprocess · Postprocess · Tracking · Event delivery
[Hardware decoder]            Decode (present: off the CPU; absent: onto the CPU)

Here is the situation that keeps recurring: accelerator utilisation is low and throughput still will not budge. The cause is usually decoding and memory copies. If there is no hardware decoder, or the pipeline is not routed through it, that load lands wholesale on the CPU. When cameras are added, this is the stage that saturates first — not the accelerator.

  • Before touching the model, split the timing by stage and measure. Optimising without measuring usually digs in an empty spot
  • Whether a hardware decoder exists and whether the chosen pipeline actually uses it are two different questions
  • Cutting copies between accelerator and CPU often beats shrinking the model
  • Postprocessing scales with camera count and frequently is not accelerated. This tends to be the real ceiling on scaling out
  • Speeding up a stage that is not the bottleneck leaves the total unchanged. Which axis actually separates devices is laid out in What Actually Decides Inference Hardware

Frames per second is the wrong unit

That structure carries straight into how you measure. Standalone model throughput is one stage of a pipeline. What determines how many cameras a system can carry is end-to-end throughput from camera input to event publication. The two numbers diverge widely even on the same box.

This is also why a throughput figure with no stated conditions is useless. Input resolution, batch size, precision, concurrent stream count, whether postprocessing is included, and how many minutes into the run the reading was taken all move the result. Even the same model on the same device cannot be compared across different conditions. Vendor figures are better used as a hint for shortlisting than as grounds for a purchase decision.

The usable form fits into one sentence. This device, at this resolution, with this model, handles this many cameras at this frame rate while running continuously for this many hours at this ambient temperature. If that sentence cannot be filled in, nothing has been measured yet.

  • Acceptance criteria must include concurrent stream count. A single-stream figure does not divide neatly by ten at ten streams
  • Measure after cooling has stabilised. The first few minutes of a run are not operating values
  • Decide the frame-dropping policy in advance. If what gets dropped under backlog is undefined, latency simply accumulates
  • Measure the burst separately. Systems break during the stretch where events overlap, not during quiet operation

What updating a model in the field means

On a server, a deployment is easy to roll back. On a field device it is not. A bad update means someone has to travel out to revive the box. So the deployment decision settles the update mechanism along with it. It is a requirement from the start, not something attached later.

And what gets updated is never a single model file. The model, the converted executable form, preprocessing and postprocessing parameters, decision thresholds, and the combination of all of them move together. If that combination is not pinned as one version, every site drifts into a different state. Not knowing which combination produced a fault is the most expensive condition in operations.

  • There has to be an automatic path back to the previous version when an update fails. A structure that only recovers when a person arrives is a cost line
  • Make staged rollout the default rather than pushing to the whole fleet. Field conditions differ site by site
  • Ship the model and its thresholds pinned as a single version. Tweaking thresholds separately is what creates irreproducible states
  • If you cannot see remotely which version a device is running, that system is not in an updatable state

The path that brings wrong calls back

A deployed model will be wrong. What matters is not how often, but whether there is a path by which the wrong ones come back. Without it the system is frozen at its deployment-day performance and degrades quietly as conditions on site change.

That path collides head-on with the boundary decision above. Keeping material for retraining means storing scenes, and not storing scenes was exactly what the boundary decided. There is no answer that satisfies both — only an agreement on how much to keep. Made by the engineering team alone, that agreement gets overturned later.

  • Site staff must be able to flag a wrong call in one action. If it needs a separate procedure, nobody does it
  • Wrong cases are not the only ones worth collecting. Cases that were right but close forecast the next failure
  • State what is collected and for how long from the outset. Decided later, the data already piled up becomes the problem
  • Before switching models, write down the cases the previous version failed. That list becomes the next evaluation set

Video sits next to personal data

Vision systems are often placed where people are recorded. Korea's Personal Information Protection Act divides this area into fixed and mobile devices. Article 25 governs the installation and operation of fixed video information processing devices; paragraph 4 requires a notice board that data subjects can readily recognise, carrying the purpose and location of installation, the scope and hours of recording, and the contact details of the person responsible. Paragraph 5 of the same article forbids operating the device for a purpose other than the one it was installed for or pointing it elsewhere, and states that the audio recording function may not be used.

Article 25-2 covers mobile video information processing devices separately. Recording for business purposes is restricted as a rule, and where it is permitted, the fact of recording must be indicated and communicated through light, sound, a notice board or similar means. Both provisions took effect on 15 September 2023 under Act No. 19234, amended on 14 March 2023. Actual scope depends on the location and purpose, so specific cases need their own review.

  • What matters for design is less the text itself than the fact that it points the same direction as the boundary decision. What was never kept needs no managing
  • Turning video into events narrows exposure as a side effect. That is one of the substantive advantages of processing on site
  • Images saved for debugging are what slip through most often. A store labelled temporary that has been there for years is a common find
  • Whether a file is genuine and whether the scene is genuine are separate questions, treated separately in What a Camera Signature Does Not Vouch For

Why the smartest model is not the answer

Lay out everything covered so far and a pattern shows. Bandwidth, heat, runtime support, conversion drift, licensing, the update path, privacy scope. None of it appears on an accuracy table. Yet most of the reasons a deployed system stalls or gets rolled back come from exactly that list.

What wins on industrial sites is rarely impressive. It runs inside a fixed power budget and a fixed enclosure, at a fixed cost, at the same speed in summer as in winter. And crucially, it is something that repeats identically across a hundred installations. The configuration that produces the best result on one box and the one that produces the same result on a hundred are not the same configuration.

Choosing model size is its own question, and it turns on how the task was defined rather than on raw capability; that is covered in Is a Small Model Enough. This article sits one step earlier. Does this workload belong in the field, and on what basis is that settled? Narrow the reason to one, draw the boundary as a single line, and fix the runtime, license and update path that the line demands — the rest tends to follow. Reverse the order and you get a system with excellent accuracy that cannot be installed.
