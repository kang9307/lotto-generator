Where Intimidation Begins Under Korean Law - Threat of Harm, Fear, and the Limits of Asserting a Right

Elements and penalties under Article 283 of the Korean Criminal Act, and where it parts from extortion and coercion (as of September 2026)

2026-09-01 Law & Legal

Someone who has not slept for days over a single line thrown out at the end of an argument, and someone who announced they would take legal steps to recover an unpaid debt and received a criminal complaint for it, end up at the same advice desk over the same provision. The two face opposite directions, but one axis decides both. How far does a threat of harm reach, and where does it stop being one.

The provision is short. Because it is short, the judgment sits outside the text, and the Supreme Court of Korea has set markers at each point where the offense is made out or is not. What follows locates those markers and shows where intimidation parts from extortion and coercion, all under the law of the Republic of Korea as in force in September 2026.

This is general information about South Korean law, not legal advice, and it does not describe the law of any other country. Whether a given case meets the elements depends on its specific facts and evidence, and the final call belongs to the investigating authorities and the courts. Anyone facing an actual matter should consult a lawyer or a body such as the Korea Legal Aid Corporation (132 in Korea). The content below is current as of September 2026.

The range the provisions set

Chapter XXX of Part II of the Korean Criminal Act is headed Crimes of Intimidation. Four provisions carry everything from the basic offense through aggravation and attempt.

Article 283(1) reads: a person who intimidates another shall be punished by imprisonment for not more than three years, a fine not exceeding KRW 5 million, detention or a minor fine. What catches the eye is that four kinds of punishment are listed at once. It shows a judgment that a single provision has to absorb everything from the trivial to the grave.

Paragraph 2 covers a lineal ascendant, and paragraph 3 is the one that will return several times below: the offenses under paragraphs 1 and 2 cannot be prosecuted against the express wish of the victim.

  • The words up to one half in Article 285 set a ceiling on aggravation, not a mandatory increase
  • The object of Article 283(2) is one's own or a spouse's lineal ascendant

What a threat of harm is

The definition the case law uses compresses into one sentence: intimidation means communicating harm of a degree that would generally be capable of causing fear in a person. Three places attract most of the misreadings.

First, feasibility is not an element. In decision 2006Do546 of 25 August 2006, the Supreme Court of Korea held on intent that it suffices to recognize and accept that one is communicating harm, and that an intention or desire to actually bring the harm about is not required. Saying something one could never have carried out is not by itself a shield.

Second, the kind of harm is not confined to life or body. Reputation, credit, property, business - any interest the recipient seeks to protect can be the subject. What was at issue in 2010Do1017, discussed below, was not physical violence but a communication that internal wrongdoing at a company would be exposed.

Third, the communication must reach the other person and its meaning must be grasped. Words that never arrive never enter the structure the offense contemplates.

  • Required - harm sufficient to cause fear, its arrival and the grasp of its meaning, and awareness that one is communicating it
  • Not required - intent to carry it out, capacity to carry it out, or that the harm concern life or body
  • Materials for the judgment - the disposition of both sides, surrounding circumstances, familiarity and standing between them, and the facts before and after the act
  • So the wording alone cannot settle whether the offense is made out. The same sentence is read in opposite ways depending on context

Must fear actually arise - what 2007Do606 settled

This is the longest-running dispute in the area. If harm was communicated but the recipient was unmoved, is that an attempt or a completed offense. The Supreme Court of Korea settled it en banc in 2007, in decision 2007Do606 of 28 September 2007, a case titled violation of the Act on the Lapse of Criminal Sentences and intimidation.

The majority reads it this way. The harm communicated must, weighed against the circumstances before and after the act, be enough to cause fear in a person generally, but the recipient is not required to actually experience fear. Once harm of that degree is communicated and its meaning is grasped, the offense is complete regardless of whether fear in fact arose.

The dissent is worth holding onto as well. Justices Kim Young-ran and Park Il-hwan treated intimidation as a result offense complete only once fear actually arises, and pointed out that the very existence of the attempt provision in Article 286 presupposes that reading.

The weight of the decision lies in moving the scale. The reference point shifted from the victim's inner state to an objective assessment. The room for attempt narrowed too: Article 286 remains, but what is left to it is confined to cases where the communication never arrived or its meaning was never grasped.

  • Test for completion - communication of harm of sufficient degree, and the recipient's grasp of its meaning
  • Not the test - how much fear the victim actually felt
  • The victim's reaction still serves as circumstantial material for judging whether the harm was generally sufficient
  • The tension with the attempt provision that the dissent identified is still debated in the literature

The side where it is not made out

Writing on this offense tilts easily toward the side where it is made out. In practice the split often runs the other way. Decision 2006Do546 of 25 August 2006 puts it thus: where the speaker's words amount to nothing more than an emotional insult or a passing display of anger, and the absence of any intent to harm is objectively clear in light of the surrounding circumstances, neither the act nor the intent of intimidation can be found.

On its face the statement in that case sounded like harm to life, and it was still assessed as an emotional outburst in the course of a heated argument. But reading this as abuse is not punished gets it exactly backwards. Two conditions are attached, nothing more than and objectively clear, and both are assessments arrived at, not starting points.

The judgment weighs not only the outward form of the act but how it came about, the relationship with the victim, and the surrounding situation. The same words in a one-off argument between equals and in a repeated pattern across a gap in power are assessed differently.

  • Emotional insult is a conclusion, not a premise. To earn it, the absence of intent to harm must be objectively clear
  • Repetition, the hour, the channel of delivery and asymmetry in the relationship can all pull the judgment the other way
  • Apologizing afterwards does not erase the elements. That belongs closer to sentencing and to the victim's wish
  • Conversely, the offense is not made out merely because the victim felt it strongly

The line against a legitimate assertion of right

The most practical question sits here. If you are owed money and you tell the debtor you will take legal steps unless it is repaid, is that asserting a right or intimidation.

Decision 2022Do9187 of 15 December 2022 met the question head on. Even where harm is communicated, the offense is not made out where the communication is within what social convention can tolerate in light of custom and ethical norms, or amounts to a reasonable means for a legitimate purpose and so does not offend social rules. Where the communication arises in realizing or exercising a right between parties with opposing civil interests, the court said to weigh the following together.

  • The relationship between the parties and the gap in their socioeconomic standing
  • Whether the disadvantage communicated goes markedly beyond what an opposing party could ordinarily foresee or accept as part of realizing or exercising a right
  • Whether a rational connection exists between the manner of communication and the purpose pursued

The dividing point is not whether the words legal action were spoken; it is whether those words connect rationally to the purpose as a means. Signalling a suit or a payment order over a genuine debt sits within what is ordinarily foreseeable.

The holding in 2007Do606 marks the same spot from the other side. A communication of harm made in the name of exercising a right or performing a duty can still constitute intimidation where authority is substantially abused.

  • Where the connection weakens - the demand reaches well past the scope of the right
  • Where the connection weakens - the disadvantage is aimed at the other side's family, workplace or reputation
  • Where the connection weakens - delivery leaves the bounds of ordinary demand and expands into repetition, late-night contact or exposure to a crowd
  • By contrast, signalling a formal step such as a certified letter or an application for a payment order is hard to fault

Harm to whom - third parties and legal entities

The harm need not be aimed at the recipient personally. Decision 2010Do1017 of 15 July 2010 held that even where the harm concerns an infringement of the interests of some other third party rather than the victim or the victim's relatives, the offense can be made out if the victim and that third party stand in a close relationship such that the content would cause fear in the victim. That third party may include a legal entity.

The same decision drew the opposite line. Intimidation contemplates only natural persons, so a legal entity cannot itself be the object of the offense. A communication of harm aimed at a company is not intimidation of the company, but if it would cause fear in an individual closely connected to that company, it can be intimidation of that individual.

  • The object is a natural person. A legal entity is not a victim of this offense
  • The harm may concern a closely connected third party, and that third party may be a legal entity
  • So identifying who the victim is becomes the first fork in a complaint

Where it parts from extortion

When intimidation is a means rather than an end, the offense changes. Article 350(1) punishes a person who extorts another and takes delivery of property or obtains a pecuniary benefit with imprisonment of up to 10 years or a fine of up to KRW 20 million, and paragraph 2 applies the same punishment where a third party is made to obtain it. Article 350-2, aggravated extortion, covers showing the force of a group or crowd or carrying a dangerous object and carries imprisonment of one to fifteen years. There is no fine option at all.

The gap in penalty is wide, and the procedural difference cuts even deeper. Extortion is not barred by the victim's express wish. A statement that the victim does not want punishment does not by itself stop prosecution.

  • The axis - was intimidation used as the means of obtaining property or a pecuniary benefit
  • Intimidation is not a property offense; extortion is. The protected interest differs
  • This is where the belief that a settlement ends everything goes furthest wrong
  • The line against asserting a right arises in extortion in the same way

Where it parts from coercion

If the intimidation left the other person doing something, or unable to do something, coercion comes into view. Article 324(1) punishes a person who, by violence or intimidation, obstructs another's exercise of a right or compels an act the other has no duty to perform, with imprisonment of up to 5 years or a fine of up to KRW 30 million. Paragraph 2 covers the force of a group or crowd or carrying a dangerous object, with imprisonment of up to 10 years or a fine of up to KRW 50 million.

Coercion is likewise not barred by the victim's express wish. One set of facts commonly carries several possible charges, and the effect of the victim's wish is then calculated charge by charge.

  • Where no result followed, the attempt provision for each offense comes into play
  • What matters is not naming the charge yourself but preserving the facts

Lineal ascendants, aggravated form, habitual offenders

There are three aggravated forms, and each is aggravated for a different reason: the object, the method, the repetition.

Intimidation of a lineal ascendant (Art. 283(2)) is aggravated because of the object. Since it covers one's own or a spouse's lineal ascendant, a spouse's parents fall within it. A spouse is not a lineal ascendant and so is not covered.

Aggravated intimidation (Art. 284) is aggravated because of the method: committing an offense under Article 283(1) or (2) while showing the force of a group or crowd, or carrying a dangerous object. And here sits the point most often missed. Article 283(3) limits the bar to the offenses under paragraphs 1 and 2, and Article 284 is not inside that wording.

Habitual offending (Art. 285) is aggravated because of repetition. Where an offense under Article 283(1), (2) or Article 284 is committed habitually, the punishment prescribed for that offense is aggravated by up to one half.

  • Aggravated intimidation is not barred by the victim's wish. That single line changes the whole direction of a case
  • Carrying a dangerous object is written as carrying, not using
  • A spouse is not within the object of Article 283(2)
  • Article 286 punishes attempts of all three, though after 2007Do606 little room is left for attempt

Express-wish bar versus complaint offense

These two terms get mixed up more than any others. Intimidation cannot be prosecuted against the victim's express wish; insult is a complaint offense. Article 312(1) of the Criminal Act makes insult prosecutable only on complaint.

A complaint offense can be prosecuted only where a complaint has been filed, and under Article 230(1) of the Criminal Procedure Act no complaint may be filed once six months have passed from the day the offender became known.

An express-wish offense works the other way. Investigation and prosecution may proceed without a complaint, and prosecution is barred only if the victim states expressly that punishment is not wanted. No six-month window attaches. Saying you cannot act on intimidation after six months carries over a rule that belongs to complaint offenses.

The timing comes from Article 232 of the Criminal Procedure Act. Paragraph 1 allows a complaint to be withdrawn before judgment is pronounced at first instance; paragraph 2 bars a person who has withdrawn from filing again. Paragraph 3 applies both where the wish to see the offender punished is withdrawn in an express-wish case.

  • A no-punishment clause in a settlement means you will not seek punishment again in that case
  • That effect runs to intimidation and intimidation of a lineal ascendant. Aggravated intimidation, extortion and coercion are different
  • Where several charges attach, the effect splits charge by charge
  • Being an express-wish offense does not mean the offense is light. It sets how the procedural door opens and closes
  • Checking the charge and the timing before settling is the right order

When it happens online or by messaging

Matters conducted by messenger or text pick up one more provision. Article 44-7(1)3 of the Act on Promotion of Information and Communications Network Utilization and Information Protection prohibits distributing information that repeatedly delivers to another person code, words, sound, images or video causing fear or anxiety. The penalty under Article 74(1)3 is imprisonment of up to one year or a fine of up to KRW 10 million.

This provision is also barred by the victim's wish. Article 74(2) provides that the offense under paragraph 1 item 3 cannot be prosecuted against the wish the victim has specifically stated. The Constitutional Court upheld it in decision 2014HunBa434 of 29 December 2016.

The mismatch in elements matters. Criminal Act intimidation can be complete on a single communication but requires a threat of harm. The network provision requires repetition instead of a threat of harm.

Where conduct is sustained and repeated, the Act on the Punishment of Stalking Crimes also comes into view. A 2023 amendment deleted the express-wish bar for stalking offenses, effective 11 July 2023.

  • Deleted messages may survive on a device or a server, but whether they can be secured varies case by case
  • Blocking an account helps safety but can work against securing evidence
  • Repetition is not only a count; the connection between the acts is assessed as well

Procedure and evidence

Reporting and filing are different doors

Calling 112 addresses the danger in front of you; a criminal complaint seeks punishment. The two are not exclusive. If you feel physically at risk, the call comes first.

A complaint is filed at a police station or a prosecutors' office. What it needs is facts specified as to when, where, who, what and how. The more the language turns to feeling, the blurrier the facts become.

Records matter less for form than for originals

The point where evidence most often collapses is the loss of the original. If you keep only a screenshot and delete the underlying conversation, there is no way to check the capture once its authenticity is disputed. Keep the original and make a copy - that is the order. Better left undone

  • Provoking the other side into repeating it - the risk rises and the prompting shows in the record
  • Keeping only an edited compilation of the exchange - the original has to be there too
  • Publishing the other party's personal details to third parties - that spreads into a separate legal problem
  • Offering punishment as a bargaining chip - the extortion discussion above comes back at you
  • Writing uncertain memories as if they were certain - that leads straight to false accusation
  • Investigators read material for specificity, not for the size of the feeling
  • You will give a statement more than once. The more accurate the first record, the steadier the rest

If you feel physically at risk

This may be the section that should be read first. If you feel that physical danger is imminent right now, calling 112 comes first. Whether the elements are met is the next question.

Korean police operate a scheme of measures for people at risk. It used to be called personal protection; in a December 2021 package on strengthening frontline response the National Police Agency renamed it Crime Victim Safety Measures. The reason for the change explains the scheme. The old term risked being read as close-quarters bodyguarding, and it could not cover the range of protective steps the police actually take.

Risk is graded very high, high and ordinary, and the measures differ by grade. At very high, a safe lodging, entry to a protection facility, support for relocating and installation of an AI CCTV are listed; at high, registration in the 112 system, tailored patrols and issuance of a smartwatch. Those covered include victims, reporters, witnesses, references and their relatives who face a risk of retaliation.

This is not bodyguarding. The renaming happened because of exactly that misunderstanding. A smartwatch routes a call to 112, and a response takes time.

  • Imminent danger - 112
  • Asking about the scheme - tell the police station handling the case, or the assigned investigator, that you wish to apply
  • Legal consultation - the Korea Legal Aid Corporation takes calls at 132 within Korea. Weekdays 09:00-11:50 and 13:00-17:50; from abroad, 82-54-132
  • The Corporation notes that phone consultation covers simple matters only and that the content is the counsellor's personal view, to be used for reference
  • Safety measures are not granted automatically on application; they go through a review
  • The types of measure and the grading criteria can change, so the current standard is best confirmed with the responsible unit

False accusation and counter-complaints

Intimidation cases often spread into both sides filing against each other, so the provision on the other side is worth reading too. Article 156 of the Criminal Act punishes a person who reports a false fact to a public office or public official with the purpose of having another subjected to criminal punishment or disciplinary action, by imprisonment of up to 10 years or a fine of up to KRW 15 million. That is heavier than intimidation.

That said, somewhat overstating a fact or misjudging its legal characterization is not the same as reporting a false fact. Where that line falls is a matter for the individual case.

Limitation periods help as well. Article 249(1) of the Criminal Procedure Act sets five years for offenses punishable by imprisonment with a maximum of less than five years, and seven years where the maximum is less than ten years. Applied here, intimidation (up to 3 years) is five years, while intimidation of a lineal ascendant (up to 5) and aggravated intimidation (up to 7) are seven.

  • Adding unverified facts to a complaint does not strengthen the case; it only adds risk
  • Before agreeing to a no-punishment settlement, check which charge it actually affects
  • Limitation has its own starting point and suspension grounds. Judging by the period alone is risky

Summary

The structure of this offense rests on four short provisions with case law built on top. The test is whether harm sufficient to cause fear in a person generally was communicated and its meaning grasped; how frightened the victim actually was is circumstance, not an element.

The other line is equally clear. Where the words amount to nothing more than an emotional insult or passing anger and the absence of intent to harm is objectively clear, it is not intimidation. A communication made in realizing or exercising a right is not intimidation either, where it stays within what social convention tolerates or amounts to a reasonable means for a legitimate purpose.

Two lines govern procedure. Intimidation and intimidation of a lineal ascendant are barred by the victim's express wish, but aggravated intimidation is not. And the statement that punishment is not wanted can be made only before judgment at first instance and cannot be taken back.

The last point is order. If danger is imminent, 112 comes first, and the provisions come after.

Once more: the above is general information about South Korean law, and whether a given case meets the elements depends on its specific facts and evidence. Which offense is made out, and what disposition follows, are decided by the investigating authorities and the courts. An actual matter calls for a lawyer or a body such as the Korea Legal Aid Corporation (132 in Korea), and if you are physically at risk, calling 112 comes first.
