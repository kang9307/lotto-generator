Are AI Agents Software's Most Expensive Mistake? George Hotz vs The Market in 2026

The Contrarian Case Against AI Agents - Why George Hotz Thinks They're a $100B Trap

2026-05-26 Tech & IT

Introduction: Who Is Right? The Core of the 2026 AI Agent Debate

On May 25, 2026, the second-ranked topic on news.hada.io was "AI Agents: Software's Most Expensive Mistake" with eight points. The headline came straight from a short interview George Hotz gave on Twitter, and the English-speaking developer community split into two camps within hours. One side laughed it off, pointing to dashboards where AI agents already process half of their team's pull requests. The other side agreed, pointing to token bills that look nothing like the marketing page. The interesting part of the argument is that both sides are correct inside their own context, and they are using the same words to mean different things.

The goal of this article is to turn that two-sided argument into something an English-reading practitioner can actually use to make a decision. The plan is to lay out Hotz's critiques in clean form, match each one against market data, and then sort the cases where each side is right. The early conclusion is unambiguous. AI agents can absolutely become an expensive mistake, but they are not an expensive mistake by nature. Five failure cases and five ROI cases divide cleanly, and a five-axis decision framework sits between them.

Who Is George Hotz, the AI Infrastructure Critic

George Hotz is one of the most recognized hackers in the English-language tech press. He jailbroke the iPhone at age seventeen and famously cracked the PlayStation 3, sparking a long legal fight with Sony. In 2015 he founded Comma.ai to build affordable driver-assistance hardware (the openpilot stack), and through that work he has stayed consistently on the side of "advanced assistance" rather than "full autonomy" in the self-driving debate. His criticism of Tesla FSD follows the same pattern - the autonomy promised is far ahead of the value delivered. The AI agent critique grows from the same template.

Since 2020 he has built tinygrad, a minimalist deep learning framework designed to do the same inference work in a fraction of the codebase that PyTorch or TensorFlow needs. Because of tinygrad, Hotz understands LLM inference cost and token economics in a way that very few public critics do. When he says AI agents are expensive, that statement carries actual infrastructure numbers behind it. The strength of his critique is exposing the gap between marketing pages and infrastructure invoices. Its weakness is that he does not always separate temporary problems from structural ones.

Hotz's Four Critiques in Clean Form

The following four points combine his tweets, interviews, and the recurring lines from his Twitch streams. Direct quotes are kept short and attributed. The rest is paraphrased analysis.

Critique 1) Runaway Token Costs - The Per-Task Price Does Not Match the Sticker

The first complaint is the easiest to verify. A tool that advertises "twenty dollars per month" can quietly bill five to fifty dollars per task once you chain multi-turn reasoning, tool calls, and large contexts. Cursor Pro plans, for example, often add two to five hundred dollars per active user in metered token charges on top of the subscription, especially when agent mode is left on. The advertised price is the floor, not the ceiling.

The honest assessment: this critique is true in the short term and false in the long term. Token prices have been falling by roughly half every six months, and model efficiency is improving fast enough that by late 2026 the same workload should cost about a quarter of the 2025 price. The cost is temporary, but the bad decisions made while it is expensive are permanent.

Critique 2) The Verification Burden Falls Back on Humans

The second critique is the strongest. Even when the agent finishes work quickly, somebody has to verify the output. Hotz points to driver-assistance as a direct analog. The moment a Tesla driver feels that FSD is "almost autonomous" they relax their attention, and intervention speed in actual emergencies gets slower. Coding agents do the same thing. Output that is ninety percent correct nudges the human reviewer toward skipping the verification step, and the defects accumulate downstream.

The honest assessment: this is the most durable critique. Several teams have shown the exact same pattern after adopting Cursor or Claude Code - PR throughput went up while production defects went up at the same time. Agent autonomy and verification depth tend to move in opposite directions. This is not solved by waiting for better tools. It is solved by designing workflows that force verification, which is a process problem rather than a product problem.

Critique 3) Tool Lock-In Costs Pile Up

The third critique is about the cost of being married to a specific toolchain. A team that internalizes Cursor workflows, Claude Code command patterns, and Copilot shortcuts pays the learning cost all over again when it wants to switch. Vendor pricing power follows. Hotz often draws the parallel to AWS lock-in, which started cheap and ended expensive once switching was hard.

The honest assessment: this critique is only half right. The tools have lock-in, but the underlying skills (prompt design, context curation, agent orchestration) port across tools with surprising ease. Teams that invested in skills rather than tools have very little lock-in. Hotz's critique applies precisely to the teams that invested in tools.

Critique 4) Real Output Falls Short of Marketing Promises

The fourth critique is the most quoted. Cursor, Devin, and Claude Code all market themselves around "agents that ship pull requests on their own" and "agents that resolve incidents end to end," but observed adoption hovers around thirty percent of the promised behavior. Devin in particular has a reputation for a wide gap between demo and reality. Cursor's Agent mode handles single-function changes well but stumbles on multi-file refactors without human guidance.

The honest assessment: this critique varies widely by tool. Claude Code delivers close to what it promises. Devin does not. Lumping every agent together under one label is the weakness in Hotz's framing. The critique sharpens when you evaluate tools one at a time, not in aggregate.

The Market's Five-Point Answer

While Hotz argues, the market votes with payments and hires. Five data points sit on the other side of the table.

Data 1) Fifteen Million Active GitHub Copilot Users

Active GitHub Copilot users crossed fifteen million in Q1 2026, roughly triple the 2024 figure. The "it is just free users" objection does not hold up either. Enterprise license share exceeds fifty percent, and quarterly churn sits around eight percent, well below the SaaS average. Price-sensitive enterprise buyers renewing for more than a year reads more like "expensive value" than "expensive mistake."

Data 2) Cursor Crossed One Hundred Million ARR

Anysphere's Cursor passed one hundred million in annual recurring revenue in 2025 and is tracking toward an estimated three hundred million by mid-2026. The interesting number underneath the headline is the average revenue per user, which exceeds forty dollars per month. Enterprises paying that rate are seeing ROI they would not pay for blindly. Payment is the most honest vote on real value, more honest than any survey.

Data 3) Claude Code Enterprise Adoption Up Double Digits Quarterly

Anthropic's Claude Code grew enterprise licenses by roughly thirty percent in Q4 2025, with similar growth continuing into Q1 2026. Of five enterprise teams the author has tracked closely, all five kept Claude Code in production for at least six months, and one designated it as the company-wide standard. The level of enterprise stickiness here does not match an "expensive mistake" pattern.

Data 4) GitHub's Own ROI Numbers

GitHub's 2025 announcement reported that Copilot users merged pull requests roughly twenty-six percent faster on average and that seventy-five percent of respondents reported higher work satisfaction. Self-reported numbers have known limits (covered in a previous article on the twelve ways AI coding measurement goes wrong), but the GitHub figures include git-log-based objective measurement, which carries more weight than survey-only studies. GitHub acknowledges some defect-rate trade-offs but argues the net effect remains positive.

Data 5) Hiring Signals Triple Year Over Year

Job listings that explicitly require "proficiency with AI agents" tripled between H1 2025 and H1 2026 across LinkedIn and major job boards. In Korea, "Cursor or Claude Code experience" now appears in roughly one in four senior backend listings. Hiring is the slowest signal to move, which makes it the most honest one. Companies that put a tool into the job description have already decided the new hire will use it on day one.

Both Sides Are Partly Right - A Synthesis

The synthesis is direct. Of Hotz's four critiques, the verification burden (Critique 2) and the marketing-versus-reality gap (Critique 4) remain strong. The token cost (Critique 1) and the lock-in concern (Critique 3) are temporary problems that the market should compress over the next twelve to eighteen months. Of the market's five data points, all five are strong on adoption but weaker on ROI proof. Adoption is the necessary condition for value, not the sufficient one.

One line captures the synthesis. AI agents are not an expensive mistake as a tool, but they can be an expensive mistake as an adoption pattern. Teams that ignore verification, skip token tracking, or skip user training prove Hotz right inside their own company. Teams that build verification, tracking, and training into the workflow prove the market right inside their own company. The answer lives in the adoption pattern, not in the tool itself.

Five Cases Where AI Agents Become an Expensive Mistake

The following five patterns, observed across both Korean and global teams, predict the expensive-mistake outcome with high reliability. Any one of them tilts a deployment toward Hotz's side of the argument.

Case 1) Auto-Merge Without Human Review

This is the workflow where an agent's pull request reaches main as soon as CI passes, with no human reading the diff. CI catches syntactic and shallow bugs, but business-logic defects pass through. A Korean startup that adopted Devin saw production defects double inside three months, and the post-mortem traced seventy percent of the increase to agent pull requests merged without human review.

Case 2) Heavy Agent Autonomy on a Junior-Heavy Team

When a team is more than sixty percent junior, raising the agent's autonomy almost always backfires. Agents produce plausible-but-wrong answers in domains they do not understand, and a team without senior reviewers cannot intercept those answers in time. The shipping speed feels great in week one and looks like a slow-motion accident by month six.

Case 3) Heavy Infrastructure on Light Workloads

Teams that process fewer than one hundred pull requests per month should not be building multi-agent orchestration infrastructure. One observed team of five spent roughly one thousand engineer-hours building agent infrastructure over six months while saving two hundred hours of actual work. That is a net negative of eight hundred hours.

Case 4) No Token Cost Tracking

This is the fastest path to runaway spend. Without per-user token dashboards and budget alerts, agent workflows escalate quietly until the quarterly invoice arrives. One observed company received an eighty thousand dollar quarterly token bill that nobody knew was coming until accounts payable flagged it.

Case 5) Tool Drop Without Training

Handing developers a Cursor license without four hours of hands-on training leaves roughly thirty percent of the powerful features unused while inappropriate autonomy settings stay enabled by default. The full price gets paid, the realized value stays at thirty percent of capacity. Training is the cheapest part of the deployment and the part that gets cut first.

Five Cases Where AI Agents Really Pay Off

The opposite list is just as clean. In these five cases, Hotz's critique mostly does not apply, and the math works comfortably.

Case 1) Bulk Migrations

Converting a hundred or more files in the same pattern (Python 2 to 3, JavaScript to TypeScript, Java 17 to 21, library version bumps) is the strongest agent use case in existence. The author's most striking observed example was a fifty-thousand-line jQuery-to-React migration completed by Claude Code in three days, replacing what was estimated as three months of human work. Verification ran through the existing test suite at near-zero marginal cost.

Case 2) Test Writing and Repair

Tests are uniquely well suited to agents because the verification step is itself automatable. "Write unit tests for this function" is a stable instruction, and running the suite is the verifier. Every team should adopt this case first. ROI almost never goes negative here.

Case 3) Documentation and Onboarding

READMEs, function docstrings, and architecture diagrams reward variety and consistency, which are exactly what agents are good at. One team cut the average time-to-full-productivity for new engineers from eight weeks to five weeks after rebuilding onboarding documentation with Claude Code in the loop.

Case 4) First-Pass Code Review

When an agent handles the first pass, human reviewers can focus on business logic and design. Agents reliably catch sixty percent or more of style, simple bugs, and surface-level security issues. Human review does not disappear - its quality goes up because the obvious work is already done.

Case 5) CI/CD Operational Automation

Auto-analyzing build failures, triggering rollbacks, and first-line incident response are all bounded problems with deterministic verification and human-in-the-loop safety nets. One SaaS team observed cut MTTR (mean time to recovery) to roughly one-third of its previous level after wiring Claude Code into its incident response pipeline.

The Five-Axis Decision Framework - Adopt or Hold

Turning those cases into an operational decision requires evaluation axes. The author uses five, each scored on a zero-to-three scale. Twelve or higher means adopt, eight to eleven means partial adoption, seven or below means hold.

  1. Axis 1) Verification Automation (0-3): Can agent output be verified automatically through tests, builds, or CI? Three if yes, zero if a human must review every output.
  2. Axis 2) Task Repetition (0-3): Are there more than one hundred similar tasks per month? Three if yes, zero for one-off work.
  3. Axis 3) Domain Knowledge (0-3): Does the team have at least fifty percent senior coverage in the relevant domain? Three if yes, zero for junior-heavy teams.
  4. Axis 4) Cost Tracking (0-3): Are per-user token dashboards and budget alerts in place? Three if yes, zero if not.
  5. Axis 5) Training Plan (0-3): Is at least four hours of hands-on training planned before rollout? Three if yes, zero if not.

Scored retrospectively across thirty Korean startups, teams with positive ROI averaged 12.3 points and teams with negative ROI averaged 6.8 points. The framework predicted the ROI outcome correctly roughly eighty percent of the time. This is a five-question pre-mortem that catches eighty percent of bad deployments before they start.

Korean SI and Startup Reality

Applied to the Korean market, two structural points dominate. First, Korean SI (systems integration) shops are structurally weak on Axis 4 (cost tracking) and Axis 5 (training). Per-month manpower contracts make it hard to pass token costs to the client, and training hours rarely get separately allocated. Korean SI deployments fall into Failure Cases 4 and 5 with depressing reliability.

Second, Korean startups are often weak on Axis 3 (domain knowledge) because senior engineer ratios run below thirty percent. Turning on Cursor or Devin in autonomy mode under those conditions is dangerous. Short-term output rises while six-month operational defects and technical debt pile up faster than the team can pay them down. For most Korean startups, the highest-ROI investment is hiring seniors first and agents second.

George Hotz vs Andrej Karpathy - A Difference in Yardsticks

Two figures sit at opposite ends of this debate. George Hotz calls AI agents "the most expensive mistake," while Andrej Karpathy calls them "a 10x productivity multiplier" in the same time window. The disagreement is real, but it is a disagreement about which yardstick to use.

Hotz measures cost - tokens, infrastructure, and verification time. Karpathy measures value - learning curves, individual productivity, and what becomes possible. Both are correct inside their own yardstick and weak inside the other's. Hotz undercounts value, Karpathy undercounts cost. The recommended workflow is to run both yardsticks side by side. Teams that maintain a Hotz-style token-cost dashboard and a Karpathy-style learning-curve dashboard at the same time make the best calls.

2026 H2 - When the Market Starts Answering

The second half of 2026 is the window where this argument starts to resolve, at least partially. Two signals to watch. First, token prices should fall another forty to fifty percent by year-end, which cuts Hotz's Critique 1 in half. Second, multi-agent verification standards are starting to crystallize, which addresses part of Critique 2.

A full bubble burst is unlikely. Adoption rates are too high, churn is too low, and revenue growth is too steep for a clean crash. What is far more likely is price normalization, tool consolidation, and the emergence of shared workflow standards. By 2027 the question "were AI agents an expensive mistake" will probably feel outdated. The replacement question will be "which adoption patterns generated positive ROI," which is a far more useful question to ask.

Conclusion + A Working Position

One sentence summary. AI agents are not an expensive mistake, but they can become an expensive mistake. The tools are neutral. The deployment pattern produces the outcome. Hotz's critique lands precisely on bad deployment patterns, and the market's data measures the outcomes of good deployment patterns. The two sides argue past each other because they have packed different deployment patterns inside the same word.

Three immediate recommendations to close. First, score yourself against the five-axis framework before adoption. If the score is below twelve, hold. Second, build a per-user token dashboard at the same time the tool rolls out. Adoption without cost tracking always escalates. Third, evaluate tools individually, never in aggregate. Claude Code and Devin are not the same category. The same company in the same quarter can run one with positive ROI and another with negative ROI. Following these three steps keeps Hotz's critique from applying to your company and lets you verify the market's data against your own results.
