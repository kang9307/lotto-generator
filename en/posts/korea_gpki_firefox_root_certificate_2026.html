Korea's GPKI Root Certificate and the Firefox Root Store 2026 - What Is at Stake

Why a National Government Wants Its Root in Your Browser

2026-06-10 Tech & IT

Introduction: The Weight of Putting a Government Root Into a Browser

In June 2026, a one-line item circulated on tech communities such as GeekNews: the Republic of Korea's government is once again attempting to add its GPKI root certificate to Mozilla's (Firefox) root store. The goal is to make browsers smoothly trust the HTTPS certificates used by .go.kr government websites. On the surface this looks like a mundane administrative convenience. Yet anyone who knows a little about security feels a heavy weight in that sentence. "Making a browser trust a government-operated root certificate" touches one of the most sensitive parts of how trust works on the internet.

This article is not written to take sides. Its purpose is to explain what GPKI and root certificates are, why the government wants this in browsers, why earlier attempts were rejected or shelved, and why trusting a state-operated CA invites a security and privacy debate - presented fact-first and with both perspectives balanced. We avoid political conclusions, and where things are uncertain we mark them as "according to reporting" or "under discussion." The aim is to give you the material to judge for yourself, not to force a verdict.

1. What Is a Root Certificate - The Anchor of Browser Trust

To understand this issue, you first need to know what a root certificate is. When you visit a site that starts with https://, your browser checks, using a certificate, that the site really is what it claims to be. That certificate is not trusted in isolation; it forms a chain of signatures going upward. The site certificate is signed by an intermediate CA, and that intermediate is in turn signed by a top-level root CA.

At the very top of this chain sits the root certificate, and it lives in a root store pre-installed in your browser or operating system. The browser treats only the roots in this store as "unconditionally trusted starting points." In other words, a root certificate is the anchor of trust. Any certificate that climbs the chain and reaches a root inside the trust store is considered valid.

A root certificate is the origin of trust on the internet. To trust it is to promise to believe, without question, every certificate that root has signed.

Here is the crux. When an organization's root enters a browser's store, the browser automatically trusts every certificate that organization issues. That is why deciding which roots to accept is done with great care. The trust of a single root directly affects the communication security of hundreds of millions of users.

2. What Is GPKI - Korea's Government Public Key Infrastructure

GPKI stands for Government Public Key Infrastructure; in Korea it is called the "administrative electronic signature" (haengjeong-jeonja-seomyeong). It is a government-only public key infrastructure used for signing electronic documents between agencies, authenticating civil servants, and accessing administrative systems. The Government PKI management center (gpki.go.kr) operates it, structured into a Root CA, CAs, and Registration Authorities (RAs).

According to public records, the GPKI root and subordinate CAs have been operated under the Korea Local Information Research & Development Institute (KLID) and the Ministry of the Interior (MOI/MOIS) lineage. GPKI began as an internal government authentication system. Over time, however, its use expanded into HTTPS for public websites - that is, the web security certificates that ordinary citizens reach through their browsers - which raised the question of whether mainstream browsers trust these certificates.

This is precisely where the friction arises. When GPKI was used only inside government, agencies could simply install the root manually on government PCs. But the commercial browsers ordinary citizens use - Chrome, Firefox, Safari - do not trust the GPKI root by default. As a result, citizens have encountered certificate warnings on government sites or have had to install separate security programs. That is the motivation behind putting the root into browser stores.

3. Why the Government Wants the Root in Browsers

From the government's standpoint, the reasons for formally registering the GPKI root in Mozilla's store are fairly clear. Combining reporting and official guidance, they break down as follows.

  • Better accessibility: Letting citizens reach .go.kr sites securely without installing extra programs or manually registering certificates. Extra installs are an entry barrier and can themselves become a vector for security incidents.
  • Compatibility: Firefox uses its own root store independent of the operating system. So even installing a root in Windows may not make Firefox trust it. Inclusion in Mozilla's store closes that gap.
  • Moving away from plugin dependence: It aligns with the long-running effort to escape Korea's notorious reliance on ActiveX, plugins, and downloadable executables on government and financial sites, toward standard web security.
  • International standards alignment: Having government certificates validated within the global browser trust framework carries weight for the international credibility of digital government.

In short, the government's case is "citizen convenience and standardization." That is a legitimate goal in itself. The catch is that the means to that goal is planting a state-operated root at the root of browser trust. And that is exactly where Mozilla's strict review and the security community's concerns enter.

4. The Background of Past Attempts and Rejections

This is not the first time. Combining Mozilla's public bug tracker (Bugzilla) and reporting, attempts to include Korean government-affiliated roots or subordinate CAs in Mozilla have occurred repeatedly since around 2015. KLID applied for GPKI root inclusion, and the MOI lineage pursued separate filings. But these requests did not progress smoothly.

According to public records, the issues raised were roughly as follows. This is a factual summary grounded in Mozilla policy and public discussion, not a political judgment.

  • Confidential documents: The Certificate Practice Statements (CP/CPS) and audit documents of some subordinate CAs in the chain of trust were kept confidential. Mozilla policy requires that every CA in the chain be publicly disclosed and audited, or technically constrained, so confidential subordinate CAs stall evaluation.
  • Certificate misissuance: A number of misissued certificates were reported from some CAs, along with operational issues such as OCSP (certificate revocation check) timeouts.
  • Certificates containing IP addresses: A self-assessment stated that SSL certificates did not include IP addresses, yet examples were found showing otherwise - a point that undermines confidence in policy compliance.
  • Audit concerns: Questions about the adequacy of the audit work for Korea's GPKI were also raised as separate bugs at the accounting-firm level.

In short, the fairest reading is that past deferrals stemmed largely from technical and procedural reasons - a lack of documentation meeting the same transparency, audit, and policy-compliance standards Mozilla applies to every CA - rather than "because it is the Korean government." That said, parts of the security community have also long criticized GPKI's certificate-management practices themselves.

5. The 2026 Renewed Attempt - What Has Changed

The 2026 movement appears tied to an inclusion request for a new SSL root CA in the Ministry of the Interior and Safety (MOIS) lineage. According to Mozilla's public records (a request tracked under a CCADB case number), this request has seen renewed activity and review through 2026. According to reporting, Mozilla asked for additional context before opening a broader public discussion.

The items Mozilla is reported to have requested include the following.

  • Accounting for past history: Acknowledge and explain known issues, concerns, or incidents associated with earlier Korean government PKI hierarchies.
  • Explaining structural differences: Describe how the new SSL certificate hierarchy differs from earlier ones in PKI architecture, scope, and governance.
  • Operational and oversight improvements: Explain what has changed in operational management, oversight, and compliance practices compared to the past.
  • Meeting current technical requirements: Be aware of and follow current CA/Browser Forum requirements such as multi-perspective issuance corroboration (MPIC) and CAA checks.

So the heart of 2026 is not simply "they applied again," but how the government answers Mozilla's demand to demonstrate how it has repaired past trust damage and what makes the new system different. This remains under review and discussion; we want to be clear that no final outcome has been settled.

6. Mozilla's Root Policy - Why So Strict

Understanding why Mozilla is so demanding about inclusion reveals that this whole process is not aimed at any one country but is the consistent application of principles. Mozilla operates its own Mozilla Root Store Policy, built on top of the industry-standard CA/Browser Forum Baseline Requirements.

The core principles are as follows.

  • Full transparency: The CP/CPS and audit reports of every CA in the chain of trust must be public. Secret subordinate CAs are not allowed.
  • Independent audits: CAs must undergo regular independent audits against recognized standards such as WebTrust.
  • No misissuance: Misissuing certificates, or issuing without proper authorization, is grounds for losing trust.
  • No MITM certificates: A CA that issues certificates usable for intercepting communications can be denied inclusion or removed.
  • Governance scrutiny: Mozilla explicitly reserves the right to deny inclusion if it judges a CA poses undue risk to user security. This includes cases where a government forces users to install a root or has used certificates to intercept communications.

These principles apply equally to every CA, not to one country. Commercial CAs and government CAs alike must clear the same bar. So GPKI's demanding review is more accurately seen as the result of standard application than as discrimination. At the same time, watching whether these standards are genuinely applied consistently across all CAs is also the community's job. 7. The Security Debate Over National CAs - Both Sides

Now the most sensitive part. Why does a "government-operated root CA" sit at the center of security debate? The crux is that the power of a root CA is inherently enormous.

7.1 The Concern Side

A trusted root CA can technically issue a valid certificate for any domain. If it wished, it could mint a certificate for google.com or your-bank.com, and the browser would accept it without warning because it came from a trusted root. Abused, this enables man-in-the-middle (MITM) interception: an attacker sits between the user and the site, decrypts and monitors encrypted traffic, and the browser shows no warning at all.

The case that proved this is not hypothetical is Kazakhstan. In 2019 and 2020, the Kazakh government told citizens, through national ISPs, to install a government-issued root certificate on their devices. Once installed, it allowed HTTPS traffic to be intercepted and decrypted - messages, passwords, transactions. In response, Mozilla, Google, Apple, and Microsoft blocked the government root at the browser level so that it would not be trusted even if installed manually. Because of this precedent, the security community is instinctively cautious about admitting government-operated roots.

7.2 The "Can Be Trusted" Side

Yet asserting "government CA, therefore dangerous" is not fair. Many countries run lawful, transparent government PKIs, and some earn browser trust through rigorous audits. What matters is not whether the operator is a government or a private firm, but the transparency of governance and oversight. The balanced view is that a government root can be trusted when the following hold.

  • All CP/CPS and audit reports are public and pass independent audits.
  • The scope of certificate issuance is clearly constrained, and issuance is publicly verifiable through Certificate Transparency (CT) logs.
  • It does not issue MITM certificates and has neither the motive nor a history of forced installation.
  • There is a fast, transparent response process for misissuance incidents.

Ultimately the question is not the binary "government or not," but whether the specific root can demonstrate the criteria above. Kazakhstan was blocked not because it was a government but because the interception purpose was manifest; GPKI's review is demanding because it seeks to confirm whether past transparency and operational issues have been sufficiently resolved.

8. Certificate Transparency and Technical Safeguards

Fortunately, the modern certificate ecosystem has technical safeguards that reduce these risks. The most prominent is Certificate Transparency (CT), a system that requires every publicly issued TLS certificate to be recorded in public logs anyone can verify.

The significance is large. If a CA secretly issues a certificate for a domain, that issuance is recorded in public logs where a domain owner or security researcher can detect it. In other words, covert misissuance becomes increasingly hard. Browsers are moving toward trusting certificates not recorded in CT logs less and less.

On top of this, Mozilla has been tightening CA practices through measures like Root Store Policy v3.0 in 2025. For example, dual-purpose roots that serve both website and email use are required to split into dedicated hierarchies by the end of 2028 - part of managing the life cycle and accountability of root CAs more strictly. This trend means a higher bar for every root seeking new inclusion. As with broader debates over digital trust and control - see our piece on the AI image-censorship debate in Korean communities - it all converges on the question of "who trusts and controls what."

9. What This Practically Means for Users

So what should an ordinary user do about this news? The bottom line: there is almost nothing to do right now. This is a trust-review process between a browser maker and a certificate operator, not a matter that requires you to install a certificate or change settings.

That said, a few things are worth knowing.

  • Do not casually install roots yourself: Always be wary of unexplained prompts saying "install this certificate." The core of the Kazakhstan case was exactly a manual-install demand.
  • Do not ignore browser warnings: On government sites or anywhere else, when a certificate warning appears, do not just click through - it is safer to find out why.
  • Watch the transparency of the review: Which roots are trusted is tied to everyone's communication security. Caring whether Mozilla's public discussion and decisions follow consistent standards is part of being an engaged citizen.
  • Guard against both panic and apathy: Neither the exaggeration that "the government sees everything" nor the indifference of "as long as it's convenient" is healthy. A fact-based sense of balance is what is needed.

10. A Comparative Lens - Other Countries and Commercial CAs

Treating this as a uniquely Korean affair misses the point. In fact, nearly every CA in the world stands before the same review bar. The table below simplifies the main perspectives around trust inclusion.

Aspect Commercial CA Government / National CA
Main motive Selling certificates / web security services Public service authentication / citizen access
Core concern Sloppy commercial operations / misissuance Interception potential / forced-install history
Trust conditions Public audits / policy compliance Public audits / policy compliance / governance transparency
Blocking precedent Distrust of misissuing CAs has happened Kazakhstan government root blocked (2019-2020)

As the table shows, even commercial CAs lose trust if misissuance is severe. The extra concern attached to government CAs is that "a state may have both the motive and the power to intercept communications," but the point is that this too can be offset by transparent governance and technical safeguards. The decisive yardstick is not origin but verifiability.

11. Open Questions - What to Watch

This matter is still in progress, and several questions remain worth watching.

  • Proof that past problems are resolved: Is the new SSL root hierarchy clearly separated from the earlier misissuance and confidentiality issues, and can that difference be documented?
  • Audit credibility: Are independent, recognized audits performed consistently and their results made public?
  • Constrained scope: Is the government root technically constrained to issue certificates only within a clearly defined scope such as .go.kr?
  • Openness of review: Is Mozilla's evaluation conducted transparently through public discussion so the community can participate in verification?

As answers to these accumulate, the shape of whether this request is accepted or deferred will emerge. And the process itself is a living textbook of how the internet's trust system actually works.

Conclusion: The Root of Trust Grows Only on Transparency

Korea's attempt to add its GPKI root certificate to the Firefox store touches, beyond a mere administrative convenience, the fundamental question of how internet trust is constituted and verified. The government's stated case - citizen convenience and standardization - is legitimate, and at the same time the security community's caution about state-operated roots is a reasonable wariness grounded in real precedents like Kazakhstan. Rather than declaring one side wholly right, recognizing both sides' legitimate concerns is the starting point.

It all converges on one point: the root of trust grows healthily only on transparency. Whether government or commercial, every CA must clear the same bar of disclosure, audit, and technical constraint. If GPKI can clear that bar transparently, trust will be granted justly; if it cannot, deferral is the right outcome. Calmly watching whether this process is applied consistently rather than aimed at any one country, and whether it is verified in the open, is the most mature stance we can hold as users. No decision has been made yet - which is exactly why facts and balance matter most right now.
