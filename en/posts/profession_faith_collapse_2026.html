When a Whole Profession Stops Believing in Its Own Career - Lost Faith and Lost Jobs Are Two Different Events

Separating the fields where sentiment shifted from the fields where the numbers did, so you can tell what is inside your control

2026-08-09 Trends & Culture

Introduction: when the mood belongs to a profession, not a person

Aaron Horwath's Noema essay «Why Is Everyone In Tech So Sad?» is not a piece about personal burnout. The subject of the question is a profession, not a person. One individual failing to find meaning in their job has been ordinary in every era. But when people across the same field start saying similar things at roughly the same time, that is less a description of individual states than a signal that the story a profession has told about itself has stopped holding. The essay circulated in Korea after appearing on GeekNews.

The trouble is that in transit it almost always compresses into «AI is destroying jobs». That is not the essay. Horwath is writing about meaning rather than employment statistics, and his target is less AI itself than the pre-existing structure that AI has stripped bare. The opposite reflex, «this is all overblown, we have seen it before», is only half right too. In some fields the numbers moved before the mood did, and that shift is recorded in published surveys and payroll data rather than in impressions.

So this piece separates three things: which professions the essay is actually about and what it claims, how far those claims are supported by published figures, and how the faith curve and the employment curve move independently of each other. It closes by sorting what an individual can and cannot control.

The essay targets workism's beneficiaries, not «tech»

The title says tech, but Horwath's scope is wider: high-earning knowledge workers clustered in finance, consulting and technology. He sets them against teachers, nurses, plumbers and electricians, and the axis of that contrast matters. It is not income. It is whether you can see the result of your own work. Fix a pipe and the leak stops. Finish a lesson and a student's face changes. Whereas what a forty-slide strategy deck actually changed is often unknown even to the person who built it.

That distinction is the spine of the argument. The concept Horwath leans on is «workism», named by Derek Thompson in The Atlantic in 2019: the diagnosis that knowledge workers began seeking from employment the identity, community and sense of calling that earlier generations drew from religion. Layered onto it is David Graeber's «Bullshit Jobs», the claim that a substantial share of roles are ones their own holders consider socially pointless. It is worth noting that Graeber's evidence leans on self-report and anecdote rather than large-scale statistics.

The thesis sits where the two overlap. When a system that asks you to find meaning in work coexists with a structure where you cannot verify what your work changed, the gap gets filled with the appearance of being busy. AI performs that filling for you. The gap does not close. It becomes visible.

  • The dividing line is visibility of results, not income - plenty of well-paid knowledge work is exempt, and plenty of modestly paid office work is not
  • The occupations Horwath names are illustrations, not a ranking - nothing in the argument says one kind of work is worthier
  • Workism was already diagnosed in 2019 - AI did not create the condition, it arrived into it
  • Graeber's «bullshit jobs» rests on self-report, which makes it weak as a size estimate - it works better as a conceptual tool
  • The claim is not «these jobs are disappearing» but «the explanation these jobs gave themselves has stopped working»

Signs of losing faith - what the essay observed and what the numbers caught

Splitting the signs into two categories is the most useful move available on this topic. One category is observation and impression. The other is measurement with a disclosed sample and method. Both are information. They do not carry the same weight.

What the essay describes - impression-based

Horwath's scenes are concrete but not countable.

  • An observed rise in people saying they want to move toward analog hobbies such as pottery, knitting or painting
  • Fantasies of disappearing, taking up farming, or going somewhere off the grid
  • The description of an indescribable melancholy rising like a tide
  • A felt widening of the distance between one's output and any real outcome
  • Reports that efficiency rose while satisfaction did not rise with it

These five are observations, not measurements. The essay does not say how the sample was drawn, what the comparison period was, or how many people did not say these things. That does not make them worthless. Changes not yet visible in indicators are usually noticed first in exactly this way. But the moment they are widened into «many people feel this», the claim has outrun its evidence.

What the numbers caught - measurement-based

Large surveys published around the same period paint a picture with a different texture.

  • Gallup's 2025 State of the Global Workplace put employee engagement at 21%, down from 23% - the steepest fall since the pandemic lockdowns
  • In the same study manager engagement dropped further, from 27% to 22%, with the sharpest declines among managers under 35 and among women managers
  • Gallup estimated the economic cost of that decline at 438 billion dollars
  • In Stack Overflow's 2025 Developer Survey, 46% said they distrust the accuracy of AI output, up sharply from 31% the year before
  • In the same survey the share who said they trust AI fell to 29%, down 11 percentage points year on year
  • Yet 64% of the same respondents said they do not see AI as a threat to their job - down from 68%, a four point move
  • PwC's 2025 survey of nearly 50,000 workers across 48 economies found curiosity and excitement running about twice as high as worry and confusion overall
  • In that same survey, close to a third of entry-level respondents said they were worried to a large or very large extent about AI's effect on their future

The combination worth staring at is the Stack Overflow pair. Trust collapsed while threat perception barely moved. Put side by side, they describe something other than «I am afraid of losing my job». They describe being unable to vouch for the quality of what you produce while the expected pace has already been reset around the tool that produced it. That is not employment insecurity. It is a problem of professional self-confidence.

The faith curve and the employment curve move separately

This is the centre of gravity. Two sentences can be true at once. In some fields sentiment shook hard while the employment figures have not moved nearly as much, and in others the numbers moved before the sentiment did. Blend the two cases and you get conclusions that are wrong in both directions.

Where the numbers moved first

  • In the UK Society of Authors survey of 787 members in 2024, 36% of translators and 26% of illustrators said they had already lost work to generative AI
  • In the same survey, 43% of translators and 37% of illustrators said the income value of their work had fallen
  • 37% of translators said they had already used generative AI in their own work, against just 12% of illustrators - the same pressure gets answered differently by different trades
  • Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, analysing US ADP payroll records, found a 13% relative employment decline among workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations
  • In the same analysis that decline came from a collapse in hiring rather than mass layoffs, and was concentrated in occupations where AI substitutes rather than assists

Three conditions recur across these cases. The output is already sliced into discrete units of work, the buyer can judge quality quickly, and the buyer is free to lower their own definition of good enough. Where those three overlap, the numbers move first.

Where the sentiment moved first

  • Among developers the trust indicator collapsed while threat perception remains a minority view - the two indicators diverged
  • Managers saw the steepest engagement drop, and Gallup does not attribute that drop to AI - remote-work restructuring, reorganisations and cost cutting landed in the same window
  • Early-career anxiety is clear in surveys, but how closely it maps onto any individual's actual hiring outcome is not knowable from the survey design
  • Even inside one company, the gap in mood between deeply AI-integrated teams and lightly integrated ones shows up repeatedly across studies

The distinction is practical because the responses differ. Where the numbers moved, the work is repricing, renegotiating contracts and restructuring a portfolio. Where the sentiment moved, the work is redesigning the job and its feedback paths. Answering a sentiment problem with a career change is an overreaction, and answering a genuine collapse in commissions with an attitude adjustment is not a response at all.

What past automation shares with now, and what it does not

«We have been here before» consoles nobody, and the reason is that the phrase usually carries the conclusion while dropping the conditions. Put the conditions back alongside the cases and the similarities and differences sharpen.

The recurring shape

  • Early fear is always phrased as «this profession is ending» - what actually disappeared was usually a bundle of tasks inside the job
  • Price pressure precedes sentiment - buyers lower their bar faster than practitioners can respond
  • Wage distributions spread - those using the tool as leverage separate from those competing against it
  • The cost of the transition lands on individuals - a recovery in the totals repays nobody who crossed the gap
  • The new roles get named afterwards - during the transition nobody can tell you precisely what to move toward

What is new this time

  • Verification is harder - a broken cash machine announces itself, while an AI output fails plausibly, and recognising the failure itself takes skill
  • The entry rung is thinning - past automation left work a beginner could safely touch, and that is exactly the band shrinking now
  • The target is judgment-bearing intermediate output - drafts, summaries, translations and design sketches are unlike rule-bound repetition
  • Diffusion runs at the speed of provisioning an account, not procuring equipment - the capital cycle no longer acts as a buffer
  • One tool enters many professions simultaneously - it does not arrive one trade at a time as before

How far to take the «spectacle» hypothesis

The strongest claim in the essay is that workism is a spectacle: a system in which appearing busy, appearing important and appearing uniquely knowledgeable carry roughly the value of actually being those things. On this reading AI feels existentially daunting not because it removes jobs but because it strips away the human effort that had been sustaining the spectacle, exposing the structure.

The claim is powerful, which is exactly why it needs handling. Split it into what can be checked and what cannot.

  • Checkable - how many of the artifacts you produced last quarter were cited in an actual decision. Anyone can count that
  • Checkable - whether the decision would have gone differently without them. Hard to answer, but askable
  • Not checkable - the aggregate claim that most knowledge work is spectacle. That is an interpretive frame, not a measured finding
  • Not checkable - the causal claim that AI reveals the structure. Revealed, or merely easier to say out loud than before, cannot be separated

Interpretive frames earn their keep. They still need filtering before you attach them to your own job, because this one runs straight toward the conclusion that your work is meaningless, and that conclusion hits mood immediately. Borrowing the frame without borrowing its verdict is the safer handling.

What the «messy middle» is actually doing

The most practical passage in the essay is here: the inefficient middle made of collaboration, argument, exploration and relationships is not waste to be removed but the substrate of creativity and of the experience of working. Horwath grounds this in Teresa Amabile's research on intrinsic motivation, where the sense of small progress in meaningful work drives how a working day feels. Gallup's long-running finding points the same way, that people do not leave companies so much as they leave people.

The live question is what AI adoption does to that middle. Several things happen at once.

  • Automating first drafts removes the time once spent arguing over a draft - the argument does not vanish, it arrives with the material already finished
  • The unit of felt progress grows - three small completions a day compressed into one a week changes the experience even at identical output
  • Review displaces creation - reviewing is necessary work, but it does not deliver the same kind of satisfaction as making
  • The narrative of mistakes disappears - with no wandering, there is nothing to tell about later
  • Juniors and seniors get stuck together less often - being stuck in the same place was how the relationship formed
  • Ownership of decisions blurs - approving what a tool proposed and judging for yourself leave the same trace in the record

The response that follows is not «stop using AI». It is deciding which stretch to deliberately keep in human hands. That choice lands hardest when an organisation makes it, but an individual can still designate one stretch of their own work.

What you can control and what you cannot

Career anxiety is unusually draining because it routes energy toward the uncontrollable. Simply writing the items into two columns reduces the drain.

There is a trap inside this table. The longer the controllable column runs, the more everything starts to feel like personal fault. So the uncontrollable column stays on the page rather than being deleted. Neither the industry's pace nor the company's budget was built by you. The balance only holds when both columns are read together.

Practical responses to anxiety and burnout

One thing needs stating first. Nothing below replaces counselling or medical care. It addresses ordinary depletion, and states beyond that line need a different kind of response.

Things you can try this week

  • Build one path back to results - pick one artifact from last month and ask how it was actually used
  • Record judgment - not what you built, but what the options were and why you chose that one
  • Fix your comparison set - use yourself six months ago rather than the top of the field
  • Designate one stretch where you deliberately do not use AI - for keeping the feel of the work, not for efficiency
  • Keep one place where you argue with a person about a problem - the conversation that sets direction, not a review of a finished draft
  • Create one activity outside work where results are visible - not a career change, just one more place where outcomes can be seen
  • Log when the anxiety rises - it usually clusters after specific meetings or after checking specific numbers

Signals worth taking to a professional

These are not diagnostic criteria. Rather than assessing yourself, treat a match as a reason to book one conversation.

  • Loss of interest, or changes in sleep or appetite, running longer than two weeks
  • Flatness spreading into parts of life unrelated to work, making daily routines hard to maintain
  • Rising alcohol or substance use, with a sense of relying on it to get through
  • Negative thoughts about your own existence or life returning repeatedly
  • People around you commenting on a change you cannot feel yourself
  • Many employers run an EAP, and most countries operate a free mental health or crisis line that answers around the clock

The counterarguments worth keeping

Among the reactions the essay drew, these carry weight.

  • The privilege objection - the people able to frame this as a crisis of meaning already have stable income, and a conclusion pointing at hobbies or mindset cannot substitute for a discussion of public safety nets
  • The historical-repetition reply - the printing press did the same to compositors. But this reply erases the fact that a recovery in the totals repaid nobody who crossed the gap
  • Causal overreach - tying falling sentiment indicators directly to AI adoption outruns the evidence. Gallup's downward trend predates the spread of generative AI
  • Overstatement on the other side - «AI will actually increase demand» rests on thin evidence too. Both confident directions share the same defect
  • Scope confusion - treating knowledge work as one block smears together fields where the numbers already moved, like translation and illustration, and fields where they have not

Labour markets differ by country

The condition most often dropped from this discussion is labour market structure. The same tool arriving into different adjustment mechanisms produces different experiences.

  • In markets with flexible hiring and firing such as the United States, adjustment shows up quickly as smaller intakes - which is part of why entry-level indicators react first
  • In European countries with strong dismissal protection and collective bargaining, the same pressure tends to be absorbed as hiring freezes and shrinking fixed-term rosters
  • In markets like Japan, where long-tenure practice persists and labour shortage is structural, substitution pressure and scarcity operate at the same time
  • Korea shows a wide gap in adjustment speed between the regular-employment market and the freelance and outsourcing market - project-based fields like translation and design feel it first
  • In markets with large content outsourcing ecosystems, including China, price pressure reflects immediately at the level of individual contracts

These five describe general differences arising from labour market structure. They are not drawn from official statistics that isolate AI's effect country by country, which is why moving one country's figures onto another is best avoided.

Left unresolved

Everything that cannot be stated flatly, collected in one place.

  • The essay's descriptions of mood come without sample, method or comparison period. They are treated here strictly as impression-based observation
  • No causal link between Gallup's engagement decline and AI adoption is established. The study itself does not name AI as the cause
  • The ATM and teller story is usually told from data running to 2010. The trend after that was not verified here, so it should not be read as «still rising today»
  • No official statistics on AI's effect on the Korean translation and illustration freelance market were located, which is why the UK figures were not transplanted onto Korea
  • The Stack Overflow survey uses voluntary participation, so it cannot be taken as representative of the whole developer population
  • The Brynjolfsson analysis is working-paper stage, built on US payroll processing data, and does not carry over unchanged to other labour markets
  • Amabile's research is covered only to the extent the essay cites it, without checking the original sample and design
  • Horwath's own affiliation and background are confirmed only as far as the publication's contributor note states

Frequently asked questions

Which professions is the essay actually talking about?

The title says tech, but the real scope is high-earning knowledge workers clustered in finance, consulting and technology. The essay contrasts them with teachers, nurses, plumbers and electricians, and the axis of that contrast is not income but whether you can see the result of your own work. So it applies less to a list of job titles than to any role where the distance between output and outcome is long.

How is a collapse of faith different from an actual drop in employment?

The two curves move separately. In the UK Society of Authors 2024 survey, 36% of translators and 26% of illustrators said they had already lost work to generative AI, and the payroll analysis by Brynjolfsson and colleagues found a 13% relative employment decline for workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations. Meanwhile 64% of Stack Overflow 2025 respondents still did not see AI as a threat to their jobs. The two situations call for different responses.

What is genuinely different this time compared with past automation?

Verification, the entry rung, and the target. A broken cash machine announces itself, while an AI output fails plausibly. Past automation left work a beginner could safely touch, and that is exactly the band shrinking now. And the target is judgment-bearing intermediate output such as drafts, summaries and translations rather than rule-bound repetition. What repeats is the shape of the early fear, which always arrives phrased as the end of a profession.

What can an individual actually control here?

Not the pace of adoption across an industry, and not a company headcount plan. What usually stays in reach is building a path to find out how your work was used, recording reasoning rather than only artifacts, choosing your comparison set, and protecting recovery time. A long list of controllable items is not the same as a verdict that everything is your fault.

When does career anxiety stop being something to handle alone?

Nothing written here replaces counselling or medical care. If loss of interest or changes in sleep and appetite run past two weeks, if flatness spreads into areas unrelated to work, if alcohol or substance use is rising, or if negative thoughts about your own life keep returning, talking to a professional is the better move. Many employers offer an EAP, and most countries operate a free crisis or mental health line.

Conclusion: name what shook before deciding anything

Reduced to one sentence: a profession losing faith in its own career and that profession's employment numbers actually falling are separate events that may arrive together or apart. Translation and illustration show commission losses recorded in surveys. Early-career hiring in the US shows contraction visible in payroll data. At the same time developers show a trust indicator in freefall while threat perception barely moves.

The distinction is practical because a different diagnosis implies a different prescription. Answering a shaken sense of meaning with a career change is an overreaction; answering a genuine loss of commissions with an attitude adjustment is not a response. So the first task is not resolve, it is identification. What shook here: the meaning of the work, the structure of the income, or both.

The consolation offered by past automation is limited. A statistic showing the totals recovered repaid nothing to the individuals who crossed the gap. But concluding that this time is an unprecedented ending also outruns the evidence. What is certain is that the cost of the transition is again concentrating on individuals, which is why personal response and policy discussion cannot substitute for each other. If there is one small thing worth doing this week, it is picking one thing you made last month and asking where it actually ended up. Whether an answer comes back or not, the question itself points precisely at where the ground is moving.
