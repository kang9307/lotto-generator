tts-bench Arrives - An Open-Source Benchmark Comparing 55 Local TTS Models on Speed, Listening, and Scores

Three lenses that let you pick a local speech-synthesis model on any hardware, and the evaluation methodology behind them

Jul 11, 2026 Dev & Infra

Intro: 55 Voices on a Single Screen

As of July 2026, text-to-speech (TTS) has moved past the era of calling a cloud API for every sentence and into an era where a personal GPU - or even a CPU alone - can produce a fairly natural-sounding voice locally. In the middle of that shift, a notable open-source project has surfaced: tts-bench, released by GitHub user 5uck1ess. The project lines up 55 locally runnable TTS models on Windows, macOS, and Linux and compares them through three lenses - Speed, Listen, and Scores. It was also featured on hada (GeekNews), drawing attention from the developer community.

This post does not stop at introducing what tts-bench does. It examines, from my own angle, why local TTS is getting attention right now, why evaluating TTS models is trickier than it looks, and how tts-bench's approach tries to address that difficulty. I will not reproduce the full leaderboard of 55 models here - the project site is the accurate place to check current rankings. Instead, this piece focuses on the evaluation methodology behind that leaderboard, and on what criteria matter when you actually bring local TTS into a real project.

1. Why Local TTS, Right Now

The renewed interest in local TTS comes down to four factors.

1.1 Privacy

Sending text to a cloud API means that content passes through an external server. For services handling sensitive text - transcribed medical consultations, legal documents read aloud, personal notes read back to a user - keeping data from ever leaving the device is fundamentally safer. Where a prompt gets processed is a first-order question, independent of audio quality or price.

1.2 Cost Structure

Cloud TTS is typically billed per character or per audio second. For high-volume workloads - automated podcast generation, bulk audiobook conversion, an always-on IVR voice - that per-unit price accumulates into real cost. Local TTS carries no additional usage fee once the initial hardware is in place, so the unit-cost advantage grows the higher the volume, past the breakeven point.

1.3 Offline Operation

In environments with unreliable or no network - embedded devices, vehicles, factory equipment, air-gapped networks - cloud TTS simply is not an option. Local TTS works without an internet connection and responds faster since there is no round-trip network latency.

1.4 Freedom to Customize

Holding the weights lets you tune pronunciation for domain-specific terms, fine-tune toward a particular voice, or quantize to fit your hardware - adjustments a closed API simply does not offer. With a voice-cloning model, you can also reproduce a specific speaker's voice from a few seconds of reference audio, entirely under your own control.

2. What Is tts-bench - 55 Models Through Three Lenses

tts-bench covers 55 locally runnable TTS models: 14 with predefined voices and 41 supporting zero-shot voice cloning. Parameter counts span a wide range, from tens of millions for the lightest models to several billion for the largest, and the models themselves were released across the past several years. The benchmark measures results on three physical rigs - a high-end desktop GPU, Apple Silicon, and a separate desktop GPU - covering CPU, CUDA, and Apple Silicon execution paths. Installation is built on the uv package manager and Python 3.11+; downloading every model requires tens of gigabytes just for the per-model virtual environments, and well over a hundred gigabytes once model weights are included. The benchmark code itself is MIT-licensed, while each model's weights carry their own license - Apache 2.0, MIT, GPL-3.0, and non-commercial (CC-BY-NC-style) terms all appear across the set.

What makes tts-bench particularly interesting is that it does not collapse results into a single score. It keeps Speed, Listen, and Scores as three separate lenses, each covered below. The project also runs a companion Voting Arena that collects direct human preference through blind A/B listening tests - you pick between two clips without knowing which model produced either - with results feeding a live, continuously updated Elo preference leaderboard. That can be read as an attempt to capture, in data, the "it just sounds good" impression that automated metrics alone cannot fully reach.

3. Why Evaluating TTS Models Is Hard

3.1 A Three-Way Trade-Off: Speed, Naturalness, Accuracy

Calling a single TTS model "good" is hard because "good" quietly bundles together three axes that pull against each other. The first is speed - the latency to the first audio sample, and the compute time needed to generate one second of audio. The second is naturalness - how close the intonation, rhythm, and breath-like qualities of the output are to a real human voice. The third is accuracy - whether the words in the text are rendered as spoken audio without being dropped or mispronounced. The trouble is that these three axes are largely a trade-off. Cutting compute to generate faster tends to flatten prosody; pushing for more naturalness usually means a deeper model that loses real-time headroom. There is no single "best TTS model" - the right answer shifts with what a given workload prioritizes.

3.2 Objectifying Subjective Listening Quality - MOS and UTMOS

Naturalness is inherently subjective. The long-standing standard for measuring it is MOS (Mean Opinion Score): multiple listeners rate synthesized speech from 1 (bad) to 5 (excellent), and the ratings are averaged. The catch is that this requires recruiting listeners, running the listening sessions, and tallying scores every time - which is costly and slow. Assembling a human panel for every one of 55 models in a benchmark simply is not practical.

The alternative that has taken hold is a neural automatic MOS predictor such as UTMOS. Trained on real human MOS ratings, it converts a speech waveform into self-supervised-learning (SSL) representations and runs an ensemble of regression models to estimate a score that correlates well with human judgment. It does not fully replace a real listener, but it is practical enough to sweep through 55 models with a consistent yardstick. That is why tts-bench's Scores lens adopts UTMOS as its naturalness metric.

4. What the Three-Lens Approach Means

What I find notable in tts-bench's methodology is that it deliberately keeps the three lenses side by side rather than collapsing them into one. Each lens answers a different question.

4.1 Speed - Is This Model Practical on My Hardware

The Speed lens reports cold/warm time-to-first-audio (TTFA), real-time factor (RTF), and memory usage across CPU, CUDA, and Apple Silicon. This is a practical question independent of naturalness: can I actually run this model on the hardware I have. The project's results page, for instance, reports that a lightweight CPU-focused model achieves a warm response of around 100 milliseconds with a processing multiple in the tens of times real-time, while a different lightweight model on GPU reportedly responds even faster still. These exact figures shift with model versions and execution environments, so the project site is the safer place to check the current numbers.

4.2 Listen - Hearing the Impression Numbers Can't Capture

The Listen lens is a gallery of audio where all 55 models read the same set of prompts, browsable either by prompt (compare many models on one sentence) or by model (audition one model across sentences), in both default-voice and voice-cloning modes. This lens preserves the "how it just sounds" impression that does not reduce to a number, complementing the score metrics covered next. The project builds on this listening data with the blind A/B Voting Arena described above, collecting real human preference with model names hidden and accumulating it into an Elo ranking.

4.3 Scores - Reproducible Objective Metrics

The Scores lens provides three metrics: UTMOS for naturalness, WER (Word Error Rate) for how closely the pronunciation matches the source text, and SIM (speaker similarity) for voice-cloning accuracy. WER is computed by running the synthesized audio back through speech recognition (ASR) and comparing the resulting transcript to the original text, counting insertion, deletion, and substitution errors - lower means more accurate pronunciation. SIM measures how close the speaker characteristics of the cloned output are to the reference audio using a speaker-embedding model and cosine similarity, and only applies to voice-cloning models. All three can be computed automatically, with the same procedure, across all 55 models - which makes them highly reproducible.

Putting the three lenses together yields one methodological insight: a fast model is not guaranteed to sound natural, and a high-scoring model is not guaranteed to run in real time on your hardware. Keeping the lenses separate reads, to me, as a design choice reflecting the judgment that a single leaderboard cannot honestly capture the trade-off among these three axes.

5. An Overview of the Local TTS Ecosystem

Rather than listing all 55 models individually, here is a rough grouping of the local TTS models developers actually run into, organized by character rather than by ranking. The focus is on what trade-off each group makes, not on asserting a specific ranking.

5.1 Ultra-Lightweight, CPU-First Models

These models have parameter counts in the tens of millions or fewer, and are designed to run well faster than real time on CPU alone, with no GPU required. They are light enough to run on low-power edge devices such as a Raspberry Pi. The trade-off is that intonation and expressiveness tend to be relatively flat, which suits short, formulaic sentences like announcements or notification sounds particularly well.

5.2 Balanced, General-Purpose Models

Roughly around a hundred million parameters - larger than the ultra-light group but still comfortably real-time on CPU. Many combine multilingual support with a permissive license (one with few restrictions on commercial use), which makes this group a common choice for services that want to balance speed, naturalness, and licensing flexibility all at once.

5.3 Voice-Cloning Specialists

Models specialized in zero-shot cloning, reproducing a target speaker's voice characteristics from just a few seconds of reference audio. Some support cross-lingual cloning across languages. These are typically heavier, requiring a GPU with several gigabytes of VRAM, and - likely reflecting concerns around voice misuse - often carry non-commercial licensing terms.

5.4 Large, Expressiveness-Focused Models

Models with parameter counts in the billions. They often excel at emotional expression and subtle intonation control, but demand correspondingly more compute and tend to struggle with real-time responsiveness. They fit content-production use cases, where quality outranks speed, better than real-time conversational services.

6. What to Weigh Before Adoption

If you are evaluating local TTS for a real project, I recommend laying out these four factors in a single table before deciding.

FactorQuestion to answer
HardwareCPU-only, or how much GPU/VRAM is available
Language supportIs the target language (e.g. Korean) actually supported and quality-verified
LicenseIs commercial use allowed, or are there non-commercial/copyleft terms
Real-time needDoes it need conversational-speed response, or can generation be batched

License is the one that is easiest to miss. Some voice-cloning models perform well but ship under licenses that restrict commercial distribution, which can work fine through prototyping and then block you right before launch. Hardware requirements carry the same risk - a demo run on a high-end GPU is common, while the actual deployment target is often a modest server or edge device. Narrowing candidates against your real deployment hardware from the start is the safer path.

7. Local TTS vs. Cloud TTS - Trade-offs

Local TTS is not a universal answer. Setting it against cloud TTS makes the boundary clear.

AspectLocal TTSCloud TTS
Data privacyNever leaves the deviceRouted through an external server
Cost structureUpfront hardware, favors high volumePer-usage billing, favors low volume
Offline operationPossibleNot possible (network required)
Initial setupEnvironment and model-selection effortReady instantly with an API key
Peak naturalnessVaries widely by modelGenerally consistently high
CustomizationFree to fine-tune and quantizeLimited to provided options

My read: for workloads that cannot let data leave the building, workloads with volume high enough that per-unit cost adds up, or environments with unreliable networking, local TTS is clearly the stronger choice. For a small project that needs to validate quickly and wants top-tier audio quality immediately, starting with a cloud API and moving to local once volume grows or privacy requirements emerge is also a reasonable path.

8. Conclusion - Three Things You Can Apply Today

I read the significance of tts-bench less in the sheer number of 55 models and more in its evaluation methodology that keeps speed, listening, and scores separate. Choosing a TTS model is not a problem a single leaderboard can settle - it is a multi-dimensional decision that requires checking your own hardware, language, license, and real-time requirements individually.

8.1 Run a Listening Test on Your Own Target Hardware

Benchmark numbers are only a reference point. Do not skip actually listening - whether through tts-bench's Listen gallery or a model you download yourself - using the language and sentences you will actually use in production.

8.2 Check the License First

Before moving from prototype to production, confirm the candidate model's license allows commercial distribution. Voice-cloning models in particular often carry non-commercial terms.

8.3 Narrow the Family to Fit Your Workload First

For real-time conversational services, start with the ultra-lightweight or balanced families; for content production, start with the large expressive or voice-cloning families. Narrowing to the right family before evaluating all 55 models is the more efficient path.
