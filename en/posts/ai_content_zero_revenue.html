Making Got Cheap, Selling Did Not - What 42 Listings and Zero Revenue Actually Point At

When supply gets common, the bottleneck moves from scarcity to discoverability

Sep 2, 2026 Trends & Culture

The instinct that a finished thing sells is an old one, and back when few people could finish anything it was mostly correct. Only a small group could typeset, print, hold stock and push it into a distribution network, so finishing was already a filter. Having passed that filter stood in for a large part of the qualification to be sold at all.

That filter has almost disappeared. What disappeared, though, was the threshold on the making side, not anything about the buying side. This piece looks at the gap through one case: a public record of 42 listings that ended with zero revenue. The question here is not what that person did wrong. It is why the problem of selling stays exactly where it was even as the cost of making approaches zero.

This piece works from one public record and from published policy documents, and it describes structure only. It is not a guide to monetisation or selling, and it is not an assessment of any particular platform, service or person. Policy wording quoted here is as of September 2026, and it is worth reading the whole thing with the premise that no general conclusion follows from a single case.

The trigger - a record of 42 listings and zero revenue

In late August 2026 a developer published the outcome of his own project and put it straight into the headline. Two books made with AI, 42 products listed, zero revenue. It circulated through a Korean developer community, and the original is still readable.

Records like this normally get consumed in one of two directions. One is see, AI-made things do not sell. The other is the method was wrong, do it properly and it works. Both miss the place the record actually points at. The first builds a generalisation out of a single case. The second writes a prescription without first looking at what got blocked.

What makes this one worth reading is not that the number was zero. It is that the author identified the blocked point precisely himself. One sentence of his carries the frame for everything below.

I bolted on plenty of gauges to measure progress, and ran the whole way without a single gauge measuring demand.

Only what the original actually states

The easiest way to go wrong with a case study is to fill in numbers that were never there. So what follows is only what the original records. No figure outside this table appears anywhere in this piece.

Read the table through and one row snags. There was exactly one traffic plan, and that one closed on 25 August. In a structure like that, whether there were 42 products or one makes no difference to the outcome at that point. The number of things made is not a route in.

What is left when the cost of making approaches zero

Production cost falling is not a new event. Movable type, photographic plates, photocopiers, desktop publishing and web posting were all steps down the same slope. What is different this time is less the size of the drop than which line item dropped. The labour that got cheap is the labour of producing a draft, not printing or shipping. It used to be that the manuscript had to exist before the rest of the process could start, and now the manuscript is the cheapest part.

A misreading follows naturally from that. If making is cheaper, then the same effort spread over more items should produce proportionally more outcome. That arithmetic only works if the route from a made thing to a person scales with the number of things. It does not. The list gets longer while the time people spend looking at lists does not.

So it is more accurate to split the line items that fell from the ones that did not. This split carries the rest of the piece.

  • Fell sharply — drafting, typesetting, covers, tables of contents, appendices: the cost of acquiring a form. In this record, that is precisely what the 3,274 lines of code took care of
  • Essentially unchanged — the number of people actually looking for that subject. Better tools do not make more people curious about a topic
  • Essentially unchanged — the time it takes to trust a seller you have never encountered. Trust comes out of history, and history only accumulates through time
  • Essentially unchanged — the human hours spent answering questions and processing refunds, along with the liability attached to those answers
  • Actually rose — the cost of being noticed in the same place. As supply grows, the share available to one listing gets divided

The bottleneck moves from scarcity to discoverability

When scarcity was the bottleneck the rule was simple: if you can make it, you win. Few people could, so the made thing itself was the asset. Under that rule, pouring time into finish quality is rational. Only a handful ever complete anything, and the ranking gets decided inside that handful.

Once supply becomes common the rule changes. The asset shifts from the finished object to the route that brings people to the finished object. This move is arithmetic, not taste. If 100 people a day look for a subject in some category, the expected exposure of any single listing differs by three orders of magnitude between a category holding 10 items and one holding 10,000. That holds regardless of how well the individual item was made.

The same structure is stated in search documentation. In Search Essentials, Google notes that meeting all the requirements and best practices does not mean it will crawl, index or serve the content. That is a plain statement that what the making side controls and what happens on the visible side are separate things.

  • In the scarcity model, finishing was the finish line. In the discoverability model, finishing is the starting line
  • So putting the same additional effort into polish returns less than it used to. Nothing got worse; the bottleneck moved
  • Raising the count fails for the same reason. Forty-two listings are 42 instances of one problem, not 42 times the chance
  • Refusing to accept that move pushes every prescription in one direction: it did not sell, so make it better, or make more of it

What sits between being able to make it and it selling

Several steps sit between finishing and selling, and they are easy to overlook. They were always there, but back when making was heavy they were hidden behind the time making took. Shorten the making and only these steps are left standing in the open. The third column of the table below is the important one.

StepWhat it requiresDid generative tools make it cheaperWhy
Demand validationAn observation that someone will actually pay for this subjectBarely at allValidation is observation, not generation. Observation means putting something outside and waiting
Distribution routeA position in a list where a person's eye landsNoThe supply of positions is fixed. More items means more competition for the same slots
TrustA reason to believe a seller you have never seenNoTrust is derived from a record of past behaviour, and records do not compress
Reviews and track recordTraces left by people who bought firstNoWithout a first buyer nothing starts. The very front of the sequence is empty
Returns and refundsA channel for complaints and the handling of themOnly partlyThe wording of a reply can be automated. The judgement and the liability stay with a person
UpdatingFixing things when the underlying facts changeOnly partlyMaking the fix got cheap. Noticing that something changed is still human work

The notable thing in that table is not that no cell got cheaper. It is that the cells that did get cheaper are all clustered at the back. Demand validation and distribution sit at the front of the sequence and did not move, so speeding up only the back shortens total elapsed time without changing the result. This is the classic shape of accelerating a section that was never the constraint.

  • These steps are ordered. However well you execute a later step, an empty earlier step stops everything at that point
  • And the earlier a step sits, the more it has the property of not compressing under technology, because it depends on observation, elapsed time and other people's behaviour
  • So as tools improve, the speed gap between the front and back widens. The felt experience of that gap is being nearly there while nothing happens
  • That widening is not a problem of personal execution. The line items that fell and the ones that did not were always different in kind

What a listing with no history runs into first

A newly posted item getting no exposure at the start is structure, not spite. The field of recommendation and ranking has a name for the situation. In Methods and metrics for cold-start recommendations, presented at SIGIR in 2002, Andrew I. Schein and colleagues used cold start for the problem of recommending items that no one in the community has yet rated. The formulation is over two decades old and it still holds.

The reason lies in the raw material. Collaborative filtering builds item-to-item similarity out of records of what was viewed together, bought together, played in sequence. With zero records there is nothing to compute similarity from. New items therefore fall back on content signals such as title, description and category, and that route puts them head to head with every existing item using the same words.

One more thing stacks on top. When ranking is learned, already-popular items tend to be over-represented, which is the popularity bias problem Himan Abdollahpouri and colleagues addressed at RecSys in 2017. The very fact that mitigation techniques get researched separately implies the default: left alone, it tilts.

  • No first exposure means no first purchase, and no first purchase means no record to justify the next exposure. It is a closed loop
  • There are roughly three ways to open it: bring people from outside, tell readers you already have, or pick a narrow position where competition is thin
  • All three are things done before or during the making, not the making itself. Starting them after finishing is when they cost the most
  • Count does not open the loop. Post 42 items at once and 42 items each stand in front of the same closed loop

What happens on the platform side

When supply rises the receiving side responds. Only what is written in published documents is covered here. How an individual storefront actually surfaces a new listing is not public, and guessing at it would invert what this piece is trying to do.

WhereWhat the published document says (as of September 2026)What it targets
Amazon KDP helpStates that the number of titles you can create at the same time is limited to 10 per book format each week, with a note that anyone who regularly needs more can enquire about an exceptionThe rate of listing itself. It does not look at whether the content is good or bad
Amazon KDP content guidelinesRequires publishers to report AI-generated text, images and translations when publishing a new book or editing and republishing an existing one, and defines AI-generated separately from AI-assistedDisclosure. It is a reporting duty, not a ban
Google search spam policiesDefines scaled content abuse as generating many pages for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings rather than helping users. Generative tools appear as an example, but the test is purposePrimary purpose and scale, not which tool was used
Google Search EssentialsStates explicitly that meeting every requirement and best practice does not mean the content will be crawled, indexed or servedExpectations. It says diligence is not the price of exposure

Lay those documents side by side and one thing is clear. The test in the rules is purpose and volume, not tooling. Nowhere does any of them say a thing is blocked because AI made it. What they do say is that listing rate has a ceiling, that generation must be disclosed, and that mass production aimed at manipulating rankings is a policy violation.

The side effects, though, do not distinguish purpose. A weekly ceiling applies without asking why you are posting so much. A carefully made new listing stands in the same queue. This is the awkward position of the diligent small producer in an era of cheap mass production: the machinery built in response to other people's bulk uploads applies to them too.

This is a different axis from quality

A story about AI-made things not selling immediately attracts a story about quality: it was slapdash, so it did not sell. That explanation fits this case poorly. The author checked 72 items from the AI draft against official sources one at a time and logged the date of each check, and in doing so caught one scheme the AI did not know about and one AI error. Measured by verification density, this is not the bottom of the quality axis.

And the result was still zero. Which is to say the case demonstrates that the two axes move independently. A well-made thing not selling and a badly made thing not selling have different causes. The first is a discovery problem, the second a production problem. Mixing them produces the wrong prescription: diagnose a discovery blockage as a production fault and the next attempt goes into making it even better, and stops in the same place.

The mirror-image mistake exists too, that solving discovery is enough. It is not. Once people arrive, quality decides the outcome, and readers decide faster than you would expect. Tabs get closed before the content is read whenever a text reads as machine-written, and those signals and what misjudging them costs are handled in a separate piece. The two problems bind in sequence; neither one dissolves the other.

  • The discovery problem — nobody has arrived yet. Raising quality leaves this cell where it was
  • The production problem — people arrive and do not stay. Raising traffic leaves this cell where it was
  • The two cells bind in order. When the first is zero, the second is not even observable
  • So when a result is zero, the first question is not whether it was made well but how many people saw it. At zero views you have obtained no information about quality at all

Gauges for progress and gauges for demand

The most portable thing in the author's write-up is this: plenty of gauges for progress, not one for demand. That asymmetry reads like a personality trait, but it actually comes from the fact that the two kinds of gauge cost different amounts to install.

Progress gauges are cheap. Lines of code, pages completed, items verified are all observable inside your own workspace. Nothing to ask anyone for, nothing to wait on. And on most days the number goes up. A number that goes up supplies the energy to keep going.

Demand gauges are expensive. Demand is not inside you. Observing it means putting something outside and then watching that thing be ignored. And on most days the number is zero. Looking at a zero every day is not pleasant. A gauge that is expensive and unpleasant does not get installed. That is arithmetic, not willpower.

  • Progress metrics share the property of closing inside your own loop, which is why they read instantly and give a sense of control
  • Demand metrics share the property of depending on other people's behaviour, which is why they arrive late and mostly carry bad news
  • The consequence of the asymmetry is predictable. Time flows toward what is observable, and what is not observable gets deferred to the end
  • Cheap demand gauges tend to have one shape: put part of it out before finishing and watch the response. That is exactly the sequence the author wrote down as his next step

Read backwards, what would have made a difference

This section is not a prescription. Nobody knows whether the three items below would have produced sales. They are useful only for throwing light backwards onto where the blockage was. What they share is that none of them is about making; they are about order.

  • Demand observed first — a state in which the fact that people want this was confirmed before building. There are many ways to confirm it, and what they share is that it happens before completion. Confirmation after completion is not confirmation, it is notification of the result
  • Readers you already have — the most reliable route around cold start. An item with no history still gets a first exposure if a person with history points at it. But that readership is itself made only out of time, so it cannot be conjured when it is absent
  • A narrow niche — a position with few competing items. Narrowing is not free, though: demand shrinks along with competition. Narrowing succeeds or fails on whether anyone is still inside the narrowed space, not on how narrow it got
  • Inverting the order — instead of making and then announcing, putting out something small, watching the response, and then making. This order defers the pleasant part, which is why in practice it rarely survives contact
  • More than one route in — the row that snagged hardest in this case. With a single route, one approval decision becomes the outcome of the entire project. That was a risk visible before any building started

One caution belongs here. This list looks obvious in hindsight, and hindsight obviousness is a different substance from foresight. During the build only the progress gauges are visible, and they were climbing. In that state, stepping outside to check for a response feels like stopping something that is going well. So the list is not usable for blaming anyone. It is only usable for knowing the structure in advance.

Is zero a failure

In the revenue column it is a failure, and attaching some other reading to that column would not be honest. But grading the whole experiment on that one column hides what remains. Counting what remains: a reusable production pipeline, a procedure for verifying draft claims item by item, and a set of coordinates for where the process stops. The last of those is the most valuable and the one that does not show up as a number.

Nor is it accurate to use that list to call zero a success. The phrase I learned something can be attached to any outcome after the fact, and the moment it is attached it blurs the judgement. The real dividing line here is narrow. Does this result change the conditions of the next attempt? If it does, it was an experiment. If the same sequence runs again unchanged, it has stopped being one.

And this piece is not saying do not do it, nor saying keep going anyway. Both pieces of advice come from someone who does not know the circumstances. Making something nobody asked for can have reasons that are independent of whether it sells, and only the maker knows what those are. What that condition itself does to the work is handled in a separate piece.

  • Zero is not information about demand. Zero views means demand was never observed, not that it was observed to be absent
  • So no conclusion about the subject being unsellable follows from this. Whether it sells has not yet been tested
  • One thing was tested: a structure with a single route in produces zero when that one route closes
  • If the next attempt reflects that, the conditions change. If it does not, a better-made 42 becomes zero again

Cheap failure comes from the same shift

There is a symmetry here. The reason this experiment was possible at all is that the cost of making fell. Doing the same thing the old way meant writing a manuscript, engaging an editor, typesetting, printing and carrying stock. Under those conditions the attempt would not have existed. An individual making two books that might not sell was simply not on the menu.

So this zero is not the old zero. The old zero came with unrecoverable spending attached, and that spending capped the number of attempts hard. Now the cost of failure has fallen, and attempts can be multiplied by as much as it fell. Cheaper making and cheaper failure are two sides of the same change.

But it is not fully zero. One line item did not fall: time. The hours that went into 3,274 lines and 72 verified items are hours that actually passed in that person's life, however good the tools get. Which means the item genuinely worth economising in this era is not money but the time spent learning the same thing twice. If an earlier attempt does not change the conditions of the next, only the money got cheap and the time goes out at full price.

  • When attempt cost falls, raising the number of attempts becomes rational. The premise is that each attempt changes the next one
  • Break that premise and the extra attempts are just extra consumption. That is the trap inside cheap failure
  • So the discipline of the cheap-experiment era lands on feeding results forward, not on trying harder
  • The minimum form of that discipline is the demand gauge from the previous section. To feed a result forward you first have to observe one

Summary

Even as the cost of making approaches zero, the problem of selling stays where it was. The line items that fell are the costs of acquiring a form; the ones that did not are demand, distribution, trust and track record, which all depend on other people's time. And when only supply grows, the share available to each item gets divided. The bottleneck moves from scarcity to discoverability.

What actually got tested in this case is not whether AI content sells. What got tested is what happens to a structure with a single route in when that route is lost, and the answer was zero. The fact that there were 42 products did not change it, because count is not a route. Quality sits on the axis after that one, and its turn to be tested never came.

One sentence survives. Becoming able to make and becoming able to sell were two different events. The first has already happened; the second has not. Knowing that distinction going in is what keeps you from stopping twice in the same place. There is not much more to extract from this record, and extracting conclusions that are not in it is the fastest way to use such a record up.

What I Could Not Verify

  • The full composition of the 42 products could not be verified. What the original confirms is two books and 30 templates; what the rest consists of is not written down
  • The names of the three platforms are not in the original. This piece therefore attributes the outcome to no platform, and does not discuss any individual review or ranking policy of theirs
  • Total elapsed time and actual spending are not given as figures in the original. Everything this piece says about time and money is general
  • The outcome of the author's stated next step, publishing writing first to see whether it gets read, could not be checked at the time of writing
  • No storefront publishes how it actually surfaces a new listing. The cold-start account here is general material from published academic literature, not a description of any particular service's behaviour
  • That Amazon KDP introduced a three-titles-per-day ceiling in September 2023 was verified only through press coverage. The figure in the published help pages as of September 2026 is 10 per book format per week, and when or how it changed in between could not be confirmed from a primary source, so it is not stated here
