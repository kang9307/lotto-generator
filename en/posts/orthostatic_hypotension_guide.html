Why You Feel Dizzy When Standing Up - Orthostatic Hypotension: Mechanism, Criteria, Red Flags, Daily Care

The correction your body has to finish in a few seconds, and what happens when it runs late

Aug 29, 2026 Health & Wellness
⚠️ Medical disclaimer. This article is general health information and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. The figures below describe criteria clinicians apply under a standardised measurement protocol; they are not a tool for grading yourself. If dizziness keeps returning or interferes with daily life, speak with a healthcare professional, and if anything in the urgent signs section applies, seek emergency care without delay.

Introduction: those few seconds on the way up

You get up from the sofa and the edges of your vision turn grey and close in. A hand goes out to the wall. Two or three steps later everything is bright again and the day carries on as if nothing had happened. It is a familiar scene, and most of the time nothing did happen.

Still, your circulation was busy in those few seconds. Changing posture is not a minor event for the cardiovascular system; it is a sizeable volume of blood moving in one direction, and the body has to reverse that shift within seconds. When the reversal finishes on time you feel nothing. When it is slightly late, you feel dizzy. When it is badly late, you fall.

What follows covers how that correction works, where clinicians draw the line, which signs should not wait, and where daily life actually offers leverage. Diagnosis and treatment decisions belong in a consulting room, so the focus here is on the information that makes that consultation shorter and more useful.

1. What actually happens when you stand up

1.1 Gravity wins first

Lying down, blood is distributed fairly evenly through the body. Standing breaks that balance. The veins of the legs and the abdominal cavity are thin-walled, stretchy containers, and once gravity is applied blood pools in them. Even in healthy adults, standing produces an orthostatic shift of roughly 500 mL of blood toward the lower extremities (Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine, 2010).

Less blood returning to the heart means less blood ejected with each beat, and arterial pressure falls as a result. None of that is disease; it is physics. What matters is how fast the body answers it.

1.2 The pressure sensors notice, and push back

Blood pressure is monitored by high-pressure arterial baroreceptors in the carotid sinuses and the aortic arch, plus low-pressure receptors in the atria, ventricles and pulmonary vessels (StatPearls, Physiology, Baroreceptors). When pressure falls, these receptors fire less, and the autonomic nervous system reads that reduced traffic as a cue to change direction.

  • Parasympathetic activity is withdrawn, so heart rate rises
  • Sympathetic outflow increases, so cardiac contractility rises while peripheral vessels narrow and systemic vascular resistance goes up
  • Arterial pressure is pulled back toward where it started

This reflex is fast. It is not something you can consciously assist with; the adjustment is already under way before the act of standing is finished.

1.3 When the correction is late, the brain notices first

The brain is unusually sensitive to a shortfall in flow. It stores almost no oxygen and keeps almost no reserve. So when the correction runs a few seconds behind, the brain is where it registers first, and the signal comes up as lightheadedness, narrowing vision or the sense of sound receding.

There is more than one reason a correction runs late. There may simply not be enough circulating volume to return (dehydration, blood loss); the sensing and signalling may be blunted (reduced autonomic function, diabetic neuropathy, Parkinson disease); or the vessel-narrowing response itself may be suppressed by medication or another factor. The same symptom can therefore sit on top of quite different circumstances.

2. The criteria clinicians use - 20 and 10, within 3 minutes

The definition shared across clinical references runs like this. After lying supine for at least five minutes, on standing or on head-up tilt to at least 60 degrees, a sustained fall of 20 mmHg or more in systolic pressure or 10 mmHg or more in diastolic pressure within three minutes is taken as orthostatic hypotension (StatPearls, Orthostatic Hypotension). The MSD Manual Professional Version gives the same numbers as the consensus definition and specifies the same three-minute evaluation window.

Korean tertiary hospitals apply the identical standard. Seoul National University Bundang Hospital describes it as a fall of 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within three minutes of standing.

Falls that sit outside that window have their own names.

2.1 These numbers are not a self-assessment tool

The same 20 mmHg means something completely different depending on how it was obtained. The definition carries conditions: at least five minutes supine, the same arm and the same device, and repeat readings at set time points. A rushed reading or two at home rarely reproduces any of that.

So the value of knowing the criteria is not that you can label yourself. It is that you know under what conditions a reading is worth taking before you go in, and that you can follow what the numbers in the consulting room were actually measuring. Reading the two blood pressure numbers themselves is covered separately in Blood Pressure Ranges and How to Read Your Numbers.

3. It is not always dizziness

The symptom range from a brief dip in cerebral perfusion on standing is wider than most people expect. StatPearls separates common from less common presentations, and the second list contains several items nobody would spontaneously connect to blood pressure.

What matters most in that list is not the aching or the fatigue but the falling. Dizziness passes; a fall leaves fractures and head injuries, and the consequences of those reshape how far a person moves for a long time afterwards. Ranking severity by how unpleasant a symptom feels gets the order backwards.

4. Signs that need care right away

🚑 Treated as an emergency. The UK National Health Service advises immediate emergency response in relation to fainting when the person:
  • is not breathing
  • cannot be woken within one minute
  • has slurred speech or trouble moving after coming round
  • has chest pain, or a pounding, fluttering or irregular heartbeat alongside it
  • has seriously hurt themselves before or after fainting
  • is shaking or jerking, or has had a seizure
  • fainted while exercising
  • fainted while lying down
Add to that repeated falls, and any episode accompanied by one-sided weakness, a drooping face, double vision or a severe headache. When travelling for care, do not drive yourself; get help.

A single faint is common and usually not dangerous, but even then the NHS advice is to get checked, on the grounds that it is important to have it looked at. If none of the list above applies but it is your first faint, it is still worth having the cause tidied up once. Fainting during exercise and fainting while already lying down are singled out because they point toward different mechanisms.

5. The usual backdrops - why certain situations make it worse

The same person is fine on one day and greying out on another, because the conditions differ. Below are the settings the references return to, and which link in the chain above each of them pulls on.

  • Dehydration — there is simply less volume available to return. Bouts of diarrhoea, vomiting or heavy sweating are the classic setting, and Korean hospital health information lists reduced body water among the non-neurological causes
  • Getting up after a long spell lying down — extended time in bed blunts the regulation that answers pressure changes. MSD names prolonged bed rest as a cause of acute orthostatic hypotension
  • After a meal — blood is drawn to the gut for digestion, and if the compensating rise in heart rate and vessel tone falls short, pressure drops. The MSD Manual Consumer Version states that postprandial hypotension occurs in up to one third of older adults and virtually never in younger people
  • Heat and alcohol — heat widens skin vessels to shed warmth and takes water out through sweat, and both directions work against holding pressure on standing. Alcohol combines vasodilation with a diuretic effect, and MSD includes it among the associated factors. Hot baths and saunas come up as things to be careful with for the same reason
  • Age and underlying conditions — age-related change in blood pressure regulation is named as a background for chronic orthostatic hypotension, and prevalence climbs clearly with age. Conditions that affect autonomic function, such as diabetes and Parkinson disease, may also sit underneath

Reading that list explains why getting up to go to the bathroom after a summer evening of drinking comes up so often. Heat, alcohol, dehydration, a long stretch of sitting and a fast rise all land in the same moment.

6. Medication may be the reason, and why you still should not stop it yourself

Medication sits near the top of every cause list. MSD names drugs as a cause in both acute and chronic forms, and StatPearls lists blood pressure lowering drugs, diuretics, vasodilating classes, alpha blockers used for prostate symptoms, some antidepressants, some antipsychotics and beta blockers among the associated classes. Korean hospital health information likewise names vasodilators and diuretics as non-neurological causes.

It is easy to take the wrong turn here. The fact that a medicine may be behind it is not permission to reduce it. Stopping a blood pressure medicine on your own brings back the risk it was managing. Antidepressants and antipsychotics need a managed approach to any change at all. Dodging the dizziness that way opens a larger problem.

The order that works is this.

  1. Gather everything you take with the names and strengths visible — prescription items, over-the-counter products and supplements alike
  2. Write the date the symptoms started next to the dates anything changed: newly started, dose altered, or stopped
  3. Note which part of the day the episodes cluster in. The gap from the time you take something may turn out to be a clue
  4. Take that to the doctor who prescribed it. Whether an adjustment is needed, and in what order, is the prescriber's call

If several clinics are prescribing for you, this matters even more. Each of them sees only their own list, while your body runs all of them combined.

7. Overlapping causes - why self-sorting is hard

Dizziness on standing branches in several directions. The NHS lists inner ear problems, migraine, dehydration, heat exhaustion, stress and anxiety, low blood sugar, iron deficiency anaemia and medication side effects side by side as causes of dizziness. The way people describe them is similar enough that the description alone rarely separates them.

8. What you can adjust - the general rule and its exceptions

Below are the non-drug measures that clinical guidance and review articles return to. The strength of evidence is uneven across them, and the same direction does not suit everyone. The fluid and salt item in particular becomes the opposite advice depending on the condition.

8.1 How you change position

  • Going from lying to standing, pass through sitting rather than doing it in one move. Sitting for a moment gives the correction time to run
  • Spend the first few seconds after rising next to something you can hold — beside the bed, in the bathroom, at the top of the stairs. First thing in the morning and the trip to the bathroom at night are the two riskiest moments, and improving the lighting at those times alone changes the odds of a fall

8.2 Using the leg muscles

There are physical counter-manoeuvres used in clinical practice: crossing the legs, squatting, or isometrically contracting the muscles below the waist for around 30 seconds (StatPearls; Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine, 2010). The principle is simple — squeeze the pooled blood back toward the heart with muscle.

  • When you have to stand for a long time, mix in rising onto the toes and back down, and avoid parking your weight on one leg and staying still in a queue
  • When the greying-out feeling starts, sit or lie down rather than trying to ride it out. Pushing through and falling is the worst available outcome

8.3 Fluid and salt - the conditions diverge here

Increasing fluid and salt has long been part of managing orthostatic hypotension, and drinking several glasses of cold water at once appears in the literature as well. The catch is that for some people this direction is actively dangerous.

⚠️ When increasing salt and fluid does not apply. With hypertension, heart failure or kidney disease, loading salt and fluid is a risk in itself. The references state that this approach has to be balanced carefully against cardiac function and the potential for end-organ damage. The same holds when blood pressure rises while lying down (supine hypertension). Whether to increase intake at all, and by how much, is for a clinician to judge, which is why no quantity is given here.

Even without those conditions, it is useful to see whether episodes cluster on days when intake was clearly low or sweating was heavy. That pattern is itself something to bring to a consultation.

8.4 Compression garments and bed angle

  • Compression stockings and abdominal binders are used, and some references note that abdominal compression is more effective than lower extremity compression (StatPearls)
  • The wrong pressure or fit leaves you with no benefit and some discomfort, and where circulation is already compromised the garment itself may not be appropriate, so it is worth asking before buying. Raising the head of the bed slightly during sleep also appears in the literature; this means tilting the whole bed, which is a different thing from stacking more pillows

8.5 Meals and alcohol

  • Large, carbohydrate-heavy meals are mentioned in connection with post-meal pressure falls, and the literature favours smaller meals taken more often over one large one
  • If something requires you to get up and move straight after eating, shifting that by a little changes the conditions
  • Standing up after drinking stacks several factors at once, and the middle-of-the-night trip to the bathroom is the classic case. Hot baths and saunas are likewise on the list Korean hospital health information suggests avoiding

9. A record makes the consultation shorter

The most frustrating moment in a consulting room is answering the question of when it started with a vague sometime recently. Orthostatic symptoms are hard to reproduce on demand and people are usually fine during the appointment itself, so a record noticeably shortens the diagnostic path.

  • Date and time — first thing in the morning, in the night, after a meal
  • The posture before it, and for how long — up from lying, up after long sitting, or after long standing
  • Duration — seconds, or beyond a minute. This links straight to the classification above
  • Accompanying symptoms — visual change, neck and shoulder ache, palpitations, cold sweat, nausea
  • The outcome — held on, sat down, actually fell, lost consciousness
  • Conditions and medication changes that day — fluid intake, alcohol, heat, meal size, sleep, and a mark on any day a medicine was started or changed

If you have a monitor, positional readings help too, but they must be taken with the same arm and the same device and noted with the time, or comparison is impossible. Tightening the measurement procedure itself is covered in Blood Pressure Ranges and How to Read Your Numbers. This record is not for grading yourself; it is material to hand to a clinician.

10. What changes in older adults - the fall as the outcome

The phenomenon is the same, but its weight is not. Frequency differs to begin with. StatPearls states that one in five community-dwelling adults aged 60 or older experiences orthostatic hypotension, and by age band gives under 5 percent at 45 to 49, around 15 percent at 65 to 69 and over 25 percent at 85 and above. The MSD Manual Professional Version gives roughly 15 to 20 percent of older adults.

The heavier point is the consequence. MSD notes that many falls in older adults may result from unrecognised orthostatic hypotension, and that it is more prevalent among those with coexisting conditions, particularly hypertension, and among long-term care residents. For a younger person, dizziness is a passing nuisance; for an older person, the same dizziness can lead to a fracture and the decline in activity that follows it.

There are things the people around them can see.

  • Whether a new habit of steadying against furniture on rising has appeared
  • Whether they flag noticeably after meals, or want to stay seated
  • Whether there has been a fall that is being waved off as nothing — repeated falls are a reason to be seen in their own right
  • Whether a new prescription started around the time walking became less steady, and whether the night-time route to the bathroom has lighting and something to hold

For an older adult, telling them to sit down when dizzy often matters less than whether there is actually somewhere to sit at that spot.

What could not be confirmed

  • The original autonomic society consensus statement behind these criteria sits behind a paywall, so the figures here rest on the MSD Manual Professional Version and StatPearls, which quote it. The two agree on the numbers
  • Prevalence data for the Korean population could not be sourced and is therefore absent. All prevalence figures here come from non-Korean references
  • The mmHg threshold and time window for postprandial hypotension were not stated in the consumer-facing reference consulted, so only the mechanism is described
  • Exact quantities for salt and fluid loading, compression pressures and bed angles vary between sources and depend on individual circumstances, so no numbers were carried over. The strength of evidence behind the individual measures is likewise uneven

Wrapping up

Dizziness on standing is usually a problem of a lazy reflex. Blood pools downward, the baroreceptors register it, and heart rate and vessel tone pull it back — the sequence just ran a few seconds late. Which is why most of the time it is not dangerous, and why changing the conditions a little reduces how often it happens.

Three things belong in a different box. First, syncope, chest pain, reduced consciousness, repeated falls and neurological symptoms do not sit in the same category as passing dizziness. Second, the fact that a medicine may be behind it is a reason to talk to the prescriber, not a reason to adjust it yourself. Third, advice that splits by condition, like fluid and salt, has to be matched to your own situation before it is applied.

One thing is worth carrying away. The measure that counts here is not how intense the dizziness felt but whether you fell, and whether you nearly did. Once that item starts appearing in the record, it is time not to postpone the appointment.

Frequently asked questions

Q1. What actually happens in the body when you feel dizzy on standing?

Standing moves a substantial volume of blood into the veins of the legs and abdomen. Less returns to the heart, pressure falls, and baroreceptors in the carotid sinuses and aortic arch respond by raising heart rate and narrowing vessels. When that lands on time you feel nothing; when it runs late, cerebral flow dips briefly and you feel it as lightheadedness.

Q2. What criteria do clinicians use?

After at least five minutes supine, on standing or head-up tilt to 60 degrees or more, a sustained fall of 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within three minutes. Falls appearing only within 15 seconds, or only after three minutes, are classified separately. Because the criteria assume a standardised protocol, they are not a tool for judging yourself from home readings.

Q3. Does it show up as anything other than dizziness?

Aching pain across the neck and shoulders (coat hanger pain), blurred vision, poor concentration and slowed thinking, weakness, fatigue and breathlessness all appear in symptom lists. If they arrive while upright and settle on sitting or lying, recording that relationship helps the consultation.

Q4. When should this be seen urgently?

Not waking within a minute, abnormal speech or movement afterwards, chest pain or an irregular heartbeat alongside, a seizure, fainting during exercise or while lying down, and serious injury from the fall are emergencies. Repeated falls or accompanying neurological symptoms should also not be put off.

Q5. Could my medication be the reason?

Blood pressure lowering drugs, diuretics, vasodilating classes, alpha blockers for prostate symptoms and some antidepressants and antipsychotics are listed among the associated classes. That is not a reason to cut down on your own. Bring the list and your symptom record to the prescriber and leave the decision with them.
