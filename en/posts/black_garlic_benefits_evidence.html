Black Garlic and the Evidence - Not Fermentation but Browning, and a Changed Compound Profile

Hold whole bulbs at 60-90C and allicin collapses while S-allyl cysteine climbs. Why a changed compound table means raw-garlic research cannot simply be carried across, how few human trials there are and how small their samples and windows run, and where the wording permitted on a package stops

Where the split happens in front of the shelf

You see a box of blackened garlic on a gift-hamper display. The packaging usually prints fermented and aged side by side, and smaller type below talks about antioxidants and vitality. It barely smells, it tastes sweet, and people who cannot swallow raw garlic can manage it. All of that is true.

The trouble starts next. An assumption slides in quietly: it is still garlic, so it inherits the garlic research. But ageing changes more than the colour. Allicin, the compound most people associate with garlic, all but disappears, and a compound that was present only in traces climbs several fold. When the profile changes, the evidence does not travel with it.

So the axis here is not good or bad but which question the evidence is actually sitting on. Other compounds sorted the same way include Propolis and the Evidence, Lutein and the Evidence and Multivitamins and the Evidence. If the marker itself is what you are chasing, How to Lower LDL Cholesterol Naturally and How to Lower Blood Pressure Naturally are the better fit.

1. What black garlic is - browning, not fermentation

Black garlic is made by leaving whole unpeeled bulbs in a chamber held at a set temperature and humidity for a long time. No culture is added and no additives are used. The 2025 review in the American Chemical Society journal Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry puts the conditions at 60-90C, 70-90 percent relative humidity, 15 to 90 days. A 2020 review in eFood spans the wider literature range of 40-90C, 60-90 percent humidity and 10 to 90 days.

The dominant reaction under those conditions is the Maillard reaction: reducing sugars and amino groups combining under heat to produce brown pigments and aroma compounds, the same family of chemistry that browns a bread crust. Because no enzyme mediates it, this is called non-enzymatic browning.

  • Fermentation has microbes as its agent. Lactic acid bacteria in kimchi, yeast in wine - living organisms breaking down sugars
  • The browning in black garlic has heat and time as its agents. What drives the reaction is temperature, humidity and duration
  • Product names commonly say fermented black garlic, but the process the literature describes is thermal ageing. The difference looks trivial until you notice that the word fermented drags along a separate set of associations about probiotics and gut health
  • Some processes step the temperature down. A four-stage profile of two days at 90C, four at 80C, four at 60C and one at 40C has been reported

In short, black garlic sits closer to the far end of cooked garlic, and the composition shifts along the way.

2. What ageing does to the compounds

Cut or crush raw garlic and alliin meets the enzyme alliinase to become allicin. That is the compound behind the pungency, the smell and the antimicrobial activity cited so often in raw-garlic studies - and it is unstable, so it does not survive heat and time for long.

As ageing proceeds the alliin family breaks down, and among the products S-allyl cysteine (SAC) accumulates. It is water-soluble, odourless and more stable than allicin, which is why it serves as the marker compound in black garlic research and product specifications.

That the ranges are this wide is itself information. Two products under the same name can differ in composition if temperature, humidity and duration differ. This is not a fixed compound but a processed food whose profile follows the process.

3. Why the raw-garlic evidence cannot be carried across

Advertising and press coverage usually explain black garlic like this. First the research on garlic in general, then a sentence noting that black garlic scores higher on antioxidant markers. Splice the two and you get the impression that the garlic evidence transfers to black garlic with a multiplier on top. That joint has nothing under it.

  • The central compound has changed. Much of the raw-garlic literature was designed around allicin and its sulphur family, and in black garlic that compound is nearly gone
  • What rose is a different molecule. The case for S-allyl cysteine standing in for allicin rests on shared chemical origin, not on a demonstration that it produces the same result in people
  • The phrase aged garlic hides two things. In the English literature, aged garlic extract is made by steeping garlic in dilute alcohol for a long period, while black garlic is whole bulbs aged with heat. Different process, different composition - yet coverage often collapses them into one term
  • A multiplier is a marker, not an outcome. An antioxidant capacity two to three times higher is reactivity measured in a tube, which is a different layer from how far a clinical marker moved inside a body

So judging black garlic calls for something narrower than the garlic literature: trials that fed black garlic itself to people.

4. How far the human trials actually go

There is no shortage of writing on black garlic's biological activity, but the layers are uneven. The activity list compiled in the 2025 review is dominated by systems that are not human - inflammatory mediators falling in macrophages, tumours suppressed in xenografted nude mice, liver injury markers lowered in rats given carbon tetrachloride.

What the table is for is not the conclusions but the scale. Randomised trials that fed black garlic to people run to a few dozen participants over 12 weeks.

  • The 2014 Korean trial did not separate on most lipid measures. What separated was HDL and apolipoprotein B
  • The effect size in the 2023 blood pressure trial was 1.8 and 1.5 mmHg. Statistically significant, but hard to call clinically large
  • That trial was funded by the manufacturer, with three authors employed by it. The limitations the authors themselves list include the absence of a dose-response analysis and the need for 24-hour ambulatory validation
  • Heterogeneity in the 2025 meta-analysis varied by measure - I²=29.76 percent for systolic pressure but 74.84 percent for total cholesterol - and, more to the point, that analysis was not restricted to black garlic

Immunity sits low even for garlic in general. The United States National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health notes that research on garlic and the common cold is sparse, that a 2022 review found only two studies, and that both were small with weaknesses in design. The same source says garlic supplements may reduce total and LDL cholesterol to a small extent, and that evidence for a blood pressure reduction in people with hypertension is limited.

Fatigue and vitality are a different animal. They lean on self-report and carry large placebo responses, so a felt improvement without a placebo control is not usable as evidence. The lifestyle axes worth looking at first are set out in Boosting Your Immune System: A Complete Guide.

6. Where garlic sits in the Korean framework

In the Korean system, garlic is an approved functional ingredient for health functional foods. According to the government policy briefing reporting the MFDS announcement, the claim may help improve blood cholesterol, effective 2 January 2014, was recognised, and the daily intake set in the MFDS notification is 0.6-1 g of garlic powder. The MFDS then stated, in the 16 August 2022 administrative notice of partial amendment to the Standards and Specifications for Health Functional Foods (Notice 2022-372), that may help regulate blood pressure would be added for garlic. That amendment reflected a 2021 re-evaluation.

  • Notified ingredients have their standards published by the MFDS, so any manufacturer meeting the requirements may use them. Garlic is one of these
  • Individually approved ingredients require the company to submit safety and efficacy data and pass review, after which only that company may use them
  • Either way, an ingredient with a disease treatment or prevention effect cannot be approved at all. The proviso to Article 15(2) of the Korean Health Functional Food Act says so explicitly
  • That is why an approved claim always stops at may help. Step across to lowers or prevents and you are outside what the framework permits

Here is where black garlic complicates matters. What is approved is garlic as an ingredient, with the standard written in terms of powder intake, while black garlic is a processed product whose composition has shifted through ageing. Whether a given black garlic concentrate, pill or preserve is a health functional food or an ordinary food varies by product, and the answer is on the label panel.

7. The one line that sorts the wording on the box

The same statement about the same ingredient is permitted in different forms depending on a product's legal status. Under Korean rules there are two forks.

7.1 If it is a health functional food

The package carries the health functional food wording and mark, and the claim appears exactly as approved - for garlic, in the form of may help improve blood cholesterol. The marker compound content, the daily intake and the cautions for use are printed alongside it.

7.2 If it is an ordinary food

Ordinary foods may also display functionality if they meet the conditions. The basis is the MFDS regulation on functional labelling or advertising that is not treated as unfair labelling or advertising, and the format is fixed.

  • Article 4(1) limits eligibility to notified functional ingredients and the like. Garlic is on that list
  • Article 6 sets the sentence form: this product contains an ingredient known (or reported) to help with a stated function
  • The same article requires the statement this product is not a health functional food to appear as well, along with wording that it is not for preventing or treating disease, a balanced-diet note and instructions for reporting adverse events
  • Article 4(2) prohibits wording aimed at children, pregnant or nursing women and the elderly, sexual function claims, and the disease-risk-reduction language reserved for health functional foods
  • Anything beyond that - protects, improves, prevents - and testimonials with no placebo control are neither regulatory wording nor evidence
Sorting it in three seconds at the shelf — if you can see this product is not a health functional food, the product is an ordinary food. The functional sentence next to it should be in the known or reported to help form, and known here means there are such reports about the ingredient, not that anything was demonstrated in this product. The distance between those two readings is the whole of that sentence.

8. Safety - where to ask first

Black garlic is a food, and it is not the kind of thing that generates serious reports at ordinary intakes. The 2014 Korean trial recorded no adverse effects across 12 weeks. The cautions that attach to the garlic family, however, remain.

Situations to raise with a doctor or pharmacist before starting
  • On an anticoagulant or antiplatelet — NCCIH states that garlic supplements may increase bleeding risk and may interact with anticoagulants or aspirin
  • Surgery or a procedure coming up — the same source advises telling the care team, since garlic may increase bleeding
  • A sensitive stomach or reflux symptoms — abdominal pain, flatulence and nausea are listed as common side effects of garlic
  • Allergy to alliums — ageing changes the colour and the taste, but the plant is the same
  • Pregnancy, breastfeeding or long-term medication — even for a food, the overlap with medicines is for the prescribing clinician to judge

One issue belongs to black garlic alone: with the smell and pungency gone, it is easy to eat a lot of it. Raw garlic gets the stomach to signal a limit early; black garlic sends that signal weakly, and concentrates or pills are compact enough to make it weaker still. If diabetes is in the picture, look at added sugar in the product as well - concentrates and preserves sometimes contain sugar or oligosaccharide, and the nutrition panel shows it.

9. Eating it as food - cooking and storage

Black garlic is a food before it is a supplement, and treating it that way makes the decision far simpler. Put flavour and convenience on the scale instead of functionality.

  • Character of the taste — as a Maillard product it turns sweet with a light acidity and a dense, jammy texture. Almost none of the raw bite survives
  • Uses — eaten a clove at a time, mashed into sauces and dressings, or stirred into a cream soup or risotto, where it leaves savouriness without a garlic spike. Since it has already had long heat, there is no reason to fry it hard again; adding it near the end serves it better
  • Storage — unpeeled, somewhere cool and ventilated; peeled or opened, sealed and refrigerated. The wetter the format, the easier mould finds it
  • Change the format and you change the amount — one clove and one sachet of concentrate are not the same quantity. The content figure on the label is the only shared yardstick

Making it at home means holding 60-90C for 15 to 90 days, which domestic equipment struggles to sustain, and a run that drifts cool goes towards spoilage rather than black garlic. As food the goal is flavour; if the goal is to move a marker, the question of which marker, over what window and against what comparator has to be settled first.

What is not settled here

The places left blank because no primary source was pinned down, gathered in one spot.

  • The marker compound and specification for the notified ingredient garlic — the marker and its floor were not located in the text of the Korean Standards and Specifications for Health Functional Foods. The daily intake quoted above rests on the policy briefing reporting the MFDS announcement
  • Whether black garlic is approved as an ingredient in its own right — the notified and individually approved lists were not searched for a black garlic entry. The 2022 blood pressure addition is likewise cited from a document at the administrative notice stage
  • A head-to-head human comparison of raw and black garlic — no randomised trial feeding both forms under matched conditions was identified
  • Black garlic trials with immunity or fatigue as the endpoint — no randomised human trial was identified
  • Drug interaction studies on black garlic specifically — the cautions above rest on institutional guidance covering garlic in general. Measured S-allyl cysteine content across products on the Korean market is also not included

Conclusion: not whether to buy, but what to treat it as

The two conclusions usually offered about black garlic are both wrong. One is that it concentrates everything good about garlic; the other is that it has no scientific footing at all. The real position is between them.

  • The chemical change is clear. Allicin nearly disappears while S-allyl cysteine and polyphenols rise, and several reviews point the same way
  • That change does not transfer the raw-garlic evidence. If the compounds changed, the evidence has to be built again. That is the axis of this article
  • Human trials exist but are small. Dozens of participants, 12 weeks, populations that already had a problem on the marker. Effect sizes were single-digit mmHg and a couple of lipid measures
  • The immunity and fatigue cells are empty. Not proven absent - simply not placed, within the scope of this article, on any trial that asked people that question
  • Use the regulatory wording as your baseline. The approved phrasing stops at may help, and for an ordinary food it steps back once more to contains an ingredient known to help. If you are on medication, the order is to ask before choosing a product

Reduced to one sentence: black garlic is a food with plenty of reason to enjoy it, and too few people have been asked about it for anyone to lean on it as a tool for moving a marker. What turned black is the colour, not the evidence.
