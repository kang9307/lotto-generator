Escaping AI Vendor Lock-In - Designing for Model Deprecation and Price Hikes

Swapping the call site takes a day. Prompts, eval sets, and embeddings are what you cannot move

July 27, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: models disappear, and the code is not the problem

Any team that has shipped an LLM feature eventually receives one of two emails. One says the model you use is being retired. The other says the pricing terms are changing. Neither is an exceptional event. In the first half of 2026 alone the major vendors attached shutdown dates to dozens of model snapshots, and as of July 2026 Anthropic's public documentation lists much of the Claude 2 and Claude 3 lineage as already Retired. Models are being operated less like infrastructure and more like consumables.

Yet the pain those teams actually feel does not come from code. Changing a model ID string and swapping an SDK is a day of work for a team that has done it once. The real trouble starts afterward. Prompts refined over months against one model's temperament behave subtly differently on the new one, there is no basis for deciding whether that difference is better or worse, and the millions of embedding vectors underpinning your search feature cannot be reused at all.

This article is not a tour of routers or gateway products. Those tools have their moment, but something has to be decided before you bolt one on: what to abstract, what to record, and where to pay down the switching cost in advance. That design judgment is the subject here. Every vendor policy and price mentioned below is as of July 2026, and because these values change often, verify each vendor's official documentation before making a real decision.

Scope note — This is a technical piece about architecture, not legal advice on contracts, terms, or data processing clauses. Service terms, SLAs, retention and retraining clauses, and data return conditions on termination should go through your organization's legal review.

1. How much warning do you get? Vendor notice policies

Planning starts with knowing how much time you are given. Fortunately the major vendors publish their notice windows, and the numbers differ from one another.

1.1 Published notice periods (as of July 2026)

VendorNotice periodLifecycle termsPractical caveat
OpenAIAt least 6 months for generally available models, at least 3 months for specialized variants, roughly 2 weeks for previewLegacy → Deprecated → ShutdownThe docs explicitly say preview models should not carry business-critical workloads unless you can migrate on short notice
AnthropicAt least 60 days for publicly released modelsActive → Legacy → Deprecated → RetiredPartner clouds such as Bedrock and Google Cloud set their own schedules, so the dates can differ
Google (Gemini API)Table dates are the earliest possible shutdown; at least 2 weeks for previewDeprecation → ShutdownThe exact shutdown date is communicated separately with advance notice

1.2 Two facts that matter more than the numbers

Read as a table, 60 days looks tight and six months looks generous. In practice the important points lie elsewhere.

  • The notice window is not a migration window. It runs from the announcement to the retirement date, and evaluation, prompt adjustment, canary rollout, and rollback preparation all have to fit inside it. Sixty days of notice is roughly forty business days.
  • Real cases hug the policy floor. Per Anthropic's public documentation, Claude Opus 4.1 was announced on June 5, 2026 for an August 5, 2026 retirement, about 61 days. Claude Sonnet 4 and Claude Opus 4 were announced on April 14, 2026 for a June 15, 2026 retirement, about 62 days. By contrast, Claude Sonnet 3.7 was announced on October 28, 2025 for a February 19, 2026 retirement, roughly 114 days. Plan on the assumption that the spread is two-fold.
  • The same model has different lifespans on different channels. Anthropic's documentation marks some models as retired except on Google Cloud, or except on Bedrock and Google Cloud. A model gone from the first-party API can still be served by a partner cloud. That is both a risk and an emergency exit.

2. Where lock-in accumulates: six layers

This is the core of the article. Treating vendor lock-in as one lump produces no plan. Split it into layers and the wildly different exit cost of each becomes obvious.

LayerLock-in strengthWhat is actually boundTime to switchWhat to do now
1. Call codeWeakSDK, endpoint, auth, retries1 to 2 daysConfine vendor-specific fields to one file
2. Output schema and tool definitionsModerateJSON schema dialects, tool call representationSeveral daysStore schemas as data, not code; own your validator
3. PromptsStrongInstructions, examples, and output conventions tuned to one modelSeveral weeksVersion them and split model-specific overlays
4. Evaluation setFatal if absentThe very basis for judging whether something is betterUndecidable without itBuild one from real requests today
5. EmbeddingsVery strongThe coordinate space the vectors live inFull regeneration plus dual runningPreserve source text and chunking rules; leave room for a second index
6. Fine-tuningStrongestWeights you cannot exportStart over from dataTreat training data and recipes as first-class assets

The message in that table is simple. Most teams fuss over layer one and neglect layers three through six, while ninety percent of the switching cost lands in three through six. The sections below take them one at a time.

3. Layer 1 - the call site is weak, but it is not free

3.1 OpenAI-compatible shape as a de facto standard

In 2026 a large share of new models ship with an OpenAI-compatible interface on day one. That genuinely makes it possible to point a client at another provider by changing the base_url and the key. The older surface area, chat completions, tool calls, and streaming, tends to line up well.

There is a clear limit, though. Compatibility leaks precisely at the newer surface the 2023-era spec never defined: how reasoning deltas are delivered, thinking-effort parameters, sampling contracts, and the finer dialects of structured output. In other words, the compatibility layer carries your average call across, but the more recently a good feature was added, the less likely it is to travel.

3.2 What still breaks at the call site

Calling layer one weak means the work is small, not that nothing breaks. Public documentation contains cases where a generation bump inside a single vendor breaks the call site.

  • Parameters disappear. Anthropic's documentation states that on Claude Opus 4.7 and later, setting temperature, top_p, or top_k to a non-default value returns a 400 error. Parameters you took for granted for years become errors in one generation.
  • Token accounting changes. The same page notes that Claude 4.7 and later use a newer tokenizer that produces roughly 30% more tokens for the same text. Even with an unchanged unit price, your invoice and your context headroom both move. Cost estimation and truncation logic have to be reviewed together.
  • Identical tool definitions cost differently. The system prompt tokens automatically added when you pass tools vary by model. In Anthropic's published table, with tool_choice set to auto, Claude Opus 4.7 is listed at 675 tokens while Claude Opus 5 is listed at 286. The more tools an agent carries, the more that gap compounds.
  • Batch and regional options move the effective price. Batch discounts and regional endpoint premiums apply differently across models. Anthropic's docs note a 10% premium for regional and multi-region endpoints.

3.3 So what do you do at layer one?

  • Do not scatter model IDs through the codebase. Inject them from one configuration surface. The strings buried in batch jobs, cron tasks, and eval scripts are always the ones left behind.
  • Keep retries, timeouts, rate limit backoff, and cost instrumentation in a shared adapter. That work survives a vendor change intact.
  • Collect vendor-specific parameters into one isolated file. Later, the size of that file is your migration difficulty.

4. Layer 2 - schemas and tool calls: similar shapes, different dialects

Structured output and tool use are conceptually near-identical across the three major vendors. You hand over a schema, the model answers in that shape, and the application parses it. The problem is that the names and the packaging differ.

  • The OpenAI family puts function definitions in a tools array and returns arguments as a JSON string.
  • The Anthropic family defines tools with input_schema and returns a tool use content block containing an already parsed object.
  • The Google family wraps function declaration objects inside a tool object, and leans on the function calling path for structure rather than offering a standalone final-response structured output option in the same way.

Another easily missed detail is what strict mode actually guarantees. Even where a mode enforces schema adherence, it may not enforce every JSON Schema keyword such as pattern, format, or minLength. If a vendor's strict mode was your last line of defense, it quietly gives way the moment you move.

The countermeasure is one sentence: keep validation outside the vendor. Do not hardcode schemas into application code; store them as data and render them into each vendor's format, then run whatever the model returns through your own validator as well. Those two habits alone shrink a layer-two migration to a matter of days.

5. Layer 3 - prompts, where the real cost begins

5.1 Why prompts do not port

A large share of prompt engineering is a response to one particular model's habits. This model produced overlong lists, so you pinned the count. That one prefaced everything with throat-clearing, so you added a prohibition. Another followed formats reliably when given two examples, so you added examples. Those adjustments only mean something while that model exists. Change the model and the habits you were compensating for vanish while new ones appear.

This is not just anecdote. Research notes that prompt design is heavily specialized to the target model, so keeping the same task working after a model swap requires re-engineering the prompt, and that automatic prompt rewriting is not yet reliable enough to lean on. Having humans identify and narrow the regressed slices iteratively remains the standard practice.

5.2 Reducing that cost by design

  • Separate prompts from code. When they live as scattered string literals, just working out what is used where takes days. Move them into files or a store and version them.
  • Split the invariant part from the model-facing part. Task definitions, glossaries, and output conventions are model-independent assets. A line like keep the list under five items is model compensation. Isolating the latter as an overlay makes the blast radius of a migration visible.
  • Record why each line exists. Annotate every prompt line with the failure it was added to prevent. During a migration, if that failure does not reproduce on the new model, the line can be deleted. Without the record, nobody dares delete anything and the debt simply rolls forward.
  • Push output conventions into schemas rather than prose. Anything layer two can enforce should not be politely requested in a prompt. Schemas are portable; polite requests are not.

6. Layer 4 - why an in-house eval set is the best insurance

6.1 The question you must be able to answer in thirty minutes

Every migration decision converges on one sentence: is it better on our task? If you cannot answer that, only two options remain. Trust the vendor's announced benchmarks, or have a few people try a few prompts and decide on vibes. The former is not your data; the latter does not reproduce.

With an eval set, that question becomes a thirty-minute job, and those thirty minutes directly cut the layer-three cost above. Once you can localize what got worse, prompt rework shrinks from everything to the failing slice. Research on model migration likewise treats automatic slicing of behavioral differences as a central tool.

6.2 What makes an eval set work in practice

  • Draw from real traffic. Invented examples do not capture real failure modes. Sample randomly from logs and deliberately mix in cases that previously caused incidents.
  • 100 to 300 items is enough to start. The most common failure is stalling while trying to build a perfect benchmark. A representative hundred beats a perfect zero.
  • Humans define the pass criteria first. Score exact-answer tasks by match rate and open-ended tasks with a checklist covering required elements, forbidden phrasing, and format compliance. Even if a model does the grading, the rubric must be a document a human wrote.
  • Measure cost and latency alongside quality. Quality alone leaves you stuck in front of the better but three times as expensive option. Put tokens per item, cost per item, and p95 latency in the same table.
  • Do not park it inside a vendor tool. The eval set is the one asset that is not vendor-bound, and putting it inside a single platform locks that asset up too. Keep the data in your repository and the runner thin.

If you pick only one thing — the highest return item in this entire article is the eval set. An abstraction layer can be built in a few days whenever you need it, but without an eval set you cannot even decide whether to migrate. Starting to build one after the deprecation email arrives is already too late.

7. Layer 5 - embeddings: regeneration math and the dual-running window

7.1 Why regeneration, not conversion

Embeddings are the odd one out in this list. Prompts can be adjusted and schemas can be transformed, but embeddings sit in a coordinate space that differs per model. Pad dimensions or renormalize to mix old vectors into a new space and nothing errors; the ranking simply degrades quietly. Because no exception is thrown, discovery is late, and by then user trust has already eroded.

So the practical standard is full regeneration. Research has proposed lightweight adapters that map new-model queries into the legacy space of an existing index, but as of July 2026 that is better treated as a research-stage option than a generalized standard.

7.2 How to size the cost

The API charge itself is simple arithmetic. Multiply your corpus token count by the published embedding rate, for example around $0.02 per million tokens for a small model and around $0.13 per million for a large one.

Corpus size (tokens)At small-model rateAt large-model rateNotes
10 millionabout $0.2about $1.3Internal wiki scale. Cost is effectively noise
100 millionabout $2about $13Mid-size document store
1 billionabout $20about $130Large knowledge base
Around 8 billionroughly $160sroughly $1,000sSame order of magnitude as published estimates for regenerating a large public retrieval corpus

The table leads to a strange conclusion. For most organizations the API charge is the cheapest line item. The real cost sits elsewhere.

  • Re-indexing time and hardware. Reports on large corpora describe many hours to a full day even with several GPUs in play. The service has to keep running throughout.
  • Storage during dual running. The old index must stay alive while the new one fills, so vector storage and memory double for a while.
  • Verification labor. Confirming that retrieval genuinely improved sends you back to layer four. Without an eval set, this step stalls at undecidable.
  • Chunking review. A new model may prefer a different chunk length, so what looked like plain regeneration often turns into preprocessing redesign.

7.3 Designing the dual-running window

The standard procedure resembles blue-green. In order:

  1. Create a new embedding column or a separate index. The existing index keeps serving traffic.
  2. Regenerate the corpus with a background job. This step is only possible if source text and chunking rules were preserved. A system that discarded originals and kept only vectors is stuck right here.
  3. Query both indexes in parallel and compare top results on a sample. Use the retrieval portion of your eval set for this.
  4. On pass, cut over at once and keep the old index for a defined rollback window before deleting it.

The design lesson is unambiguous. Preserving source text and recording chunking rules is the only insurance at the embedding layer. Vectors are derived artifacts and derived artifacts can be rebuilt, but nothing rebuilds a source you no longer have.

8. Layer 6 - fine-tuning, the strongest lock-in

Fine-tuning is the hardest thing on this list to move. Models trained through a commercial API generally cannot have their weights downloaded and are reachable only through that vendor's API. Switching vendors means training from scratch.

There is a fork here. When an open-weight model is tuned on your own infrastructure or through a provider that hands back adapter files, portability changes fundamentally. This is not an argument that one path is correct; it is that this fork is the practical dividing line of lock-in strength, and it deserves to be recognized at adoption time rather than at migration time.

If you do use fine-tuning, hold at least the following.

  • Treat training data as a first-class asset. Keep it in your own repository with versions, licensing, and personal-data handling history. You may not be able to move the model, but you can move the data.
  • Document the recipe. Hyperparameters, preprocessing, filtering criteria, and evaluation results, in a reproducible form.
  • Verify that fine-tuning is truly necessary first. If prompting, retrieval augmentation, or schema enforcement would have solved it, you bought the most expensive lock-in to solve the cheapest problem.

9. The abstraction trap - how far should you wrap?

Read this far and the natural conclusion is to abstract everything. That is a well-worn trap.

9.1 What over-abstraction costs

Cover every vendor with a single interface and that interface inevitably exposes only what all vendors support in common: the lowest common denominator. What you give up is concrete.

  • You lose fine-grained control of prompt caching, such as where to place cache boundaries. That has a direct billing impact.
  • Thinking-effort controls, server-side tools, batch discounts, and generally whatever a vendor shipped most recently are the features least likely to exist in a common interface.
  • Error handling and rate limit signals get flattened, making production root-cause analysis harder.
  • The abstraction layer becomes its own maintenance object, so every vendor API change means you fix your layer too.

9.2 A workable boundary

To avoid deciding this by feel, you need a rule. Something like the following is realistic.

Wrap this (worth unifying)Do not wrap this (allow vendor-specific branches)
Auth, retries, timeouts, backoffModel-specific parameters such as thinking effort and sampling
Cost, token, and latency instrumentation and loggingPrompt cache boundary placement
Prompt storage and version controlThe contents of model-specific prompt overlays
Output schema storage and your own validationPer-vendor schema rendering details
The eval runner and result storageServer-side tools, multimodal, and other unique features

One note on sequencing as well. Abstract only after you have actually wired up a second vendor. An abstraction designed while using exactly one vendor almost always ends up tracing that vendor's shape, and has to be redesigned the moment a second one arrives. Wire up two and what is genuinely common shows itself in the code.

10. Why multi-vendor is not always the answer

The usual conclusion to a lock-in discussion is to run several vendors at once. That choice comes with an itemized bill.

  • Prompt cache efficiency drops. Caching typically prices writes at a premium (for example 1.25x for a five-minute cache and 2x for a one-hour cache) and reads at a steep discount (for example one tenth of base input). In other words, you profit only by reading the same prefix repeatedly. Split traffic across two vendors and both caches stay lukewarm, so you keep paying write premiums. Agents that carry long system prompts or large documents lose the most.
  • Operational surface multiplies by vendor count. Incident procedures, billing reconciliation, rate limit tier management, log schemas, and data processing reviews all come in duplicate. The smaller the team, the heavier that relative cost.
  • Quality variance reaches users. When the same question yields a different voice and format today than yesterday, that inconsistency is itself read as a quality defect, which is fatal in summarization or support where consistency is trust.
  • Negotiating leverage disperses. Volume discounts and commitment terms usually favor concentrating usage in one place.

A better practical default, then, is dual readiness rather than dual running. Keep traffic concentrated to preserve cache efficiency and operational simplicity, but maintain a state in which you could move at any time through adapters, eval sets, and prompt overlays. Then verify that the state is real by running one migration rehearsal per quarter. Rehearsal does not mean an actual switch; running your eval set against a candidate model is enough. Without it, the adapter tends to become decoration that has never actually worked.

There are of course situations where multi-vendor is correct: services whose availability requirements cannot tolerate a single provider outage, cases where regulation or contract requires a specific region or provider, and workloads split cleanly enough that different models are decisively better for each. The deciding question should never be because lock-in is scary, but which specific risk am I buying, and at what price.

11. When the deprecation email arrives - a 30-day checklist

Whether the notice window is 60 days or six months, it is safer to finish the work in the first 30. Every week you postpone is rollback room you no longer have.

WindowTaskDefinition of done
D+0 to D+2Fix the blast radiusExport a usage audit to list real call sites. Grep every model ID across code, batch jobs, cron, eval scripts, and docs
D+3 to D+7Pick two candidates and take a baselineRun the existing eval set unchanged for a three-column table of quality, cost, and p95 latency. Do not touch prompts yet
D+8 to D+14Localize regressions and adjust promptsEdit overlays only for the failing slices. No wholesale rewrite
D+15 to D+21Canary rolloutRamp 5% then 25% with an instant revert switch in place. Watch error rate, drop-off, and re-ask rate
D+22 to D+28Full cutover and cost re-measurementConfirm the real invoice reflecting tokenizer changes, cache hit rate, and tool overhead
D+29 to D+30Cleanup and recordRemove old model paths and write up what broke and why

Three items are missed most often, so they are worth listing separately.

  • Model IDs embedded in eval scripts. Teams fix the service code, leave the evaluation pipeline calling the old model, and lose both on retirement day.
  • Cost estimation logic. When tokenizers or tool overhead change, the invoice moves even at an identical unit price. Budget alert thresholds need adjusting alongside.
  • Partner cloud paths. First-party and partner cloud schedules can differ, so organizations using both must check each one separately.

12. Three things you can do today

Compressed to three lines, in this order.

  1. Build a 100-item eval set today. Eighty random samples from logs plus twenty past incident cases. A single-page checklist is enough for pass criteria. This one item cuts the cost of every other layer.
  2. Collapse model IDs into one configuration surface. Grep code, batch jobs, cron, and eval scripts and eliminate the scattered strings. It takes half a day and buys back the first two days after a deprecation notice.
  3. Confirm you still hold source text and chunking rules. If your pipeline kept only vectors and discarded originals, the embedding layer is already a dead end. Fix that before anything else.

Conclusion

Vendor lock-in does not live in a contract; it accumulates inside your own repository. The call site changes in a day, but prompts shaped around one model's habits, embeddings produced by that model, and fine-tuned weights you cannot export do not travel. And the sharpest pain among them is the absence of a basis for judgment. Without an eval set you cannot even decide whether to move.

As of July 2026 the vendors do publish their notice windows, and those windows range from 60 days to six months. Having a policy is welcome, but a policy promises a minimum, and real cases run close to that floor. Pricing behaves the same way. Token rates have fallen broadly over the past year and a half, yet scheduled increases are also published in advance, such as an introductory rate reverting to standard pricing on a fixed date. Do not assume the direction only goes one way.

Preparedness, in the end, is neither going multi-vendor nor abstracting everything. It is knowing which layer your lock-in is piling up in, and paying down the most expensive layer early. Build the eval set, record why each prompt line exists, and never throw away the source text. For a team that has done those three, a deprecation notice is not an incident. It is a scheduled item.
