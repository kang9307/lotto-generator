How to Read Liver Test Results - What AST, ALT, ALP, GGT, Bilirubin and Albumin Each Measure

Some of those six lines count what leaked out of injured cells. Only two of them measure what the liver still manages to build. Separate the two and the report reads completely differently

August 18, 2026 Health & Wellness
Before we start: this article is for general information and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Have your test results interpreted at a medical institution. The reference ranges quoted here are values that specific institutions published for their own laboratories, not standards to apply to yourself. Your standard is always the reference range printed on your own report. Nothing here recommends or discourages any drug or supplement, and none of it may be used as a basis for starting, stopping or changing the dose of anything you are taking.

Introduction: six lines, four different questions

Say your report shows AST 52 and ALT 61 in red, with (0–40) in the bracket beside them. Most people open a search box at exactly this point, and out comes a wall of words: fatty liver, hepatitis, alcohol, supplements.

There is something to settle first. The name liver function test is half wrong. AST and ALT do not measure how well the liver is doing its job. They measure how much of what belongs inside liver cells has ended up outside them. Because the name and the reality do not match, a small rise gets read as a proportional loss of function, over and over.

The six lines record different events: whether cells are leaking, whether bile is flowing, whether the liver is processing and clearing substances, and whether it is still building proteins. Mix those four together and you cannot tell which line is a heavy signal and which is a light one.

This article is not about managing fatty liver. Diet, exercise and disease management live in the complete guide to reversing fatty liver disease, and the rest of a routine blood panel lives in how to read your blood test results. What is covered here is one thing only: how to read the liver lines.

1. What the label liver function test gets wrong

The aminotransferases, AST and ALT, are supposed to stay inside cells. When a cell membrane is damaged they spill into the blood, and that spill is what the number counts. The MSD Manual Professional Version puts it plainly: these enzymes leak from damaged hepatocytes, which makes them sensitive indicators of hepatocyte injury. Indicators of injury, not of function.

The same source goes one step further and notes that the degree of elevation may not reflect the extent of the injury. A value twice the limit does not mean twice the damage. Running the other way, when a great many cells have already been lost there is less enzyme left to leak, so the number can fall. A falling value and an improving situation are not automatically the same event.

So where is function actually measured? Three places.

  • Albumin — a protein the liver builds. It reflects synthetic capacity.
  • Prothrombin time and INR — the capacity to build clotting factors. Synthetic capacity again.
  • Bilirubin — not building but processing and clearing.

Why this distinction matters becomes obvious when you put two situations side by side. In one, AST and ALT sit at roughly twice the upper limit while albumin and INR are untouched. In the other, the enzymes are near normal but albumin is low and INR is prolonged. The first says cells are leaking. The second says the capacity to build is itself failing. Those are not the same weight. This is also why international guidance has moved from the old phrase liver function tests toward liver chemistries.

2. What each item actually reflects

Laid out as one table, where each line comes from and what it points at:

Item Where it mainly lives What it reflects Sources outside the liver
ALT Hepatocyte cytosol Hepatocyte injury (leakage) Skeletal muscle, relatively little
AST Liver, heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, red cells, pancreas Cell injury (leakage), low liver specificity Muscle injury, myocardium, haemolysis
ALP Bile duct epithelium, bone, placenta, small intestine, white cells, kidney Impaired bile flow (cholestasis) Growing bone, pregnancy, bone disease
GGT Bile duct epithelium Whether an ALP rise is hepatobiliary in origin Alcohol, enzyme-inducing drugs
Bilirubin (total, direct, indirect) Red cell breakdown product processed by the liver Processing and excretion, biliary drainage Haemolysis, Gilbert syndrome
Albumin Synthesised in the liver Chronic synthetic capacity Inflammation, nutrition, renal or gut protein loss
PT / INR Clotting factors synthesised in the liver Acute synthetic capacity Anticoagulants, vitamin K deficiency, malabsorption

The column people skip is the last one. Not one of the six is exclusive to the liver. ALT is the most liver specific of the group and it still exists in skeletal muscle. AST is spread across heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, red cells and pancreas. ALP is distributed widely and is particularly abundant in bone. So the fact that a line went up never on its own establishes that the liver is the source.

3. Same test, different reference range

This is the most immediately useful section in the article. Reference ranges differ from laboratory to laboratory. That is not a soft caveat, it is a discrepancy you can read off published sources side by side.

ALT 0–40 IU/L up to 40 IU/L 4–36 IU/L

Read across and the gaps jump out. The AST ceiling is 40 in one place and 30 in another. GGT is split by sex in two sources and not split at all in the third, and the male ceiling reads 63, 71 and 50 across the row. Albumin appears in two different units. Prothrombin is a time in seconds in one source and a standardised INR index in another. The Korean public health portal states outright that reference ranges vary between laboratories, and StatPearls attaches the same caveat to the numbers it prints.

Why do they diverge? Because a reference range is not a constant handed down from above. It is an interval that a given laboratory produced with its own analyser, its own reagents and its own reference population. Change the measurement method or the conditions and the scale shifts. Change how the reference group was selected and the ceiling moves with it.

Cross a border and the spread widens. The American College of Gastroenterology guideline on abnormal liver chemistries observed that US institutional upper limits for ALT run from 30 or 40 up to 70 or 80, and proposed a healthy normal ALT of 29–33 IU/L for men and 19–25 IU/L for women based on populations without liver disease risk factors. Where the printed ceiling is 70, an ALT of 70 lands in the normal column and sits far above that band.

Three working rules
1. The standard for judging your result is the bracket printed next to it. Do not apply numbers from the internet or from someone else report.
2. To follow a trend, use the same laboratory. If the lab changes, you cannot tell whether the value moved or the yardstick did.
3. Being inside the range does not mean no risk. With risk factors present, a value near the ceiling is still worth raising.

4. The AST/ALT ratio - how far it reads and where it stops

Because the two enzymes are distributed differently, which one rose more is a clue to the origin. The comparison is known as the De Ritis ratio.

  • ALT predominant — weight shifts toward injury of liver cells themselves, since ALT is the more liver specific of the pair.
  • AST predominant, particularly a ratio of 2:1 or above — a pattern commonly described in liver disease associated with chronic alcohol use.
  • The ratio alone is not enough. The absolute magnitude matters too. The same ratio built from values near the reference range and from values in the hundreds describes very different situations.

The limits are just as clear. Because AST comes from outside the liver as well, muscle injury and cardiac events produce an AST predominant pattern too. Someone who never drinks can produce a ratio above 2. And once the liver is already scarred, the ratio has long been noted to lose much of its ability to separate causes.

Put simply: the AST/ALT ratio is an arrow telling you which direction to check first, not a diagnosis. Feeding two numbers into a calculator and reading off a disease name is not how the measure is used.

5. Not mistaking a muscle-driven AST for a liver problem

This is where misreading happens most often. Saying that exercise raises liver enzymes is not an exaggeration, and the effect lingers longer than most people assume.

Healthy men with no weightlifting experience performed a single one hour resistance session in a study by Pettersson and colleagues (Br J Clin Pharmacol, 2008). The findings:

  • AST, ALT, lactate dehydrogenase, creatine kinase (CK) and myoglobin all rose.
  • All five markers stayed elevated for at least seven days.
  • AST rose earlier than ALT, and the rise was seen in every participant.

The paper title itself makes the point: muscular exercise can produce liver test results that look severely pathological in healthy men. If your first ever gym session was three days before the blood draw, that report may be describing muscle rather than liver.

The clue that separates them is CK, which rises steeply with muscle injury. When AST, ALT and CK rise together and the magnitudes are concordant, a muscular source is suggested. That call still needs the full panel plus an examination, so it is not a conclusion to reach alone. Nor is hard training the only muscular factor.

  • Resistance work or long-distance running at an unaccustomed intensity
  • Muscle trauma, severe bruising, prolonged compression
  • Conditions that involve the muscle itself
  • Breakdown of red blood cells (haemolysis) — AST is present in red cells as well

6. ALP, GGT and bilirubin - signals from the bile side

6.1 ALP is partly a bone test

ALP exists as several isoenzymes distributed around the body. It is particularly abundant in bone and also present in placenta, small intestine, white cells and kidney. Jumping from an isolated ALP rise straight to the bile ducts is therefore premature. Adolescents in a growth phase run higher than the adult range because bone is actively being built, and late pregnancy adds a placental contribution.

6.2 GGT narrows down the source of ALP and no more

When ALP is up, checking GGT alongside it helps establish whether the rise is hepatobiliary. The MSD Manual Professional Version notes that GGT is more sensitive than ALP for a hepatic origin of cholestasis. If ALP is elevated but GGT is not, bone moves up the list.

GGT is sensitive but not specific, though. As the same source points out, alcohol and certain drugs induce hepatic enzymes and markedly increase GGT, which limits its specificity. A report with GGT as the only raised line does not carry a diagnosis on its own. How much someone drinks and what they take have to sit next to it before interpretation begins.

6.3 Bilirubin rises long before anything looks yellow

Bilirubin is produced when red cells break down; the liver processes it and sends it out in bile. Total bilirubin therefore tracks the flow of processing and clearance. Splitting it into total, direct (conjugated) and indirect (unconjugated) narrows down where the bottleneck sits.

  • Indirect predominant — suggests either more being produced (haemolysis) or less conjugation being done.
  • Direct predominant — suggests processing happened but export is obstructed.
  • Visible jaundice appears once total bilirubin passes roughly 2–3 mg/dL. The sclerae yellow before the skin does.

Gilbert syndrome is the common catch here. A UGT1A1 variant leaves the conjugating enzyme at about 30 percent of normal activity, and the condition is estimated to affect 3 to 7 percent of Americans. MedlinePlus Genetics describes it as causing no permanent liver damage, with episodes of raised unconjugated bilirubin when the body is under stress: dehydration, fasting, illness, vigorous exercise, menstruation. A mild indirect rise found on a fasting draw sometimes belongs here. Confirming that still happens in a clinical setting, alongside the rest of the panel.

7. Albumin and PT/INR - where real function starts

These two lines differ in kind from the enzymes above them, because they count what was built rather than what leaked.

Albumin has a half-life of about 20 days. Its blood level therefore takes weeks to move, and it does not reflect acute liver injury in real time. Damage that began yesterday has not reached albumin yet. A low albumin can point toward a chronic situation, but the value is pushed around by many things beyond the liver: inflammation, nutritional state, protein lost through the kidneys or gut, and volume status. Low albumin alone does not name the liver.

PT/INR runs on the opposite clock. Some of the clotting factors the liver builds have very short half-lives, with factor VII at about six hours. When synthetic capacity drops, PT lengthens within hours to a day. That is the line that shows synthetic failure quickly in an acute setting.

It is not liver-exclusive either. Warfarin and similar anticoagulants, vitamin K deficiency and malabsorption all prolong it. So this line is always read together with what the person is currently taking.

Reading the combination: enzymes up with normal albumin and INR is a different weight from near-normal enzymes with low albumin and a prolonged INR. The second says the building capacity itself is unsteady, and that warrants prompt medical attention. The assessment does not end with one panel; examination and further testing go with it.

8. Normal with a problem, raised with a healthy liver

The two most dangerous misreadings live in this section, and they point in opposite directions.

8.1 Inside the range is not a certificate of health

A result inside the reference range is closer to saying the panel was biochemically quiet at that moment. StatPearls, the clinical reference hosted by the US National Center for Biotechnology Information, states that normal liver chemistries do not exclude clinically meaningful liver disease, and that metabolic dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease and even fibrosis can be present while aminotransferases sit inside the reported range.

The ACG healthy normal band (men 29–33, women 19–25) matters again here. On a report where the printed ceiling is 40, an ALT of 38 comes out in black ink, yet by that band it is already high. The absence of red ink and the absence of anything happening are not the same statement. With risk factors such as weight, alcohol intake, metabolic markers or family history, a value near the ceiling is worth raising at the appointment.

8.2 And a raised value may have nothing to do with the liver

The preceding sections already supplied several examples. Collected:

  • Hard exercise or muscle injury → AST, ALT and CK rise together, potentially for a week or more
  • Red cell breakdown → AST and indirect bilirubin can move together
  • Growth phase, late pregnancy, bone-related states → ALP rises
  • Gilbert syndrome → mild indirect bilirubin rise with no liver damage
  • Enzyme-inducing drugs → GGT rises

So the actual task is not guessing a disease name. It is identifying which combination you are looking at: which lines moved together, which direction they moved relative to the previous report, and what happened to your body in the last few weeks. With those three in hand, the next test can be chosen. That choice is the clinician to make.

9.1 Why supplements get their own paragraph

The fourth row deserves expansion. The US National Institutes of Health drug-induced liver injury resource LiverTox reports that the share of cases in the US registry network attributed to herbal and dietary supplements rose from roughly ten percent into the mid teens, with 145 of 899 analysed cases falling into that group. Green tea extract is described as the single most commonly implicated herbal agent, and multi-ingredient weight-loss products and anabolic steroids recur.

The structural background is recorded there too. Supplement manufacturers do not need approval before marketing, which leaves regulators judging safety only once a product is already on shelves. Several ingredients sold under a liver-health banner have no established clinical benefit.

This article recommends nothing and forbids nothing. But one thing is clear from the interpretation side: writing down everything you are currently taking makes the reading more accurate. Over-the-counter drugs, herbal preparations, supplements, protein powders and training aids all count.

10. Preparing for a repeat test, and when to go in

10.1 Before the repeat

  • Use the same laboratory where possible. A change only means something when the reference range behind it is the same.
  • Do not take up a new, harder training routine in the days beforehand. Holding your usual activity level is better.
  • If you have cut down or stopped drinking, record the date. Without it, the reason a number moved becomes unreadable.
  • Build a complete list of what you take: prescriptions, over-the-counter drugs, herbal preparations, supplements, training aids.
  • On fasting, follow the instructions from the testing facility. Depending on the panel, six hours or more may be required.
  • Bring the previous report. Direction and slope say more than a single value.
  • Do not set the interval yourself. Depending on the working hypothesis it may be days or months.

10.2 Signals not to postpone

If any of the following applies, seek care rather than waiting for the scheduled repeat. If it feels urgent, that means emergency services.
  • The whites of the eyes or the skin look yellow
  • Urine has turned dark brown or stools have gone pale grey
  • Severe abdominal pain that persists, particularly in the upper right
  • Vomiting that will not settle, or an inability to keep anything down
  • Clouded consciousness, slurred speech, heavy drowsiness or unusual confusion
  • Fever alongside the above
  • Values printed at several times the upper limit of the reference range
  • Elevation that persists or climbs across repeated tests
  • Numbers that rose after starting a new drug or a new supplement

This list is a basis for deciding whether to be seen, not a basis for diagnosis. Nor does an absence from the list mean safety. If your body is clearly different from usual, seeing someone beats checking it against a list.

10.3 What this article could not confirm

  • Sensitivity and specificity figures for an AST/ALT ratio above 2 — specific percentages circulate in secondary sources, but the original papers could not be opened, so they were left out.
  • The exact multiples the ACG guideline uses to separate borderline, mild, moderate and severe elevation — only summary-level material could be confirmed, so the bands are not described here.
  • A healthy normal ALT ceiling derived from a Korean population — the 29–33 and 19–25 figures used above come from the US guideline.
  • A full comparison of reference ranges across laboratories — only the three published sources in the table were checked.

Frequently asked questions

Q. My liver numbers are slightly high but I do not drink. Why?

Alcohol is one route among many. Muscular events, current medication or supplements, metabolic states and viral causes all raise these lines, and AST additionally lives in heart, muscle, kidney and red cells, so it moves for reasons unconnected to the liver. If GGT alone is up in someone who does not drink, medication is the next thing examined. Which branch applies is narrowed by the combination of items, comparison with earlier results, and examination.

Q. Some items on my report have sex-specific ranges and others do not.

GGT is the classic example of an item with sex-specific reference ranges, and both Korean sources in the table above list separate male and female intervals. It is worth checking that you are reading the bracket that matches your sex. Whether an item is split by sex or age is a laboratory policy decision.

Q. My number is lower than last time. Does that mean improvement?

Usually a welcome direction, but not automatically. Enzyme levels measure what leaks, so they can also fall when there are fewer cells left to leak. That is why enzymes are not read alone, and why albumin, bilirubin and INR are checked for direction at the same time. And if the laboratory changed, part of the change may be the yardstick.

Q. If I stop drinking for a few days before the test, will the numbers come back normal?

Arranging conditions to produce a better score works against the purpose of a repeat test. It is an observation intended to narrow a cause, not an exam to pass. Reporting recent intake and the date you stopped is what allows the change to be interpreted. Information withheld tends to come back as extra testing.

Q. How should I treat a value sitting right at the top of the range?

On its own it does not settle anything. Without risk factors and with a result similar to last time, the usual approach is observation. With weight, alcohol, metabolic markers or family history in the picture, or a clear climb from the previous value, the same number is handled differently. The ACG healthy normal band sitting below many printed ceilings is also worth keeping in mind. Which case applies is best decided at an appointment with the report in hand.

Conclusion

To restate the liver lines: AST, ALT, ALP and GGT count what leaked out, bilirubin tracks processing and clearance, and albumin with PT/INR measure what is still being built. One name on the panel, four different measurements underneath it.

Two constraints sit on top. First, reference ranges differ between laboratories — published sources put the male GGT ceiling at 63, 71 and 50. The standard is always the bracket printed beside your own result. Second, no single measurement decides anything. One unfamiliar workout can push AST up for over a week, and a value resting neatly inside the range guarantees nothing.

So the most useful thing to do with a report is not to search a disease name but to assemble the context and take it with you: the last few weeks of exercise and alcohol, everything you are currently taking, and the previous report. With those three, the same numbers say a great deal more.

To repeat: this article is for general information and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Have your test results interpreted at a medical institution. The figures above are reference values published by the institutions cited, and your own standard is the reference range on your own report. The final judgement belongs to the clinician treating you, and nothing here is a basis for delaying care or for adjusting drugs or supplements on your own.
