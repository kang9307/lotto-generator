Cate - The Infinite Canvas IDE That Drops Tabs and Windows 2026

An experiment in spreading editors, terminals, browsers, and AI agents across one plane

Jun 14, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: Why We Are Asking What an IDE Should Look Like Again

For the past two decades the outward shape of the code editor has been remarkably stable. A file tree on the left, a stack of tabbed editors in the middle, a terminal at the bottom, side panels on the right. Whether VS Code or a JetBrains tool, this grid of rectangular panels inside a rectangular window has become the de facto standard. And yet in June 2026, a project that reached the top of GeekNews (news.hada.io) takes direct aim at that assumption. Its name is Cate: a desktop IDE that removes the partitions of tabs and windows and lets you place editors, terminals, browsers, documents, and AI agents freely on an infinitely zoomable canvas.

This article uses Cate as a concrete starting point to examine a larger question from a developer-tools and UX angle: can an infinite canvas actually become a coding workspace? We will look at what Cate is and how it is built, what the tabs-and-windows model fails at, where this idea sits within the spatial UI trend that runs through Figma, tldraw, and Muse, and how spatial memory connects to productivity. Finally we will weigh the real limits, learning curve and performance, and ask honestly who this is for.

1. What Cate Is - A One-Sentence Definition

In one sentence, Cate is "a desktop IDE that spreads all of a project's tools across one infinite canvas." The official repository (0-AI-UG/cate) describes it as spreading "editors, terminals, browsers, docs, and AI agents across freeform space instead of stacking windows and tabs." The name itself reads as an acronym for Canvas Terminal Editor, putting the core identity right on the surface.

The usage model is intuitive. Open a folder and it becomes a workspace. You right-click on the canvas to add panels, summon tools with the command palette (Cmd+K), and drag panels onto the dock to bundle them into tabs or splits. A panel can float freely, dock into tabs and splits, or be detached into its own OS window. Reopen the folder and Cate restores the entire layout. In other words, the unit of work is the equation "a project equals a space."

2. The Stack and Panel Types - What It Is Made Of

Cate stands on familiar web technology. The stack stated in the official docs is as follows.

  • Runtime: Electron 41 with a React 18 frontend. It is a desktop app, but the interior is web rendering.
  • Editor: Monaco 0.52 (the engine behind VS Code), with syntax highlighting, multi-cursor, diffs, and Markdown preview.
  • Terminal: xterm.js 5.5 plus node-pty 1.0, so native shells run inside the canvas.
  • Styling and state: Tailwind 3.4 and Zustand 5, built with electron-vite, electron-builder, and electron-updater.

The panel types are varied too. Beyond code editors and terminals, there are document panels for PDFs, DOCX, and images, browser previews, and a Git-aware file tree (staging, branches, worktrees, history, inline diffs) handled as a panel on the canvas. The license is MIT, with prebuilt releases for macOS (DMG, ZIP), Windows (NSIS, ZIP), and Linux (AppImage, DEB, tar.gz). So while the idea is experimental, the implementation rides on a stack of proven components.

3. The Limits of Traditional IDEs - The Partition of Tabs, Windows, and Panels

To understand Cate's premise, you first have to name what traditional IDEs cannot do. The core metaphor of the traditional IDE is stacking. Tabs stack many files in the same spot and show one at a time; panels carve the screen into rectangles and cram a tool into each. This model is good for looking at one thing deeply, but it creates friction when you need to spread several contexts at once and compare or explore across them.

Tabs answer "what am I looking at now" well, but they make you forget "what was next to it." Partitions aid focus and sever context at the same time.

The concrete pain looks like this. Comparing the same file in several places means repeated splitting, and each new split makes every pane narrower. Seeing a terminal, a preview, and a document at once means opening multiple windows and arranging them at the OS level, an arrangement that tends to evaporate when the session ends. Above all, it is hard to mentally reconstruct yesterday's particular arrangement today. The order of tabs and the position of panels are highly volatile and leave almost no anchor for human spatial memory to hold.

4. The Infinite Canvas Alternative - From Stacking to Spreading

Cate's answer is to replace stacking with spreading. Instead of cutting the screen into rectangles, it places panels by coordinates on an endless plane. A terminal to the right of the editor, a browser preview above it, an AI agent in one corner, and you move between them by panning and zooming. Zoom out and the whole terrain of the project comes into view; zoom in and you focus on the details of a single file.

The key benefit of this approach is the coexistence of context. You can lay two files you want to compare side by side rather than trapping them in narrow splits, and while debugging you keep logs (terminal), code (editor), and results (browser) in the same field of view, moving only your eyes. Better still, Cate saves and restores this arrangement per project, so "yesterday's working landscape" remains intact. Notably, Cate does not try to replace the OS window manager. Instead it organizes space only within a project's scope, drawing a line that avoids fighting the operating system's window management.

5. Cate Within the Spatial UI Trend - The Figma, tldraw, and Muse Lineage

The infinite canvas is not something Cate invented. Over the past few years, "infinite canvas" has become a category of its own across design, thinking, and collaboration tools. The microsite the Muse team built (infinitecanvas.tools) calls it "a new foundational document type," where "infinite" refers not to vast space but to openness and possibility.

Tool Domain Use of the infinite canvas
Figma / FigJam Design / whiteboard Many users spread design and ideas on one shared plane
tldraw Open-source SDK A React toolkit for embedding an infinite canvas in products
Muse Spatial thinking / notes A canvas for arranging thought spatially on iPad and Mac
Cate Coding IDE Editors, terminals, browsers, and AI on one plane

Cate's novelty is not the infinite canvas itself but that it drags the metaphor into the most conservative domain there is: coding. It applies a spatial way of working that designers and product thinkers adopted first to a live development environment of real terminals, real editors, and roaming AI agents. That dovetails with the observation that the IDE has been "static" for nearly twenty years, and it reads as one instance of a broader effort to reimagine the shape of the tool.

6. The Meaning of AI Agent Integration - Humans and Agents on One Plane

The most of-the-moment part of Cate is that AI agents are first-class citizens of the canvas. Per the official description, Cate ships an embedded coding agent (Pi) and supports multiple providers: Anthropic, OpenAI, GitHub Copilot, Gemini, OpenRouter, Groq, Mistral, and DeepSeek. What matters is less "which model you use" and more "where the agent is."

In a traditional IDE, AI is usually confined to a chat box in a side panel. The files the agent touches, the terminal it runs, the preview it spawns, are all scattered into different boxes, so the human is forever ping-ponging between chat and code. Cate removes that partition by putting the agent panel in the same space as the files, terminals, and browser it works on. What the agent is looking at and what it is changing enter a single field of view.

This design connects to a larger question, "the UI problem of AI coding agents." Now that agents are part of the coding loop, we may still be cramming them into rectangular chat boxes designed for humans. A spatial workspace offers one answer by letting human tools and agent work share context on the same plane. For the broader context of agent-centric development, continue with Claude Code's dynamic workflows and the harness.

There is a deeper reason this matters as agents grow more capable. When an agent edits five files, runs a build, and opens a preview, the human's job shifts from typing to supervising, and supervision is fundamentally a problem of attention. A chat transcript forces you to reconstruct what changed by reading a linear scroll, whereas a spatial layout lets the changed files, the failing test, and the live preview sit in your peripheral vision at once. In other words, the canvas is not just a prettier container; it is an attempt to give the human a dashboard for watching several autonomous processes without losing the thread. Whether that promise holds up under real workloads is exactly what the next year of usage will reveal, but the framing of the problem is sound: as more of the typing is delegated, the layout of what you watch becomes the bottleneck, not the speed of what you write.

7. Spatial Memory and Productivity - Remembering by Location

At the heart of the case for the infinite canvas is the idea of spatial memory. People are remarkably good at remembering where things are in physical space. We recall which kitchen drawer holds the scissors and which corner of the desk has the sticky notes without conscious effort. This ability is evolutionarily old, and it grips a spatial arrangement far more strongly than an abstract list.

The hypothesis of the infinite canvas IDE is that pinning tools and files in space lets us borrow this ability for coding too. If you remember context by location, "auth logic top-left, payment flow bottom-right, log terminal in the center," you cut the cognitive cost of scanning a list of tabs to find a file. The benefit is largest in exploratory work like debugging, where you must hold several contexts at once.

There is a counterweight, though. Spatial memory works only when the arrangement is stable. If positions change often, the memory anchor wobbles and you end up hunting for "where did I put that." In short, the productivity of the infinite canvas comes not from free placement itself but from the user's discipline in creating meaningful arrangements and keeping them stable.

8. The Upsides, Summarized - What Gets Better

Compressing the discussion so far into benefits:

  • Coexistence of context: Spread many files and tools side by side on one plane instead of trapping them in narrow splits.
  • Layout persistence: Save and restore the arrangement per project so "yesterday's working landscape" stays intact.
  • Use of spatial memory: Remember context by tool location, cutting the cognitive cost of exploratory work.
  • Tools and AI living together: The agent sits in the same space as what it works on, reducing context breaks.
  • Flexible docking: Move panels freely between floating, tabs, splits, and separate windows.

9. Limits and Criticism - The Price of Freedom

For balance, let us state the other side clearly. The infinite canvas is not free, and the discussion right after launch raised several concerns.

  • Absence of structure equals cognitive overload: Freedom to place anything means it can become a mess. One commenter worried that "the lack of structure will cognitively overload my brain and at some point every canvas will become a mess," comparing it to chaotic Miro boards and bloated browser tabs.
  • Learning curve: For hands and minds trained on tabs and windows, coordinates and zoom are a new habit, and they can clash with fast keyboard-only navigation.
  • Performance and stability: Early versions reported bugs, unresponsive window controls, inconsistent drag-and-drop, and erratic cursor behavior. The more panels you open, the heavier the rendering load.
  • The re-implementation debate: Critics asked why terminals and editors need to be rebuilt rather than embedding native apps, and some argued a proper tiling window manager would serve the core idea better.
  • No remote or headless: Requests followed for remote and headless operation, navigation within large canvases, and basics like terminal renaming.

These criticisms share a single root: freedom is responsibility. The user has to do the tidying that partitions used to enforce, so whether the tool supports good organizing habits decides success or failure.

10. Who It Is For - Fit and Misfit

So who is an infinite canvas IDE like Cate for? A tool's value depends heavily on the type of work.

Dimension Good fit Poorer fit
Nature of work Exploratory work across many services and repos Deep, focused single-file work
Way of thinking People who think spatially and remember layouts People who navigate fast by list and keyboard
Environment Local desktop, large screen or one infinite plane Remote, headless, SSH-centric environments

In short, Cate is less a "next-generation IDE for everyone" and more a tool that strongly fits a particular way of thinking and working. For people who keep many contexts open and move between them with their eyes, who want the AI agent, the code, and the preview in one field of view, it can feel liberating. For people who immerse in one file or fly around by keyboard alone, it may instead be friction.

11. The Bigger Picture - The IDE Has Started Moving Again

The real significance of Cate is not whether it replaces VS Code tomorrow. The more important signal is the fact itself: the shape of the IDE, static for nearly twenty years, is once again a subject of experiment. As AI agents entered the development loop, the very composition of "coding" changed, and the shape of the workspace is being re-examined to match.

This trend is not limited to Cate alone. Projects with similar ideas are appearing at the same time, tools billing themselves as "mission control" for agents, others foregrounding a multi-agent canvas. You could say the whole tool ecosystem is groping for "a new UI for work in which AI is a participant." The consolidation of front-end and build tooling continues in VoidZero joining Cloudflare and Vite, and the agent-centric framework trend in Angular v22's AI agent flows.

12. Conclusion + 3 Things to Try

Cate is one concrete, brave answer to the question, "what becomes visible if you drop tabs and windows?" It spreads editors, terminals, browsers, and AI agents on an infinite canvas to borrow human spatial memory for coding. Its strengths are the coexistence of context, layout persistence, and tools living with AI; its weaknesses are the cognitive overload of structurelessness, the learning curve, and early performance. Which weighs more depends on your type of work and your tidiness habits.

If you want to judge for yourself, three suggestions:

  1. Start with one small project. Before spreading many repos at once, place an editor, terminal, preview, and agent on a single canvas for one project and feel whether spatial memory actually takes hold.
  2. Decide on a meaningful fixed arrangement. The productivity of the infinite canvas comes from a stable layout, not from freedom. Setting your own rule for "what goes where" is the key.
  3. Judge by the work, not the tool. Use "does my exploratory work actually get faster" as the criterion, not "is it cool." The infinite canvas is not for everyone; it is for a particular way of working.
