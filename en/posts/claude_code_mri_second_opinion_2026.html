Introduction: "What Would an AI Say About My MRI?"

After an MRI scan, most people face the same wall of confusion when they receive the radiology report. Terms like "T2 hyperintensity," "mild white matter changes," or "L4-L5 disc herniation" read like a foreign language. What do they actually mean for daily life? What should you ask your doctor? And the next appointment is a week away, with anxiety building in the interim.

This is why more people are turning to general-purpose LLMs like Claude Code, feeding in their radiology report text and asking for explanations. In June 2026, a firsthand account titled "Getting an AI Second Opinion on My MRI with Claude Code" gained traction on GeekNews. Using that as a starting point, this analysis examines what AI can genuinely do, where it hits hard limits, and what medical YMYL risks are embedded in this practice — grounded in research current as of June 2026.

The bottom line upfront: AI was genuinely valuable for explaining terminology and organizing questions. But treating AI output as a medical opinion is where real danger begins. Both of these statements are true at the same time.

1. AI in Medicine as of June 2026 — How Far Has It Come?

Using general-purpose LLMs for medical purposes has moved beyond experimentation into everyday practice. Research published in Nature in June 2026 showed that Anthropic's MIRA (Medical Intelligence for Reasoning and Action) and Google's AMIE (Articulate Medical Intelligence Explorer) achieved 87.8% diagnostic accuracy in emergency cases — exceeding the 78.1% benchmark for physicians in the study. AMIE also outperformed physicians on treatment precision, investigation choices, and guideline adherence.

Before accepting those numbers at face value, context matters. First, these results reflect controlled experimental conditions for specific task types — not the full complexity of a real clinical encounter. Second, Claude Code as a general-purpose LLM is categorically different from these specialized medical AI systems, which are fine-tuned on large clinical datasets, undergo regulatory review, and operate within structured liability frameworks. None of that applies to a general chatbot.

Within medical imaging specifically, a study on prostate cancer MRI found that 91% of patients were comfortable with AI serving as primary reader alongside a radiologist, and 79% accepted it as secondary reader. Separate research demonstrated that patients who received AI-explained MRI reports showed significantly higher comprehension than those who read original radiology reports. The case for AI as a comprehension aid is already evidence-backed.

2. What Actually Happens When You Feed an MRI Report to Claude Code

Examining patterns from real user accounts, people typically ask three things when entering radiology reports into an LLM. First, terminology explanation ("What does T2 hyperintensity mean?"). Second, clinical context ("How serious is this finding?"). Third, question preparation ("What should I ask my doctor at the next appointment?").

2.1 Where AI Excels — Breaking Down the Language Barrier

The clearest strength of general-purpose LLMs is translating medical language into everyday terms. What a herniated disc is versus a bulging disc, what T2-weighted signal changes indicate about tissue state, whether mild white matter changes fall within normal aging — for these conceptual explanations, Claude Code delivers structured, textbook-quality responses that are far more efficient than piecing together search results.

Generating a pre-appointment question list is another practical win. Ask Claude "What are the ten most important questions to ask my doctor given these MRI findings?" and you get a structured list covering symptom correlation, follow-up scheduling, lifestyle modifications, and when to consider further imaging. Bringing this into a time-limited appointment meaningfully improves the conversation.

Research as of June 2026 confirms this pattern: patients who received AI-aided report interpretations engaged in higher-quality dialogue with their physicians at subsequent visits.

2.2 Where AI Creates Dangerous Illusions — Confident Wrong Answers

The problem is that when AI is wrong, it is often wrong with great confidence. Medical hallucination is one of the most seriously studied risks in healthcare AI. A 2026 systematic review found that more than 90% of clinicians had personally encountered AI medical hallucinations, with 85% judging them capable of causing patient harm.

What kinds of errors occur? Fabricated incidence statistics ("this finding is common in adults under 30" when the actual epidemiology differs), nonexistent guideline citations ("according to the 2024 neurology society guidelines..." when no such guideline exists or the content is misrepresented), and personalized risk misassessment (presenting generic population statistics as if they applied to the individual's specific clinical context) are the three most common failure modes. Interestingly, some research suggests general-purpose LLMs produce fewer hallucinations than specialized medical LLMs — because they more frequently say "I don't know" in uncertain domains. But this is not reliably consistent.

2.3 The Fundamental Difference Between AI Output and a Real Second Opinion

A real medical second opinion is a licensed specialist reviewing original images and records under legal and ethical accountability. AI processes text-described information through statistical patterns, carries no legal liability, and is distinct from specialized medical imaging AI that analyzes actual DICOM files with clinical validation. AI's advantages in cost and accessibility are real — but AI output is reference material, not a medical opinion. Blurring that line is the core danger.

3. Medical Data Privacy — What Should Never Go Into an AI

The second major issue when using AI for medical information is personal data protection. Feeding MRI results into commercial AI services involves privacy risks most users underestimate.

3.1 The Risk of Uploading Original Medical Records

Radiology reports typically contain full name, date of birth, hospital name, scan date, and attending physician. Uploading this to a commercial AI platform means sensitive medical information may be retained on servers or incorporated into training data, depending on the platform's terms of service and data policies. This is a non-trivial concern.

Anthropic launched Claude for Healthcare in January 2026 with HIPAA-compliant enterprise configurations. However, this requires enterprise contracts and a signed Business Associate Agreement (BAA) — it does not apply to individual users on standard claude.ai plans. Entering medical records into a general consumer AI account is not recommended from a privacy standpoint.

3.2 The Safer Approach — De-identify Before Inputting

A practical middle path exists. Remove all personally identifiable information (name, date of birth, hospital, physician, scan date) from the report text, then input only the medical findings as plain text. Instead of a full report header, input only: "T2 hyperintense foci x3, L4-L5 disc herniation, need plain-language explanation." This approach preserves most of the AI's explanatory utility while substantially reducing privacy exposure.

4. Five Evidence-Backed Ways AI Genuinely Helps in Healthcare

Having established the risks, here are the validated positive use cases — areas confirmed by research and real-world experience as of June 2026.

4.1 Medical Terminology Translation

Explaining anatomical terms, imaging modality differences, and pathology concepts in plain language is the safest and most reliable AI use case in healthcare. Conceptual questions ("What is the difference between disc herniation and disc bulging?") consistently receive high-quality, educational answers.

4.2 Pre-Appointment Question Lists

AI-generated question lists covering symptom correlation, follow-up frequency, lifestyle restrictions, medication needs, and referral criteria improve the quality and efficiency of doctor-patient conversations. This is AI supporting the physician encounter, not replacing it.

4.3 Medical Literature Summaries

AI can summarize research papers or medical guidelines in plain language, and explain statistical concepts (NNT, odds ratios, confidence intervals) that patients encounter in medical literature. Verification against the original source remains essential.

4.4 Symptom Journal Structuring

Organizing symptoms, onset timelines, aggravating and relieving factors, and medication lists with AI assistance before an appointment gives physicians clearer, more complete information — directly improving diagnostic accuracy.

4.5 Cross-Language Medical Information Access

For patients receiving care abroad or navigating foreign-language medical records, AI translation and explanation meaningfully lowers language barriers. Critical items should still be verified with a professional medical interpreter.

5. The Danger of Overreliance — "The AI Said I'm Fine"

The most serious AI-in-medicine failure scenario is when AI reassurance leads someone to delay real medical care. "I asked the AI and it said there's nothing to worry about" is dangerous for two interconnected reasons.

First, AI does not know the individual's complete clinical context. Age, comorbidities, medications, family history, symptom progression — all of these can entirely change the significance of the same MRI finding. Information not provided in text cannot be considered by the AI. Second, LLM output is not a medical safety guarantee. A 2026 study documented LLM care plans undertriaging medical concerns and unreliably addressing mental health issues, with patients unable to distinguish between adequate and inadequate AI advice.

The word "mild" in a radiology report does not automatically mean reassuring. "Mild white matter changes" may fall within normal aging in elderly patients but represent neurologically significant findings in younger patients or specific symptom presentations. Delegating that contextual judgment to AI is the wrong move.

6. The Future of Medical AI — Promise and Necessary Guardrails

As of June 2026, medical AI is rapidly evolving on both regulatory and clinical integration fronts. Specialized medical AI systems — radiology AI, clinical decision support AI — are increasingly deployed within healthcare systems under regulatory approval, with clear liability structures. Japan's FY2026 medical fee revision included AI and ICT utilization promotion as a core policy principle. The EU's EHDS regulation is institutionalizing anonymized data use in medical research.

But all of these developments are happening within healthcare systems, under professional supervision. Individuals entering radiology reports into general consumer chatbots operate outside these frameworks entirely. The former has accountable parties, clinical validation, and error-tracking. The latter has none of these.

As Anthropic's Claude for Healthcare expands and medical LLMs integrate with specialized imaging AI, the quality and safety of patient-accessible AI medical tools will improve substantially. Even then, the final clinical judgment of a licensed physician will remain irreplaceable. AI advances the democratization of medical information — it does not replace medical expertise.

7. A Practical Guide for Using AI Safely as a Health Aid

7.1 De-identify Before Input

Strip name, date of birth, hospital, physician, and scan dates. Input only the medical findings text. Do not upload DICOM imaging files to general-purpose AI.

7.2 Limit AI Output to Question Preparation

Use AI-generated explanations and question lists to prepare for the physician encounter. Do not act on any AI conclusion without physician confirmation. AI output is preparation material, not a verdict.

7.3 Watch for Uncertainty Language

Good AI responses include qualifiers: "may indicate," "should be confirmed by a physician," "this varies significantly by individual." If AI gives overly definitive diagnostic statements or treatment directions, treat that as a warning sign.

7.4 Use AI to Decide Whether to Seek a Real Second Opinion

If a real second opinion is warranted, seek a specialist — not AI. Use AI at the prior stage: "Given these findings, what criteria would indicate I should seek a specialist neurology second opinion?" This is AI supporting a decision process, not replacing it.

7.5 Always Verify Specific Numbers

When AI cites specific statistics, prevalence rates, guideline thresholds, or medication details, verify independently. Any treatment or medication-related information must be confirmed with a physician or pharmacist before acting.

Conclusion: AI Is a Medical Translator, Not a Doctor

Using Claude Code to analyze an MRI report is genuinely worthwhile. Having unfamiliar medical jargon explained clearly, arriving at an appointment with a structured question list, and replacing anxious uncertainty with specific information — these are real improvements in medical information accessibility that cannot be dismissed.

But the next sentence must always follow: no AI output replaces a licensed physician's diagnosis and clinical judgment. Research published in June 2026 shows specialized AI systems outperforming physicians on certain metrics in controlled conditions — but that is specific tasks in experimental settings. What my MRI findings mean for my symptoms, my history, and my life can only be properly assessed by the clinician who knows me.

AI is a powerful tool for democratizing access to medical information. But every tool requires knowing its proper use. Just as you would not ask a translator to make your medical decisions, you should not ask AI to make your diagnosis.
