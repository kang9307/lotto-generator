Introduction: Why Leg Veins Become Twisted and Bulging

Varicose veins are twisted, enlarged veins that bulge just beneath the skin, usually appearing blue or purple. They are an extremely common vascular condition, but because they are often dismissed as a purely cosmetic issue, some people delay care until they develop leg pain or swelling - or, in rarer cases, skin ulcers. If you stand for long hours at work, are pregnant, or have a family history of vein problems, you may already be familiar with the sight of bulging veins on your legs.

Varicose veins develop when the one-way valves inside leg veins - the valves that normally keep blood flowing toward the heart - stop working properly. When these valves weaken or become damaged, blood flows backward and pools in the vein, and the resulting pressure causes the vein to stretch and twist. Left unaddressed, this process can progress to chronic venous insufficiency, which is why understanding the causes and symptoms early matters.

This guide, current as of July 2026, draws on publicly available guidance from health and medical institutions to cover the causes and risk factors of varicose veins, symptoms, diagnosis, treatment options from compression stockings through sclerotherapy, radiofrequency/laser ablation, and surgery, prevention and lifestyle management, and the complications of leaving varicose veins untreated. 1. What Are Varicose Veins - Vein Valves and Reflux

1.1 How a Healthy Vein Works

Leg veins must move blood against gravity, back up toward the heart. Multiple one-way valves inside the veins make this possible. Each time the calf muscles contract, they squeeze the veins and push blood upward; the valves then open and close to prevent blood from flowing back down. This mechanism is often called the "calf muscle pump."

1.2 From Valve Failure to Varicose Veins

When valves weaken or become damaged, blood cannot be pushed fully upward, and some of it flows backward and pools in the vein. Over time, this sustained pressure stretches the vein wall, causing it to lose elasticity and twist into the bulging shape recognized as a varicose vein. This most commonly develops in the legs - particularly the calves and inner thighs - because standing and walking place more pressure on leg veins than on veins elsewhere in the body.

2. Causes and Risk Factors

2.1 Age and Genetics

As people age, vein valves naturally wear down and weaken, increasing the risk of varicose veins. Having a family member with varicose veins is also associated with a greater chance of developing them yourself, suggesting that genetic factors influence the strength of vein walls and valves.

2.2 Pregnancy and Hormones

Women tend to develop varicose veins more often than men, largely because hormonal changes around menstruation, pregnancy, and menopause tend to relax vein walls. During pregnancy, increased blood volume and a growing uterus that presses on pelvic veins add further strain to leg vein circulation. If varicose veins were not present before pregnancy, they often improve naturally after delivery, though this varies by individual.

2.3 Lifestyle Factors - Prolonged Standing, Obesity, and Sedentary Habits

Prolonged standing or prolonged sitting reduces blood circulation in the legs and raises the risk of varicose veins, which is why occupations involving long hours on your feet - such as retail, healthcare, and teaching - are associated with higher rates. Carrying excess weight also increases pressure on leg veins, further raising risk.

3. Symptoms - From Spider Veins to Pain

3.1 Common Symptoms

Symptoms vary widely from person to person. Some people have visibly bulging veins with no discomfort at all, while others experience:

  • Twisted, bulging veins that appear blue or purple
  • Heaviness, dull aching, or throbbing in the legs
  • Swelling in the legs and ankles
  • Leg fatigue that worsens in the evening or after prolonged standing
  • Frequent nighttime calf muscle cramps
  • Itching around the veins, or tingling and burning sensations in the legs

3.2 Spider Veins vs. Varicose Veins

Spider veins are thin, web-like red or blue veins just beneath the skin's surface. They are smaller than varicose veins and are usually painless. Varicose veins, by contrast, are larger, bulge visibly, appear twisted, and are more often associated with pain or heaviness. Both share similar underlying causes but differ in size and symptom severity, and spider veins can occasionally be an early sign of chronic venous insufficiency, so persistent symptoms are worth having evaluated.

4. Diagnosis - Doppler (Duplex) Ultrasound

If varicose veins are suspected, a doctor will typically begin with a visual and physical examination of the legs. A Doppler ultrasound (duplex ultrasound) is considered the standard next step to assess the direction and speed of blood flow in the veins. This test combines imaging with Doppler technology to detect abnormally prolonged backward flow (reflux) and pinpoint which valves or vein segments are not functioning properly. It is painless, involves no radiation exposure, and is reported as the recommended first-line test for patients with suspected chronic venous disease.

5. Treatment 1 - Conservative Management: Compression Stockings

Compression stockings are commonly recommended as the first step in managing varicose veins. Using graduated compression - tighter at the ankle and gradually looser higher up the leg - they support the vein wall and help blood circulate more efficiently back toward the heart. One study found that wearing stockings with 18-21 mmHg of pressure for one week reduced leg aching and discomfort compared with regular stockings, and graduated pressure in the 20-30 mmHg range is generally recommended. However, compression stockings cannot eliminate varicose veins that have already formed, and they only provide symptom relief with consistent daily use, generally for at least 12 hours a day.

6. Treatment 2 - Sclerotherapy

Sclerotherapy involves injecting a special solution, either liquid or foam, into the affected vein, causing inflammation of the vessel's inner wall that leads it to gradually close. The closed vein is reabsorbed by the body over time, and blood naturally reroutes through healthy veins. It is commonly used for smaller varicose veins and spider veins, while ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy, performed with real-time imaging to track the vein's location, is also used for somewhat larger veins. The procedure is typically done quickly in an outpatient setting.

7. Treatment 3 - Radiofrequency and Laser Vein Ablation

For larger veins, such as the great saphenous vein, which is often the underlying source of an entire network of varicose veins, thermal endovenous ablation is widely used.

  • Radiofrequency ablation (RFA): A thin catheter is inserted into the vein and delivers radiofrequency energy that denatures the collagen in the vein wall and damages the inner lining, causing the vein to close.
  • Endovenous laser ablation (EVLA/EVLT): A laser fiber is threaded into the vein through a catheter and delivers laser energy that coagulates the vein wall, closing it. This is usually performed under local (tumescent) anesthesia.

Both procedures are minimally invasive and are reported to produce excellent results with a relatively short recovery period. That said, minor complications such as bruising, post-procedure pain, superficial thrombophlebitis, and, rarely, skin burns have been reported. These procedures are sometimes combined with sclerotherapy or phlebectomy as needed.

8. Treatment 4 - Surgical Treatment

When ablation or sclerotherapy does not provide sufficient improvement, or when varicose veins are extensive, surgical treatment may be considered. Traditional vein stripping removes the problem vein through an incision, while ambulatory phlebectomy removes visible varicosities through very small incisions. Advances in minimally invasive procedures have reduced how often traditional surgery is needed, but it can still be appropriate depending on the size, location, and severity of the veins and the patient's overall condition. The right treatment should be determined through an evaluation by a vascular surgeon or cardiothoracic surgeon.

9. Prevention and Lifestyle Management

Varicose veins cannot be entirely prevented, but the following habits are reported to help lower risk and ease symptoms.

  • Elevate your legs: Raising your legs above heart level while sitting or lying down makes it easier for venous blood to return to the heart.
  • Exercise regularly: Aerobic activity such as walking supports the calf muscle pump and promotes circulation.
  • Manage your weight: Maintaining a healthy weight reduces pressure on leg veins.
  • Change position often: If you must stand or sit for long periods, shift position or walk periodically to prevent blood from pooling.
  • Avoid tight clothing: Clothing that constricts the waist or groin can restrict venous blood flow.
  • Wear compression stockings: If you are at higher risk or already have symptoms, discuss consistent use of compression stockings with your healthcare provider.

10. What Happens If You Leave It Untreated - Complications

Long-neglected varicose veins can progress to chronic venous insufficiency. As this worsens, it can cause brownish skin discoloration, venous eczema (dermatitis), and lipodermatosclerosis, in which the skin and underlying tissue become hardened. The most serious complication is a venous (stasis) ulcer - an open wound that is slow to heal. If an ulcer becomes infected, it can progress to cellulitis and, rarely, to osteomyelitis or a limb-threatening infection. Superficial thrombophlebitis, inflammation of a vein caused by a blood clot, can also occur near varicose veins. If you notice leg swelling, changes in skin color, or a wound that won't heal, don't wait - see a doctor.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. What are varicose veins?

Varicose veins occur when the valves inside leg veins fail to function properly, allowing blood to flow backward and pool, which stretches and twists the veins so they bulge visibly beneath the skin. Varicose veins most often appear in the legs, particularly the calves and thighs, because standing and walking place greater pressure on leg veins.

Q2. How are spider veins different from varicose veins?

Spider veins are small, thin, web-like red or blue veins just beneath the skin and are usually painless. Varicose veins are larger, bulge more visibly, appear twisted, and are more often accompanied by pain, heaviness, or swelling. Spider veins can occasionally be an early sign of chronic venous insufficiency.

Q3. What's the difference between sclerotherapy and radiofrequency/laser ablation?

Sclerotherapy injects a solution into the vein to close it gradually and is commonly used for smaller veins. Radiofrequency and laser ablation use a catheter to deliver heat energy that closes larger veins. Which approach is appropriate is determined by a vascular surgeon.

Q4. Will compression stockings alone make varicose veins go away?

Compression stockings cannot eliminate varicose veins that have already formed, but they support circulation and can reduce heaviness, swelling, and pain, potentially slowing progression. Consistent daily use, generally at least 12 hours, is important for symptom relief.

Q5. What happens if varicose veins are left untreated?

Untreated varicose veins can progress to chronic venous insufficiency, skin discoloration, venous eczema, lipodermatosclerosis, and in severe cases, venous ulcers, which carry an infection risk if left unmanaged. See a vascular surgeon if you have persistent symptoms.
