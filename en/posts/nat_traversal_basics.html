Why Two Computers Struggle to Find Each Other — How NAT Traversal Works

Why hole punching works, the conditions under which it does not, and everything that shifts the moment a relay steps in

Aug 27, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: Both Are Online, So Why Can They Not Reach Each Other?

Your laptop is connected to the internet. The desktop at home is connected to the internet. Yet typing the desktop address into the laptop gets you nowhere. Both sit on the same internet, so why can one not call the other? The answer is not written down in any single protocol. It is the accumulated residue of compromises made back when addresses were running out.

In August 2026 a tool called tailcat, released by Tailscale, surfaced on GeekNews. It is presented as a netcat-style tool that links two machines with nothing but a short connection token — no account, no control server. The repository documentation contains one interesting passage: the connection starts out through a DERP relay while both sides exchange endpoint information and attempt a direct UDP path, and on success the traffic is upgraded from the relay to that direct path.

That short description contains this entire article. Why can the two sides not connect directly from the start? What makes the upgrade possible at all? And what changes when it fails? The subject here is not how to operate a tool but the structure of the network that those sentences take for granted. One line drawn in advance: there are no configuration commands here, no config files, and no recommendation of any particular product. Nor does this cover getting past a path your workplace or school network has closed. That is not a problem technology solves; it is a question of permission and policy.

1. Why Neither Machine Can Speak First

The starting point is plain arithmetic. An IPv4 address is 32 bits, and the number of devices in the world passed that figure long ago. So homes and offices share one public address, or a few at most, across many devices. The box that makes the sharing possible is a NAT, and what a NAT does is translate. When an inside device sends a packet out, the NAT rewrites the source address and port to the public address and a port of its own choosing, and records the correspondence as one row in a table. When a reply comes back, it looks up that table and returns the packet to the original device. From outside, the whole household looks like a single host with one address.

  • An inside device sends a packet outward — only then is a row created in the table
  • The NAT rewrites the source to the public address and an arbitrary port, recording the mapping
  • When a reply reaches that public address and port, the table is consulted and the packet is returned inward
  • After a period of silence the row is deleted — the table is finite and temporary

Here the asymmetry appears. Rows are created only when traffic leaves from the inside. If nothing has gone out and a packet arrives from outside first, there is no row telling the NAT which device it belongs to, and a packet with an unknown destination is dropped. That is the real nature of the structure that prevents anyone speaking from the outside inward. Not because it was forbidden, but because nobody wrote down a return address.

1.1 Mapping and Filtering Are Separate Axes

Treat a NAT as one undifferentiated thing and every explanation that follows turns to mush. RFC 4787 splits NAT behaviour into two axes. Mapping is the rule by which an external port is chosen and reused; filtering is the rule deciding whose packets arriving at that port are accepted. The two are independent, and these are exactly the two things to separate when diagnosing a failure.

AxisTypeBehaviourImplication for connectivity
MappingEndpoint-IndependentReuses the same external port for any destinationYour outside address is a single fixed value
Address-DependentReuses only when the destination IP is the sameThe learned address may not hold for other peers
Address and Port-DependentReuses only when destination IP and port both matchSymmetric — there is no address to hand over
FilteringEndpoint-IndependentOnce something has gone out, accepts from anyoneMost permissive — best for direct paths
Address-DependentAccepts only after you sent to that IPRequires sending toward the peer IP first
Address and Port-DependentAccepts only after you sent to that IP and portSensitive to timing

The first requirement in RFC 4787 is explicit: a NAT must have Endpoint-Independent Mapping behaviour. The reason is stated alongside it. Failing that requirement forces the use of a UDP relay, which is very often impractical.

2. Hole Punching — Going Out Together Opens the Way

Now the mechanism. If a packet arriving from outside first is discarded, only one option remains: both sides send outward at nearly the same moment.

A sends a packet toward B public address. That packet will probably be dropped at B NAT, since B has never sent anything toward A and has no row to match. Yet the failed attempt is not wasted, because a row pointing at B now exists in A NAT. If B then sends toward A public address, that packet meets a matching row on A side and passes — and at the same instant a row pointing at A appears on B side, so A packets pass thereafter. Both directions are open.

The name invites a misunderstanding worth clearing up. What hole punching opens is not the other side firewall but the table in your own NAT. It is not a procedure that disables someone else equipment; it is both parties stepping out of their own doors. Which is why it can never work one-sidedly. The peer cooperation is a precondition of the protocol.

  • Each side gathers its own candidate addresses — the local one and the outside one learned from a mirror
  • They exchange those candidates over a third channel — without that channel nothing can even begin
  • Both send toward the peer candidates at nearly the same time — rows appear on each side, and a pair confirmed in both directions is used

2.1 How Do I Learn My Own Outside Address? What STUN Does

One problem remains. An inside device knows only its private address; it has no idea which public address and port the NAT attached to it. The thing that tells it is STUN.

A STUN server behaves like a mirror. When a request arrives it copies the source address of that request straight into the response. RFC 8489 specifies that this value goes into the XOR-MAPPED-ADDRESS attribute. As the response travels back out through the NAT, the NAT rewrites the IP header but leaves the value inside that attribute untouched, so the client can read the reflexive address the outermost NAT allocated to it.

The single most important line on this topic is the one where the same RFC pins down its own limits: STUN is not a NAT traversal solution by itself, but a tool to be used in the context of a NAT traversal solution. A mirror shows you your face; it does not open a door.

2.2 An Introduction Is Still Required

Even once candidate addresses are known, there must be a path to hand them to the peer. RFC 5128 calls this role a rendezvous server. It carries no data and does nothing but introduce; the actual traffic flows directly between the two sides once the introduction is over. In the tool that prompted this piece, the connection token stands in for that role. The repository documentation describes the token as a short encoded value carrying the server public key plus information about which relay to use. A token carried by hand thus doubles as part of the introduction channel, and that design buys convenience by moving responsibility onto the person.

As for how often it works, section 4 of RFC 5128 cites measurements of the day: UDP hole punching worked on more than 80% of the NAT devices tested, TCP on just over 60%, and up to 88% with more sophisticated techniques. That document appeared in 2008, however, and carrier-grade NAT has spread widely since. Today distribution should not be guessed at from those numbers.

3. When It Does Not Work — Symmetric NAT, Firewall Policy, CGNAT

Hole punching rests on one premise: the outside address you handed the peer must still be valid when the peer sends to you. There are three places that premise breaks.

The first is symmetric NAT, which corresponds to Address and Port-Dependent Mapping in the table above. Because the external port changes with the destination, the address learned on the way out to a STUN server is valid only for talking to that STUN server. Handing that value to a peer only means the peer packets go looking for a row that does not exist. The face in the mirror is a different face from where the peer stands.

The second is firewall policy. Some organisation networks close outbound UDP entirely. That is intent, not breakage: a choice to leave only paths an administrator can control and record. In that case all a tool can do is fall back to a relay, and if the relay path is also covered by policy, the connection not forming on that network is correct behaviour.

The third is CGNAT. When the address a subscriber receives is already from a private range, traffic passes through NAT twice counting the home router beneath it. RFC 6598 sets aside 100.64.0.0/10 for exactly this purpose and states that the range is not globally routable address space. If the WAN address on a subscriber router falls in that range, treat it as a double-NAT environment.

  • Symmetric mapping — no stable outside address exists that could be announced in advance
  • Address and port-dependent filtering — if the two send times drift apart, the earlier packet is dropped
  • Outbound UDP blocked — the attempt never leaves the network at all
  • Double NAT and CGNAT — the carrier device behaviour cannot be observed or changed by the subscriber
  • Row lifetime — a long silence deletes the row and quietly closes a path that had opened

CGNAT adds a resource problem too. RFC 6888 requires a carrier-grade NAT to support limiting the number of external ports assigned per subscriber. Ports are a budget. With nothing changed on the user side, the same tool on the same line can connect directly yesterday and drop to a relay today. That is why these failures are probabilistic and hard to reproduce.

4. Where the Relay Fits — TURN

Failing to find a direct path is not a reason to abandon the connection. Hence relays. With TURN, a relay server allocates a public address and port on the client behalf and forwards traffic arriving there to the client. The peer simply sends to that allocated address, so from the peer point of view it is sending to an ordinary public host.

RFC 8656 is candid about what this costs. Using a TURN server to connect two hosts behind NATs is very likely to work, but it comes at a high cost to the provider, since such a server typically needs a high-bandwidth connection to the internet. Consequently, it says, it is best to use a TURN server only when a direct communication path cannot be found.

  • A relay is not a failure but part of the design — the connection has to work even for combinations with no direct path
  • Cost accrues with every byte that passes through, and the operator of the relay pays it

The tool that prompted this piece has the same shape. Its documentation notes that if no direct path is found the relay remains as a fallback and the connection keeps working, only with limited throughput.

5. Lay Out the Candidates and Test Them at Once — ICE

In practice nobody sits down and decides whether this particular connection will be direct or relayed. You cannot know in advance which NAT the other side is behind, so the standardised approach does not classify at all — it tries everything. That approach is ICE, in RFC 8445.

ICE gathers every address each participant can use as a candidate, pairs the two sides candidates and orders them by priority, then runs connectivity checks over each pair using STUN transactions. A pair becomes valid only when a check sent by one side is answered and the other side own check receives an answer in return. Confirming both directions matters: a path that works one way is useless in practice.

  • Host candidates — addresses actually bound to a local network interface
  • Server-reflexive candidates — learned through STUN, the address the NAT presents to the outside
  • Peer-reflexive candidates — unexpected mappings that only surface during connectivity checks
  • Relayed candidates — addresses a TURN server allocated on your behalf

This design is sensible precisely because it gives up on diagnosis. Rather than straining to work out which NAT the peer is behind, it tests the possible combinations in parallel and uses whichever forms first. There is a price. Exchanging candidates means handing the peer your list of addresses, private ones included.

6. Connecting and Knowing Who Answered Are Different Problems

Say NAT traversal succeeded. Packets are flowing. Where does the guarantee come from that whatever sits on the other end is the peer you called? Finding a path and verifying an identity are separate layers, and none of the procedures described so far guarantees identity. Even STUN knows the distinction. RFC 8489 defines separate authentication mechanisms based on a shared secret for message integrity. Even inside a protocol whose job is telling you an address, the question of who had to be handled apart.

Look at encrypted tunnels and it is clearer still. WireGuard uses the Noise_IK handshake from the Noise framework, in which each peer must know the other static public key in advance. The public key is the identity, and with a peer whose key you do not hold the handshake never forms at all. The documentation notes that an optional pre-shared symmetric key can be mixed in when an additional layer is wanted, for post-quantum resistance for instance.

  • Establishing a connection is a question of paths; verifying identity is a question of keys — neither guarantees the other
  • With public keys you must hold the peer key beforehand, and where that knowledge came from is the root of the trust
  • A pre-shared secret is only as safe as the channel that carried it — a value pasted into a chat belongs to everyone who can read that chat

So the precise meaning of connecting without an account is not that authentication vanished but that the root of identity moved from an account to a key. The responsibility a central service used to carry lands on whoever passes the token. This is exactly the place where easier and safer must not be treated as the same word.

7. What IPv6 Changes and What It Does Not

Half the problems described so far came from address scarcity, and IPv6 removes that half. Give every device a global address and there is no mapping table, and none of the asymmetry the table produces.

The firewall, however, stays. RFC 6092, covering the so-called simple security capabilities a residential IPv6 gateway should have, states as a general operating principle that transport traffic is not forwarded into the interior network unless interior transport endpoints solicited it explicitly — matching the reverse path of previously forwarded outbound traffic, or an exception the administrator configured. Different wording, familiar result: the address problem disappears and stateful filtering remains.

What is striking is how the same document frames the situation as a tension. On one side sits the desire of users for transparent end-to-end connectivity; on the other, the need of local network administrators to detect and prevent intrusion by unauthorised public users. That a standard wrote down both rather than erasing one tells you what kind of problem this is. Not a technical defect but a negotiated balance of interests.

  • What goes away — the mapping table born of address sharing, symmetric mapping, the CGNAT port budget
  • What stays — stateful inbound filtering, meaning the requirement that both sides go out first

There is a constraint as well. The path is decided by the intersection of both sides, so if only one end can use IPv6 the benefit does not materialise. Testing both paths as candidates will remain necessary for some time yet.

8. Security Implications — A Direct Path Reveals Your Address, a Relay Moves the Trust

This is the part most often left out. When a direct path forms, the peer learns your public IP. Not a side effect of the implementation but the definition of a direct path: packets cannot arrive without passing through an intermediary unless the peer knows where to send them. That is why the story of IP exposure keeps resurfacing around video calls and competitive games. Look wider and it is not one final address that leaks. The list already crossed over during candidate exchange.

  • Public IP and port — a value from which rough geography and the subscriber carrier can be inferred
  • Private address ranges and interface count — hints about how the internal network is arranged
  • Connection time and duration — this survives even when the contents are encrypted

Use a relay and the peer sees only the relay server address, but the relay operator enters as a new observer. With end-to-end encryption in place the contents stay unreadable, yet who talked to whom, when, and how much is visible to anything the traffic passes through.

AxisDirect pathVia relay
LatencyUsually lower, though path quality is not guaranteedOne extra leg, so it grows
ThroughputBounded by both end linksBounded by relay policy and bandwidth
Who paysThe two partiesThe relay operator — hence the limits on free relays
Exposed to the peerPublic IP and the candidate address listThe relay server address
What third parties seeMetadata visible to on-path observersThe relay operator sees the flow existence, timing and volume
Failure modeSome NAT combinations simply never formIf the relay is blocked the connection drops entirely

So this is not a question of safe versus risky but a question of who goes on your trust list. A direct path reveals a little more of you to the peer while placing nobody in between. A relay hides you from the peer while putting the relay operator on the list. Neither option is free.

The reason organisation networks control these paths comes from the same list. To an administrator, a direct path means a route that bypasses the observation point, and that touches policy, audit and incident response. So there is an order to things. Before trying to change which paths open by technical means, the question is whether opening that path is permitted at all.

9. Which Is Right in Which Case

Understand the mechanism and the judgement follows. The table below is not a tool selection chart but a chart of what to check first in each situation.

SituationCheck firstWhy
Two devices on the same LANWhether host candidates settle itThere is no reason to leave the network
Bulk transfer, both ends on home linesWhether a direct path formsRelay bandwidth becomes the bottleneck
Latency-sensitive real-time trafficPrefer direct, but measureDirect is usually shorter, though a relay on a good backbone can beat it
You do not want the peer to learn your IPWhether the relay can be pinnedHiding costs you the addition of the relay operator to your trust list
One or both ends behind CGNATDefine behaviour when the relay failsAssuming a direct path forms invites unreproducible incidents
Company or school assets and linesPolicy, not toolingPermission comes first. Technical possibility is not authorisation
A service that must always be reachableDesign toward a fixed addressTraversal is probabilistic and cannot underwrite availability

One structure repeats across the table. A direct path is an optimisation; a relay is a guarantee. If performance matters, establish first whether direct works; if reliability matters, define first what happens when direct fails. There is no option that maximises both at once.

What Could Not Be Confirmed

  • The success rates cited by RFC 5128 are measurements from a 2008 document. No recent public measurement covering the distribution since carrier-grade NAT spread was found
  • Per-carrier CGNAT coverage and per-subscriber port allocations in Korea could not be verified from public sources
  • Direct-connection success rates or throughput figures for the tool that prompted this piece are not in its repository documentation
  • What default filtering settings residential IPv6 routers actually ship with, by manufacturer, could not be confirmed

Wrapping Up

Two computers struggle to find each other not because of a security design but as a residue of address scarcity. NAT arrived so that many devices could share a public address, and because a NAT table fills only when traffic leaves from the inside, speaking from the outside inward became structurally impossible.

Hole punching offsets that asymmetry by having both sides send at once. What it opens is your own table, not the peer firewall, which is why the peer cooperation is a precondition. STUN is a mirror rather than a solution, and against symmetric mapping, blocked UDP or double NAT the approach simply does not hold. That is where the relay steps in. Even after a connection forms, whether the peer is who you called remains a separate problem, and whether you already held the key — and where that knowledge came from — is the root of the trust.

What is left at the end is not technology but a choice. A direct path reveals you to the peer while leaving the middle empty. A relay hides you while concentrating trust in one place. Which one is right is decided by who the peer is and what you want to keep hidden. No tool makes that judgement for you.
