The Complete Guide to SPAC Investing and the Merger Process

SPAC Structure, the De-SPAC Merger, and Investor Risks Explained

Jul 26, 2026 Finance & Economy
Investment Disclaimer: This article provides general financial education about the concept and structure of SPACs (special purpose acquisition companies). It does not recommend buying or selling any specific SPAC or financial product, nor does it guarantee investment returns. A SPAC is a risk asset where principal loss can occur through merger failure, post-merger price drops, or share dilution, and the actual rules and terms vary substantially by listing market, timing, and individual SPAC. Responsibility for investment decisions and their outcomes rests entirely with the investor, and you should always reconfirm the terms in each SPAC prospectus and official sources before investing.

Introduction: A Company That Lists Before It Has Anything to Buy?

The idea of a company that goes public before it has even decided which business to combine with can sound strange. Yet that is exactly what a SPAC is. A SPAC (special purpose acquisition company) runs no business of its own and lists on a stock exchange with a single purpose: to merge with another company. It places most of the money raised at listing into a trust account, then, within a fixed window, looks for a promising private company to merge with and bring to the public market.

This article covers how a SPAC is formed and listed, how the de-SPAC merger process works, how Korean KOSDAQ SPACs differ from US SPACs, and the risks and decision criteria every investor should understand. Recent reporting notes a growing number of SPACs dissolving or being delisted after failing to find a merger target in time, so the notion that a SPAC is a risk-free, principal-guaranteed investment deserves particular caution. This article is informational and does not recommend any specific security.

1. What Is a SPAC?

A SPAC is a special shell company whose business purpose is the merger itself. When an ordinary company lists, regulators review a real entity with revenue, operations, and staff. A SPAC, by contrast, goes public with only cash in trust and a plan to find a good company to merge with. Buying SPAC shares is therefore often described as betting in advance on a future merger target that has not yet been chosen.

The rationale for SPACs is usually framed as opening a transparent, regulated route for a private company's reverse listing. A company with a strong business model that finds the traditional IPO process burdensome can obtain a listing relatively quickly by merging with an already-listed SPAC. For investors, the appeal is often cited as a chance to invest in a growth company while a trust account provides a floor - though, as we will see, that floor has clear limits.

2. SPAC Structure and Listing: The Trust Account Is Central

The most important mechanism in a SPAC is the trust account. Most of the money a SPAC raises at IPO (in Korea, above a set proportion by rule and in practice close to the full amount) is placed with an outside trustee so the sponsors cannot spend it freely. This trust funds the merger if a deal completes, and becomes the source of returns to shareholders if the deal fails.

Korean KOSDAQ SPACs are commonly listed at a share price of around 2,000 won, while US SPAC units are commonly priced around 10 dollars. The trust earns interest - typically at a level similar to a bank term deposit - and at dissolution the principal plus that interest is generally returned. In theory, then, an investor can get back roughly the IPO-price principal plus a little interest even if the merger fails - but this applies strictly to investors who bought at the IPO price, an important qualification.

3. The De-SPAC Merger Process: From Target Search to Approval

The central events for a SPAC are the target announcement and merger approval. After listing, the SPAC's management searches within the fixed window for a private company to merge with. Once a target is set, the merger valuation and exchange ratio are determined and put to a shareholder vote for approval through the exercise of voting rights.

3.1 Redemption Rights: The Right to Object

Shareholders who dislike the proposed deal are generally granted redemption rights. If they are unconvinced by the exchange ratio or the target's industry and outlook, they can vote against at the shareholder meeting and redeem their shares for a set price - typically a pro rata share of the trust. This mechanism means investors are not dragged into an unwanted merger and retain the option to recover their capital, a defining feature of SPACs.

3.2 Deadline and Dissolution

Korean SPACs are generally granted a life of about three years, and if a merger is not completed within that window the SPAC is dissolved and the trust is returned to shareholders. In practice, however, the review and registration steps take time, so the period actually available to source a target is shorter than the headline figure.

4. Korean SPACs vs. US SPACs: What Differs

The basic frame is similar, but the details differ. The table below summarizes frequently cited differences; treat it as a general tendency to aid understanding rather than an absolute rule.

The warrant common in US SPACs is a right to buy shares later at a set price. It offers investors additional upside, but exercising it increases the share count and dilutes existing shareholders - a two-sided feature. In every market, SPAC terms can vary by venue and timing, so always confirm the specific terms in the relevant SPAC prospectus before investing.

Even under the same "SPAC" label, where your money is locked up and how you get it back differs by market. Always re-confirm the specifics in the individual SPAC's charter and prospectus.

The two most important rows are the last two. In Korea and the US alike, what you get back is based on the per-share trust value, not on "the price I paid". The SEC investor bulletin makes the same point: investors who buy SPAC shares in the secondary market receive only the original per-share trust value on redemption, not their purchase price. That single sentence explains the most common route to a loss in SPAC investing.

5. How to Invest in a SPAC: IPO Subscription and Open-Market Buying

There are two broad routes. The first is subscribing at the IPO, buying at the offering price (around 2,000 won in Korea). Here the trust floor operates at the IPO-price level, so the chance of recovering principal even if the merger fails is relatively higher. The second is buying on the open market after listing.

There is one crucial caution here. If you buy when the market price sits above the IPO or trust value (a premium), then even if the SPAC dissolves and you receive the trust amount back (roughly the IPO price plus interest), that premium can become a loss. So when buying after listing, it is generally wise to check how far the current price sits above the trust value per share.

6. The Risks of SPAC Investing

Because of the trust structure, SPACs are easily mistaken for principal-guaranteed instruments, but several real risks exist.

  • Merger failure / dissolution risk: If no suitable target is found in time, the SPAC dissolves. Principal is generally returned, but capital was tied up and the hoped-for merger gain never materializes.
  • Post-merger price-drop risk: Shares may spike on merger-announcement enthusiasm, then fall sharply if the combined company's results disappoint - a frequently cited pattern.
  • Dilution risk: When new shares are issued or warrants exercised during the merger, the total share count rises and the value of existing holders' stakes can be diluted.
  • Conflict-of-interest risk: Sponsors are often rewarded only if a merger closes, giving them an incentive to push a deal that may not align with public shareholders' interests.
  • Premium-loss risk: As noted, any amount paid above the trust value is not protected by the trust-return structure.

7. Decision Checklist

Here is a set of items worth checking before approaching a SPAC. Use it as a framework for judgment, not as a recommendation of any specific security.

  • How far does the current price sit above the trust value (roughly IPO price plus interest)? The larger the premium, the greater the loss risk if the merger fails.
  • How much of the SPAC's life remains? As the deadline nears, there is concern that a rushed deal may be forced through.
  • If a target has been announced, are the merger valuation and exchange ratio reasonable, and is the target's business verifiable?
  • If you object to the merger, what are the price and procedure for redemption?
  • How much future dilution could arise from warrants and similar instruments?

7.1 What to Check Before a Target Is Announced

A SPAC with no announced target is, in practice, nothing but trust cash plus remaining time. At this stage the useful checks are numerical ones.

  • Find the trust value per share. Dividing total trust assets shown in the prospectus or periodic reports by shares outstanding gives an approximate per-share figure.
  • Calculate the premium ratio yourself: (current price − trust value per share) ÷ trust value per share × 100. That is the size of the portion that can vanish if the merger fails.
  • Check the trust ratio and the interest rate on the trust. How much of the offering is deposited, and at what yield, determines the expected payout at maturity.
  • Count the remaining life in months. Subtract the time needed for review, the shareholder meeting, and registration, and the window actually available to source a target is shorter still.
  • Look at the sponsor group and the lead underwriter. The merger and dissolution record of SPACs they previously launched can be traced through filings and press coverage.
  • Check the size of sponsor-held shares and convertible bonds. If those convert into shares at the merger, they become a dilution factor.
  • Review lock-up volumes and their release dates. Selling pressure concentrated at release can weigh on the price.
  • Check trading volume and free float. A SPAC that barely trades may be hard to sell at the price you want.

7.2 What to Check After a Target Is Announced

Once a target is disclosed, a SPAC stops being a "box of cash" and becomes an investment in that company. Your checklist has to change with it.

  • Are the target's revenue, operating profit, and other results verifiable in filings, or is the case built mainly on future plans?
  • Are the basis for the merger valuation and the exchange ratio disclosed, and are the assumptions defensible?
  • Has the post-announcement spike pushed the premium over trust value to an excessive level? Re-apply the same formula you used before the announcement.
  • Have you tracked the shareholder-meeting date and the deadline for filing notice of dissent? Exit rights usually require following a prior procedure.
  • How are the price and exercise window for the exit right spelled out in the disclosure?
  • What is the status and schedule of the exchange's preliminary review of the merger listing? Deals are reported to collapse at this stage as well.
  • What about post-merger overhang and listing-maintenance standards? A small market capitalization after the merger has been flagged as a potential burden.

8. How a SPAC's Share Price Moves: Trust Floor, Merger-Hope Ceiling

A SPAC's share price does not move the way an ordinary stock's does. With no revenue and no earnings, it cannot be explained by results; instead it is usually understood as a structure in which the trust supports the downside while merger expectations lift the upside. While no target exists, the price tends to trade in a narrow band near the per-share trust value; when a rumor or an announcement lands, it can jump sharply on expectations - a pattern described as recurring.

  • Per-share trust value: acts as the effective floor, and creeps upward over time as trust interest accrues.
  • Remaining life: with the deadline far off there is "time to wait" and expectations hold; as it nears, the possibility of dissolution gets priced in and the share tends to converge toward the trust level.
  • Merger-related information: target announcements, valuation disclosures, and preliminary review outcomes each move the price materially.
  • Market view of the sponsor and underwriter: SPACs launched by groups with a record of completed mergers are perceived to carry more expectation.
  • Supply-demand and rule changes: free float is small, so modest buying can spike the price, and changes to public-offering allocation and lock-up rules are said to affect volatility right after listing.

The easiest misreading here is "the trust is a floor, so it cannot fall below that". The trust is an amount settled at dissolution, not a guarantee for the daily share price. The opportunity cost of capital locked up until dissolution, liquidation costs and taxes, and the possibility of a trading halt can all produce stretches where the share trades below per-share trust value. Conversely, buying into a spike leaves the floor unchanged while only your own cost basis moves up. Loss of principal is entirely possible in a SPAC.

9. What Investors Can Do After a Merger Is Announced

When a target is announced, investors reach a fork in the road. The options are simple enough, but each carries its own deadline and procedure, and missing one can push you into an outcome you did not choose.

  • Hold: if the merger is approved, you become a shareholder of the combined company at the exchange ratio. From that point you are exposed to that company's results and valuation, not to a SPAC.
  • Sell in the market: sell at the market price before the shareholder meeting. You may realize a gain during the post-announcement spike, but if enthusiasm fades the price can retrace to pre-announcement levels.
  • Object and exercise your exit right: in Korea this generally means filing notice of dissent before the shareholder meeting and claiming within a set window. In US SPACs the corresponding right is described as redemption, and it is generally exercisable separately from how you vote.
  • Do nothing: this is also a choice. Once the procedural deadlines pass, the chance to object or claim disappears and the result is the same as having chosen to hold.

One procedural trap is flagged repeatedly. In Korea, exercising the exit right generally requires meeting prior conditions such as filing notice of dissent before the shareholder meeting. In the US, the SEC investor bulletin warns that missing a warrant redemption (call) notice deadline can leave warrants worthless. Either way, "I will look into it later" is the most dangerous stance. The safer habit is to move every date printed in the filings and notices onto your calendar first.

10. IPO Subscriptions vs. SPAC Investing: What Differs

SPACs are often lumped in as "a kind of public offering", but the nature of the risk an investor actually takes is quite different. The table below compares an ordinary company's IPO subscription with SPAC investing across entry method, return structure, and risk.

ItemOrdinary IPO subscriptionSPAC investing
What you invest inA real company with revenue and assetsA shell company with no target chosen yet
Entry methodBrokerage subscription (equal and proportional allocation), usually with a depositSPAC IPO subscription, or buying on the market after listing
Price settingOffering price fixed after institutional bookbuildingIn Korea, standardized at around 2,000 won per share
Downside protectionNone; market price from day oneTrust-return structure, for offering-price buyers
Return structureThe gap between offering price and post-listing priceWhether the merger closes, and the value of the merged company
How long capital is tied upA few days from subscription to refund; tradable right after listingUp to about three years, until merger or dissolution
Main risksOverpriced offering, price decline right after listingFailed merger and dissolution, post-merger slump, dilution
Right to object or exitGenerally noneExit right, and return of the trust on dissolution

According to Korean financial institutions' explanatory materials, domestic IPO subscriptions apply equal and proportional allocation together and normally require a subscription deposit. The points where the two most often diverge in practice are worth restating.

  • An IPO is closer to a short, concentrated event; a SPAC is closer to a long wait. The same money is tied up in a different way.
  • An IPO has no downside device but lets you analyze the company now; a SPAC has the trust floor but gives you nothing to analyze yet.
  • A SPAC's trust floor works fully only for those who bought near the offering price. The moment you pay a post-listing premium, much of that advantage disappears.
  • Neither supports the generalization that "subscribing means going up", and loss of principal is possible in both.

11. Recurring Patterns in SPAC Failures

Korea's SPAC market has been going through a difficult stretch. According to a 16 July 2026 report by the Korea Economic Daily, around 20 KOSDAQ SPACs entered delisting or liquidation procedures this year while only five completed mergers. Greened Daily likewise reported figures showing the SPAC merger success rate falling from roughly 60% a year in the past to the 30% range. Without naming any individual security, the patterns repeatedly cited in this environment can be grouped as follows.

  • The deadline scramble: with the life of the SPAC running out, a target is chosen in haste, only to run into shareholder objections or review delays over the terms or the business case.
  • The review hurdle: a merger agreement is signed but fails at the exchange's preliminary review of the merger listing, or the agreement is withdrawn.
  • The expectation retrace: the price spikes right after the announcement, then drifts back toward the trust level, leaving only those who bought near the top with a loss.
  • The post-merger shortfall: the merger closes, but the combined company falls short of the plan it presented and the share price declines over a long period.
  • The listing-maintenance squeeze: the merged company is too small and faces pressure over listing-maintenance requirements. Several outlets report that KOSDAQ's market-capitalization threshold for maintaining a listing is being raised in stages from 2026 (reported as roughly 15 billion won in 2026 rising to 30 billion won in 2028), so the exact standard should be confirmed against Korea Exchange rules.

What these patterns share is that the premise "it goes up as long as the merger closes" did not hold. What decides the outcome in SPAC investing is less whether a merger closes than on what terms and with which company it closed - plus the price you paid. The categories above summarize general tendencies noted in the past; they do not predict future outcomes and cannot be used to judge whether any particular SPAC will succeed.

12. Conclusion: How to Approach a SPAC

A SPAC is a distinctive instrument that combines a trust-account floor with a growth bet on a merger. But that floor works only in a limited form - principal recovery for IPO-price buyers if the merger fails - and misreading it is a mistake. First, if buying after listing, always check the premium over the trust value. Second, rather than getting swept up in the post-announcement spike, coolly assess the target's business and the deal terms. Third, given the period your capital may be locked up and the dilution and dissolution risks, approach only with spare funds.
