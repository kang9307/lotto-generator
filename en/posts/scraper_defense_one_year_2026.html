Introduction: why you have to read logs, not graphs

When an operator talks about traffic to their own site, the evidence is almost always an analytics dashboard. And the dashboard is structurally incapable of seeing this problem. Web analytics counts a hit only when JavaScript executes in a browser, and automated collectors almost never run JavaScript. So how hard the server is actually being pounded is never recorded there at all.

A set of real numbers showing how wide that gap can get has now been published. Nick Gray, who runs the philanthropy donor research database PatronView, released a year of server-log observations from his 1.5 million-page site. In one week the server handled 2.5 million external requests and served 1.28 million pages. Over the same window, analytics recorded 5,977 human pageviews. That is under half of one percent.

This article does not read that record as a war story. It lands on one question: am I being hit too, and if so what do I do first? It covers which signals to look for in the logs, how effect, cost and side effect diverge across countermeasures, and finally which overreactions a small site must never make.

To be clear up front. This is written from the defender's side only. It does not cover collection techniques or ways around blocks. And the most dangerous item here is not the scraper but false-positive blocking of search crawlers. One badly written rule leaves the scrapers untouched and removes only your index. That is handled separately in section 7.

1. Why analytics cannot see this problem

If you do not change the measuring instrument, the diagnosis never begins. Get this wrong and every judgement downstream is wrong with it.

1.1 A JavaScript tag counts humans only

Gray reported using a self-hosted Plausible Community Edition. Whatever the tool, the property is the same. A tag embedded in the page has to run in a browser before a hit is registered, and collectors generally take the HTML and never execute the script. So the analytics number is a usable estimate of human traffic and completely unusable as a measure of server load.

  • In Gray's records there were roughly 214 invisible page loads for every one that analytics captured
  • So a dashboard showing 1,000 pageviews a day does not mean the server worked 1,000 times. The multiplier differs per site, and nobody knows theirs until they measure it
  • If you sit behind a CDN or reverse proxy, the origin log will not hold all of it either. Edge logs or the dashboard request count have to be read alongside
  • If you are on static hosting with no log access, look at bandwidth consumption and request-count graphs at minimum. Both count bots

1.2 The four things to pull the first time you open the logs

The tooling need not be elaborate. If an access log is in reach, these four alone will outline the situation.

# 1) Top 20 request counts by User-Agent
awk -F'"' '{print $6}' access.log | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -20

# 2) Top 20 request counts by client IP
awk '{print $1}' access.log | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -20

# 3) Status code distribution (a 404 spike signals URL enumeration)
awk '{print $9}' access.log | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn

# 4) Total requests (place this beside the analytics number for the same window)
wc -l access.log
  • Lining up 1 and 4 over the same window is the whole point. Mismatched windows make the multiplier meaningless
  • If the top IPs in 2 are concentrated in one or two addresses, the response is easy. If instead they are spread evenly across tens of thousands of IPs, IP blocking is not the answer
  • An abnormal number of 404s in 3 means something is walking URLs that do not exist. Real users do not produce that shape
  • A short log rotation window shows you nothing. If you plan to diagnose, extending retention comes first

2. The signals that identify a scraper in your logs

Used alone, any single signal produces misjudgement. Decide only where several overlap.

2.1 Signals and the false-positive risk of each

Signal visible in the logWhat it suggestsFalse-positive risk
Total requests are tens to hundreds of times analyticsAutomated collection dominates total loadLow, though images and API calls must be separated out
Empty User-AgentA script running on stock defaultsLow. Some legacy clients also send it empty
Requests pouring from a single datacentre ASNA collector running in the cloudMedium. Corporate proxies and VPN users share ASNs
Tens of thousands of IPs each sending only a few hitsDistributed collection through residential proxiesHigh. Mobile carrier NAT looks much the same
Browser versions several years out of dateA forged UA nobody bothered to updateMedium. Real users on old devices exist
Listing pages walked in strict sequence to the endWhole-site collectionLow. Humans do not move in that order
404 ratio suddenly spikesURL pattern enumerationLow, except right after a site restructure

Referer always emptyURLs constructed and called directlyHigh. Plenty of legitimate traffic sends no Referer
More traffic overnight than during the dayAccess unrelated to the human activity curveMedium. Normal for sites with an overseas readership

2.2 How to use this table

  • Never build a rule on a single signal. Block on a high-risk row alone and humans are what you catch
  • The safest combination is datacentre ASN plus no static assets plus sequential listing traversal, all three at once
  • Conversely, an empty Referer on its own must never be rule material. Privacy settings, in-app browsers and bookmark entries all land there
  • Before blocking, always run the rule in log-only mode for several days to see how many hits it takes per day and whether humans are among them
  • Gray did exactly that with his stale-browser-version check and reported that only 0.54 percent of live traffic matched it. Measuring how much a rule catches comes before enforcing it

3. Crawl-to-referral ratio - reducing the decision to one line

The criterion Gray settled on is not technical but economic. How many pages does this crawler take, and how many humans does it send back? If the ratio is bad, it gets blocked.

3.1 The published measurements

CrawlerTakenHumans sentRatio
Googlebot--46 pages per visitor
Bingbot (before blocking)--406 pages per visitor
Bingbot (later observation)158,610 pages680 visitorsroughly 233:1
Claude-SearchBot420,680 pages a week (4.63GB)12 visitors (175KB)roughly 35,000:1

The row worth staring at is Googlebot's 46:1. Gray judged that one to be earning its keep. That is to say this criterion is not a device for treating all bots as hostile, but a ruler that separates crawlers who pay their way from those who do not.

3.2 Where this metric breaks down

  • The answer inverts with site type. If being cited in AI answers is itself the goal, zero referrals is no reason to block
  • Referral measurement is itself imprecise. Many paths send no Referer, so actual referrals may be undercounted
  • Some services show an attribution without linking. Brand exposure exists and the log records nothing
  • Conversely, referrals arriving does not tell you whether those referrals are worth anything. Ratio without conversion is half the picture
  • Gray himself framed the issue as pricing rather than technology, saying that with a per-crawl payment scheme he would have sold access at a fair price. Blocking is the fallback when there is no way to negotiate

4. What scrapers actually do

To choose a response you first have to sort the shapes. Gray's records contain three groups with entirely different characters.

4.1 The ones that announce themselves

Crawlers from large operators put their own name in the User-Agent. They follow the spec, which makes them the easiest to handle and the fastest to verify.

  • Anthropic stated in February 2026 that its three crawlers respect robots.txt
  • Gray's records matched that statement. Claude-SearchBot dropped from about 60,000 requests a day to 25 within days of a block, roughly 99.96 percent
  • The counter-example also exists. Amzn-SearchBot kept arriving after a blocking rule was in place, logging 43,150 requests. It keeps knocking after receiving a denial
  • The response to self-identifying crawlers is simple. Measure the ratio, block by name if it is bad. Risk up to this point is low

4.2 The ones that hide - residential proxies and forged UAs

This is where the trouble starts. Traffic that gives no name, poses as an ordinary browser, and arrives over rented household internet connections.

  • Gray reported observing a botnet posing as Chrome versions and routed through thousands of ordinary home connections rented from proxy companies. Every individual request looks like a real person
  • On 31 July the unique IP count jumped from 18,000 to 124,000 in a day. That is the point at which IP-level blocking becomes meaningless
  • There was also a case of 23,000 pages taken in one hour from Microsoft Azure, split across more than 80 IPs so that existing rate limits never triggered
  • So the axis of response shifts. From IP to ASN, and from the single request to the behaviour of the whole session

Why session-level detection is the current direction. Looking at one request, a forged header and a real browser are indistinguishable. So commercial defence products have moved from per-request fingerprints to the movement pattern of the entire session. The background to that approach is covered separately in Precursor Arrives - Why Cloudflare Reads Whole Sessions to Catch AI Agents.

4.3 The one-day surge

Not a gradual climb but a several-fold jump inside a single day. This is closer to a load incident than to scraping.

  • On 22 April 2026, Gray's site logged 3.6 million requests from 361,844 unique IPs in one day, almost all originating in China
  • Cloudflare's automatic challenge processed 1.18 million in the first ten hours alone
  • He applied a country-level block the next day, later adding Vietnam and Singapore when the same pattern appeared
  • The defining trait of such a surge is that the alarm arrives late. As long as the server holds, error rates do not rise, and you find out when the invoice does

5. Effect, cost and side effect of each countermeasure

This is the destination of the article. Each measure gives and takes something different. Get the order wrong and you collect the side effects without the benefit.

5.1 Comparison of measures

5.2 The order this table implies

  • Cache comes first. It is the only measure that reduces load with essentially no side effect. Less scraper defence than basic hygiene
  • Next is the verified-bot skip rule. It has to sit ahead of the blocking rules so those rules never meet a search bot. Invert this order and everything after it becomes dangerous
  • Third is name-based blocking. Name the crawlers whose ratio is bad and block those. A list, not a wildcard
  • Fourth is ASN and rate limiting. False positives begin here, so measure in log-only mode first
  • Challenges and country blocks come last. Reach for them only when the invoice or the response time is genuinely broken

5.3 The rule set Gray settled on

For reference, the firewall configuration Gray arrived at after a year had nine stages. It demonstrates that the order is the design.

  1. Block specific countries (China, Vietnam)
  2. Block 12 SEO tool crawlers by name
  3. Block AI crawlers, with the robots.txt path allowed as an exception
  4. Let verified bots skip the rules that follow
  5. Managed challenge for regions outside North America
  6. Challenge on empty User-Agent
  7. Challenge on 46 datacentre ASNs
  8. Challenge on browser versions years out of date
  9. Challenge above 30 requests per 10 seconds per IP

Look at the positions of rules 3 and 4. Even with a rule blocking AI crawlers, Googlebot survives only because the verified-bot exception sits behind it. And the reason rule 3 leaves the robots.txt path allowed is equally clear. Block robots.txt itself and even a crawler that wants to comply cannot read what is forbidden.

6. What robots.txt does and does not do

This is where misunderstanding runs deepest. robots.txt is not a blocking mechanism. The specification says so itself.

6.1 What RFC 9309 actually prescribes

The Robots Exclusion Protocol was standardized as RFC 9309 in September 2022. The key provisions run as follows.

  • The document states that these rules are not a form of access authorization. There is no enforcement
  • If the server answers a robots.txt request with 4xx, the crawler may access any resource on the server. An absent file reads as blanket permission
  • Conversely, on 5xx the crawler must assume complete disallow. An unstable path serving robots.txt can halt crawling entirely
  • Caches should not be used beyond 24 hours. Change a rule and some crawlers still run on the old one for a day
  • Parsers must read at least 500 KiB. Rules written past that point may never be read
  • The security section is blunter still: listing paths merely exposes them, so use a real security measure where access control is needed

6.2 So how do you use robots.txt

  • Use it as a declaration of intent. It genuinely works on those who honour it. Claude-SearchBot falling 99.96 percent after a block is the evidence
  • Do not list secret paths. The moment an admin path goes into Disallow it is public. What needs authentication does not belong there
  • Always leave robots.txt itself reachable. If a firewall rule blocks the file, even cooperative crawlers cannot read the rules
  • Do not let this file return 5xx. The spec reads that as complete disallow, which can shake the whole index
  • Enforcement happens separately at the firewall layer. robots.txt and firewall rules are two devices with different purposes

7. Block a search bot by mistake and the index disappears

This section matters most. The largest thing scraper defence can cost you is not server spend but search visibility.

7.1 How the accident happens

  • The most common accident is a rule that blocks anything whose User-Agent contains bot, crawler or spider. Googlebot and Bingbot go down with it
  • Second is a rate-limit threshold set too low. Search crawlers sweep fast by design. Set the threshold to human pacing and the crawler trips first
  • Third is a country block covering a crawler's origin. Which regions a search provider crawls from is not under the site owner's control
  • Fourth is challenging every request. Crawlers do not solve challenges. The challenge screen gets indexed instead of the page
  • Fifth is blocking the robots.txt path itself. If the rules cannot be read, cooperation becomes impossible

7.2 The real danger is that the loss surfaces late

  • Rankings do not fall the moment you block. Pages start leaving the index after the recrawl interval comes around. That takes days to weeks
  • So the causal link from rule to traffic drop is invisible, and most operators suspect something else first and lose time
  • Recovery is not symmetric either. Lift the block and crawl budget still needs time to come back
  • Three safeguards. First, put a verified-bot exception ahead of the blocking rules. Second, name block targets explicitly. Third, watch crawl stats and index counts in Search Console for two weeks after deployment
  • Cloudflare judges verified bots on two criteria: honest self-identification (a cryptographic signature, a published IP list with a stable UA, or reverse DNS) and non-abusive behaviour (honouring robots.txt, reasonable request rates), and it publishes that list. That is the practical basis for avoiding false positives
  • Gray blocked AI crawlers while continuing to allow Googlebot and Bingbot. That is arithmetic, not generosity. 46:1 and 233:1 were ratios that paid their way

8. The cost the defence itself imposes

This is the item most often left out of the evaluation. The blocking machinery is not free either. It charges in speed and accessibility as well as in fees.

8.1 Performance cost, confirmed in numbers

  • Gray reported that Cloudflare's JavaScript detection consumed 2,875 milliseconds on a mid-range phone. The site's own JavaScript came to 278 milliseconds
  • In other words the security feature was producing more than ten times the delay of the site code
  • Turning it off lifted the mobile Lighthouse score from 58 to 99 within an hour
  • There was a price. After it came off, a wave took 23,000 pages in an hour from Azure. The trade-off laid bare
  • The lesson is plain. After enabling a security feature, measure performance again. Turning something on and being able to leave it on are different questions

8.2 Challenge solve rate is a gauge of the burden on real users

If you have deployed challenges, you must monitor the solve rate. That number tells you what is actually being stopped.

  • Gray's 48-hour observation: 106,437 challenges issued, 252 solved, a 0.24 percent solve rate
  • Traffic from Singapore solved 3 out of 7,089. India failed 99.14 percent of the time, Iraq 100 percent
  • In the 24 hours after the final rule set: 46,729 requests blocked outright, 63,969 challenges issued, 552 solved
  • Read it this way. A solve rate around 0.2 percent means almost everything is automated. A rate around 30 percent means humans are being caught in bulk, and the rule needs narrowing
  • There is a reason not to read solve rate alone. People who give up the moment a challenge appears never enter the failure statistics. Abandonment happens outside the number

8.3 How the bill moved

  • Gray put ordinary infrastructure cost at around 90 dollars a month (Workers, D1 and so on)
  • During heavy scraping that figure rose by roughly 500 percent
  • The higher plan needed for the security rules ran 25 dollars a month
  • Gray noted that on a fixed-capacity server this traffic would have become an existential cost problem. Metered versus flat-rate makes the same traffic wear a different face
  • The practical conclusion follows. Check your billing model first. On flat-rate hosting the response time breaks before the bill does; on metered pricing the response stays fine and the invoice arrives first

9. Overreactions a small site must not make

This is where mistakes cluster. A site with a few hundred visits a day that copies the rule set of a 1.5 million-page database gains nothing and loses plenty.

  • Do not challenge every visitor. On a low-traffic site, one abandonment at a challenge screen is a straight traffic loss. The visits lost exceed the spend saved
  • Do not copy someone else's block list. ASN and UA lists are built around that site's audience composition. Start from where your own readers come from
  • Do not open with a country block. That is a measure for after the source is identified. Colour in the map before you know the cause and only humans get stopped
  • Do not write broad UA regexes. Most accidents originate here. Listing names one by one is tedious and safe
  • Do not set rate limits at human pacing. Opening one page in a browser fires dozens of asset requests. Thresholds set with a page-level intuition catch real users
  • Do not make JavaScript execution a condition of access. Users who browse with scripts off, assistive technology users and old devices are stopped together
  • Do not buy a paid product before reading logs. Layer a product on without knowing the cause and, when a false positive hits, you cannot even tell which layer blocked it
  • Do not make bot traffic itself the target. What has to come down is not the request count but the bill and the response time. Trying to solve with blocking what cache would solve buys only side effects
  • Do not deploy rules all at once. Turn on nine simultaneously and you cannot tell which one caused the problem. One at a time, several days each, in log-only mode
  • Do not treat a block as finished. The other side moves. Without a quarterly re-measurement the rules remain and the effect is gone

There is a counter-argument too. Commentary on this case included the view that much of the problem lies not with bots but with site architecture. If 1.5 million dynamic pages cannot be cached, the load arrives eventually with or without bots. And for some sites, in tourism or commerce, bot access is a benefit. Blocking is not a universal remedy but a choice determined by your own site's economics.

10. Where to start - the order of operations

The order is itself the safeguard. Follow it and a serious accident becomes hard to cause.

  1. Measure first. Put server request count and the analytics number for the same window side by side. Without the multiplier no judgement is possible
  2. Check the cost. Has the bill actually risen, has the response actually slowed? If neither, you may stop here
  3. Fix caching. Raise the hit rate and reduce heavy dynamic paths. The only stretch with no side effects
  4. Rank by User-Agent. Pick the self-identifying crawlers from the top of the list and measure crawl-to-referral ratio for each
  5. Deploy the verified-bot exception first. Keep it ahead of every blocking rule that follows
  6. Block only crawlers with a bad ratio, by name. As a list, with no wildcards
  7. Log-only mode for several days. Confirm how many hits each rule takes per day and whether humans are among them
  8. Apply ASN and rate limits. False positives start here, so one change at a time
  9. Monitor the index for two weeks. If crawl stats or index counts fall, revert immediately
  10. Challenges only if it still hurts. Watch the solve rate alongside; narrow the rule if it reaches 30 percent
  11. Re-measure every quarter. When the other side moves, so does the effect of your rules

11. What could not be confirmed

Items lacking sufficient evidence, gathered in one place.

  • The full wording of the original article and the exact firewall rule expressions. Figures here follow the values carried in the GeekNews summary and the PPC Land report. The actual rule syntax was not cross-checked
  • The nature of the 43,150 Amzn-SearchBot requests after blocking. Whether those received a denial response or arrived by another path was not confirmed
  • The volume of search traffic lost across the year. No figure was published for how many real users the country blocks and challenges cost
  • Retry patterns of blocked crawlers. Whether any returned under a different name was not confirmed
  • Reproducibility at other site sizes. These numbers come from a 1.5 million-page database site. There is no basis for expecting the same multiplier on a site of a few hundred pages
  • Who actually commissioned the residential-proxy traffic. Logs cannot tell you who is collecting or why
  • Equivalent options at other providers. This case presupposes one CDN provider's feature set. Whether the same features carry the same names and behaviour elsewhere was not confirmed

12. Frequently asked questions

How do I tell whether my site is being scraped?

Look at server access logs, not analytics. Tools like Plausible or GA count only visitors who execute JavaScript, and most scrapers never run any. In the week of logs published by PatronView operator Nick Gray, the server handled 2.5 million requests and served 1.28 million pages, while analytics recorded 5,977 human pageviews, under half a percent. Four things are worth pulling from the logs. First, the gap between total request count and the analytics number over the same window. Second, request volume ranked by User-Agent. Third, requests concentrated in a single IP or a single ASN. Fourth, URL traversal in an order no human would follow. If two of those four line up, automated collection is running against you.

Will robots.txt stop scrapers?

It will not. RFC 9309, which standardized robots.txt, states plainly that these rules are not a form of access authorization, and its security section says listing paths merely exposes them publicly, so anyone needing access control should employ a real security measure. In other words robots.txt is a request that binds only those who choose to honor it. That does not make it useless. It genuinely works on crawlers that follow the spec. In Gray's records, Anthropic's Claude-SearchBot fell from roughly 60,000 requests a day to about 25 within days of a block. Against operators who ignore the spec it does nothing, so keep robots.txt as a statement of intent and enforce actual blocking at the firewall layer.

If I block AI crawlers, will my search visibility drop too?

It depends entirely on how the rule is written, and the risk is real. A broad regex that blocks any User-Agent containing words like bot or crawler will catch Googlebot and Bingbot along with everything else. When a search crawler is blocked, pages fall out of the index, and that loss does not reverse the moment you remove the rule. The safe approach has two parts. First, place a verified-bot skip rule ahead of every blocking rule so Googlebot and Bingbot never reach the rules behind it. Second, name your block targets explicitly in a list rather than with wildcards. After deploying, watch crawl stats and index counts in Search Console for at least two weeks.

Is country-level blocking acceptable?

It is effective and it is the bluntest instrument on the list. On 22 April 2026 Gray saw 3.6 million requests from 361,844 unique IPs in a single day and blocked China the next day, adding Vietnam and Singapore when the same pattern appeared. For a site whose audience is domestic, that can be a reasonable trade. Three consequences come with it. First, real users in that country, plus expatriates, travellers and roaming visitors, are blocked together. Second, scrapers simply relocate, so the effect is not permanent. Third, country blocking is what you reach for after the logs have identified a source, not the first card you play while the cause is still unknown. If international visitors touch your revenue, try ASN-level blocking first.

Does a small site need a paid WAF?

Not until the monthly bill actually hurts. Defensive machinery carries its own cost, and not only in subscription fees but in performance and accessibility. Gray reported that Cloudflare's JavaScript detection consumed 2,875 milliseconds on a mid-range phone, and that turning it off lifted the mobile Lighthouse score from 58 to 99 within an hour. The mechanism meant to filter bots was degrading perceived speed for humans first. The test is simple. Has traffic growth actually raised your bill or slowed your responses, or does the graph merely look alarming? If it is the latter, raising cache hit rate and rate-limiting only the expensive paths will usually settle it.

Conclusion: three things to take away

First, change the diagnostic instrument before anything else. Analytics was built to count humans and is therefore incapable in principle of seeing this. Simply placing server request count next to the analytics number for the same window settles half the decision. In Gray's records the multiplier was roughly 214. Nobody knows their own until they measure it.

Second, the criterion is value, not bot count. Reduce it to crawl-to-referral ratio and the response becomes simple. Googlebot's 46:1 earns its keep; 35,000:1 does not. But this criterion inverts per site. Where being cited is itself the point, zero referrals is not a loss. Decide your own site's economics and the rules decide themselves.

Third, the biggest thing at risk is not server spend but the index. One broad regex, one low rate-limit threshold, and the search crawler is what gets stopped. That loss surfaces days later and does not come back the instant you revert. A verified-bot exception ahead of the blocking rules, block targets written as an explicit list, and two weeks of index monitoring after deployment. Those three are not negotiable.

One last thing. What endures from this case is not the nine firewall rules but their sequence. Measure, check cost, cache, exempt, block, observe. Follow that order and most sites will stop at step three. And stopping at step three is usually the right answer.
