Facial Recognition in Public Space: What You Need to Know Before Judging It

Technical branches, where errors land, eight institutional controls and five proportionality questions

Aug 16, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: The four seconds it takes to pass a gateline

Walking through a ticket gate takes a few seconds. If, in those seconds, a camera captured your face, converted it into numbers, compared it against a list and then discarded it, the experience is indistinguishable from nothing having happened at all. That is precisely what makes this debate hard. The person affected rarely knows an intrusion occurred, and nobody can calculate whether it worked until the operator publishes numbers.

Splitting into camps first therefore stalls the conversation. Calling it a surveillance state and calling it obviously necessary for safety both stop at the same point. The questions that actually move things forward are narrower. What exactly does this system do? When it is wrong, who absorbs that error and in what form? What evidence supports the claim that it works? And is the information needed to answer those questions actually published?

What follows takes those four questions in turn. This is not an assessment of any particular country or agency, and it does not cover ways of evading cameras. The aim is to lay out the axes a judgement needs, and to keep clear which parts of each axis are established and which are not.

1. What is confirmed, as of August 2026

Start narrow, with what has been made public. What this article establishes is a live facial recognition (LFR) trial led by the British Transport Police with support from Transport for London. It is a trial, not a permanent or network-wide rollout. Based on the Transport for London press release of 11 August 2026, the confirmed scope is as follows.

  • Status — a trial. It extends a pilot that began at key transport hubs on 11 February 2026, and it runs for roughly four more months.
  • First deployment — the first use inside an Underground station took place at Victoria station on 11 August 2026.
  • Scope — not every station, but selected stations chosen on the basis of intelligence and crime data analysis, rotating between Underground and mainline rail stations. A full list of stations is not available in the published material.
  • Targets — people on an authorised watchlist connected to serious and high-harm offences including robbery, knife crime and sexual assault, those wanted by police or the courts, and those breaching bail conditions or court orders.
  • Non-matches — the stated policy is that images of people who do not match the watchlist are not retained and are deleted automatically and immediately.
  • Notification — deployments take place in signed areas so that passengers can make an informed choice about walking through.
  • Operator — the trial is run by the transport police using trained officers of that force, and the announcement states that a data protection impact assessment and an LFR policy have been published.

Going any further than that would not be accurate. A trial is a trial, and whether it becomes permanent depends on the evaluation and the decisions that follow it. The converse does not hold either: being a trial is not a reason for lighter controls. Because the word trial so often ends up standing in for governance, a trial only really counts as one when it has an end date and an evaluation that gets published.

2. Facial recognition is not one technology: identity matching and attribute estimation

Several quite different technologies sit inside the single phrase facial recognition, and about half the confusion in this debate starts there. The broad split is between systems that produce an identity and systems that produce a classification without any identity.

2.1 The identity-matching family

A face captured on camera is reduced to a vector of several hundred numbers encoding the geometric relationships between eyes, nose and jawline. That vector is then measured against the vectors on a prepared list. The closer the distance, the higher the similarity, and once similarity crosses a defined threshold the system raises an alert. If the list holds one person, it is one-to-one verification; if it holds many, it is one-to-many identification. Matching passers-by against a wanted list at a station is the latter.

2.2 The attribute-estimation family

The same facial region is used, but there is no list. The output is a classification: age band, gender, expression. Age estimation at an alcohol counter, audience composition counts on advertising screens, dwell-time analysis in shops. Emotion inference belongs here too, though its underlying scientific validity is itself contested in the research literature and deserves separate handling.

What trips people up in practice is that both families can run on the same camera, on the same frame. So when assessing a system, the question to ask is not what the hardware is called but what it outputs. Does this system ultimately produce the identity of a specific person, or an anonymous classification? That single answer determines both the nature of the risk and which legal regime applies.

3. What an accuracy figure conceals: thresholds and denominators

The sentence 99 percent accurate carries almost no information on its own, because facial recognition performance is a curve, not a point. Raise the similarity threshold and false positives fall while missed matches rise. Lower it and the reverse happens. Without knowing where the threshold was set, there is no way to know which point on the curve a published accuracy figure describes.

The second thing that matters is the denominator. A false alert rate of one in 6,000 means entirely different things depending on what is being divided. If the denominator is people who walked past the camera, then at a station handling tens of thousands of passengers a day it implies several wrong alerts every day. If the denominator is alerts generated, the scale is completely different. Where published material leaves this unspecified, the calculation simply cannot be done.

The third is what happens after the alert. An alert does not automatically become an identity check or a stop; a human looks at the screen and decides. That step filters some errors and introduces others. The tendency for a machine suggestion to pull human judgement toward it has been studied for decades under the name automation bias. System accuracy and end-to-end operational accuracy are therefore two different numbers, and the second one has to be measured and published separately.

In short, verifying an accuracy claim requires at least four things together: the threshold setting, the denominator of people scanned, the size and composition of the list, and capture conditions such as lighting, angle and face coverings. Miss any one of them and the figure is unverifiable, and an unverifiable figure cannot serve as evidence for either side.

4. Who the errors land on

False positives are not evenly distributed. The most widely cited source here is the demographic effects section of the facial recognition algorithm evaluation published by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) in December 2019. State the scale and conditions first: 189 algorithms submitted by 99 developers, tested against 18.27 million photographs of 8.49 million people drawn from operational databases of the State Department, the Department of Homeland Security and the FBI.

  • In one-to-one verification, false positive rates for Asian and African American faces ran higher than for Caucasian faces, with the gap ranging from 10 to 100 times depending on the algorithm.
  • Among US-developed algorithms, Native American demographics recorded the highest false positive rates.
  • In one-to-many identification, false positives were elevated for African American women.
  • However, variation between developers was very large. Some algorithms developed in Asian countries showed no such dramatic difference between Asian and Caucasian faces.
  • The researchers noted that the composition of training data may influence this difference, while stating that they had not established causation.

Those last two points are the heart of the finding. What follows from it is not that facial recognition is inherently biased, but that bias is a property of a particular algorithm, a particular threshold and particular capture conditions. Which means neither a demonstration of fairness nor a demonstration of unfairness transfers from one system to another. Each deployment has to be measured again. That conclusion is equally inconvenient for advocates and for opponents.

4.1 The UK measurement and its conditions

In the UK, an equitability study of facial recognition in law enforcement carried out by the National Physical Laboratory was published in March 2023. Its reported conclusions have to be read together with their conditions. At a face-match threshold of 0.6 or above, the false positive rate was very low and differences across race, age and sex were not statistically significant. Lowering the threshold to 0.58 and 0.56 produced more false positives, and within those, racial disparities were observed, with Black people incorrectly flagged relatively more often. No demographic performance differences were reported for retrospective or operator-initiated matching.

The value of that result is not that it grades any particular system as acceptable. It is that it demonstrates the threshold is a policy variable, not a technical constant. If one setting changes the equity profile of identical hardware, then that setting is not a matter for operator discretion; it is something to be published and supervised. It also has to be held alongside two caveats: the figures describe one vendor system measured under conditions at one point in time, and the extent to which they generalise has been contested by researchers.

4.2 What a single false positive actually leaves behind

Translating probabilities back into people changes the picture. At a high-footfall station, one false positive is a rounding error in the statistics. For the person flagged, it is being stopped in a public place, giving their name and explaining what they are carrying. Three things follow from that.

  • Asymmetry — the system sends an alert and forgets it; the individual spends time and social standing on the spot. The distribution of cost is lopsided from the outset.
  • Accumulation — when false positives recur within a particular group, what changes is not a set of individual incidents but that group's experience of travelling. This is the route by which a statistical gap becomes a social one.
  • Records — unless it is settled whether a post-alert contact generates a record, how long it is kept and how it is erased, a false positive can produce consequences long after the moment has passed.

5. How a claim that crime fell can be checked

Crime reduction is the justification offered most often, and it can be put into a verifiable form. It should be. Arrest counts are not by themselves evidence of a fall. More arrests means more detection, not less offending, and there are periods in which the two indicators move in opposite directions.

5.1 Minimum design requirements

  • Control group — comparison against similar stations without deployment. Before-and-after alone cannot separate an underlying trend from the intervention.
  • Observation window — long enough periods on both sides. A few weeks is at the mercy of seasonality and day-of-week effects.
  • Displacement — check whether offences rose at adjacent stations or in adjacent hours. Crime may have moved rather than fallen.
  • Reporting rates — awareness of cameras can change willingness to report, sending incidents and reports in different directions.
  • Regression to the mean — deployments usually follow a spike in a place and time, so numbers fall back toward the average with no intervention at all.
  • Alternative explanations — the deterrent effect of uniformed officers simply standing there has to be separated from the technology. A comparison arm with the same staffing and no cameras is what makes that possible.

5.2 The numbers that make verification possible

  • Number of deployments and cumulative hours of operation
  • Estimated number of people scanned and how the estimate was derived
  • Number of alerts generated
  • Number that became an actual contact after human review
  • How many of those were genuinely on the list and how many were not
  • Demographic distribution of people stopped in error
  • Threshold used, list size, and counts of additions and removals from the list

Without these, the claim that it works and the claim that it does not are both unverifiable. That symmetry matters. A shortage of transparency does not only weaken the case for deployment; it weakens the case against it by exactly as much. Demanding publication is therefore not a partisan position but closer to a precondition for having the argument at all.

6. An institutional control checklist

Independently of technical performance, which safeguards are attached changes outcomes considerably. The eight rows below capture the points that recur as failure modes in biometric processing in public space. It works better as a list of things to look for in published material than as a scorecard for any particular scheme.

ControlWhat it asksWhat happens without it
Legal basisIs there a basis that specifically permits this processing, rather than an interpretation of a general clause?Justification gets assembled after the fact, and the lawfulness fight is pushed onto individual litigants
Purpose specificationIs the purpose expressed as particular offences and situations rather than as safety?A broad purpose leaves no criterion for refusing expansion
Watchlist criteriaWho goes on the list, under what test, and when and how do they come off?People whose cases are resolved stay on, or the inclusion criteria widen quietly

Using the table is simple. When a deployment is announced, count how many of the eight rows are filled in by documents. Filling all eight does not make a deployment legitimate, but if half are blank, there is no material with which to discuss legitimacy at all.

7. Translating this into Korean statutory provisions

This section is a general description of a regulatory framework, not legal advice. Whether any specific scheme or incident is lawful turns on the facts, so a concrete determination requires a lawyer or the consultation channels of the Personal Information Protection Commission. Readers outside Korea should check the equivalent instruments in their own jurisdiction.

Looking at the same situation under Korean law, two layers overlap. Installing the camera and feeding the footage into facial recognition are governed by different provisions, and conflating them tangles the discussion.

7.1 The camera layer: Article 25

Article 25 of the Personal Information Protection Act restricts the installation and operation of fixed video processing devices. Installing them in open public places is prohibited as a rule, and one of the following exceptions must apply.

  • Where a statute specifically permits it
  • Where it is necessary for the prevention and investigation of crime
  • Where a duly authorised party operates it for facility safety and management and for fire prevention
  • Where a duly authorised party operates it for traffic enforcement
  • Where a duly authorised party operates it for the collection, analysis and provision of traffic information

The same article imposes a duty to install a notice sign that data subjects can readily see, and prohibits arbitrarily manipulating the device, pointing it elsewhere, or using audio recording functions. A requirement to prepare an operation and management policy sits alongside. A 2023 amendment added Article 25-2 to deal separately with mobile devices such as drones and body-worn cameras.

7.2 The recognition layer: Article 23

This is the decisive one. Being permitted to record video does not automatically carry permission to extract facial features from that video and identify individuals. Article 23 restricts the processing of sensitive data as a rule, and through the 2023 amendment, information about physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics generated through certain technical means for the purpose of identifying a specific individual — biometric identification data — was brought within that category.

Processing of sensitive data is restricted in principle, with exceptions where the data subject gives separate consent or where a statute requires or permits the processing of such data. Yet obtaining separate consent from an unspecified crowd walking through a public place is structurally hard to construct: consent presupposes a genuinely free ability to refuse, and a corridor people must pass through is not an easy place to create that condition. In practice the discussion therefore converges on whether a statute specifically requires or permits it, and how specific that basis is becomes the contested point.

7.3 The rights side

  • Article 35, access — the categories of personal data processed, the purpose, retention period and third-party provision history can be requested.
  • Article 36, correction and deletion — inaccurate content can be corrected or deleted on request.
  • Article 37, suspension of processing — processing can be asked to stop, and refusal requires one of the grounds set out in the statute.
  • Article 37-2 — the 2023 amendment introduced rights to refuse and to obtain an explanation of automated decisions. Whether a decision finalised by a human still counts as automated is open to divergent readings, so how this provision applies to a facial recognition alert is a contested question.

8. Proportionality: five questions for judging whether a measure goes too far

Lawful and justified are different layers. Even with a statutory basis, a problem remains if the means are excessive relative to the purpose. Proportionality sounds abstract, but split into five questions it becomes fairly concrete.

QuestionSigns it passesSigns it fails
Is the purpose specific?Target offence types and situations are specified in documentsDescribed only through umbrella words like safety or order
Does the measure actually contribute to the purpose?An evaluation design with a control group exists in advanceOnly arrest counts are produced afterwards
Is there a less intrusive alternative?Targeted investigation, staffing and lighting or layout changes were considered on the recordThe technology is chosen first and alternatives never assessed
Does the gain exceed what is given up?Loss of anonymity for the general public and chilling effects are booked as costsThe cost column contains only budget and equipment
Is it bounded, with an end condition?Stations, duration and operator are fixed, with a published end and evaluation pointThe trial is extended indefinitely and the scope grows quietly

The third and fifth questions are the ones that collapse most often in practice. Alternatives assessments tend to be written up after the technology decision has already been taken, and a trial without a stated termination condition drifts into permanence. Scope creep usually arrives not through bad intent but through the convenience of infrastructure that is already installed. A system launched for serious-offence wanted lists then adds missing persons, then persistent fare evaders, then people banned from the premises, and each step looks reasonable in isolation. Without a procedure that puts all five questions again at every step, expansion effectively runs on its own.

9. Exercising rights: what is available and where it stops

What an individual can do lies in using the channels the law provides rather than in avoiding cameras. It helps, though, to start out knowing where those channels deliver value and where they run into walls.

9.1 Where a request is worth making

  • Operation and management policy and policy documents — the basis for installation, the area covered, retention periods, any processing entrusted to third parties and access permissions should be written down here.
  • Impact assessment documents — these show whether risks were identified and mitigations considered before deployment.
  • Deployment times and locations — where a public body is the operator, these can fall within freedom of information requests.
  • Watchlist inclusion and removal criteria — asking for the criteria themselves, rather than about a specific individual, tends to produce an answer.
  • Records left after an alert — if you were actually stopped, the records created at that moment are where access, correction, deletion and suspension requests acquire real purchase.

9.2 Where it structurally stops

There is a paradox here that deserves to be stated honestly. A policy of immediately deleting the images of people who did not match is a privacy safeguard and at the same time a source of unverifiability. Data that no longer exists cannot be accessed, and cannot be used to prove it was handled wrongly. Both are true, and describing only one of them misrepresents the policy. Which is why, the stronger the deletion policy, the more the publication of aggregate statistics and audit logs becomes the only remaining substitute for an individual right of access.

  • It is hard for an individual even to establish that they were scanned, and a request that cannot specify its target struggles to get off the ground.
  • Where several bodies are involved, responsibility splits. A venue operator and a police force will send different answers.
  • Processing for investigative purposes carries its own restrictions and exemptions, so it does not proceed like a request to an ordinary commercial operator.
  • One person's request will not halt a deployment. What a request usually changes is how much documentation becomes public, and that becomes the material for the next stage of the discussion.

It is therefore more efficient to separate the individual and collective tracks. Individually there are access and suspension requests and complaints to the supervisory authority; collectively there are freedom of information requests, local councils and public hearings, and coordinated civil society action. Each route yields something different.

10. What this article could not establish

Gathered in one place, the items left out for want of a sufficient basis.

  • The similarity threshold applied in this trial and the vendor of the system used
  • The size of the watchlist, the detailed procedures for adding and removing entries, and who authorises them
  • The full list of stations covered and the deployment schedule
  • The metrics to be used in the post-trial evaluation, and when and in what form results will be published
  • The contents of the impact assessment and policy documents the operator states it has published
  • The per-demographic tables in the full text of the UK equitability report. This article worked only from the summarised conditions and conclusions
  • Whether live identity matching is operated at transit facilities in South Korea, and on what legal basis
  • The types of records created when a post-alert contact occurs, and how long they are retained

How short that list gets is a fair measure of how far a society is able to judge the system at all.

Frequently asked questions

How does live identity matching differ from facial attribute estimation?

Identity matching turns a face into numeric features, compares them against a list and raises an alert past a threshold. The output is a specific person's identity. Attribute estimation has no list and produces only classifications such as age band or gender. Errors land differently: the first turns into a stop on the spot, the second into denial of service or differential treatment. Both can run on one camera, so identify the system by its output.

Can a claim of 99 percent accuracy be trusted?

Not from a single number. False positives and false negatives move in opposite directions with the threshold, so performance is a curve rather than a value. Verification needs the threshold, the denominator of people scanned, the list size and the capture conditions. An alert is also not an arrest, and the error rate of the human review step has to be measured separately.

Is it true that errors fall more heavily on some demographic groups?

In the NIST evaluation published in December 2019, one-to-one matching showed higher false positive rates for Asian and African American faces than for Caucasian faces, with gaps of 10 to 100 times depending on the algorithm. The same evaluation reported very large variation between developers, and some algorithms developed in Asian countries showed no such difference. Bias behaves less like a fixed property of the technology and more like a property of a specific algorithm, setting and condition.

Which legal provisions come into play in South Korea?

Installing cameras falls under Article 25 of the Personal Information Protection Act; extracting facial features to identify people falls under Article 23. Article 25 prohibits fixed devices in open public places as a rule, with exceptions for specific statutory authorisation, crime prevention and investigation, facility safety, management and fire prevention, traffic enforcement and traffic information collection, and requires a notice sign. Article 23 treats characteristic information generated to identify a specific individual as sensitive data whose processing is restricted. Whether any given scheme is lawful depends on the facts and is not asserted here.

What can an individual actually do?

Access, correction and deletion, and suspension requests under Articles 35, 36 and 37 are the standard routes, and where a public body runs the system, freedom of information requests can reach the operating policy and impact assessment. The limits are clear: immediately deleted non-match images no longer exist, so there is nothing to access. Practical value therefore sits with policy documents and aggregate statistics, and if you were actually stopped, the records created then become the target.

Conclusion

A judgement about facial recognition in public space has nothing to do with liking or disliking technology. It needs four confirmations. Whether the system produces an identity or a classification. Whether the conditions behind its accuracy, including threshold and denominator, are published. Whether claims of effect rest on a design with a control group and an adequate observation window. And how many of the eight control rows, from legal basis through to independent oversight, are actually filled in by documents.

Both sets of arguments deserve the same weight. A design that confines the list to wanted persons for serious offences, posts signage, deletes non-match images immediately and submits to evaluation on a fixed timetable is genuinely different from indiscriminate collection. At the same time, processing biometric data in a corridor that an unspecified public crosses daily draws down anonymity as a common good, and that cost is paid by individuals who never register having paid it. Neither consideration automatically defeats the other.

The most useful practical stance, then, is to ask for the numbers. Threshold, people scanned, alerts, contacts, and errors with their distribution. When those five lines are published regularly, both support and opposition become verifiable arguments; when they are not, both sides trade impressions. What is genuinely worth contesting in this debate is far less the existence of the technology than whether those five lines see daylight.
