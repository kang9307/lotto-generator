Where the 1-Won Mismatch Occurs in VAT Calculations - Placing Rounding Logic in System Architecture

The absence of statutory sub-won rounding in Korean tax law, arithmetic drift between line-item and aggregate tax calculations, and policy encapsulation in billing engines

2026-09-13 Dev & Infra

Among the most perplexing operational defects encountered by software engineers building e-commerce billing platforms, payment gateway settlement pipelines, and double-entry accounting ledgers is the sudden failure of batch reconciliation jobs caused by a discrepancy of exactly 1 Korean Won. The sum of value-added tax calculated individually across discrete line items frequently disagrees with the tax computed by applying the statutory 10 percent rate once across the aggregate order supply price. In terms of nominal currency, one won represents negligible economic purchasing power. Yet in enterprise general ledgers where debits and credits must balance to zero, or in electronic tax invoice validation engines governed by national tax authorities, a 1-won discrepancy triggers complete transaction rollbacks, batch aborts, and catastrophic transmission rejections.

When this 1-won mismatch surfaces during integration testing or production deployments, software developers almost invariably consult statutory tax databases. The instinctive engineering hypothesis assumes that somewhere within national tax law, a definitive legal clause must exist stating whether fractional won amounts below 1 won should be truncated, rounded half-up, or adjusted across installment intervals. However, a comprehensive line-by-line examination of the entire Value-Added Tax Act and all 152 articles of its Presidential Enforcement Decree reveals an unexpected reality: the statutory framework contains absolutely no explicit clause governing whether sub-won fractions must be truncated or rounded, nor does it specify whether rounding must occur at the line-item level or at the aggregate invoice level.

This structural statutory void constitutes the foundational discovery of this technical analysis: the handling of tax rounding is fundamentally an internal domain architectural policy rather than an external statutory mandate. Because the law does not enforce a micro-level rounding algorithm, distinct enterprise systems—including custom checkout frontends, third-party payment gateways, ERP packages such as SAP or Douzone, and tax clearance gateways—inevitably implement divergent rounding sequences. This article distinguishes the commercial boundaries between the Value-Added Tax Act and public finance legislation, dissects the mathematical mechanics causing 1-won drift across discrete integer currencies, analyzes the hazards of reverse tax decomposition from gross payment totals, and establishes comprehensive architectural guidelines for isolating rounding responsibilities within domain models.

The First Reality Confronting Developers When Searching Tax Statutes for a 1-Won Mismatch

When software engineers observe that summing line-item VAT yields an amount that deviates by 1 won from multiplying the aggregate supply price by 10 percent, the typical engineering response is to search for a definitive authoritative formula. Developers naturally operate under the mental model that a state tax authority must maintain a standardized mathematical function, and that any reconciliation failure simply stems from failing to invoke that canonical statutory rule.

Yet examining the official statutes published by the Ministry of Government Legislation—specifically the Value-Added Tax Act (Act No. 21065) and its accompanying Enforcement Decree—demonstrates that no such prescriptive formula exists. Article 29 (Tax Base) establishes the structural baseline that the value-added tax base for supplies of goods or services shall be the aggregate sum of the supply values provided during the relevant taxable period. Article 30 defines the general tax rate as 10 percent. Article 32 (Tax Invoices) mandates that tax invoices must state the supply value and the value-added tax amount as required entries.

Crucially, nowhere in Article 29, Article 30, or Article 32 does the legislation dictate that fractions under 1 won resulting from multiplying the supply value by 10 percent must be floored, rounded, or ceiled. Furthermore, examining Article 67 of the Enforcement Decree of the Value-Added Tax Act reveals requirements to list supply items, unit prices, quantities, and dates of supply, but the administrative decree remains entirely silent regarding fractional sub-won quantization.

This absence frequently astonishes software practitioners who expect hyper-specific regulations within highly formalized tax jurisdictions. However, statutory frameworks deliberately refrain from micro-managing integer quantization at the single-cent or single-won level because commercial trade patterns vary widely across industries. The primary concern of the tax administration is whether the aggregate tax base reported across the entire taxable period accurately reflects statutory tax liabilities, leaving the exact timing and direction of intermediate sub-won rounding to established commercial practices and corporate accounting discretion.

The Boundary Between the VAT Act and the National Funds Management Act — Why Treasury Rules Do Not Apply

In their search through legal corpora, developers frequently encounter provisions containing terms such as fractional calculation and mistakenly believe they have discovered the governing rule. Specifically, developers stumble upon Article 47 (Calculation of Fractional Sums of National Funds) of the National Funds Management Act and Article 109-2 of its Enforcement Decree. Believing that Korean law mandates truncating amounts under 10 won, engineers sometimes incorrectly conclude that commercial VAT must also truncate figures under 10 won or absorb fractional remainders into the initial payment installment.

Applying this treasury regulation to commercial software architectures represents a severe conceptual error. The National Funds Management Act is a public finance statute governing the receipt, disbursement, and management of national state treasury funds. Article 109-2, Paragraph 1 of the Enforcement Decree specifies that when the state collects, receives, or disburses national funds in installments, any installment amount under 10 won or any fractional remainder under 10 won shall be aggregated into the initial receipt or disbursement sum. This rule exists solely to eliminate the administrative overhead of minting, circulating, and collecting fractional physical coinage during direct interactions between government agencies and citizens.

Commercial transactions between private enterprises, consumer retail checkouts, and B2B electronic tax invoices governed by the Value-Added Tax Act are entirely outside the statutory scope of the National Funds Management Act. Commercial tax invoices and credit card sales slips routinely express supply values and tax amounts down to the exact 1-won integer. Conflating treasury fund collection rules with private commercial billing would result in truncating a legitimate tax amount of 137 won down to 130 won, creating catastrophic accounting distortions and tax reporting liabilities.

  • Jurisdictional Scope of National Funds Legislation: Restricted strictly to state administrative bodies, public treasuries, and tax offices collecting final national revenues or disbursing state budgetary funds.
  • Jurisdictional Scope of the Value-Added Tax Act: Encompasses all independent taxable persons supplying goods or services for commercial purposes, governing B2B and B2C commercial trade.
  • Quantization Granularity Disparity: The National Funds Management Act operates at a 10-won threshold, whereas commercial value-added tax accounting mandates integer precision at the 1-won level.
  • Doctrinal Limits of Statutory Analogy: Under the principle of legality in taxation, tax liabilities and calculation methods must derive strictly from designated tax codes; public treasury procedural rules cannot be analogized to private commercial billing.

Line-Item Tax Calculation vs Aggregate Supply Calculation — The Arithmetic Origin of the 1-Won Drift

Having established that tax law does not impose a single mandatory rounding algorithm, the true root cause of the 1-won discrepancy emerges: it is an inherent arithmetic consequence of operating within a discrete integer currency lacking sub-units, combined with the commutativity failure between intermediate rounding and summation. Unlike the United States Dollar with its fractional cent or the Euro with its euro cent, the South Korean Won operates strictly as an integer currency in physical transactions and modern electronic payment ledgers. Consequently, any fraction generated during percentage computations must be quantized into an integer.

In software engineering practice, billing architectures implement one of two primary tax calculation sequences:

Method A: Line-Item Tax Calculation — The system multiplies the supply value of each individual order line item by 10 percent, applies an integer rounding function to determine that item's exact tax, and then sums these discrete integer taxes to arrive at the total order VAT. This approach offers significant operational advantages for post-order workflows: because each line item holds a permanently fixed integer supply value and tax amount, partial cancellations, selective refunds, and line-item returns can be processed without retroactively recalculating the tax distribution of untouched items.

Method B: Aggregate Supply Calculation — The system first sums the supply values of all order line items to compute the aggregate order supply price, and then applies the 10 percent tax rate once to this total. While this method minimizes the percentage deviation between the total invoice supply price and the final tax figure, it introduces complex challenges when line items must later be posted to separate departmental accounting sub-ledgers requiring discrete itemized tax values.

To demonstrate the mathematical drift concretely, consider a shopping cart containing exactly three identical items, each having a supply value of exactly 3,333 won. Multiplying 3,333 won by 10 percent yields a raw decimal value of 333.3 won, providing a pristine benchmark to observe the systemic impact of integer quantization.

Analyzing the values in the comparative table illustrates precisely how the 1-won discrepancy materializes. When applying standard half-up rounding, Method A rounds each line item to 333 won, producing an aggregate tax sum of 999 won (333 + 333 + 333). Conversely, Method B computes the aggregate supply price of 9,999 won and multiplies it by 10 percent, producing a raw tax value of 999.9 won, which rounds half-up to 1,000 won. Despite utilizing identical input prices, the identical statutory tax rate, and the identical mathematical rounding algorithm, the simple sequencing choice of whether integer quantization occurs before or after aggregation generates an unavoidable 1-won disparity.

The 1-Won Collapse When Decomposing Gross Payment Amounts into Supply Price and VAT

While B2B enterprise billing typically computes tax outward from net supply prices, B2C retail commerce operates predominantly on tax-inclusive gross amounts. Under Article 29, Paragraph 7 of the Value-Added Tax Act, when consideration received by a taxable person includes value-added tax or when the division is ambiguous, the supply value is deemed to be the consideration multiplied by 100 divided by 110.

Critical accounting integrity bugs emerge when payment systems receive a captured consumer credit card transaction for a single gross amount (e.g., exactly 10,000 won) and subsequently attempt to decompose that gross figure into separate supply price and tax fields for general ledger posting. Decomposing 10,000 won produces recurring non-terminating decimals: raw supply price equals 10,000 / 1.1 = 9090.909090... won, while raw tax equals 10,000 * 0.1 / 1.1 = 909.090909... won. Converting both to integers without an explicit balancing strategy inevitably shatters accounting balance.

Evaluation of Decomposition Strategies for a Gross 10,000-Won Transaction
Decomposition Strategy Derived Supply Price Derived VAT Amount Sum of Derived Values Reconciliation with 10,000-Won Principal
Strategy 1: Dual Independent Floor 9,090 won 909 won 9,999 won 1-won evaporation (reconciliation failure)
Strategy 2: Floor Supply, Balance Residual Tax 9,090 won 910 won 10,000 won Sum matches, but 9,090 won × 10% = 909 won mismatch
Strategy 3: Half-Up Round Supply, Balance Residual Tax 9,091 won 909 won 10,000 won Perfect balance (9,091 won × 10% = 909.1 won → 909 won)
Strategy 4: Half-Up Round Tax, Balance Residual Supply 9,091 won 909 won 10,000 won Perfect balance (identical outcome to Strategy 3)

Under Strategy 1, independently truncating both components via floor functions results in a combined total of 9,999 won. Exactly 1 won vanishes from system records, violating the fundamental accounting identity that Gross Amount = Supply Price + Tax Amount and causing automated ledger audits to fail.

Strategy 2 forces reconciliation by assigning the residual amount to tax (10,000 - 9,090 = 910 won). However, applying 10 percent to the stated supply price of 9,090 won yields exactly 909 won. A tax auditor or customer viewing an invoice with a 9,090 won supply price and a 910 won tax charge will perceive an arithmetic error, as the stated tax exceeds 10 percent of the stated net value.

In contrast, Strategies 3 and 4 achieve complete harmony: by rounding the supply value half-up to 9,091 won and deriving the tax as the exact subtraction residual of 909 won, the components sum perfectly to 10,000 won. Furthermore, multiplying 9,091 won by 10 percent produces 909.1 won, which rounds half-up to exactly 909 won, satisfying internal formula consistency and gross reconciliation simultaneously.

Floor, Half-Up Round, and Ceil — Business Policy Choices in the Absence of Statutory Mandates

Given the absence of statutory enforcement, system architects must deliberately evaluate whether to standardize on floor, half-up rounding, or ceil operations across their billing engines. The realization that statutory law leaves this unconstrained delegates full architectural responsibility to the engineering organization.

Each mathematical operation carries distinct domain implications:

  • Floor (Truncation): Historically prevalent in manual paper bookkeeping and Korean retail traditions. Truncation provides psychological reassurance that consumers are never overcharged even by a fraction of a won. However, in large enterprise baskets containing dozens of line items, systematically flooring sub-won fractions causes compounding downward bias, eroding total tax liability and causing effective tax rates across orders to drift below the nominal 10 percent (how far depends on item count and price distribution, so this piece does not assert a specific figure).
  • Half-Up Rounding: The standard in modern software engineering, supported by IEEE 754 floating-point specifications and enterprise ERP defaults. Because remainders below 0.5 round down while remainders at or above 0.5 round up, rounding errors exhibit an expected value of zero across large volumes of transactions, preventing systemic ledger drift.
  • Ceil (Upward Rounding): Rarely adopted in commercial taxation engines. Applying upward rounding risks customer disputes regarding unauthorized charges above statutory rates. It is considered only in ultra-conservative margin preservation engines where under-collecting fractional liabilities must be prevented under strict contractual boundaries.

The cardinal rule of financial system design is that the specific choice between rounding methods matters far less than ensuring that the chosen policy is executed uniformly across checkout, payment settlement, invoice generation, and accounting journalization without intermediate divergence.

Inter-System Inconsistencies Across Invoicing, Accounting Pipelines, and Enterprise ERPs

The destructive potential of a 1-won mismatch is realized when data flows across heterogeneous system boundaries. Catastrophic pipeline breakdowns occur when custom checkout platforms, third-party ERP suites such as SAP or Douzone, and electronic tax invoice Application Service Providers (ASPs) calculate tax using divergent internal logic.

Consider a standard failure mode: an e-commerce checkout engine adopts Method A (line-item half-up rounding) and charges a customer 10,998 won for three 3,333-won items (9,999 won supply price plus 999 won tax). During nightly synchronization, this order record is transmitted to the enterprise ERP system. The ERP, configured under default settings to compute tax via Method B (aggregate supply calculation), multiplies 9,999 won by 10 percent and computes tax as 1,000 won, projecting an expected settlement of 10,999 won.

At this moment, automated journal creation halts. The ERP journal generation interface rejects the batch because the recorded payment debit of 10,998 won fails to match the calculated credit expectation of 10,999 won. Because double-entry ledgers reject unbalanced transactions, thousands of settlement records are blocked, requiring manual financial intervention.

Furthermore, when transmitting electronic tax invoices to the National Tax Service (Hometax) via standard XML schemas, validation engines reject documents if the header tax amount conflicts with itemized line totals. If a merchant submits an invoice where individual line items sum to 999 won but the invoice header declares 1,000 won, the clearance gateway rejects the document, generating compliance penalties and administrative friction.

Software Architecture for Isolating Rounding Rules in Database and Billing Engines

To insulate billing platforms permanently from 1-won reconciliation failures, software architects must enforce rigorous engineering principles throughout the persistence and calculation layers:

  • Prohibit On-the-Fly Dynamic Recalculation: Eliminate all code in presentation templates, export scripts, and reporting queries that computes tax dynamically via inline formulas like order.supplyPrice * 0.1. Tax must be computed exactly once during the initial order creation transaction and persisted in the database as discrete, immutable integer columns (supply_price, tax_amount, and total_amount).
  • Encapsulate Rounding in Domain Services: Ban arbitrary invocations of Math.floor or Math.round across application controllers and repositories. All arithmetic transformations must be encapsulated inside a dedicated, versioned domain service (e.g., VatCalculationPolicy), ensuring a single point of modification for corporate policy adjustments.
  • Apply the Largest Remainder Method for Discrepancy Allocation: For composite orders where both the aggregate order tax and itemized line taxes must align perfectly, systems should first establish the authoritative aggregate tax, distribute proportional shares to individual items, and allocate any remaining 1-won fraction to the line item possessing the highest decimal remainder.

The following JavaScript reference implementation demonstrates a resilient domain calculation function that decomposes a gross transaction amount into supply price and VAT without risking 1-won reconciliation failures.

/**
 * Standard domain policy for decomposing gross payment amounts into supply and VAT.
 * 
 * Design Principles:
 * 1. Derives supply price via half-up rounding (VAT Act Article 29 Paragraph 7 ratio of 100/110).
 * 2. Derives VAT amount as the exact residual subtraction, guaranteeing zero reconciliation drift.
 * 3. Enforces integer values corresponding to non-fractional currency units (KRW).
 */
function calculateVatFromGrossAmount(grossAmount) {
    if (!Number.isInteger(grossAmount) || grossAmount < 0) {
        throw new Error("Gross payment amount must be a non-negative integer.");
    }

    // 10,000 / 1.1 = 9090.9090... -> 9,091 won (half-up round)
    const supplyPrice = Math.round(grossAmount / 1.1);

    // 10,000 - 9,091 = 909 won (preserves exact principal total)
    const vatAmount = grossAmount - supplyPrice;

    return {
        grossAmount: grossAmount,
        supplyPrice: supplyPrice,
        vatAmount: vatAmount
    };
}
