Life Tile: The On-Device iPhone App That Turns Your Day Into a Mondrian Grid

Why drawing time as colored tiles works, what "on-device" actually protects, and where this app sits in the lifelogging lineage (as of July 2026)

Jul 23, 2026 Life & Practical

Intro: When a Handful of Rectangles Becomes a Day

Standing in front of a Mondrian painting has an oddly calming effect. A few thick black lines, a handful of red, blue, and yellow blocks, and the rest left white. It looks simple, but your eye immediately senses that every placement is holding a balance. The first thought I had on seeing the iPhone app Life Tile was: "someone took that visual language and mapped it directly onto a single day." The app itself is pitched, in its own words, as turning your day into a Mondrian-inspired grid.

This piece is not meant to promote Life Tile's feature list. It is an attempt to work through the question the app raises - what does it mean to see time as a grid of colors, and why should that record live only on your device, with no server at all - against the backdrop of lifelogging and time-management tools that came before it. Everything presented as fact here is what could be verified as of July 2026; everything else is clearly my own reading.

1. What Life Tile Is - The Verified Facts

1.1 What it logs and how it displays it

Life Tile is an iPhone-only app listed under Productivity. Its App Store subtitle is "Track Your Time, See Your Day," and it detects location changes in the background to automatically render the day as a color-filled grid. Beyond the main tile view, it also offers a Tree Map that shows activity share by area, a map view, and a list view, and it integrates sleep and workout data alongside the grid. Widgets and Shortcuts support let you check today's grid straight from the home screen, and there's a CSV import option for past records.

1.2 On-device design and pricing

The core of the maker's published privacy stance is unambiguous: it runs no servers of its own and does not transmit personal data to third parties. All data stays in local on-device storage (SwiftData), with iCloud sync offered as an optional layer. The app itself is a free download, while unlocking full functionality requires a one-time $9.99 in-app purchase, with a free trial period offered up front. The listed developer appears to be a small, independent project, and the app requires iOS 26 or later, which tells you it is aimed squarely at users on current devices and the latest OS.

2. The Fun of the Idea - Seeing Time as a Grid

2.1 Mondrian and De Stijl - a language of grids and primary colors

The Dutch painter Piet Mondrian joined the De Stijl movement in 1917 and pushed painting down to a minimal vocabulary: horizontal and vertical lines, primary colors (red, blue, yellow), and the neutrals black, white, and gray. What he was after was not realistic depiction but what he called "dynamic equilibrium" - the tension and harmony created by differently sized areas and colors. The distinctive trait of that language is that it minimizes information while still letting the overall structure be read at a glance.

What makes Life Tile interesting is that it borrows exactly that language to turn a "day" - normally a continuous, narrative stretch of time - into a discrete bundle of tiles. Where Mondrian's canvas was a composition arranged by the artist's hand, Life Tile's grid is a composition automatically filled in by the time you actually spent. The arrangement is different every day, so a slightly different picture emerges each time - and that, at least as an idea, is the app's most striking appeal to me.

2.2 The moment a day becomes an abstraction

The pleasure of abstraction lies in erasing detail and leaving only structure behind. Replaying a day second by second is just a tedious log, but compressing it into a handful of color blocks turns it into a composition in its own right. Impressions like "the commute block was unusually wide today" or "the focus-work block clustered in the morning" land faster, and more emotionally, than a table of numbers ever could. Treating the whole day like a "piece" is itself the fresh source of fun this framing adds to lifelogging.

3. Life Tile's Place in the Lifelogging and Time-Management Lineage

3.1 The quantified self and the history of time trackers

The urge to record every moment isn't new. In 2001, Microsoft Research's Gordon Bell wore a SenseCam around his neck that took thousands of photos a day, and spent nearly fifteen years digitizing his entire life in the MyLifeBits project. What he was aiming for was "Total Recall" - fully replacing biological memory with digital memory. The so-called Quantified Self movement followed, and wearables tracking sleep, steps, and heart rate, along with time-tracker apps, became its practical descendants.

3.2 Bullet journals and time blocking - time management by hand

There's an entirely different lineage, too. The Bullet Journal system, devised by Ryder Carroll, is an analog method built around short, handwritten "rapid logging" to manage tasks, events, and notes. Time blocking divides the day in advance into hour-sized blocks and assigns tasks to each one, a method many executives and productivity writers have recommended for years. What both share is an emphasis on planning the future.

3.3 Life Tile's difference - a portrait instead of a schedule

Life Tile doesn't fully belong to either lineage. Like the quantified-self movement, it leans on automatic recording, but unlike Bell's project, it doesn't stockpile everything indefinitely - it compresses each day into a single view. Like time blocking, it works in time blocks, but it isn't a planning tool; it's a retrospective one, drawing an already-finished day after the fact. In other words, its clearest point of difference is that it produces a "portrait" of a day gone by, not a table or a schedule. Worth noting: Bell himself later expressed skepticism that his extreme lifelogging experiment had delivered the payoff he expected - a telling example of the fatigue that can come from trying to "record absolutely everything." A lighter, compressed approach like Life Tile's may actually hold up better over the long run.

4. The Weight of Choosing "On-Device"

4.1 What having no server actually means

Life Tile's maker states plainly in its policy that it runs no servers of its own and does not send data to third parties. That's not a marketing line - it's an architectural choice. Having no server means (1) there is no server to be the target of a data breach in the first place, (2) your records already on your device aren't affected even if the company shuts down or changes its policy, and (3) there is no structural incentive at all for ad tracking or third-party data sales.

4.2 Why location and sleep data are especially sensitive

The data Life Tile handles happens to sit among the most sensitive kinds. A location history is information that can tightly reconstruct where you were and when; sleep patterns are a clue that can be used to infer health status. In a cloud-based service, this data passes through a provider's servers and carries the risk that its scope of use could change with a terms-of-service update or an acquisition. On-device design removes the point where that risk could originate in the first place, which is why I think it's a particularly good fit for this category of data.

4.3 The trade-offs of on-device

Nothing is free, though. Without a server, real-time sync across multiple devices has to depend on a separate layer like iCloud, and losing or resetting the device can mean losing the record entirely without a backup. Features that naturally require a server - a web dashboard, sharing with other people - have to be given up from the start. On-device is a trade: you gain privacy but give up some breadth of convenience features, and it's worth choosing with that clearly in mind.

5. Why Visual Records Tend to Stick

5.1 Design that lowers logging friction

A point that comes up often in habit-formation discussions: for a logging tool to survive, "input friction" has to be low. A tool that requires you to type out what you did every single time tends to get abandoned within days. Design that detects automatically in the background, leaving the user only to fix the parts that got caught wrong, cuts that burden dramatically. I suspect that's exactly why Life Tile appears to have chosen "auto-detect plus easy correction" over full automation - even imperfect automation won't hurt consistency if the act of fixing it stays light.

5.2 The urge to collect, and the reward of retrospection

People have an urge to collect and lay out traces of themselves - not so different from collecting stamps or records. A grid that accumulates one tile at a time each day naturally taps into that urge. On top of it comes the pleasure of retrospection. Lay a week's or a month's worth of grids side by side and patterns like "this week had a lot more commuting" or "a certain activity kept repeating during this stretch" register before words do. If a text journal only reveals its meaning once reread, an arrangement of color blocks leaves an impression on a mere glance - which is what makes the barrier to looking back so low.

6. Practical Uses - Time Awareness, Habit Patterns, Digital Wellbeing

6.1 Recovering a sense of time

Anyone who's been glued to a phone knows the feeling of a day slipping away without noticing where it went. The simple act of looking at the grid after the day ends forces an honest look back at "what did I actually spend time on today." The point isn't to induce self-criticism or guilt, but to visually confirm the gap between how the day felt and how it actually went.

6.2 Spotting habit patterns by eye

As daily grids pile up, recurring patterns surface. Regularities like unusually frequent travel on a certain day of the week, or activity clustering at a particular time of day, are hard to see from any single record, but stand out the moment several are placed side by side. That visually fast-tracks the first step of improving a habit - recognizing that there's a pattern at all.

6.3 As part of a digital wellbeing routine

Built-in features like Apple's Screen Time or Google's Digital Wellbeing focus on telling you how much time you spent in each app. Life Tile shows a different axis of life outside the screen - physical movement, activity, and sleep - rather than app usage, which means it can complement Screen Time rather than compete with it. Looking at "how much I used inside the screen" alongside "what I did outside the screen" gives a fuller, more three-dimensional read on the balance of a day.

7. How It Compares to Screen Time and General Time Trackers

Its position becomes clearer next to existing time-management tools. Apple's Screen Time and Google's Digital Wellbeing specialize in per-app usage statistics, and general-purpose time trackers are built around manually starting and stopping a timer per project. Both groups are strong at presenting results as "numbers and tables," but neither offers the aesthetic experience of compressing an entire day into a single picture.

8. Limitations and Caveats

Listing only the upsides wouldn't be honest, so a few limitations deserve to be named directly. First, automatic detection inevitably carries errors, and if the user has to fix those errors by hand, some amount of manual burden remains no matter what. Second, this applies to self-quantification tools in general: having your day's time judged through color and area, day after day, can feel to some people less like insight and more like compulsive self-surveillance. As Bell's MyLifeBits case shows, the idea of "recording absolutely everything" can breed fatigue over the long run. Third, the iOS-only, iOS-26-plus requirement, combined with having no server, likely limits things like real-time multi-device sync or viewing your data on the web. Fourth, because the automatic detection relies on location, it requires always-allow background location permission, which - separate from on-device storage - is a call each person has to weigh for themselves in terms of battery drain and how comfortable they are granting that permission.

Conclusion - Who This App Actually Suits

I'd describe Life Tile less as a productivity-optimization tool and more as a retrospective lifelogging app that turns a day gone by into a single Mondrian-style picture. If you need second-by-second precision, team collaboration, or a cloud dashboard, another tool will serve you better. But it's worth trying if you (1) find motivation in a visual record that builds up a little at a time, (2) don't want sensitive personal data like location or sleep sitting on an outside server, or (3) prefer glancing at a picture to rereading a text journal when you look back. In the end, what this app is genuinely good at isn't time management itself - it's designing an experience that makes you want to look at your time again.
