Overdraft Credit Line Complete Guide

Structure, interest calculation, and a comparison with personal loans - plus smart ways to use and repay it

Introduction: A Convenient Product You Need to Understand Precisely

An overdraft credit line (sometimes called a line of credit attached to a checking account, or in Korea a "negative account" - 마이너스통장) sets a preapproved limit on an ordinary checking or transaction account and lets the balance dip below zero, up to that limit, with the shortfall automatically funded as a loan. Because there is no separate loan application each time - you simply draw on the account as if withdrawing your own money - it is often set up alongside a salary or primary transaction account for convenience.

The trouble is that this convenience cuts both ways. Used well, it is an efficient way to keep liquidity on standby for emergencies. Used without understanding the structure, it can quietly rack up higher interest than a personal loan through habitual draws, and it can create credit-score consequences you did not anticipate. This article is general educational information on what an overdraft credit line is, how its interest is calculated, how it differs from a personal loan and a credit card cash advance, and how to use it wisely while avoiding its traps. Specific rates and limits vary enormously by country, financial institution, and point in time, so treat the figures here as a rough picture as of July 2026, and always confirm official terms with the financial institution before choosing a product.

1. What Is an Overdraft Credit Line?

1.1 Definition and How It Works

An overdraft credit line is a facility in which a bank pre-assigns a limit to a checking account - based on creditworthiness, income, and similar factors - and permits the balance to go negative within that limit. If a card payment or automatic transfer would otherwise leave the account short, the shortfall is funded automatically, with no separate process, up to the approved limit; any later deposit is applied automatically to reduce the outstanding balance. In other words, this is not a separate loan account - the checking account itself doubles as a lending facility.

1.2 A Concept That Goes by Different Names in Different Countries

This kind of credit facility is not unique to any one country. In the US and UK, a personal line of credit based on individual creditworthiness is widely used; the US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) describes it as revolving credit you can draw down in small amounts as needed and borrow again as you repay. As the country comparison later in this article shows, however, products with a similar name can differ enormously in whether they are secured and how the rate is structured, so it is a mistake to assume a familiar name guarantees familiar terms elsewhere.

2. Overdraft Credit Line vs. Personal Loan - The Fundamental Difference

2.1 How You Receive the Money

A personal loan is reviewed and, once approved, disbursed as a lump sum into your account, which you then repay in installments of principal and interest over a fixed term. An overdraft credit line works differently: no lump sum arrives up front. Only a limit is set, and you draw down whatever amount you need, whenever you need it, which means cost accrues only on the amount actually used and only for the time it is outstanding.

2.2 How the Limit Is Set

Personal loans tend to weigh income, length of employment, credit score, and existing debt comprehensively and often extend a relatively larger limit as a result. Overdraft credit lines weigh similar factors, but the resulting limit is typically smaller than a comparable personal loan, and your broader relationship with the bank - direct deposit of salary, transaction history, and so on - commonly influences both the limit and the rate you are offered.

2.3 Why the Interest Rate Differs

Pulling together various market sources, an overdraft credit line's rate tends to run roughly 0.3 to 0.5 percentage points higher than a personal loan for a comparable borrower at the same bank. From the bank's point of view, an overdraft credit line is standby money whose draw timing and amount are hard to predict, unlike a personal loan with a fixed amount and a set repayment schedule. The rate premium reflects that added uncertainty in funding management.

3. How Interest Is Calculated - The Logic of Daily Accrual

3.1 The Formula and a Worked Example

The core of overdraft credit line interest is daily accrual. Interest is not charged on the full approved limit, but only on the amount actually drawn negative, and only for the number of days it stays that way. The formula is roughly:

Interest = Amount used x Annual rate x Days used / 365

For example, drawing down about USD 8,000 (or the local-currency equivalent of KRW 10 million) at a 5% annual rate for 30 days works out to roughly 33 units of currency in interest under this formula. Repay even one day earlier and the interest drops accordingly - early repayment translates directly into savings, which is a real practical advantage over a personal loan, where the interest schedule is largely fixed from disbursement.

3.2 Calculated on the End-of-Day Balance

Interest is typically calculated on the balance at the close of business, not the largest amount drawn at any point during the day. For example, if you withdraw a large amount in the morning and redeposit part of it in the afternoon, that day's interest is calculated on the net end-of-day balance. Understanding this end-of-day convention explains why depositing spare cash even a little earlier - on payday or whenever funds become available - genuinely reduces the interest you pay.

3.3 The Billing Date and the Risk of Compounding

Interest is usually debited automatically from the account on a set date each month. Here is a trap worth flagging clearly: if the account balance is insufficient on that billing date, the unpaid interest can be added to the principal, meaning interest starts accruing on that unpaid interest the following month - a compounding effect. This mechanism is one reason an overdraft credit line balance can feel like it "never shrinks" quite the way you expect.

4. What Determines Your Limit and Rate

The limit and rate on an overdraft credit line are set by a combination of factors: income level and stability (length of employment, employment type), credit score, existing loan and card usage history, and your broader relationship with the bank (direct deposit of salary, card spending, savings and time deposits held there). Terms can differ meaningfully across banks, and between digital-only banks and traditional branch banks, for the same applicant, so comparing offers across several institutions rather than settling for the first one is the sensible approach. If your credit improves or your relationship with the bank deepens, a renewal may bring a lower rate or a higher limit; conversely, if your credit situation worsens, a renewal review could shrink the limit or even decline to renew the facility at all - worth keeping in mind.

5. The Advantage: Liquidity and an Emergency-Fund Substitute

The single biggest advantage of an overdraft credit line is liquidity. Because it generally does not accrue interest unless you use it (though you should separately confirm whether an annual fee or similar cost applies), it is well suited to keeping a limit on standby for money you do not need right now but might need unpredictably. That gives you a buffer for an unexpected medical bill or urgent expense without tying up a lump sum in a dedicated emergency-fund account. Because it works like an ordinary account - free to draw down and repay at will - you can also chip away at the balance with small repayments whenever spare cash appears, a flexibility that meaningfully reduces total interest paid.

6. The Traps: Habitual Use and Credit Score Impact

6.1 The Risk of Treating It Like "Money You Already Have"

When an overdraft credit line is attached to your salary account, it is easy to start treating the negative balance as if it were simply your own money. If you opened it for emergencies but gradually start dipping into it whenever your regular budget runs a little short, the balance can creep upward and prove surprisingly hard to bring back to zero. It is worth being explicit with yourself that an overdraft credit line is a standby buffer, not a product designed to routinely top up everyday living expenses.

6.2 The Entire Limit Can Be Recorded as Debt, Even If Unused

In many credit-reporting frameworks, the entire approved limit of an overdraft credit line - not just the amount you have drawn - gets recorded as if it were already a live loan, regardless of actual usage. In practice, that means the moment you open a facility with, say, a USD 40,000 limit, your credit file may show USD 40,000 of debt even if you have not drawn a single unit of it. This can inflate your reported total debt when you later apply for a mortgage or another personal loan, potentially shrinking the amount you qualify for or slowing approval.

6.3 Utilization Ratio and the Compounding Burden

A sustained high ratio of used-to-available limit (utilization ratio) is another thing to watch. If utilization stays above a certain threshold (commonly cited around 30%) for several months running, credit scoring models can interpret that as a sign of financial strain and weigh it negatively. Combine that with the compounding effect described earlier, and it becomes easy to fall into a cycle where the principal barely shrinks while interest quietly generates more interest.

Because cash advances (and card loans generally) tend to carry the highest rate of the three, it is sensible to treat them as a last resort rather than a first stop.

8. Smart Strategies for Using and Repaying It

  • Restrict its use to genuine emergencies. Set a personal rule against letting it become a routine top-up for a shortfall in your regular budget.
  • Keep the utilization ratio low. Draw only the minimum you need, and avoid leaving the limit maxed out for extended periods.
  • Repay the moment you have spare cash. Because interest accrues daily, repaying even one day sooner directly reduces the cost.
  • Always check your balance before the billing date. This avoids unpaid interest being folded into principal and starting to compound.
  • Compare terms periodically. If your relationship with the bank deepens or your credit improves, ask about a preferential rate or a limit adjustment.
  • Manage it alongside your total debt. Remember that the overdraft limit itself can affect how other loan applications are assessed.

9. It Varies Enormously by Country and Institution - What to Always Check

The concept behind an overdraft credit line exists in many countries, but whether it is secured and what rate it carries differ enormously by country and product. A few examples make the point clearly.

9.1 Korea - An Unsecured, Credit-Based Product

In Korea, the overdraft credit line (마이너스통장) is generally unsecured and based on personal creditworthiness. Pulling together market-reported figures, rates for well-qualified customers at traditional banks and digital-only banks commonly fall somewhere in the roughly mid-4% to 6% annual range. This is only a rough tendency that shifts with the point in time and individual creditworthiness - it should not be read as a confirmed rate at any specific bank.

9.2 Japan - An Extreme Gap Between Secured and Unsecured Products

Many Japanese banks automatically offer, with no separate application, an auto-overdraft service on a comprehensive deposit account (総合口座) when the customer holds a time deposit there: the time deposit serves as collateral, and any shortfall in the ordinary deposit balance is automatically covered up to a limit. The rate on this secured overdraft is typically the collateralized time deposit's own rate plus roughly 0.5 percentage points, which in periods of low time-deposit rates can land at around 1% annually or even lower. By contrast, unsecured personal card-loan-type borrowing in Japan is reported to commonly run in the roughly 13-18% annual range. That such an extreme gap can exist within a single country, purely based on whether collateral is involved, illustrates clearly why comparing "overdraft" products across countries or institutions at face value is risky.

9.3 China - A Credit-Card Overdraft Structure With a Grace Period

In China, a credit card's overdraft (透支) structure is more widely known than a checking-account-linked overdraft credit line. For purchase-type overdrafts, no interest applies if the balance is repaid by the due date - a grace period commonly running several dozen days - but once that period is missed, or for cash-advance-type overdrafts, a daily interest rate applies and is typically reported to compound on a monthly basis. In other words, the cost structure differs sharply depending on whether the same overdraft facility is used for a purchase or for a cash withdrawal.

9.4 The Takeaway - A Shared Name Does Not Mean Shared Terms

As these three examples show, products sharing a name like "overdraft" or "line of credit" can differ by multiples in real cost, depending on whether they are secured, how interest is calculated, and whether a grace period exists. Whatever country or institution you are considering, always read the specific product's terms, its rate-setting method, and whether it is secured before signing up, and treat the figures in this article as general reference points rather than confirmed terms for any specific product at any specific time.

10. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. If I open an overdraft credit line but never use it, do I still pay interest?

No. Interest is charged only on the amount you actually draw down, calculated day by day for however long the balance stays negative. Simply opening the line without ever letting the balance go negative generally does not trigger interest, though some banks may charge an annual fee or a limit-maintenance fee, so check the terms.

Q2. Does having an overdraft credit line hurt my chances of getting another loan?

It can. Many credit reporting systems record the entire approved limit as if it were already borrowed, regardless of how much you have actually used. A large limit can inflate your total reported debt when a lender reviews a new loan application, which may reduce the amount you qualify for or complicate approval.

Q3. Which is better, an overdraft credit line or a personal loan?

It depends on the purpose. If you need a lump sum once and have a clear repayment plan, a personal loan usually carries a lower rate and is the better fit. If you cannot predict exactly when or how much money you will need, the flexibility of an overdraft credit line, where you pay interest only on what you use, can be more valuable.

Q4. Why is the overdraft credit line rate usually higher than a personal loan rate?

From a bank's perspective, an overdraft credit line is standby money that could be drawn at any time in any amount, which is harder to plan around than a personal loan with a fixed amount and a set repayment schedule. That added uncertainty is generally reflected in a somewhat higher rate compared with a personal loan.

Q5. Is it fine to max out my overdraft credit line limit?

It is not recommended. A high utilization ratio sustained over a long period can be read by credit scoring models as a sign of financial strain, which can hurt both your credit score and future loan applications. It is better to draw only what you need and repay as soon as you have spare cash.

Conclusion: A Liquidity Tool, Not a Way to Cover Everyday Expenses

An overdraft credit line is a genuinely useful tool for securing liquidity the moment you need it, without borrowing a lump sum in advance. Because you pay interest only on what you use, calculated daily, careful management can actually cost less than a personal loan. But the convenience of having it tied to your salary account can slide into habitual use, the full limit can be recorded as debt regardless of whether you have used it - affecting how other loan applications are assessed - and unpaid interest can compound. These are traps worth remembering.
