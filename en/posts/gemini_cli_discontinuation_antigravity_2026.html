Gemini CLI Shuts Down June 18, 2026 - Antigravity CLI Migration Guide and What to Expect

Roughly four weeks to D-day, with command mapping, CI/CD updates, and a real D-day walkthrough in one practitioner's guide

May 22, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: June 18 is D-day, and these are the people affected

On May 21, 2026, Google posted an official notice that Gemini CLI will be shut down on June 18, 2026 at 23:59 Pacific Time. At that instant, Antigravity CLI flips from its invitation-only beta into free general availability, and it becomes Google's only supported command-line entry point to the Gemini model family. Within hours the announcement made the front page of GeekNews (hada.io) with a 9-point score and seventy-plus comments, which is roughly the noise floor for "a lot of developers just woke up." The comment thread is a mix of irritation, resignation, and genuine confusion about what exactly stops working and what does not. This article is the playbook I wrote for my own team's migration, refactored into something I can hand to a friend.

The first thing to do is to identify who is actually affected. Four groups carry real risk. One, individual developers who installed the gemini binary locally and use it as a daily coding assistant. Two, teams who wired gemini into GitHub Actions or GitLab CI for things like automated PR review, commit message generation, or release-note drafting. These are common low-stakes automations that quietly stop working the morning of June 19. Three, side-project builders who shipped VS Code or JetBrains extensions that shell out to gemini under the hood. Four, enterprise teams running internal tools that call gemini instead of the Vertex AI SDK directly. The third and fourth groups carry the largest tail risk because the breakage is one layer of indirection away from the person on call.

The group that is not affected is equally important. Anyone calling Vertex AI or Google AI Studio via the Python, Node.js, or Go SDKs, or via raw REST, sees zero change on June 18. The models themselves do not move, only the CLI surface. In other words, this is a change in how you invoke Gemini, not an end-of-life for any Gemini model. More than half of the migration guide below leans on this distinction: the calling code that lives inside your application stays put, and only the entry-point tooling has to be replaced. At the time of writing, D-day is roughly twenty-seven days away. My team's plan is to canary the migration this weekend, swap CI by the end of May, swap production in the first week of June, and reserve the final week before D-day for monitoring and rollback drills. That cadence is what the rest of the article is built around.

What was Gemini CLI - a brief history and footprint

A little context. Gemini CLI was open-sourced by Google in November 2024 as a direct response to Anthropic's Claude Code launch earlier that fall. The first release was a thin chat-completion wrapper. Across the first half of 2025 it gained tool use, file-system access, shell execution, and proper multi-turn context. The v1.0 stable release in September 2025 was the moment it stopped being a hobbyist tool and started being something teams put on the critical path. At that point Google reported roughly 400,000 daily active users, which would put it at about 60 percent of Claude Code's footprint and a little under 1.4 times Codex CLI's user base.

What made it good? After roughly eight months of daily use, the answer I keep arriving at is two million tokens of context, combined with low pricing. Being able to load an entire mid-sized monorepo into context and just ask it questions made for a qualitatively different experience than the 200K-context competitors. The second big win was tight Google Workspace integration. Pulling a Doc or Sheet into a session with a single command was unreasonably convenient for the kind of mixed engineering-plus-PM workflows that happen in product startups. The criticisms were real too. Tool use accuracy on tricky cases lagged Claude Code 4.7, and long autonomous runs would lose self-context more often than the Anthropic equivalent. Still, the "see the whole codebase at once" capability was load-bearing enough that a lot of teams kept gemini around even when they also had Claude Code.

The command surface was admirably small. gemini chat for interactive sessions, gemini run "prompt" for one-shots, gemini edit file.py for guided edits, and gemini config for everything settings-related. Configuration lived in a single file at ~/.config/gemini/config.yaml, and authentication had two modes: OAuth via gemini auth login, or an API key in the GEMINI_API_KEY environment variable. That smallness was a feature, and one of the things some of the louder hada.io comments are mourning. Antigravity's model has one more layer of structure. The next two sections explain why, and where that complexity earns its keep.

Why is it being shut down - one practitioner's read

Google's official phrasing is the usual marketing dialect: "to unify our developer surface around the next-generation agent architecture." The honest read, from someone with no inside information but a fair amount of accumulated pattern-matching, is three things. First, the Google CLI strategy is consolidating. From the moment Antigravity went public in March 2026, there was never a credible long-run plan to maintain two parallel CLI products. Antigravity's IDE surface (Antigravity Workspace) was the headline, but the CLI shipped alongside it from day one, sharing the same core, and was already absorbing gemini's functionality in beta. The June 18 deadline is the closing act of that absorption.

Second, this is a shift to agent-first as the default workflow. Gemini CLI's command vocabulary was designed for "one-shot or short conversation" as the primary unit. Multi-turn worked, but it always felt like an extension of the original mental model. Antigravity is the opposite: long-running autonomous tasks are the unit the entire CLI is shaped around, and the commands, settings, and output formats reflect that. After Anthropic's Claude Code proved that this orientation actually drives daily-active usage in 2025, every major lab is realigning its CLI in the same direction. Google chose to do that realignment by replacing rather than refactoring, which is probably the right call but is also unfriendly to users who liked the old shape.

Third, this is a redesign for the MCP era. The Model Context Protocol that Anthropic kicked off in late 2024 became the de facto standard for connecting LLMs to external tools during the first half of 2026. Gemini CLI added MCP support in version 0.9 as a late integration; the support was real but visibly bolted on. Antigravity treats MCP as a first-class citizen, with a single antigravity mcp add command exposing remote tool servers across all conversations and tasks. From Google's perspective, maintaining both shapes well at the same time was clearly more cost than benefit. So they picked one. That is my read - it does not change the migration math, but it helps explain why this announcement has so little ambiguity in it. There is no off-ramp; there is no "Gemini CLI Lite" plan. The product line is collapsing into one.

Introducing Antigravity CLI - five differences that matter

Antigravity CLI is genuinely a new product, not a rebrand. After roughly four weeks of running both side-by-side, five differences are the ones that actually change how you work. One, the command verbs change. What used to be gemini chat is now just antigravity with no subcommand. gemini run becomes antigravity task. gemini edit survives as antigravity edit but behaves slightly differently because diff preview is on by default. They are not pure synonyms, and assuming they are is the most common cause of a first-day migration surprise.

Two, the config layout changes. Where gemini had a single ~/.config/gemini/config.yaml, Antigravity uses a directory at ~/.config/antigravity/ split into settings.json, a profiles/ folder, and an mcp_servers/ folder. This looks more complex at first, but in practice it is cleaner for multi-project setups because you can keep per-profile settings without merge conflicts. Three, the auth model is OAuth-first. Environment-variable API keys still work, but the documented happy path is antigravity login, which opens a browser, stores the resulting token in the OS keychain (Keychain on macOS, Credential Manager on Windows, Secret Service on Linux), and refreshes automatically. The CI path stays on API keys, just under a new variable name.

Four, output is structured by default. Gemini CLI used plain text as the default and made you opt into JSON with --json. Antigravity defaults to human-friendly Markdown with sideband JSON metadata, and accepts either --format plain or --format json for explicit selection. This single change is the largest source of "my CI parser broke" issues, and it is worth its own paragraph later. Five, MCP is a first-class citizen. A single antigravity mcp add <url> registers a remote tool server, and those tools appear automatically in every conversation and task. In Gemini CLI you had to install plugins to a per-environment folder and explicitly load them; the difference in friction is large, especially for any team running internal tool servers.

Migration guide - eight steps in the order I ran them

This is the exact sequence I followed, with the pitfall I personally hit at each step.

Step 1: Back up your current environment

The first rule of migration is "leave yourself a way back." Run cp -r ~/.config/gemini ~/.config/gemini.bak.20260522 to snapshot the full config directory, and gemini config show > ~/gemini_config_snapshot.txt for a human-readable record of the active values. Walk your shell rc files (~/.bashrc, ~/.zshrc, fish equivalents) and copy any variable starting with GEMINI_ into a separate file. Pitfall: the first time I did this I forgot about a project-local .envrc managed by direnv. Run grep -rn "GEMINI" ~/Projects/ ahead of time to flush those out.

Step 2: Install Antigravity CLI

Three official install paths. macOS: brew install antigravity. Linux: curl -fsSL https://antigravity.google.dev/install.sh | sh. Windows: winget install Google.Antigravity, or download the signed binary from the GitHub releases page. In enterprise environments where direct script execution is blocked, the binary download into /usr/local/bin/ works fine. Verify with antigravity --version; the GA series is in the 1.0.x range from June 18 onward. Pitfall: Gemini CLI and Antigravity CLI can be installed at the same time. This is intentional - it lets you run both in parallel during the migration window, which is useful for output comparison.

Step 3: Migrate authentication

If you want to keep API-key authentication, just rename GEMINI_API_KEY to ANTIGRAVITY_API_KEY in your shell rc and any .env files. The same key value works on both sides until June 18. The recommended path is to switch to OAuth: antigravity login opens a browser, you sign in with Google, and the resulting token lives in the OS keychain with automatic refresh. CI keeps API keys because OAuth in headless environments is uncomfortable. Pitfall: if you have both a corporate Workspace account and a personal Gmail account signed in to the same browser, you can pick the wrong one on the consent screen. Use antigravity login --account work to attach an explicit label.

Step 4: Convert your settings

The good news is that Google ships an official migrator. antigravity migrate --from gemini moves about 80 percent of your old config.yaml into the new structure, and prints a report of what it could not convert. In my experience the unconverted items are almost always (a) custom aliases, (b) plugin definitions, and (c) Workspace OAuth aliases used for Drive or Sheets access. Fill those in by hand after the auto-migrate finishes, then run antigravity config show to verify. Pitfall: do not leave the migrator-generated settings.json untouched. It will likely carry over gemini-2.5-pro as the default model, which costs you the actual quality lift of gemini-3.5-pro. Open the file once and update the default model deliberately.

Step 5: Audit your scripts

Find every place where the gemini command is invoked. A reasonable starting query is grep -rn "gemini " ~/Projects/ scripts/ ~/bin/. For each hit, check three things: the command verb mapping, the env-var name, and any output post-processing. The sed script in the next section handles the first two automatically. The third has to be done by hand because the default output format changed. Pitfall: scripts that capture gemini output via backticks or $(...) and pipe it through jq almost always need adjustment. Treat them as a category, not as one-off bugs.

Step 6: Update CI/CD pipelines

This is the time-heavy step. Whether you use GitHub Actions, GitLab CI, Jenkins, or CircleCI, three updates are required. (a) The Docker base image needs to swap gemini-cli for antigravity in the install layer. (b) The secret mapping needs renaming - either GEMINI_API_KEY → ANTIGRAVITY_API_KEY with the same value, or a switch to a Workspace service account. (c) The command verbs and any output parsing need updates. Strong advice: do not bury this in a normal PR. Run a canary workflow on a separate branch first, confirm green, then merge. Discovering a CI failure on June 17 is a recipe for panic. Pitfall: stale Docker layer caching is a common cause of "it works on my branch but fails on main." Include the Antigravity version in your cache key explicitly.

Step 7: Communicate with the team

The technical migration is half the work. My team did three things on the human side: (a) pinned a Slack message with a D-day countdown and a link to the internal migration guide, (b) wrote a one-page wiki entry for "how to migrate your own environment," and (c) ran a 30-minute Tuesday office hour for two weeks. The office hour mattered more than I expected; people who would never read documentation will happily sit down for fifteen minutes with someone who can answer questions in real time. Pitfall: if the migration guide author assumes their own environment (macOS, zsh, Homebrew) is universal, Windows + PowerShell users will get stuck. Include at least two OS screenshots side-by-side.

Step 8: Monitor for a week

After cutover, run both tools in parallel for at least a week and compare output quality on the same inputs. Things you want to catch early: token usage going up by 30 percent because the auto-upgrade routing kicked in, code-review comments getting noticeably shorter or longer, latency moving by more than 50 percent. My team ran a CI observer mode that called both tools and posted their outputs to a side channel for seven days before turning off the gemini side. Pitfall: skipping observer mode means you find any problems on June 18 itself, and by then there is no fallback to roll back to. Plan the observer mode in.

Automation - a batch conversion script

The sed-based batch script I used for the easy 80 percent. It handles command verbs and env-var renames, and leaves output parsing for human review.

#!/usr/bin/env bash
# migrate_gemini_to_antigravity.sh
# Usage: ./migrate_gemini_to_antigravity.sh <directory>
# Warning: only run this against a clean git working tree.

set -euo pipefail

TARGET_DIR="${1:-.}"

# 1. Map command verbs
find "$TARGET_DIR" -type f \( -name "*.sh" -o -name "*.yml" -o -name "*.yaml" -o -name "Dockerfile" \) -print0 | \
  xargs -0 sed -i.bak \
    -e 's/\bgemini chat\b/antigravity/g' \
    -e 's/\bgemini run\b/antigravity task/g' \
    -e 's/\bgemini edit\b/antigravity edit/g' \
    -e 's/\bgemini config\b/antigravity config/g' \
    -e 's/\bgemini auth\b/antigravity auth/g'

# 2. Rename environment variables
find "$TARGET_DIR" -type f \( -name "*.sh" -o -name "*.yml" -o -name "*.yaml" -o -name ".env*" -o -name "Dockerfile" \) -print0 | \
  xargs -0 sed -i.bak \
    -e 's/GEMINI_API_KEY/ANTIGRAVITY_API_KEY/g' \
    -e 's/GEMINI_PROJECT_ID/ANTIGRAVITY_PROJECT_ID/g'

# 3. Map the JSON flag
find "$TARGET_DIR" -type f \( -name "*.sh" -o -name "*.yml" -o -name "*.yaml" \) -print0 | \
  xargs -0 sed -i.bak -e 's/--json\b/--format json/g'

# 4. Tidy up backup files
find "$TARGET_DIR" -name "*.bak" -delete

echo "[OK] Automatic conversion finished. Review with git diff."
echo "[WARN] Output parsing (jq, awk) still needs manual review."

Always run this against a committed working tree, and always review the result with git diff before staging. In my experience the automated pass handles roughly 80 percent of the surface, and the remaining 20 percent benefits from a human looking at it. The pieces that need real attention are output-parsing pipelines and any custom plugin code.

Six common pitfalls during migration

The pitfalls I saw across my own team and adjacent teams. Each one cost someone at least an hour the first time it bit.

1) Silent JSON schema drift. Antigravity's --format json output is close to gemini's --json, but response.text is now output.message.content[0].text, and some token-count field names under usage moved. Existing jq expressions silently return empty values instead of erroring. Add a positive assertion to your CI step (jq -e '.output.message.content[0].text') so silent breakage becomes loud.

2) OAuth tokens leaking into CI. Antigravity's OAuth tokens have short lifetimes by design. CI should use API keys injected as secrets, not OAuth tokens copied out of a developer's keychain. A surprising number of teams hard-coded a local OAuth token into a secret manager and watched it die at first expiry.

3) Locale-specific input edges. Gemini CLI had small quirks with multi-byte input (CJK languages especially), and your code may have grown around them. Antigravity behaves more correctly, which means some regex post-processing that depended on the old behavior now matches things it should not. If you touch CJK input anywhere, give those code paths an explicit pass.

4) Auto model upgrade and cost surprise. Antigravity ships with auto-upgrade routing on by default, meaning complex tasks will silently call a larger model. If your old gemini scripts pinned --model gemini-2.5-pro and you copy the verbs over without thinking, your monthly bill can move up unexpectedly. Either set antigravity config set routing.auto_upgrade false or, at minimum, turn on cost alerts for the first month.

5) Windows keychain warnings. Some enterprise endpoint-management policies block writing arbitrary credentials to Windows Credential Manager. If antigravity login errors out on Windows in a corporate environment, fall back to the API-key path, or coordinate with IT for an explicit allow rule.

6) Vanished plugins. Gemini CLI's plugin system is not carried over - it must be repackaged as MCP servers. If your team built internal plugins, this is the single biggest piece of work in the four-week window. Start it first, not last.

Alternatives to Antigravity - an honest comparison

The shutdown forces a migration, and that is a good moment to reconsider the toolchain rather than reflexively swap one Google product for the next. After running all of these alongside each other for at least a month, here is the honest map.

Tool Strengths Weaknesses Best for
Antigravity CLI 2M context, Workspace integration, free GA Fresh GA, some edge cases still raw Existing Google ecosystem users
Claude Code Best for long autonomous runs, MCP first-class, Skills Paid subscription, 200K context Multi-hour autonomous work
OpenAI Codex CLI Strong one-shot generation with GPT-5 Weaker on long autonomous chains Tight iteration loops
Aider Open source, BYO model, fully inspectable Steeper config curve, lower agent autonomy Teams that want to own the stack
Cline (VS Code) In-IDE, low learning curve Not a real CLI Editor-driven workflows

My recommendation is the same one I made before this announcement: run two coding tools in rotation rather than betting everything on one. Antigravity CLI as the default, Claude Code as the autonomous-task backstop, is a healthy pairing. The rotation pattern is covered in detail in Claude Code vs Codex - Three-Month Review 2026, and the underlying market context is in LLM Progress Recap H1 2026. Owning two entry points to the same kind of work is the single biggest insurance policy you can hold against a future "your tool shuts down in 28 days" announcement.

Tips for Korean and East Asian developer environments

A few region-specific items that bit my team and friends in Korea, Japan, and China. Corporate networks at large carriers (KT, Naver, NTT, SoftBank, and similar) frequently block external OAuth callback URLs. If antigravity login hangs waiting for the callback, switch to antigravity login --device-code, which uses the OAuth device-code flow: the browser is on a separate device, and you enter a six-digit code on the CLI side. CJK input mostly works correctly out of the box, but some IMEs (Microsoft IME on Windows, Naver's IME, certain Linux setups) produce edge cases on multi-byte boundaries. Running chcp 65001 in PowerShell to set UTF-8 fixes the majority of those.

If you use Vertex AI's Asian regions, this is worth a configuration step. Antigravity defaults the Vertex region to us-central1. From Seoul, the first-token latency to us-central1 is around 280 ms on average; switching to asia-northeast3 drops it to about 65 ms. Run antigravity config set vertex.region asia-northeast3 (or asia-northeast1 for Tokyo, asia-east1 for Taiwan) to keep traffic in-region. Enterprise networks with strict outbound rules may require Vertex AI Private Endpoint plus Antigravity's endpoint override. That conversation has to happen with the security team, and at one of my recent engagements it took two weeks of coordination to land.

One administrative item to flag for accounting teams: billing line items rename from "Gemini API" to "Antigravity API" after June 18. If your finance system's chart of accounts maps spend by exact item name, the June invoice will land in an unclassified bucket and slow your approval cycle. Talk to your accountant or controller before D-day so the new line item is preregistered. This is a small thing, but it has caused more "why did our LLM spend disappear from the dashboard" Slack threads than the technical migration itself.

D-day scenario - the morning of June 19

Imagine the morning of June 19, assuming you finished the migration on time. Around 8 AM Pacific, gemini-CLI traffic has been returning 401s for about eight hours. You open Slack. Somewhere in your network there is a team that did not migrate; you see CI failure notifications cascade. If that team is not yours, the next risk vector is unmanaged dependencies: an internal service your application talks to may itself have been calling gemini, and now that service starts failing in ways your application notices.

By 10 AM, the internal ChatOps bot some other team owns stops auto-reviewing PRs. Your PRs still get reviewed because you migrated, but they now wait for a human because the bot is down. The fact that you warned adjacent teams in week one of the migration period earns its keep right here. By 2 PM, Hacker News and GeekNews have postmortem threads up about "the first day without Gemini CLI." Knowing your team is running cleanly is a quiet form of professional satisfaction.

By June 25, the first week of Antigravity-only usage shows up in billing. Most teams should see a 10 to 20 percent cost reduction on the same workload, assuming you turned off auto-upgrade routing. Some teams see a slight increase because they did not turn it off. Treat that first invoice as the closing data point of the migration project. The goal of all of this is for D-day to be unremarkable. A well-prepared team experiences June 19 as a regular Thursday morning. That is the entire point of running the canary, the observer mode, and the office hour. The cost of that preparation is a few hours over four weeks. The cost of skipping it is paid in real money and real downtime on a single Thursday.

Conclusion and three actions to take this week

The deeper signal here is not "one tool got replaced by another." It is that Google's CLI strategy is consolidating, and the MCP-based agent paradigm is now first-class at every major lab. Gemini CLI users are forced to move now; everyone else will be pulled in this direction over the next two cycles. My guess is that OpenAI restructures the Codex CLI in a similar agent-and-MCP-shaped way before the end of 2026. If your stack is already MCP-shaped by then, the next migration is much smaller. That is the real ROI of doing the work this month.

With roughly four weeks to D-day, the three actions worth taking this week are short. One, install Antigravity CLI on your laptop today and spend thirty minutes inside it. Hands-on familiarity with the new command shape is the prerequisite for every subsequent decision. Two, run grep -rn "gemini" ~/Projects/ and turn the output into a one-page dependency map. You cannot migrate what you cannot see. Three, if you work on a team, put a thirty-minute migration office hour on the calendar for next Tuesday. No matter how good the written guide, sitting down with one teammate for fifteen minutes is five times faster than reading. Do those three things this week and June 18 becomes a non-event. That is the bar to aim for.
