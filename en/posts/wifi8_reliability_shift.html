The Generation That Stopped Chasing Speed - What Wi-Fi 8 Chose Instead of Peak Rate

A standard aimed at dropouts, re-associations and the tail of latency rather than the number printed on the box, and how to judge your own setup today

Aug 24, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: the number on the box and six o'clock in the living room

Every new router box carries a bigger number than the last one. And yet, after installing it, the video call still swallows a syllable now and then, the stream still freezes for a couple of seconds while you walk from the sofa to the bedroom, and pages still crawl at the hour when everyone in the house is online. The link between the number on the box and what you actually experience is weaker than the packaging suggests.

Wi-Fi 8, the next wireless LAN generation, is the first one to admit that gap outright. Instead of raising the peak transmission rate, it sets its goals around how much less the connection wobbles under the same signal conditions. This article walks through what that choice means technically and how it should change the decisions you make at home or in a small office. It also lays out exactly how far standardisation has come, because the answer is not finished yet and that matters for any decision you make now. No product recommendations here.

1. What actually changes, and where the standard stands

At the IEEE the work is called 802.11bn, and the subtitle attached to it is Ultra High Reliability, or UHR. The name itself tells you what this generation is selling. It is not the High Throughput of 802.11n, nor the Extremely High Throughput of 802.11be. This is the first time the word throughput has dropped out of the headline.

The project declares three goals, measured against the EHT operation that Wi-Fi 7 defined.

  • Raise throughput by 25 percent at a given signal-to-interference-and-noise ratio (SINR)
  • Cut the 95th percentile of the latency distribution by 25 percent
  • Reduce MAC protocol data unit (MPDU) loss by 25 percent

None of those three sentences mentions peak rate. Even the first one carries the qualifier at a given SINR. It is not a promise to extract more when the signal improves; it is a promise to extract more when the signal is exactly as bad as it is today.

The standardisation stage is published openly on the IEEE 802.11 TGbn progress page. As checked on 24 August 2026, it reads as follows.

  • In July 2025, Draft 1.0 went out for a 45-day working group technical letter ballot
  • At the July 2026 Montreal meeting the group resolved roughly 900 comments across PHY and MAC topics to produce Draft 2.0, and the same meeting approved the start of a 30-day working group letter ballot on D2.0
  • The schedule beyond that is published as a D3.0 letter ballot in January 2027, an initial SA ballot on D4.0 in May 2027, final 802.11 working group approval in March 2028, and RevCom and SASB approval in May 2028

In short, the second draft is currently out for ballot. The feature list has largely settled, but the wording can still move, and there is no finished standard document to point at. The 2028 dates are a target schedule, not fixed facts.

Certification is a separate track. On the same date, the Wi-Fi Alliance certification programme listing does not include a Wi-Fi CERTIFIED 8 entry, and Wi-Fi 7 is presented there as the latest generation. Draft standard, certification programme and shipping product run on three different clocks.

On the physical layer side, the parameters sit on the same line as Wi-Fi 7. The bands remain 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz, the maximum channel width stays at 320 MHz, and the maximum spatial stream count is unchanged. No new band, no wider channel, no extra antennas. Those are not this generation's cards.

2. Why peak rate stopped being a useful metric

The number on the box is not a lie. It is, however, the physical-layer ceiling that a single device can reach under ideal conditions. For that number to hold, all of the following must be true at once.

  • The widest channel is used in its entirety, which at 320 MHz means holding that whole width alone
  • Every spatial stream is in use, which requires a client with that many antennas
  • The signal stays clean enough to sustain the highest modulation order, and no other device nearby is using the same channel
  • Exactly one client is associated

In a home, those conditions are essentially never simultaneously true. On top of that, the figure is a PHY rate, quoted before frame headers, acknowledgements and the time spent waiting and contending for the channel are subtracted. Real file transfer starts below it.

What decides the felt experience is a different set of four.

  • Congestion — wireless is a shared medium. On one channel only one device speaks at a time and the rest wait. Add devices and what grows is not each device's rate but the time spent waiting for a turn
  • Interference — neighbouring access points, microwave ovens and Bluetooth gear share the band. As interference rises the modulation order drops, failed frames are retransmitted, and those retransmissions occupy the channel all over again
  • Movement — as you walk, signal strength and reflection paths keep changing. The rate adapts each time, and the adaptation itself costs latency
  • Re-association — the gap when a client switches access points. It is the interval people notice most reliably, and the one that catalogue speed never reflects

And people perceive the tail, not the average. If 99 packets out of 100 arrive on time in a video call and one arrives very late, the one you remember is the late one. That 802.11bn names the 95th percentile rather than mean latency as a goal is a direct move on that point.

By analogy, this generation chose to fix the signalling and the merge lanes rather than raise the posted speed limit. The number on the sign stays the same while the commute changes.

3. What raising reliability means technically

Reliability is a word that works too well in marketing copy, which makes it suspect. It is better to take the mechanisms in the draft one at a time and explain the principle behind each.

The tail of latency: priority queues do not remove the tail

The existing scheme sorts traffic into four access categories, and higher-priority categories wait a shorter interval before trying to seize the channel. The catch is that this is still probabilistic contention. Voice frames collide with other voice frames, and once enough such flows are present, collisions and retries pile up and the far end of the latency distribution stretches out. That is why calls are fine most of the time and only break up when the place fills with people.

The approach that entered the 802.11bn discussion is not another priority tier but a path that lets a frame which has hit its retry threshold temporarily skip contention. It is a device for cutting the tail, not for lowering the mean.

Interference avoidance: a busy primary channel does not mean everything is busy

A wide channel is assembled from several narrow ones. Under the existing rules, if the primary channel of that bundle is occupied by a neighbour, transmission cannot start even when every secondary channel is completely idle. Where access points are packed close together, that constraint turns straight into waiting time.

Non-primary channel access (NPCA) loosens the rule. When the access point announces an alternative primary channel position and switching rules in advance, a client can move to the idle side and transmit while the primary is blocked. It does not obtain more spectrum; it stops leaving the spectrum it already has idle.

Uplink power: raising output while respecting a regulatory ceiling

Low-power indoor operation in 6 GHz caps power spectral density rather than total power, meaning a limit on how much may be radiated per unit of bandwidth. Pack subcarriers into a narrow slice and total transmit power is cut hard, which weakens the direction from client up to access point in particular. That is the same shape as downloads being fine while uploads stay shaky.

The distributed resource unit (DRU) spreads subcarriers across a wide band. Density per unit of bandwidth stays under the regulatory cap while the aggregate power goes up. It does not break the rule; it uses the way the rule is calculated.

Roaming: break then make, or make then break

Roaming has traditionally meant disconnecting and then connecting, and that ordering creates a gap by construction. 802.11k hands the client a neighbour list in advance, 802.11v steers it toward a better target, and 802.11r trims the re-authentication procedure to shorten the transition. All three make the gap shorter; none of them removes it.

The direction under discussion in 802.11bn is to reverse the order. Access points bound into the same mobility domain receive the client's operating context ahead of time, the client attaches to the new one, and only then is the old association torn down. The old access point keeps draining buffered downlink data in the meantime. With re-authentication gone and a make-before-break shape in place, those few seconds while you walk disappear.

Multi-link: the floor Wi-Fi 7 laid

Multi-link operation, holding several bands at once, arrived with Wi-Fi 7, and the Wi-Fi Alliance describes it as supporting more efficient load balancing of traffic among links. Wi-Fi 8 stacks roaming and inter-access-point coordination on top of it. This is not a sudden change of direction but the next storey on a floor already laid.

4. What it means that access points start talking to each other

The last row of that table is the strangest item in this generation. Until now a neighbouring access point has been noise to be avoided. You picked non-overlapping channels, lowered output, and waited when you sensed each other. Multi-AP coordination (MAPC) inverts the premise and proposes sharing resources with the neighbour instead.

  • Coordinated spatial reuse — two access points agree on their transmit powers and send at the same time. Because the method simply caps power so that interference at the other side's clients stays tolerable, it works from received signal strength measurements without needing channel state information. It is the simplest of the set and therefore the likeliest to land first
  • Coordinated beamforming — each access point forms a main lobe toward its own client and a null toward the other's. The gain is large, but it requires a joint sounding procedure, and the form under discussion operates with two access points and a limited stream count
  • Coordinated TDMA — the access point that won the channel hands part of its transmission opportunity to a neighbour and takes back whatever goes unused. Order is set by agreement rather than contention
  • Coordinated restricted target wake time — a window reserved for latency-sensitive traffic is announced to neighbouring access points so they stand aside during it. Unlike the previous item, protection is scheduled rather than granted per transmission opportunity
  • Coordinated channel recommendation — access points agree in advance on a channel list for direct device-to-device links and advertise it

Two practical obstacles follow. First, all of this only means anything when there is more than one access point. In a studio flat with a single unit there is nothing to trigger. Second, equipment from different vendors has to synchronise time and agree on power levels. Being written into a standard does not make cross-vendor operation happen, so it is reasonable to expect early implementations inside single-vendor sets first.

Joint transmission, where several access points send the same data simultaneously to maximise reliability, was discussed but is recorded as falling outside the scope of this amendment. The wish list and the amendment are not the same document.

5. What earlier generations actually raised

The end of the speed race did not arrive suddenly with Wi-Fi 8. The axis moved earlier than that.

The Wi-Fi Alliance's own wording makes the trend clearer. Wi-Fi 6's channel-splitting transmission is introduced as effectively sharing channels to increase network efficiency and lower latency, and scheduled wake times as a feature that significantly improves network efficiency and device battery life. Even in that generation the centre of the marketing copy was not speed.

On the Wi-Fi 7 page there are two speed sentences. The 320 MHz channels available in the 6 GHz band provide twice the throughput of Wi-Fi 6, and the higher-order modulation mode achieves 20 percent higher transmission rates. The first holds only where 320 MHz is available in 6 GHz, and the second only where the signal is clean enough to sustain that modulation order. Once the conditions break, both numbers vanish and what remains are features like multi-link operation that work precisely when conditions are bad. Wi-Fi 8 is best read as the generation that kept only the second kind.

6. The order of reasons things are slow at home

It is easy to forget while discussing a new standard, but the wireless segment is the culprit far less often than people assume. Narrowing in order is faster.

OrderWhere to lookHow it presentsHow to separate it
1Subscribed line and planEvery device measures similarly lowMeasure over a direct cable connection
2Remote server or serviceOnly certain sites or certain hours are slowCompare against other services
3Wired segmentA gigabit line stalls around 100 MbpsCheck cable category and port speed
4Wireless segmentFast next to the router, slow in the bedroomMeasure the same device in both places
5The client deviceResults differ by device in the same spotMeasure side by side with another device

One habit covers most of this. Compare a measurement taken right beside the router with one taken from where you normally sit and the wireless loss separates itself out. If the two are nearly identical and things are still slow, wireless is innocent and a router built to a new standard will not change anything. How to make the measurement itself trustworthy is covered separately in testing internet speed and isolating the bottleneck.

Even when wireless genuinely is the bottleneck, the cause is usually placement rather than standard generation. A single wall counts for more than several metres of distance. Adding access points to shorten distance often beats raising the generation, and the trade-offs of that layout are covered in how mesh Wi-Fi works and how to choose one.

7. Should you upgrade? Both ends have to support it

Wireless only switches on features that the access point and the client both know. Replace one side and the link falls back to the older method both ends understand. This generation's headline features are especially exposed to that constraint.

  • Multi-link operation needs the client to be able to hold several links at once
  • Coordination needs two or more access points that can actually coordinate, and seamless roaming needs every access point in the mobility domain to support it
  • The uplink power improvement only matters if the client can use that transmission format

So the conditions under which an upgrade genuinely helps are narrow.

  • A household with many devices whose active hours overlap. If congestion is the dominant cause, a generation change does show up
  • A space where people move around and more than one access point is in use. Roaming improvements only apply here
  • Environments where access points overlap densely. That is exactly what interference avoidance and coordination target
  • Uses where a dropout is the quality problem, such as video calls, cloud gaming and wireless audio

The cases where it will not help are equally clear.

  • The line itself is the bottleneck. However far wireless is raised, the ceiling stays at the line
  • The client is an older generation. Replacing only the router leaves that client on the old method
  • A small space where one access point suffices. Neither coordination nor roaming ever triggers
  • Physical conditions such as walls or distance. A standard generation does not drill through concrete
How to treat equipment sold before a standard is finalised is a question that has recurred in every generation. Draft-stage silicon can in principle diverge from the final text, and before a certification programme opens there is no common basis for verifying interoperability. This article says nothing about when, what or at what price products will appear. Only the standardisation schedule is confirmed.

8. The Korean context: density and 6 GHz

The problem this generation targets maps unusually well onto Korean housing. Statistics Korea's 2024 Population and Housing Census (as of 1 November 2024) reports that of 22.29 million ordinary households, 53.9 percent live in apartments, and apartments account for 65.3 percent of the total housing stock.

Translated into wireless terms, that means the following.

  • Several neighbouring access points sit one wall away. Every one of them becomes a variable when your radio decides whether the channel is free
  • 2.4 GHz effectively offers three non-overlapping channels, so in a dense block any choice overlaps and the primary channel is frequently occupied. Non-primary channel access aims squarely at this situation
  • Concrete between floors and units attenuates heavily, which paradoxically also works to reduce interference. Density does not automatically mean the worst case

The spectrum side is already settled. On 15 October 2020 the Ministry of Science and ICT announced the supply of 1.2 GHz across 5925 to 7125 MHz as next-generation unlicensed spectrum, the second country to do so after the United States. Indoors the whole band may be used under a 250 mW limit, while device-to-device links such as tethering are confined to the lower 520 MHz, from 5925 to 6445 MHz, indoors or out.

In other words, the regulatory floor for using 6 GHz in Korea was laid six years ago. What remains is client adoption. A router that supports 6 GHz sits on an empty band if phones and laptops do not. Turned around, simply having more devices able to leave the crowded 2.4 GHz band improves conditions for everything that stays behind. That is an improvement available without waiting for a new standard.

Widespread gigabit lines belong in the same picture. The faster the line, the further the bottleneck shifts toward the wireless segment and the client, and if that bottleneck is congestion and interference rather than peak rate, a bigger catalogue number does not resolve it.

9. What to watch next

Separating what is settled from what is not makes future news easier to read.

  • Settled — the three project goals and the draft schedule IEEE has published. Both are written down in official material
  • In flux — the outcome of the ballot on the second draft. The earlier ballot drew comments in the thousands and resolution is still ongoing, so the details of individual features can change
  • Separate track — certification programmes and product launches, which do not run on the same clock as standard approval

The metrics used to judge equipment have to change alongside it. Comparing peak rates across a generation that did not move peak rate is pointless. What to look at instead is this.

  • 95th percentile latency under congestion, the end of the distribution rather than the average
  • Transition time when moving between access points, and packet loss during it
  • How aggregate throughput behaves as client count rises, and whether the measurement conditions are disclosed at all

These are hard to print on a box. They cannot be reduced to one line, and stating the conditions makes the number look smaller. So a big number will keep occupying the packaging for a while, and only people who know what it means will ask a different question.

What could not be confirmed

Collected in one place, here is what this article does not answer.

  • When Wi-Fi CERTIFIED 8 certification opens. As checked on 24 August 2026, the Wi-Fi Alliance certification programme listing carries no such entry and shows Wi-Fi 7 as the latest generation. Trade press names specific dates, but that could not be confirmed in the alliance's own material
  • Product timing, pricing and models. There was no basis to confirm any of it, so none is discussed
  • The text of Draft 2.0, and measured performance. IEEE drafts are not open for public reading, so the mechanism descriptions here rely on published progress reports and open literature, and the three 25 percent figures are project targets rather than measured results
  • The final specified peak rate for Wi-Fi 8. It is confirmed that the physical-layer parameters sit on the same line as Wi-Fi 7, but with no ratified document, no specific figure is quoted
  • The current status of the 320 MHz channel width rule in Korea. Reporting that the technical standard would be amended during the first half of 2024 was confirmed, but the present text of the notification was not

Conclusion: a generation that does not raise the number is the more honest one

Measured by the habit of raising a big number every generation, Wi-Fi 8 is a dull upgrade. But the number on the box stopped producing felt improvement a long time ago. Peak rate is what one device reaches under ideal conditions, and homes and offices do not have those conditions. They are congested, they have interference, people walk around, clients switch access points. Declaring throughput at a given signal condition and the 95th percentile of the latency distribution as goals is an admission that this is the reality worth measuring. Whether the goals will be met is unknown. The second draft is out for ballot, and 2028 is a schedule rather than a fact.

What you can do now is independent of all that. Work out whether the slowness is even wireless; if it is, fix placement and band before raising the generation, and look at hardware after that. The numbers to look at then are not on the front of the box but in latency under congestion and transition time while moving. That the next generation has chosen to sell those values is, at minimum, a sign that the question has moved to the right place.
