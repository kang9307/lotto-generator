Why MVNOs Cost Less, and What Actually Changes When You Switch

The network is borrowed, so it stays the same. What splits the bill is service, and the items that flip the maths sit outside the price table

Aug 25, 2026 Life & Practical

This is general information about how mobile contracts are structured, not advice about any particular provider or plan. Prices, discounts and add-on terms differ by company and change over time, so confirm them in the provider's own terms before signing. The regulatory points below describe Korean rules, cited from public official sources, and are not legal advice on any individual contract.

Put two price sheets side by side and the MVNO column is visibly cheaper. Then two objections show up. One is that calls drop more often. The other is that support is harder to reach. One of those is structurally wrong and the other is structurally right, and knowing which is which makes every remaining decision faster.

The short answer: the radio signal comes from the same network. An MVNO does not build towers. It leases access to a network someone else operates, which means coverage and call quality rest on whatever network it leased. What does change is everything on the other side of the ledger — support channels, add-on services, bundle discounts, loyalty tiers and roaming all follow the company you sign with. That split is the spine of this article. Sort every feature into network-bound or seller-bound and the arithmetic the price sheet hides becomes visible.

Trimming a line you intend to keep — auditing usage, tightening data settings — belongs to a different problem, and is covered in the Complete Phone Plan Optimization Guide. This piece takes the next step: what changes when the line itself moves, and where the savings quietly reverse. Korea is used as the worked example throughout, because its rules are written down and citable, but the structure applies wherever wholesale network access exists.

1. An MVNO is a company that leases someone else's network

A mobile market contains two kinds of company. One holds spectrum, builds base stations and runs the network. The other pays for access to that network and sells service to subscribers under its own name. The second is the MVNO — a Mobile Virtual Network Operator. The word virtual means it does not own the infrastructure, not that the service is somehow simulated.

Regulators call the leasing transaction wholesale access. The facility operator supplies network use, the MVNO pays a wholesale fee, and that fee is the single largest input in an MVNO's cost base. In Korea the government sets the framework for how that fee is calculated and on what conditions access must be offered. The Ministry of Science and ICT has also signalled a policy direction toward MVNOs that hold their own switching equipment and can design tariffs independently, rather than simply reselling a wholesale package.

  • The facility operator — holds spectrum and runs base stations and transport. This is where coverage and quality physically live
  • The MVNO — leases access, designs its own plans, and handles sign-up, billing and support under its own brand
  • Wholesale access — the leasing deal itself. Its price and conditions are regulated, and that price forms the floor under every MVNO tariff
  • The equipment-holding variant — an MVNO with its own switching capability and wider tariff-design latitude. Policy in Korea is pointed at growing this type

The point that matters most is that an MVNO is not one company. Dozens of them lease different networks and sell entirely different plans. So sentences beginning with the words this or that MVNO category rarely hold. The sentence that does hold is: which network does this specific plan lease, and what has this specific seller attached to it?

2. What stays the same and what changes

The quickest way to decide is to sort features into two columns. Network-bound items are identical for anyone riding that network. Seller-bound items change wholesale the moment the name on the contract changes.

Coverage and call qualityNetworkIdentical on the same network. Move to a different one and the dead spots in your routine shift

Compressed into one line: the engineering follows the network, the convenience follows the seller. Which means the decision is not settled by worrying about call quality. It is settled by asking which convenience items you were actually using.

  • If you called support a handful of times in the past year, the support gap is small once converted into money
  • If you used a loyalty discount every month, work out that monthly figure — it is what the price gap has to beat
  • If you travel abroad often, how roaming is provided moves to the front of the decision, ahead of the headline price

3. Why it costs less — where the cost base thins out

The discount is not magic. Specific cost lines are genuinely absent, and once you see which ones, you also see what you are giving up in exchange.

No capital spending on the network

Building towers, acquiring spectrum and maintaining transport is the heaviest fixed cost in telecoms. An MVNO carries none of it and pays a wholesale fee instead. The corollary is that it has no lever to improve the network. If quality is poor in one neighbourhood, asking the MVNO to fix it asks for something outside its control.

A thin retail and marketing layer

A national store estate and heavy advertising are themselves a cost. Selling mostly online and advertising less leaves room to price lower. In exchange, the number of places you can walk into shrinks. For someone comfortable resolving things online that is a non-issue; for someone who is not, it is the whole issue.

Fewer things bolted on

Loyalty programmes, partner discounts, bundled content subscriptions and multi-product packages are not free. Their cost is inside the tariff. Strip them out and the tariff falls. Which leaves one question, always the same one: were you using the thing that got stripped out?

  • Network capex — absent, so cheaper, and you lose any say in network quality
  • Retail and advertising — absent, so cheaper, and walk-in help thins out
  • Add-ons and bundles — absent, so cheaper, and perks you relied on disappear
  • Support capacity — leaner, so cheaper, and time-to-resolution when something breaks may change

MVNO pricing is therefore a difference in composition, not a discount. The real size of the saving depends on whether you were consuming the parts that were removed.

4. What catches you when switching, part 1: contracts and device balances

Here is where the price sheet stops being useful. Most of the reversals — decisions made on a headline gap that turn out to be losses — live in this section and the next.

  1. Remaining commitment term — if a discount was granted in exchange for staying a set number of months, leaving early normally triggers a settlement that claws back some of what you received. The longer the remaining term, the larger it gets
  2. Device instalment balance — moving the line does not clear the purchase price of the handset. You either keep paying the original seller or settle it at once, and any instalment interest or associated charges come with it
  3. Conditions attached to a device subsidy — if the support you received when buying the handset carried a minimum usage period, the contract will say what happens if you fall short. The clause names differ by company, so look for the clause rather than a number
  4. Device insurance and loss cover — if it was an add-on attached to the line, it ends when the line moves. The new seller may not offer an equivalent

One regulatory point is worth pinning down. Korea's Mobile Device Distribution Improvement Act — the statute that governed handset subsidies — was repealed on 22 July 2025. According to the government's own briefing, the repeal removed the obligation to publish subsidy amounts and the cap on additional retailer subsidies, which had been limited to 15 per cent of the published subsidy, and it also became possible to take a retailer's additional subsidy while receiving a tariff discount. Subsidy discrimination based on region of residence, age or physical condition remains reportable after the repeal.

What that means in practice is that purchase terms for the same handset can now vary far more between outlets. Where a single published number once anchored comparison, total outlay now depends on where and on what terms you bought. So when and how you bought the handset becomes an input to the switching calculation, not a footnote.

5. What catches you when switching, part 2: bundles and loyalty tiers

Bundle discounts — the bill that rises belongs to the lines that stay

This is the item that reverses the cheaper verdict most often. If broadband, TV or family lines were bundled for a discount, that discount usually conditions on how many lines are in the bundle, or which combination. Pull one line out and the condition breaks, so the discounts on the lines that remain shrink or vanish. Your own bill went down while the household's total went up.

  • Write out every line and product in the bundle before starting. A single invoice will not show them all
  • Ask the current provider what happens to each remaining line's discount once yours is removed
  • Check whether the bundle itself carries a commitment term that removing a line would breach
  • Run the maths on the household's total bill, not your own line's difference

Loyalty tiers and add-ons do not transfer

Loyalty tiers run independently at each company. A tier built up over years disappears the moment the line moves, and there is no guarantee the new seller operates a comparable scheme. Partner discounts and points behave the same way.

  • Check what the loyalty programme actually saved you over the last few months. The measure is money used, not tier name
  • If content subscriptions or add-ons were attached to the line, check whether you will have to re-subscribe and pay for them separately
  • Data gifting between family members and cross-line sharing work only inside one company. They end at the switch

6. The skeleton of the number-porting process

The procedure itself is codified. In Korea the Korea Telecommunications Operators Association runs mobile number portability as a neutral body, on the basis of article 58 of the Telecommunications Business Act and a Ministry of Science and ICT notice. The detailed handling differs by provider, so what follows is only the skeleton — enough to know where things get stuck.

  1. Check the restriction window — according to that neutral body, portability is restricted for three months from a previous port, a new subscription or a change of account holder. If any of those happened recently, count from that date first
  2. Identity verification — the account holder is verified. Lines where the holder and the actual user differ, lines held by minors and corporate lines follow different procedures, so check in advance
  3. Choose the activation method — a physical SIM you insert, or an eSIM profile you download. Per the Ministry of Science and ICT, smartphone eSIM sign-up became available in Korea on 1 September 2022, through both facility operators and MVNOs
  4. Confirm handset compatibility — if you plan to keep your current device, confirm it supports the network you are moving to and the activation method you chose. eSIM only works if the handset supports it
  5. Cut over and clean up — once the new line is live the old one is terminated. Final charges, any instalment balance, direct debits and identity-verification registrations then need tidying

In practice the first two steps are where people stall. The three-month window catches people who did not realise it applied, and account-holder problems surface only at the counter. How long a cutover takes depends on the activation method and the provider's process, so plan on the assumption that the number may be unreachable for a while. Avoid days heavy with work calls or financial errands that need SMS verification.

7. Unlocked handsets — buying the device and buying service become separate decisions

Unlocked handsets come up in every MVNO conversation for one reason: both move in the same direction, unbundling things that used to be sold as one.

Buy a handset through a carrier and the purchase and the service contract fuse. You take support in exchange for conditions on the tariff or the usage period. Buy the device by itself from a manufacturer or retail channel and it is yours from the start, with the question of which line goes into it left entirely open.

  • The decision splits in two — which device, and which plan, are decided separately. Disliking one no longer drags the other along
  • Changing plans gets lighter — with no instalment tangled into a commitment term, a device balance stops interfering with a tariff decision
  • The upfront cost is higher — you fund the device yourself, so cash goes out first. In a tight month that is a real argument against
  • Compare on total outlay — device price plus the full commitment term's tariff, against the same figure on the other side. Look at one half only and either option can be made to win

If you are switching anyway and buying a new device, secure the device first and pick the line second — that ordering keeps the options open. For the device decision itself, the 2026 iPhone vs Galaxy Complete Comparison covers that ground.

9. Who comes out ahead, and who does not

The same price gap is worth different amounts to different people. Below are the conditions that create the difference; if several rows describe you, their direction is the answer.

Your situationWhich way it leansWhy
One line, nothing bundledStraightforwardly toward the MVNOThere are no bundle benefits to lose, so the price gap is the whole gain
Broadband, TV or family lines bundledCalculate before decidingThe real gap appears only after subtracting the rise on the lines that stay
A long commitment term still runningWaiting it out is a live optionAn early settlement can eat several months of the price gap

Frequent business travel or trips abroadRoaming becomes the leading criterionRoaming is handled differently by every seller, and some offer none

Two axes run through the table. How much is currently tied together, and whether the convenience items are actually being used. With nothing tied and nothing used, the calculation leans one way. With a lot tied, a number larger than the price gap is sitting on the other side.

10. Where to go when something goes wrong

Botched activations and charges that do not match what you were told happen at every kind of provider. Knowing the order of escalation makes the response faster.

  • The provider's own support — the first stop. Record the date, time and what you were told; it becomes evidence at later stages
  • Telecom dispute mediation — in Korea, articles 45-2 through 45-9 of the Telecommunications Business Act establish a Telecommunications Dispute Mediation Committee. Its scope covers disputes over the conclusion, use or termination of a contract, service delivered differently from the published terms, service quality, and failure to disclose or misstatement of material items such as fees, contract conditions and discounts
  • Timeline — according to the committee, a case is handled within 60 days of filing, extendable once by up to 30 days where unavoidable
  • Consumer counselling — for general contract questions, the Korea Consumer Agency also operates a counselling channel

How a mediation proceeds and what it concludes varies case by case. What can be stated here is that the channel exists as a matter of statute, and which provisions establish it. Filing requirements and forms are best read from the committee's own guidance.

11. What could not be verified

Stated plainly. The methodology for setting wholesale access fees, and the text of the most recent notice amending it, were not read in the original. So the article goes only as far as the structure — that a wholesale transaction exists and that the government sets its conditions — and cites no figure for the fee level or any reduction. The discount rate for the tariff-discount option and the notice underpinning it were likewise not read in the original, so the article records only that the scheme continues. The exceptions to the three-month portability restriction and the detailed filing requirements at the neutral body were not read in full either, so only the existence of the restriction and its start date are covered. Roaming, add-on and carrier-billing support vary by company and over time and are not the kind of thing to fix in print, and the same goes for prices. That space is handled by the What to ask column instead.

12. Frequently asked questions

Will calls drop more often if I move to an MVNO?

The towers belong to the facility operator, and the MVNO leases access to them. On the same network, coverage and call quality rest on the same foundation. Which network a plan leases does vary by seller and by plan, so moving to a different one can shift the dead spots in your routine. What to check is not the brand but which network the plan runs on.

If the network is the same, why is it cheaper?

Because there is no network capex, no national store estate or heavy advertising, and only a thin layer of memberships and bundles. The price falls by roughly what those three cost. For the same reason the benefits they produced also shrink, and whether the trade favours you depends on whether you were using them.

Can I keep my existing number?

Number portability exists for that. In Korea a neutral body, the Korea Telecommunications Operators Association, runs it under article 58 of the Telecommunications Business Act and a Ministry of Science and ICT notice. One limit: per that body, portability is restricted for three months from a previous port, a new subscription or a change of account holder, so if any of those happened recently, count from that date first.

Can I switch with a device balance outstanding?

The balance is the purchase price of a handset, so moving the line does not clear it. You keep paying the original seller or settle at once. The calculation therefore includes the remaining balance, instalment interest, and whether a discount tied to the instalment falls away. Handling depends on the contract and the company.

Korea changed its handset subsidy rules — what happened?

The statute governing handset distribution was repealed on 22 July 2025. Per the government briefing, the obligation to publish subsidy amounts and the cap on additional retailer subsidies both went, and it became possible to take a retailer's additional subsidy alongside a tariff discount. Discrimination by region, age or physical condition remains reportable. The practical effect is that purchase terms for the same handset now vary more widely.
