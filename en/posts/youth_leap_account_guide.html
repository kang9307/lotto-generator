Korea's Youth Leap Account - New Signups Closed: Holding, Early Exit and the Successor

Everything about Korea's flagship youth savings account, rewritten for July 2026

Jul 28, 2026 Finance & Economy

Introduction: where the Youth Leap Account stands in July 2026

Search for the Youth Leap Account today and you will still find plenty of pages that open with a cheerful call to sign up. Yet as of July 2026 the account no longer accepts new enrollment. Its tax exemption rests on Article 91-22 of Korea's Restriction of Special Taxation Act, which covers accounts opened on or before 31 December 2025, and the Ministry of Economy and Finance let that sunset clause expire on schedule in the 2025 tax revision. In other words, this is no longer a product you open. It is a product existing holders manage until their five-year term ends.

That splits the audience in two. The first group opened an account between 2023 and 2025 and now wonders how to handle the remaining years: how much matching accumulates, whether there is any way to reach the cash without closing the account, and when closing hurts least. The second group is simply looking for a savings vehicle and stumbled onto this scheme. For them the useful information lies with the successor product, Youth Future Savings, launched in June 2026.

This guide serves both. It sets out eligibility and the income-tiered government match with the full history of amendments, then focuses on the decisions that actually cost money: the early-termination break-even, the special termination grounds, and partial withdrawal. It closes with the relationship to Youth Future Savings and the exact switching sequence. One framing note for readers outside Korea: the Youth Leap Account is a Korean government policy finance product for young residents of Korea, not a product available to non-residents or to foreign nationals living abroad.

The rules and figures below were researched as of July 2026. Since its 2023 launch the Youth Leap Account has been revised repeatedly — matching ceilings, early-termination benefits and the introduction of partial withdrawal have all changed — and the governing statutes and guidelines may change again.

Before opening, closing or withdrawing, confirm the current requirements and amounts with the Korea Inclusive Finance Agency (KINFA) and your own handling bank. The calculations here are illustrative models built on stated assumptions; actual interest rates vary by institution and by individual preferential conditions, and they change over time.

1. The product structure at a glance

The Youth Leap Account lets a young saver deposit freely each month while the government adds a matching contribution proportional to the deposit, and the interest earned is exempt from income tax. Where an ordinary savings account competes on interest alone, this one stacks three effects — interest, government matching and tax exemption — to lift the effective return. The price of that stack is a five-year commitment.

The detail most people miss is flexible deposit. You are not required to pay 700,000 won every month to keep the account alive. In a month with no income you can skip the deposit and the account survives; in a better month you can top back up to the cap. What you cannot do is recover the matching for a skipped month, because the contribution is calculated on what you actually deposited that month. For that reason, the reflex of closing the account because income turned unstable is usually premature.

2. Eligibility - four tests and the fastest self-check order

Even with enrollment closed, the criteria still matter. Your income tier is re-assessed each year in the maintenance review, and the successor product reuses almost the same skeleton. The Youth Leap Account required all four of the following.

TestStandardWhat to watch
Age19 to 34 as of the account opening dateMilitary service periods are excluded from the age count, up to six years
Personal incomePrior-year total salary of 75 million won or less, or global income of 63 million won or lessAbove 60 million won in salary means tax exemption only, no matching
Household income250 percent or less of the standard median income for the household sizeMeasured on the whole household, not the applicant alone
Financial incomeExcluded if subject to comprehensive financial income taxation in any of the past three tax yearsA single qualifying year is enough to disqualify

2.1 Run the self-check in this order

Checking the four tests in random order wastes time. It is far quicker to start with the tests that disqualify most often and are easiest to verify.

  1. Step one, count your age. Confirm you were 19 to 34 on the opening date. If you are past 35, subtract your military service period (up to six years) and count again. Not knowing this exemption is the single most common reason people rule themselves out unnecessarily.
  2. Step two, check your financial income history. This is an absolute bar with no exceptions, so if it applies there is no point examining anything else. Past filings can be checked on the National Tax Service portal.
  3. Step three, locate your income tier. Compare the prior tax year's total salary or global income against the thresholds. Remember that the ceiling for eligibility and the ceiling for receiving matching are different numbers.
  4. Step four, check household income. Plenty of applicants with low personal income fail because a parent's or spouse's income is added in. Household membership is determined from the resident registration record, so confirming your household composition first is the faster route.
  5. Step five, remember the check is systemic, not paper-based. Income and household data are pulled electronically by the handling institution and KINFA, so understating income or omitting household members simply does not work. Filing information that differs from the facts exposes the applicant to clawback of contributions and sanctions, and this article does not describe such methods.

Note that the age at which Korean policy considers someone a young adult varies by scheme — some stop at 34, others run to 39. If that ambiguity is causing confusion, our companion piece on Korean youth and child age standards untangles it.

3. The government contribution - matching by income tier

The matching contribution is the heart of the product, and its structure was substantially rebuilt from the January 2025 deposit onward. Before that, lower incomes earned higher matching rates but the deposit amount eligible for matching was capped at 400,000 to 600,000 won. Someone earning 24 million won or less could deposit the full 700,000 won and still see matching stop at 400,000 won, freezing the contribution at 24,000 won a month. There was, in effect, a band where extra saving earned nothing extra.

From January 2025 the Financial Services Commission widened the matching ceiling to the full 700,000 won deposit cap in every income tier and applied a 3.0 percent rate to the newly added band. The change applied not only to new savers but also to everyone who had opened an account before January 2025.

A note on where these numbers come from. The 33,000 won for the lowest tier and the 29,000 won for the second tier are taken directly from Financial Services Commission releases. The 25,200 won and 21,000 won figures are calculated from the rule the commission published — the original rate up to the original ceiling, then 3.0 percent on the extended band — and they match the two ends of the 21,000 to 33,000 won range quoted in bank guidance. Rounding conventions may shift the amount credited to an individual account slightly, so verify your own figure with KINFA and your handling bank.

3.1 One practical decision falls out of this table

Read across the rows and you can see that the deposit level at which matching works hardest differs by tier. In the lowest tier, depositing 400,000 won earns 24,000 won, or 6.0 percent of the deposit. Adding another 300,000 won to reach the cap adds only 9,000 won, or 3.0 percent of the addition. The first 400,000 won and the next 300,000 won differ by a factor of two in matching efficiency.

  • If spare cash is tight, filling the base matching band (400,000, 500,000 or 600,000 won depending on tier) gives the highest return per won saved.
  • If spare cash is ample, filling the cap is naturally better in absolute terms, since 3.0 percent on the extended band is still extra money.
  • Whether you can lock away 700,000 won a month for five years is, however, a separate question from matching efficiency. If you cannot last three years, the losses described later outweigh the matching gains.
  • Filling the cap while holding no emergency fund is hard to recommend, because it creates pressure to close the account when cash is suddenly needed. Short-term money belongs somewhere designed for it, as covered in our parking accounts and CMA guide.

4. What it is worth at maturity - a calculation with stated assumptions

The phrase most often attached to this account is roughly fifty million won in five years. Breaking that figure apart reveals the character of the product far better than the headline does. The calculation below rests on the following assumptions.

  • Assumption 1: the same amount is deposited every month for 60 months with no gaps.
  • Assumption 2: an interest rate of 5.0 percent simple interest. Real rates depend on each bank's base rate and preferential add-ons, and a floating segment means the rate is not fixed to maturity.
  • Assumption 3: the income tier stays unchanged for five years, whereas in practice the maintenance review re-assesses it annually.
  • Assumption 4: the interest tax exemption applies, meaning the account is held to maturity without early termination.
  • Installment interest accrues only for the months each deposit is actually in the account, so it is not simply principal times rate times five years. Simple interest over 60 months works out to roughly monthly deposit multiplied by annual rate multiplied by 152.5.
CasePrincipal depositedTotal government contributionInterest (under assumptions)Estimated maturity value

Salary 24m or less, 400,000 won/month24.0m won1.44m won (24,000 x 60)about 3.23m wonabout 28.67m won

What matters in this table is not the individual totals but the proportions. In the 700,000 won cases, roughly 85 percent of the maturity value is money the saver deposited, with the government contribution around 4 percent and interest around 11 percent. The scheme does not build a lump sum for you. It adds roughly two million won and a tax waiver to the 42 million won you assemble yourself over five years. Starting with that understanding prevents the common disappointment that the balance grew less than expected.

The tax exemption can be priced too. Applying Korea's ordinary 15.4 percent interest tax to the 5.59 million won of interest in the first case gives about 860,000 won. The exemption alone is therefore worth roughly 860,000 won in avoided tax, and adding the 1.98 million won of matching brings the advantage over a plain savings account to about 2.84 million won. For the underlying mechanics of deposit rates and taxation, see our guide to deposit and savings interest rates.

5. The tax exemption and Article 91-22

The tax exemption here is not a bank courtesy but a statutory tax preference. It rests on Article 91-22 of the Restriction of Special Taxation Act, headed tax exemption for the Youth Leap Account, which provides that no income tax is levied on interest and dividend income arising in a Youth Leap Account opened by a qualifying resident on or before 31 December 2025.

That date governs everything else in this article. It explains why enrollment ended and why the successor arrived under a different name. In the 2025 tax revision the Ministry of Economy and Finance confirmed it would let the expiring clause lapse as scheduled.

5.1 The three-year clawback rule deserves attention

The same article contains a less publicised provision: if the account is closed before three years have passed from the first contract date, an amount equivalent to the tax relieved through the exemption is recovered. Closures for unavoidable reasons such as the holder's death or emigration are excluded.

  • This rule is what makes three years the real dividing line in the product. Independently of the five-year maturity, crossing or missing the 36-month mark changes the tax outcome.
  • An amendment effective 1 January 2025 went further: holding for three years or more preserves the exemption on early termination and still pays part of the government contribution, reported at around 60 percent. That amendment underpins the break-even analysis below.
  • The exemption applies to interest income, not to the government contribution. The contribution is policy support rather than taxable interest, and its payment follows separate rules.
  • Exemption does not automatically improve your position under comprehensive financial income taxation. This interest is excluded from the aggregation, but if you hold substantial other financial income the overall burden still has to be assessed separately.

6. The maintenance review - your contribution moves with your income

Many holders assume the income recorded at opening applies for all five years. It does not. The account is subject to a maintenance review that, starting one year after opening, re-checks personal income annually and updates the contribution rate.

  • The review runs on a yearly cycle beginning one year after opening.
  • Which tax year is used depends on when the review falls. Reviews between January and June use income from two years prior; those from July to December use the previous year, because of when National Tax Service data is finalised.
  • A raise that moves you into a higher tier reduces the contribution. Conversely, a job change or leave of absence that lowers income can increase it.
  • The maintenance review examines personal income. It is not described as an annual re-assessment of the household income verified at opening.
  • Crossing 60 million won in salary drops the contribution to zero, but the tax exemption continues. A promotion is not a reason to close the account.

The practical implication is clear: depositing heavily in your lower-income years maximises total matching. For someone early in their career, weighting deposits toward the first two or three years and easing off as salary rises is the rational pattern from a matching standpoint. That is a matching-only calculation, of course, and day-to-day cash needs come first.

7. Early termination - why three years is the break-even

Five years is a long time. Job changes, marriage, a lease renewal or an unexpected medical bill tend to arrive at least once inside that window. The most valuable thing to know about this product is therefore exactly what closing it costs.

The table exposes a sharp step at month 36. Closing in month 35 versus month 37 differs by only 700,000 won of principal, yet the outcomes are no contribution with taxed interest on one side and 60 percent of the contribution with the exemption intact on the other. If you are weighing closure and the three-year mark is only months away, pricing those few months should come first.

7.1 A closure checklist

  1. Count the months since opening. Before month 36, work through every item below. After month 36, the loss shrinks considerably.
  2. Check the special termination grounds first. If your situation qualifies, you can exit without loss, so there is no reason to rush an ordinary closure.
  3. See whether partial withdrawal covers the amount. If you need less than 40 percent of the principal paid in, closing may be unnecessary altogether.
  4. Total the contributions already received. Before three years these are subject to clawback, so that sum is an immediate loss.
  5. Ask your bank for the early-termination rate directly. The gap from the contracted rate is often larger than expected and differs by institution.
  6. Consider other sources of cash first. This article does not recommend any specific loan product or lender, and borrowing introduces a new interest burden that has to be weighed.

8. Special early termination - qualifying grounds and timing

Special early termination covers closures for the unavoidable reasons defined in the Enforcement Decree of the Restriction of Special Taxation Act. In those cases the account keeps both the government contribution and the tax exemption despite closing early. Because the outcome differs so completely from an ordinary closure, hitting the close button without checking this list is the costliest mistake available.

8.1 Grounds that qualify

  • Death of the holder
  • Emigration
  • Retirement or separation from employment
  • Closure of the business premises
  • Natural disaster
  • Illness requiring three months or more of treatment or convalescence
  • Purchase of a first home
  • Marriage and childbirth, including a spouse's childbirth, added in the 2025 revision

8.2 The timing traps

Qualifying on the grounds is not enough; many people fail on when they apply. Two points matter most, particularly around home purchases.

  • First-home purchase is described as claimable only after registration of title is complete. That means the money cannot be pulled out when the deposit or the balance of the purchase price is due. This is precisely where the plan of saving here to buy a home falls apart in practice.
  • For grounds other than death and emigration, a time limit of six months applies between the triggering event and the application, per the published guidance. Applying long after the event may not be accepted.
  • Every ground requires documentary evidence — a certificate of separation for retirement, proof of business closure, a medical certificate for illness, and so on, differing by ground.
  • Some sources mention size and price conditions attached to the qualifying home, but we could not confirm those figures in a primary source, so no specific numbers are stated here. Anyone planning to close on this ground should confirm the details with KINFA or the handling bank beforehand.

In short, special early termination is the option to verify before closing anything. Processing a qualifying situation as an ordinary closure simply discards the contribution and the exemption.

9. Partial withdrawal - reaching the cash without closing

The partial withdrawal service introduced in 2025 is the most practical improvement the product has received. Until then the only choices were keep everything or close everything; now part of the balance can be taken while the account stays alive.

  • Eligibility: accounts held for at least two years from opening
  • Frequency: once, at any point up to the day before maturity
  • Limit: within 40 percent of the principal deposited as of the applicable cut-off date
  • Treatment of the remainder: the balance after the withdrawal keeps its government contribution and tax exemption
  • Timing: announced for implementation in the second half of 2025. Whether it is live, where it sits in each bank's app, and how the cut-off date is computed can differ by institution, so check first

9.1 Choosing between withdrawal and closure

The test is simple: compare the amount you need against 40 percent of the principal deposited.

  1. If the amount falls within that 40 percent, partial withdrawal is almost always better. The account survives, matching and exemption continue, and you can resume deposits for the remaining term.
  2. If the amount exceeds 40 percent and you are under three years in, check the special termination grounds next. Qualifying means full recovery without loss.
  3. Only when neither route applies and the sum is genuinely large does it make sense to price out an ordinary closure and decide.
  4. Remember the withdrawal is a one-time right. A plan to split it across two occasions is not available, so it should be used at the moment it is genuinely needed.

10. Can it be combined with other youth support schemes?

Korea's youth support schemes carry confusingly similar names and sit under different ministries. Only what could be confirmed is set out here.

  • Youth Hope Savings (Financial Services Commission, 2022) — could not be held simultaneously with the Youth Leap Account, though holders could move over once it matured. In 2024 a temporary linked route allowed the maturity proceeds to be paid into a Youth Leap Account as a lump sum, from a minimum of two million won up to the full proceeds. That window has since closed.
  • Youth Future Savings (Financial Services Commission, 2026) — cannot be held at the same time. The commission stated that the Youth Leap Account must be closed to join, and provided a switching procedure that avoids losing benefits, covered in section 11.
  • Youth Tomorrow Savings Account (Ministry of Health and Welfare) — guidance conflicts because the sponsoring ministry differs. Both simultaneous enrollment and restriction are asserted in circulation, so this article takes no position either way. Anyone weighing both should confirm directly with each scheme's administering body.
  • Youth Housing Dream Subscription Savings (Ministry of Land) — this is a housing subscription account, a different category from asset-building products. Guidance exists on paying Youth Leap Account maturity proceeds into it as a lump sum, but the conditions and limits must be confirmed with the ministry and the handling bank.
  • ISA — not a youth-specific scheme but a general tax-advantaged account open to all residents, so it sits on a different axis. No rule is known that prevents holding both. For its structure see our complete ISA guide.
  • Local government asset-building programmes — rules vary by municipality and no general statement is possible. Checking your local authority's notice is the only reliable route.

11. Youth Future Savings and the switching decision

On 22 June 2026 the successor product, Youth Future Savings, launched. Per Financial Services Commission announcements the first round ran on a schedule of applications 22 June to 3 July, screening 6 to 24 July, and account opening 27 July to 7 August, and applicants had already passed two million cumulatively as of 1 p.m. on 2 July. A second enrollment round is provisionally set for December 2026, with half-yearly operation signalled thereafter.

ComparisonYouth Leap AccountYouth Future Savings
TermFive yearsThree years
Monthly deposit cap700,000 won500,000 won (not exceeding 6 million won per year)
Government contribution3.0 to 6.0% by income tier, up to 33,000 won monthlyStandard 6% (up to 30,000 won) / preferential 12% (up to 60,000 won)

New enrollmentClosed December 2025Open, in half-yearly rounds

Structurally, Youth Future Savings is a design that shortens the term and raises the matching rate. It pulls in savers who balked at five years by cutting to three, and offsets that with a 12 percent match for the preferential tier. The trade-off is a lower monthly cap of 500,000 won, so the total sum accumulated is necessarily smaller. The preferential tier is described as covering employees of small and medium enterprises, new hires at such firms, and small business owners, and the rule that salary above 60 million won (or global income above 48 million won) leaves only the tax benefit carries over from the earlier product.

11.1 The switching sequence - getting the order wrong forfeits the benefit

KINFA sets out the linked-enrollment procedure as follows. The order is itself a requirement, so it has to be followed exactly.

  1. Apply for Youth Future Savings
  2. Confirm the eligibility notice for Youth Future Savings
  3. Open the Youth Future Savings account (opened at zero, deposits restricted)
  4. Carry out the special early termination of the Youth Leap Account for linked-enrollment purposes
  5. Begin deposits into Youth Future Savings
  • The biggest trap is closing the Youth Leap Account first. The guidance states explicitly that prior closure makes linked enrollment impossible.
  • Linked enrollment fails if the resident registration or foreign registration numbers on the two products differ. Anyone with a name change or a registration number change in their history should update the records in advance.
  • Going through the special early termination for linked-enrollment purposes is described as preserving the Youth Leap Account's interest tax exemption and the government contribution on amounts already deposited.
  • The commission noted that the stage-by-stage periods for application, screening and account opening may be extended or deferred depending on screening volume.

11.2 How to weigh whether to switch

Which product wins depends on the individual. These are the variables to substitute in.

  • Time remaining — with one year left on a Youth Leap Account there is little reason to restart a three-year clock. Having just completed year one, the comparison is four remaining years against a fresh three.
  • Preferential tier status — qualifying for the 12 percent match makes Youth Future Savings superior on matching rate alone. Without it, the 6 percent rate is comparable to or below the lower tiers of the older product.
  • Monthly capacity — for anyone able to deposit 700,000 won a month, the 500,000 won cap is a binding constraint.
  • Age — some applicants will turn 35 before the next round and lose eligibility. The commission noted that those born between 8 August and 31 December 1991, who reach 35 after the first round, may face limited further opportunities to join.
  • This article does not judge which bank is better. It is nonetheless a fact that handling institutions differ in base rates and in the conditions attached to preferential rates such as salary transfers, card usage or first-time customer status, so comparing those conditions yourself is worthwhile.

12. Seven common misconceptions

  1. You can still sign up. No. New enrollment ended on 31 December 2025. A great many how-to-apply pages still in search results simply were never updated.
  2. Missing a 700,000 won deposit closes the account. No. Deposits are flexible and the account survives a skipped month. Only that month's matching is lost.
  3. Save for five years and the government hands you fifty million won. A misreading. At the 700,000 won level roughly 85 percent of the maturity value is your own principal, and the total matching is around two million won.
  4. Early closure eats into the principal. The principal is not reduced. What is lost is the government contribution (before three years), the tax exemption, and the gap between the contracted rate and the early-termination rate.
  5. Save here to buy a home. First-home purchase is a qualifying ground, but the claim is described as available only after registration of title. It is therefore hard to use for a deposit or the balance of a purchase price.
  6. A pay rise means you should close. No. Matching may shrink or disappear, but the tax exemption continues. A promotion is not grounds for closing.
  7. Youth Future Savings can sit alongside the Youth Leap Account. No. They cannot be held simultaneously; a switching procedure is required, and getting its order wrong forfeits the benefit.

Conclusion - three principles for managing the remaining term

The Youth Leap Account is no longer something to open; it is something to manage. Compressed into three principles, here is what this guide argues about handling the remaining years.

First, treat three years as the reference line. Read the three-year clawback rule in Article 91-22 alongside the January 2025 amendment and the difference between an account past 36 months and one short of it is stark. The moment closure crosses your mind, the first number to count is how many months old the account is.

Second, check two detours before pressing close. Whether your circumstances match a special termination ground, and whether partial withdrawal (two years held, within 40 percent of principal, once only) covers the amount. Running an ordinary closure without knowing these two amounts to voluntarily surrendering matching and exemption you were entitled to.

Third, if you intend to move to Youth Future Savings, confirm the order in writing before acting. Open the Youth Future Savings account first, then carry out the special early termination of the Youth Leap Account. Reversing that sequence blocks linked enrollment. The first round's account-opening window ran to 7 August 2026, and the second round is provisionally signposted for December 2026.

One closing observation. Since its 2023 launch this scheme has changed shape repeatedly in a short span: a wider matching ceiling (January 2025), preserved benefits at three years (January 2025), partial withdrawal (second half of 2025), the end of new enrollment (December 2025) and a successor product (June 2026). It is, in other words, a product where old guidance cannot be trusted at face value. Re-check the primary sources immediately before you act.
