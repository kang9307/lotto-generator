Why OCR Is Slow - The Image Is Seen at Once, but the Output Comes Out One Line at a Time

The asymmetry decoding creates, the ways of breaking it, and the conditions for reading a published speedup

2026-09-04 Tech & IT

When someone says it takes a few seconds to turn one page of a document into text, most people assume the time goes into the model working out what the letters are. In the way this is done now, that is not where it goes. Recognising the letters finishes almost instantly, and writing the result out consumes everything.

This piece looks at that structure. It is not an argument that some product is good; it is an account of exactly where OCR is slow today and why it is slow there, and of what the attempts to fix that spot gain and give up. It also sets out how to read the speedup figures that get published without simply believing them.

Every performance number in this article comes from published measurements, and the conditions of measurement are given alongside each one. The same technique produces very different multiples once those conditions change, so treat them as reference points only until you have measured your own workload.

The trigger - a bare multiple tells you nothing

In early September 2026 a write-up appeared in a Korean developer community describing OCR decoding accelerated with diffusion. The headline claim was 3.85x faster decoding with no quality loss. Sentences like that travel easily, and once they have travelled almost no information survives. What 3.85x is measured against, on what hardware and at what batch size, and whether that multiple carries through to total request time — all of it is missing.

The original post in fact lists three different multiples from the same system, side by side. Measure only the token-generation window and it is 3.85x. Measure the full request, including image encoding and prefill, and it is 1.85x. Measure page throughput with layout and region handling attached and it is 1.34x. All three are honest figures, and all three answer different questions. Which one you should quote is decided by what you are actually trying to reduce.

So the interest here is not the multiple but the structure that produces it. Once you understand the structure you can guess roughly what multiple your own documents would see, and you can also see in advance the places where it will not apply at all.

Classic OCR and generative OCR do different jobs

The older approach has two stages. First it locates the regions of the image likely to contain text, crops them out, and then converts each crop into a string. Detection and recognition are separate, and because each crop is recognised independently, many crops can be processed at the same time. The output is a list of strings and coordinates. What order those items should be read in, and where one table starts and ends, is decided afterwards by post-processing rules.

The recent approach folds that structure away. The whole image goes into an encoder, and a decoder generates the result as a string in a format like Markdown or HTML. Table rows and columns, formulas, heading hierarchy, and reading order all live inside the output format itself. What used to be assembled by post-processing rules is written in one go by the model. Where it fits, this is far cleaner — especially for structures that coordinates alone cannot reconstruct, such as tables with merged cells or multi-column pages.

AspectDetection plus recognitionGenerating text from the image
OutputA list of strings and coordinatesA document string carrying structure
ParallelismSplit by region and processed concurrentlyOutput tokens run sequentially from the front
Tables and formulasAssembled by rules. Weak on merged cellsStructure lives inside the output format
Reading orderDepends on coordinate sorting rulesDecided by the model as generation order
When it is wrongCoordinates point at the region that failedYou must diff against the original to find it
Main driver of latencyNumber of regions and image sizeNumber of output tokens

That last row is the starting point of this article. Changing approach moved the cause of slowness from the image side to the text side. The case of pushing past the page-level limit at the architecture level is covered separately in the piece on parsing long documents in a single pass, so here we look only at decoding.

Where the bottleneck sits - parallel in, sequential out

An image encoder handles the whole frame in one bundle of computation. It is not the case that the top-left pixel must be seen before the bottom-right one, so it simply sees all of them together. A language-model decoder, by contrast, cannot compute the next token until the previous one is fixed. A thousand output tokens means a thousand sequential passes.

How large that asymmetry really is becomes visible when you measure output length. The post referred to above, working over cropped regions from an English document benchmark, reports an average of 888 tokens per crop for table regions and 74 tokens per crop for free-text regions. In other words, two images of the same size differ by more than a factor of ten in sequential passes once one of them contains a table. This is why financial documents dense with tables feel particularly slow.

autoregressive : [forward] -> t1
                 [forward] -> t2
                 [forward] -> t3   ... repeat per output token

block diffusion: [forward] -> draft t1..t32 all at once
                 [forward] -> verify, commit only the matching prefix
                 (one round = 2 forwards)

And during that sequential stretch the hardware is largely idle. Producing one token at a time means so little arithmetic that speed is set not by the compute units but by the memory bandwidth needed to fetch the weights. If several tokens can be produced for the same computational cost, that is simply free. The hardware side of this is laid out in more detail in the piece on what actually decides inference hardware.

What diffusion decoding actually changes

Diffusion-style text generation, instead of filling blanks left to right, starts with many slots masked, fills them in one pass, and refines. It carries over to discrete tokens the same idea as repeatedly removing noise in image generation. The principle itself is unpacked in the piece on diffusion-based text generation, so it is not repeated here.

What matters here is that the practical form works block by block. Treating a whole document as one chunk forces you to fix the length in advance and makes cache reuse hard. So only a fixed block, something like 32 tokens, is filled by diffusion, and between blocks the model still moves left to right. This compromise between autoregression and diffusion became widely known through the Block Diffusion paper published by Marianne Arriola and colleagues in 2025.

The approach in this case goes one step further. The same model both drafts and verifies. Each round runs two passes: the first drafts all the tokens in a block at once, and the second checks that draft with the ordinary sequential path. Only the matching prefix is committed, and the first position where they diverge comes free from the verification side. Measured on the reported setup, roughly 19 tokens are committed per round on average, which works out to 9.6 tokens per forward pass.

Why it suits OCR in particular

Filling many slots at once does not always work well. In open-ended writing, earlier words strongly shape later ones. Several candidates fit the same slot, and which one you chose changes the answer at the next slot. Fill them simultaneously and they frequently fail to agree with each other.

OCR is different. The answer is already on the screen. There is very little room for the model to invent, and the answer at each position is bound far more tightly to the pixels corresponding to that position than to surrounding context. So filling several positions at once is unlikely to produce contradictions. The measured ordering supports that: committed tokens per block came out as 25.4 for tables, 20.7 for formulas, and 16.9 for free text. Tables have regular delimiters and alignment markers and are easy to predict; free text is relatively less so.

  • The lower the uncertainty in the output, the more tokens get committed in one go. The speed gain comes out of predictability
  • Which means badly printed scans and handwriting may see a smaller gain from the same technique, because uncertainty goes up
  • Conversely, fixed-layout forms sit at the favourable end. Repeating structure makes prediction easy
  • The caution is that this ordering is about size of gain, not size of accuracy. Tables coming out fast does not mean tables come out right

The thing to notice is that 3.85x and 1.34x are numbers from the same system. The first isolates the window in which tokens are drawn; the second is close to the time a user actually waits. Neither is an exaggeration, but the one that ends up in the marketing line is always the first.

It also splits by content type. The source reports 3.50x for tables, 1.93x for formulas, and 1.65x for free text, attributing the gap to tables having long output so that fixed costs are diluted well. That places these multiples on the side that includes fixed cost. A workload consisting of nothing but short text crops is therefore the one where fixed cost dominates and the acceleration is felt least.

There is one more thing to see on the quality side. The overall score fell by 0.32 points, yet the source states it confirmed that verified output is byte-for-byte identical to the original autoregressive decoding under greedy settings. If so, those 0.32 points come not from decoding but from the process of converting the model to run with a diffusion path. That distinction matters. Trading accuracy for speed and losing accuracy as a conversion cost are two different stories.

Raise the batch and the gain evaporates

This is the point most often missed in practice. The source states that as the batch grows, the advantage of the self-speculative approach shrinks from 1.85x at batch 1 to 1.08x when peak throughput of each method is compared.

The reason is simple. The root cause of sequential generation being slow is not the amount of arithmetic but the failure to fill the hardware. Bundle many requests together, though, and the autoregressive method fills the hardware too. Once it is full there is nothing left to fill, and the benefit of parallel decoding disappears. Put plainly:

  • This is a latency technique, not a throughput technique
  • A team grinding tens of thousands of pages overnight in batch gains almost nothing. They are already filling the hardware with batching
  • Conversely, a service where one person uploads one page and waits feels it directly
  • So the first question in an adoption review is not performance but how many concurrent requests our traffic has. If that number is large, do not expect the headline multiple
  • Decide up front whether the target is single-request latency or total throughput. The two do not point the same way

What it shares with speculative decoding, and what it does not

This is not the first attempt to break sequentiality. The most widely used is speculative decoding. The paper by Yaniv Leviathan, Matan Kalman, and Yossi Matias, published in 2022, is its starting point: a small fast model draws several tokens ahead and the large model checks them in a single pass. That paper reported 2x to 3x without changing the output on T5-XXL.

Verification is the crux. A wrong draft is discarded in favour of the large model's answer, so a poor draft model does not degrade output quality. The only thing that degrades is speed. This OCR case uses the same skeleton, but the draft comes from the diffusion path of the same model rather than a separate small one.

ComparisonSpeculative decodingDiffusion draft plus autoregressive verify
Who produces the draftA separate small modelA different path of the same model
Extra things to manageTraining, deploying and version-matching a draft modelOne converted checkpoint
MemoryTwo models residentEffectively one
When the draft is poorAcceptance rate drops and it slows downThe same. Accuracy is preserved
Accuracy guaranteeThe verification rule preserves the distributionUses the same verification skeleton
Shared limitationGains shrink as batch size grows, and verification costs extra computation

Both rest on the same idea. Produce several candidates cheaply, then confirm them in one pass with the expensive but reliable method. The trade only works because confirming costs far less than generating.

What CER and WER actually count

Talking about speed drags accuracy in behind it, and the numbers used there need their conditions read just as much. The most common are character error rate and word error rate.

Character error rate is the number of edits needed to reach the reference string, divided by the reference length. The widely used definition is CER = (S + D + I) / N, where S is substituted characters, D is deleted ones, I is characters inserted that were not there, and N is the number of characters in the reference. Word error rate performs the same calculation over words.

  • Both are edit distances. They count only the differences that remain once the result is flattened into a single line
  • If a lot of content is inserted that was not there, the value can exceed 1. The standard library documentation says so explicitly. Do not read 100 percent as an upper bound
  • Which is why some places use a normalised variant. This is exactly why figures from two different reports cannot simply be compared
  • They tell you nothing about the kind of error. A wrong particle and a wrong digit in a currency amount both count as 1
  • Preprocessing shifts the value too. Whether you normalise whitespace, fold case, or strip punctuation changes the result

What those metrics miss - tables, reading order, columns

Edit distance compares strings, so it cannot see whatever disappears the moment you make a string. Whether a table's rows and columns drifted, whether a two-column page was read right column first, whether a footnote was spliced between body paragraphs. Those errors leave every character correct, so character error rate comes out low. Yet feed that result into a search index or a question-answering system and the content is thoroughly scrambled.

This is why document-parsing benchmarks split their metrics. Taking one widely used public benchmark as an example: body text gets a normalised edit distance, tables get a tree-edit-distance-based similarity, formulas get a separate character detection matching metric, and reading order gets yet another edit distance. Its 1,651 pages mix ten document types and five layout types, with multi-column pages held as their own category.

Look at how the overall score is computed, though, and it is the average of three things: body edit distance, table similarity, and the formula metric. Reading order is not among them. Which means the 0.32-point drop from the previous section tells you nothing about what happened to reading order. That is not a flaw in that benchmark; it is a property every summary metric has.

  • When reading a benchmark number, first work out what it is the average of. An overall score is usually several items combined
  • If an item is not folded in, that item is not being managed by that number
  • If your documents are table-heavy, look at the table metric; if they are multi-column papers, look at reading order separately
  • The surest thing is measuring 30 of your own pages yourself. There is no reason a public benchmark's document mix should match yours

What changes in Korean, Chinese, and Japanese

Every measurement above was taken on English documents. There are several reasons those multiples cannot be carried straight over to Korean or Chinese documents, and those reasons are not about performance but about the writing system.

The first is the number of characters. The Unicode CJK Unified Ideographs block runs from U+4E00 to U+9FFF and holds 20,992 code points with every position assigned. That is only the basic block gathering the most common characters; several extension blocks exist besides. Hangul has composition rules, so the count falls out of arithmetic: 19 initial consonants times 21 medial vowels times 28 final consonants gives 11,172 precomposed syllables. This is a different size of problem from handling twenty-six letters.

The second is token count. The generative approach produces tokens, not characters. With subword tokenisers it is common for a single ideograph or Hangul syllable to split into several tokens. So two pages of equivalent content yield different output token counts by language. That is the most practical reason a multiple published in tokens per second cannot be moved between languages. To compare, you need a unit less swayed by language, such as pages per second.

The third is that word error rate is barely well defined. Chinese and Japanese do not space between words, and Korean does space but norms and actual usage often disagree. Once what counts as a word depends on a morphological analyser's configuration, the number becomes a function of that configuration rather than of OCR quality. So in these languages character error rate is the steadier of the two.

The fourth is vertical writing. Unicode maintains a separate specification defining a default orientation per character for vertical layout, whose backbone is that ideographs, kana, and Hangul stay upright while Latin letters lie on their side. On a vertically set page reading order and line-breaking rules themselves differ, so metrics from a model trained on horizontal documents cannot be expected to carry over.

Where slowness actually becomes a problem

Any claim of improved speed should be rewritten as whose waiting, and which waiting, is being reduced. Each setting tolerates a different latency, and each therefore needs a different remedy.

  • Bulk batch processing — grinding tens of thousands of pages overnight. Nobody sees the latency of a single page. What matters is pages per hour, and raising the batch removes almost all of the parallel-decoding gain as shown above
  • Immediate check after upload — the user uploads a file and waits in front of the screen. Seconds are felt directly, and this is where parallel decoding fits best
  • Live translation through a camera — it has to be overlaid on the screen, so it must finish within hundreds of milliseconds. At that point model size and where it runs get decided before the decoding method does
  • Conversational document queries — if documents can be processed in advance, latency moves to indexing time. From the user's side OCR speed is invisible
  • Reprocessing — changing the model and rerunning everything comes round periodically. That behaves like batch processing

The last two lines are the ones that get forgotten. If work can be done ahead of time, much of the speed problem stops existing. It is worth checking first whether real-time is genuinely required, and if the work has to run on equipment in the field, it is worth reading what that placement decision already settles first. Processing ahead of time and splitting the result into searchable units takes an even more extreme form in media with a time axis.

When 99 percent accuracy is still not enough

Ninety-nine percent character accuracy sounds like a good number. But if a page holds 3,000 characters, it means 30 of them are wrong. Whether a document with thirty errors on a page can be passed straight to the next stage is decided by the use.

What is more awkward is not the count but the location. You do not know which 30. A wrong grammatical particle inside a sentence usually causes nothing at all, but a wrong digit in an amount, one digit of an account number, or one character of a person's name invalidates the entire document. The assumption that errors cost the same breaks down right here.

  • Reviewing everything by hand removes the reason for automating. Place review only where the risk concentrates
  • Ahead of any human, filter out what rules can catch: totals reconciled against line items, date formats, digit counts, numbers carrying a checksum
  • Another option is reading the same page a second way and diffing the two. Where the two results disagree is your suspicion zone
  • Confidence scores from the model are worth a glance but are hard to lean on in generative systems. Confidently wrong output is possible
  • Keeping the correspondence between the original image and the output position cuts review cost sharply. A surprising number of pipelines throw that correspondence away
  • Assume errors will happen and build the correction path into the design. Bolted on later, it usually does not attach

When classic is enough and when generative is needed

With the structure in view the selection criteria are short. The newer approach is not always better, and in particular the fact that the cause of slowness has moved is itself a basis for choosing.

  • For pulling out short values at fixed positions — a receipt number, a licence plate, an identity document field — detection plus recognition is faster, cheaper, and more predictable
  • When output is short the generative advantage nearly vanishes, and as seen above fixed cost dominates, so acceleration techniques have little effect either
  • For tables with merged cells, multi-column pages, or documents laced with formulas, generative is the better side. Coordinate sorting rules cannot reconstruct that structure
  • If the result is not read by a person but consumed directly by the next program, reading order and table structure become important. Choose on character error rate alone and you will be choosing again later
  • Mixing the two is common. Catching layout with detection and passing only the complex regions to a generative model is often the better value
  • Either way, measure on a sample of your own documents before deciding. Published multiples and scores came out of someone else's document mix

Where a technique like diffusion decoding belongs also follows from this. It is an option that means something only to those who have already chosen generative, and among them it pays only for those whose problem is single-request latency. Outside that condition, no multiple however large will be felt.

What I could not verify

  • The several related papers cited by the triggering post were confirmed only as a list; I did not open each paper directly. The body therefore does not rest any claim on their contents. What I did confirm directly is the block diffusion paper, the speculative decoding paper, and the document-parsing benchmark
  • Whether the 0.32-point drop in the overall score came from body edit distance or from table similarity could not be determined, as no breakdown is given. The same applies to any change in the reading-order metric
  • The multiple the same technique achieves on Korean, Chinese, and Japanese documents could not be verified. The published measurement targets English documents
  • How committed token counts change on vertically set pages or on handwriting could not be verified either. That the gain should shrink as predictability falls is an inference from structure, not a measurement
  • I could not find an industry-standard tolerance for character error rate. It appears to vary by document type and use, with no single published threshold
