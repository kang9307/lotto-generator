Why AI Proofs of Concept Succeed but Never Reach Production

Six pilot traps, and the transition gate to run before a proof of concept is signed off

Jul 27, 2026 Finance & Economy

Introduction: the demo got applause, and six months later nobody uses it

Every organization that has tried to adopt AI has a version of the same scene. A two-month pilot wraps up and there is a demonstration for the executive committee. Someone types a question and an answer appears. Someone drops in a document and a summary comes back. There is applause, and then a conversation about budget. Six months later, nobody is using that system in their daily work. The project was never formally cancelled. It simply never crossed into production and quietly stopped moving.

The argument of this article fits in one sentence. A proof of concept asks whether it can work. Production asks whether it works every day. Those are different questions. Passing a test built around the first question guarantees nothing about the second. Worse, the easier a pilot is designed to pass, the harder the transition becomes, because every condition removed to make the pilot succeed is a condition production will impose anyway.

So this article does not argue about whether AI delivers value, and it does not cover how to measure performance once a system is live. It covers exactly one thing: what breaks, and in what order, in the stretch between a successful proof of concept and a running production service, and how a pilot should be designed differently to prevent that collapse. It starts by sorting out the widely quoted failure-rate figures along with their methodology and their limits, then works through six recurring transition failure patterns as cause, warning signs and alternative, and closes with a transition gate checklist that can be used on the day a pilot ends.

1. Start with the numbers - how far can the 95 percent claim be trusted

Most writing on this subject opens with a shocking failure rate. The trouble is that those figures are counting different things. Before quoting any of them, it is worth separating what each one actually measured.

1.1 Four different numbers

The most quoted figure is the 95 percent from The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025, published by the MIT Media Lab NANDA initiative in August 2025. What that study reports, however, is closer to no measurable profit-and-loss contribution having been observed than to the technology having failed. According to public descriptions of the work, it combined a review of roughly 300 publicly disclosed initiatives, structured interviews with organizations, and a survey of 153 senior leaders.

Criticism followed almost immediately. Fortune and Futuriom, among others, pointed out that the underlying data behind the 95 percent conclusion was not released, leaving the number unverifiable. Wharton professor Kevin Werbach was reported as saying that if the supporting data were not going to be released, the report should be retracted. Separately, commentators raised a conflict of interest: NANDA is a project that sells corporate memberships and the report positions its own technology as the remedy for the divide it describes. In short, this figure is directional context, not an established measurement.

The study closest to the subject of this article, and with a clearer stated methodology, comes from S&P Global Market Intelligence and its Voice of the Enterprise: AI & Machine Learning research. Results published in early 2025 reported that among more than 1,000 enterprise respondents across North America and Europe, 42 percent had abandoned most of their AI initiatives, sharply up from 17 percent the previous year. The same research reported that the average organization scrapped 46 percent of its proofs of concept before they reached production. Cost and data privacy and security risk were among the leading reasons given.

The two Gartner figures need one label attached to them: they are predictions, not observations. In a July 2024 announcement, Gartner predicted that at least 30 percent of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, citing poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs and unclear business value. In a February 2025 announcement, drawing on a third-quarter 2024 survey of 248 data management leaders, Gartner reported that 63 percent of organizations either lack the data management practices needed for AI or are unsure whether they have them, and predicted that through 2026 organizations would abandon 60 percent of AI projects that are not supported by AI-ready data.

1.2 So what is the honest conclusion

Honestly stated: the true failure rate is unknown. Definitions differ across studies, and respondents have little incentive to report their own failures precisely. What does carry weight is that studies built on quite different methods keep pointing at the same place. Projects do not stall because the technology refuses to work. They stall in the stretch after someone has confirmed that it works, while trying to turn that into something that runs every day. And the reasons that recur are strikingly consistent: data, integration, cost and risk control.

A rule for citing these numbers. If a failure rate is going into an internal report, do not move the number alone. Move the publisher, the year, the sample and the definition of failure with it. 95 percent and 42 percent are not counting the same thing, and 30 percent and 60 percent are forecasts rather than measurements. A report that drops those distinctions does not make the discussion more precise, it makes it louder.

2. A pilot and production answer fundamentally different questions

To understand transition failure structurally, start by separating what each stage validates. A proof of concept validates feasibility. Production validates reliability. Feasibility is proven by succeeding once. Reliability is proven only by not failing repeatedly. The logical structure of the proof is different in each case.

DimensionWhat the pilot asksWhat production asks
DataDoes it work on a curated sampleDoes it work on live data with nulls, duplicates and legacy schemas
EvaluationDoes it look good to a human reviewerDoes it clear the bar on a frozen dataset without regression
Failure toleranceIf it fails, just run it againFailure reaches a customer and leaves a record
SpeedA few seconds of waiting is fineAre p95 and p99 latencies inside target
CostDoes it fit the experiment budgetIs fully loaded cost per item below the alternative
PeopleThe owner is sitting right thereDoes it run while the owner is on leave
AccountabilityNothing arises that requires accountabilityIs there an owner and a rollback path for bad output
ObservabilityNobody needs to read the logsCan what happened be reconstructed after the fact

The important thing about this table is not the content of any row. It is that the right-hand column was never tested at any point during the pilot. And validating the right-hand column usually costs more time and money than the pilot did. This is where a common misconception does damage. Executives tend to perceive a completed proof of concept as roughly 80 percent of the journey, when in many cases the pilot was 20 percent and the transition is the remaining 80 percent. When budgets and timelines are built on that misconception, resources run out precisely at the transition.

There is a further paradox: organizations with high pilot success rates may have lower transition rates. When there is pressure to make a pilot succeed, the owner naturally selects conditions that make success likely. Clean data, a cooperative department, a lenient reviewer, cases without exceptions. A success built that way says nothing about production. All six traps below descend from that one dynamic.

3. Trap 1 - the curated data trap

3.1 Cause: the sample was chosen for explainability, not representativeness

Pilot datasets are usually assembled by a person, and the selection criterion is rarely whether the sample represents the population. It is whether the sample is easy to explain. Poorly scanned documents, fields where every department uses a different code scheme, files written on a form that was retired five years ago, records where someone pasted personal notes into a structured field: all of these fall out of the sample naturally. They fall out without anyone registering that they were dropped.

A second effect compounds it. During a pilot, the data stands still. A snapshot is extracted once and the team works on it for two months. In production, data keeps moving. Source schemas change, new product codes appear, and one day a field starts arriving empty. This is exactly the gap behind Gartner reporting, from a third-quarter 2024 survey of 248 data management leaders, that 63 percent of organizations either lack AI-ready data management practices or are unsure whether they have them. The problem is not that the data is messy. It is that the pilot was designed so that it would never meet the mess.

3.2 Warning signs

  • There is no document recording who selected the pilot data and on what basis
  • Preprocessing exists only as a one-off script on somebody's laptop
  • Meeting notes contain a line like exceptional cases were excluded from this phase
  • Data access was granted temporarily for the pilot and the production-grade approval has not been started
  • Nobody can explain the refresh cadence or the schema change history of the source systems

3.3 Alternatives

  • Build the sample in two parts: a random sample plus a deliberately worst-case sample drawn from the dirtiest segment you can find
  • Move preprocessing off the laptop into a reproducible pipeline where the same input yields the same output
  • Write a data contract per source system covering schema, refresh cadence, quality thresholds and the owning team
  • Define drift monitoring metrics during the pilot, including what magnitude of change should raise an alert
  • Start the production-grade data access request in the first week of the pilot, since approval alone takes months in many organizations

4. Trap 2 - the gaps a human quietly filled

4.1 Cause: the pilot owner's labour never appears in a log

When a pilot returns something odd, the owner runs it again. Tweaks the prompt a little. Removes the strange page from the input document. Deletes the obviously wrong sentence from the output before it goes into the deck. None of these actions are perceived as part of the work, so none of them are recorded anywhere. And in production, that person is not there.

This inflates apparent performance dramatically. If the real system performance is 70 percent and the owner silently fills the remaining 30 percent, the demonstration shows 100 percent. Then, at the transition, it becomes clear that someone has to fill that 30 percent at production volume, and the headcount savings that justified the project evaporate. The learning gap identified in the MIT NANDA report cited earlier, the observation that tools do not retain feedback or adapt to context, describes the same phenomenon from the other side. What a human patched every single time, the system never learned.

4.2 Warning signs

  • The pilot owner eyeballs the results every day, and that time appears in no cost model
  • Re-run counts and prompt edit counts are not being recorded
  • The phrase we just need to adjust the prompt slightly recurs in every meeting
  • The demonstration deck contains not a single failure case
  • The pilot effectively stopped during a period when the owner was away

4.3 Alternatives

  • Promote human intervention to a measured quantity: log intervention rate, re-run rate and characters edited
  • Measure a separate hands-off baseline. Performance with nobody touching it is the real performance
  • Where intervention is genuinely required, do not hide it. Make it an explicit exception path in the design. Humans in the loop are not the problem; unrecorded humans are
  • Include reviewer time in the fully loaded cost per processed item
  • Do not discard intervention records. Feed them back into rules, validation logic and evaluation data

5. Trap 3 - the evaluation axis moves

5.1 Cause: a demo is judged on impression, production on a contract

The pass criterion for most pilots is that it looks fine. It is rarely written down that way, but what actually decides the outcome is the impression in the room. Production criteria are different: response latency targets, monthly availability, an acceptable error rate. These are promises agreed as numbers. The goalposts do not move so much as the entire axis of evaluation is swapped out.

Latency in particular is almost never validated in a pilot. Nobody complains about waiting a few seconds in a demonstration, but real users abandon conversational responses that take more than a couple of seconds. And what matters in production is not the average but the tail. An average of one second with a p99 of twenty seconds means one user in a hundred experiences the worst case every time. Layer on the fact that autoscaling can take tens of seconds to respond during a traffic spike because of cold starts and model weight loading, and the system becomes slowest exactly when it is busiest.

5.2 Warning signs

  • The pilot has no numeric pass or fail criterion written down
  • The grader is the developer who built the system
  • The evaluation sample is twenty or thirty items and contains no hard cases
  • The team has never agreed on what counts as a failure
  • Latency and availability targets do not appear in any document

5.3 Alternatives

  • Write the operational targets before the pilot starts. Fix p95 latency, availability and acceptable error rate as numbers, then run the pilot under those constraints
  • Build a frozen labelled dataset and version it. Evaluations run on a different sample each time cannot be compared
  • Keep offline quality evaluation and online operational metrics separate rather than blending them into one score
  • Grade failures by severity: critical, major, minor. Not every error carries the same weight
  • Appoint the grader from the department that will actually use the system, not from the build team

6. Trap 4 - the cost structure inverts

6.1 Cause: pilots count inference, production counts everything

Pilot costs are usually startlingly low. Processing a few hundred items can cost less than a team lunch. The forecast built from that number is tokens per item multiplied by expected item count, and that forecast is almost always lower than the eventual invoice, because three things are missing from it.

The first is non-linear growth. Failed calls and retries, contexts that accumulate as conversations lengthen, and outputs that grow as users request more detail all multiply together. Output tokens are typically priced higher than input tokens, so longer responses hurt more than they appear to. The second is the categories the pilot never counted: human review labour, the cost of running evaluation and regression suites, log storage and observability tooling, and headroom provisioned against peak load. The third is the cost of designing for peak. Sizing for maximum load rather than average load lowers utilization and raises effective unit cost.

The result inverts the expectation that unit costs fall with scale. A prototype whose monthly bill was a rounding error turning into a five-figure monthly bill at real user volume is a commonly reported pattern in the industry. The problem is not the amount itself. The problem is that the amount was not predicted at the point the budget was approved.

6.2 Warning signs

  • The cost estimate is a single line of multiplication
  • Failed calls and retries are not tracked separately
  • Nobody knows the cache hit rate
  • Peak traffic assumptions and concurrency limits appear in no document
  • There has been no analysis of what happens if the vendor changes its pricing policy

6.3 Alternatives

  • Define unit economics. Fully loaded cost per item equals inference plus retries plus review labour plus observability plus infrastructure, compared against the cost of the alternative such as the current manual process or outsourcing
  • Design the cost ceiling first. Attach quotas, rate limits and threshold alerts before increasing traffic, not after
  • Test caching, model routing and output length limits during the pilot. Bolting them on later destabilises quality
  • Segment the workload by difficulty to identify work that can safely run on cheaper models
  • Plan capacity for peak and prepare a degraded mode that responds at lower quality rather than not responding at all

7. Trap 5 - nobody owns the bad output

7.1 Cause: a pilot has no victims and production does

When a pilot produces something wrong, the owner laughs and moves on. When production produces the same output, it reaches a customer, it leaves a record, and it can become a financial loss or a regulatory issue. Yet a large share of organizations attempt the transition without ever naming an owner for that situation. When an incident occurs, IT and the business unit look at each other.

Accountability structure connects directly to regulation. The EU AI Act applies obligations for high-risk AI systems from 2 August 2026 (as of July 2026; proposals to delay parts of the timetable have been discussed, and the current status should be checked independently). Deployers are required to keep logs generated by the system for at least six months under Article 26, and Article 14 requires that high-risk systems be designed so that natural persons can effectively oversee them. Organizations outside the EU still need to assess applicability if they serve European customers. The detail of any single article matters less than the direction: who is accountable, what is recorded, and where a human intervenes are becoming the common questions of AI regulation.

7.2 Warning signs

  • There is no documented owner for the case where a wrong output reaches a customer
  • No rollback procedure or manual fallback path has been prepared
  • Incident severity tiers and escalation routes are undefined
  • The retention period and access controls for input and output logs have not been decided
  • Legal and information security review has not started as the transition approaches

7.3 Alternatives

  • Name three kinds of owner: a product owner for what ships, a technical owner for how it runs, and a risk owner for when it gets switched off
  • Build the kill switch and manual fallback first. A system that cannot be switched off is better left switched off
  • Write an incident runbook and actually rehearse it. A document nobody has practised will not be read on the day
  • Retain inputs, outputs and the basis for decisions in an auditable form, with retention aligned to regulatory and contractual requirements
  • Disclose AI involvement to affected users and open a route to contest a decision

8. Trap 6 - integration cost was underestimated

8.1 Cause: pilots run in isolation, production runs in the middle of everything

A pilot typically runs on CSV files and notebooks, a personal account and a temporary API key. Production is different. Single sign-on, least-privilege access models, audit logging, network segregation policy, change management, deployment pipelines, monitoring, backup and disaster recovery all attach themselves to the system. Individually these are unremarkable. Together they take longer than building the pilot did.

Technical integration is not even the harder half. Organizational integration is. Changing one field on an agent's screen brings with it business unit approval, user training, revised operating procedures and adjusted performance metrics. This is the context in which the MIT NANDA report identified workflow integration rather than infrastructure, regulation or talent as the barrier. The reason individual productivity gains from chatbots fail to become organizational results is that the tool lives outside the existing workflow rather than inside it.

8.2 Warning signs

  • The pilot ran on a personal account and personal key rather than a managed one
  • The permission model was effectively read everything
  • The teams that own the systems to be integrated never attended a single meeting
  • Nobody on the team knows where change management and deployment approvals go
  • User training and procedure revision do not appear on the schedule

8.3 Alternatives

  • Hold integration interviews in the first week of the pilot, meeting every owner of every system to be connected and hearing the constraints up front
  • Design with least privilege and keep access audit logs from day one. Reducing permissions later is far harder than granting them narrowly
  • Do not build a new screen. Attach the capability to the screen users already work in. Separate destinations lose adoption
  • Where feasible, run the pilot on production infrastructure. Environment differences account for a large share of transition cost
  • Put training, manuals and procedure revision on the project plan as deliverables, not as incidental work

9. The transition gate - what to verify when a pilot ends

Invert the six traps and a checklist appears. One principle governs it: only include items that can be verified with an artefact rather than a statement in a meeting. We reviewed it is not evidence. The table below can be used directly as the table of contents for a pilot closing report.

A gate is a judgement tool, not a pass filter. This does not mean all eleven items must clear before anything can go live. There is an enormous difference between crossing into production with the unmet items explicitly recorded and crossing without knowing what they are. The first is manageable debt. The second is an incident waiting for a date.

10. So how should a proof of concept be designed differently

10.1 Design in reverse

The single most effective change is to invert the order. Write the production requirements first, then build the pilot as a scaled-down version of those requirements. If the latency target is two seconds, run the pilot inside two seconds. If audit logs will be required, have the pilot write audit logs. Pilot success rates fall when you do this, but the meaning of a pilot success changes: passing now implies that production is reachable.

10.2 Aim at refutation rather than success

Change the purpose of a pilot from demonstrating that it works to finding the condition under which it breaks fastest, and the design changes with it. Start with the messiest department rather than the cleanest data, and show it to the most sceptical users rather than the most cooperative ones. If two months later the conclusion is that it does not work under these conditions, that pilot did not fail. It succeeded at the lowest possible price. The cost of discovering the same thing after the transition grows by orders of magnitude.

10.3 Decide the ending at the beginning

  • Fix an end date. A pilot that extends indefinitely easily becomes a device for never reaching a conclusion
  • Agree the stop criteria at the same time, stating which results would cause the effort to be abandoned
  • Define the deliverable as a transition gate report rather than a demonstration
  • Prefer the smallest possible real production deployment over running a second pilot
  • When narrowing scope, narrow the type of work rather than the number of users. Running one work type end to end teaches far more

11. Reading this in a Korean enterprise context

Domestic data is limited, but one survey is worth noting. Research by the Korea Chamber of Commerce and Industry with the Korea Institute for Industrial Economics and Trade, covering IT and planning executives at 500 Korean firms and published in August 2024, found that while 78.4 percent of firms said AI was necessary, actual utilisation was 30.6 percent. Manufacturing sat at 23.8 percent against 53 percent for services, and utilisation ran 48.8 percent at large enterprises, 30.1 percent at mid-sized firms and 28.7 percent at small and medium enterprises. The leading reasons for hesitation were insufficient technical and IT infrastructure at 34.6 percent and cost burden at 23.1 percent. The survey dates from 2024, so current figures may differ.

One structural factor is specific to the region. Pilots and main projects are frequently separated contractually. When a proof of concept is procured as its own engagement and the production build then goes out to a fresh competitive tender, the team running the pilot has little incentive to design for a transition it may not be part of. The operational knowledge earned during the pilot evaporates at the contract boundary. One way to mitigate this is to name the transition gate report itself as a contractual deliverable at procurement. If the sampling rationale, the hands-off performance figures, the unit economics estimate and the list of integration constraints are all part of what is handed over, the knowledge survives even when the supplier changes.

Segregated network environments and personal data handling requirements raise transition difficulty further. A pilot validated against an external API may need to become an on-premises or closed-network model serving deployment in production, at which point performance, cost and latency all change. Organizations in that position generally move faster by validating the pilot in the deployment form production will actually use. Proving it on a cloud API and then porting it to a closed network is, in practice, building the thing twice.

Conclusion: start by changing the success criteria of the next pilot

Studies built on different methods keep pointing at the same place. The problem is not that the technology fails to work. Projects stall in bulk after someone confirms it works, while trying to turn that into a system that runs every day. The S&P Global finding that the average organization scrapped 46 percent of its proofs of concept before production is a measure of how thick that stretch is.

The six traps described here are not independent of one another. Starting with curated data meant a human had to fill gaps; because a human filled them, the evaluation was inflated; the cost estimate was built on that inflated performance; and that estimate ignored integration cost and accountability structure. It is a chain descending from a single design decision. Which is why changing the purpose of the pilot works better than fixing the six items one at a time.

Three things are actionable tomorrow. First, in any pilot currently running, start counting how often a human touches the output. That single number removes most of the illusion. Second, for any pilot not yet started, write the operational service level objectives first. Third, replace the table of contents of the pilot closing report, changing it from a demonstration script to the eleven transition gate items. As of July 2026, the bottleneck in AI adoption is not model capability. The bottleneck is an organization's ability to swap the question from can it work to does it work every day.
