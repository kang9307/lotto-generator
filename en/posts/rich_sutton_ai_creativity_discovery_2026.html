Rich Sutton - AI Creativity, Discovery, and the Limits of Supervised Learning 2026

Where Imitation Cannot Reach - Experience and Exploration as the Engine of Real Discovery

Jun 12, 2026 Tech

Introduction: The Deep Gap Between Imitation and Discovery

Today's artificial intelligence is astonishingly capable. It writes code, summarizes papers, composes poetry, and solves exam questions. Yet most of this capability shares one thing in common: it is imitating what humans have already done. The reinforcement learning pioneer Richard S. Sutton stops precisely here and asks: by merely imitating human-made data, can AI ever discover what humans do not yet know?

In 2026, in a video recorded for a "Science for AI" workshop, Sutton explained the sense in which generative AI trained by supervised learning is limited when it comes to creativity and discovery. His thesis is simple but weighty: imitation alone cannot reach real discovery. Genuine novelty comes not from copying answers but from experience - interacting with the world and going through trial and error.

This article is not a transcription of Sutton's talk. It works through who he is and why his words carry weight; how supervised and reinforcement learning differ; why imitation has a ceiling; what role experience, exploration, and reward play in discovery; and what this whole debate means in the LLM era - fact-first, yet without losing the philosophical tension underneath.

1. Who Is Rich Sutton

It would not be an exaggeration to call Rich Sutton an architect of modern reinforcement learning. He studied psychology at Stanford, then earned a PhD in computer science at the University of Massachusetts under Andrew Barto. The fact that he began in psychology is telling: at the root of his work lay, from the very start, the question of how animals and people actually learn.

He pioneered temporal-difference learning and policy gradient methods - techniques that became the foundation of nearly every reinforcement learning algorithm in use today. The textbook he wrote with Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction, became the standard reference in the field. And in 2024, Sutton and Barto jointly received the Turing Award - often called the Nobel Prize of computing - "for developing the conceptual and algorithmic foundations of reinforcement learning."

Today he is a professor at the University of Alberta and Chief Scientific Advisor at the Alberta Machine Intelligence Institute, and he researches continual learning and artificial general intelligence (AGI) at Keen Technologies, a company founded by game developer John Carmack. In short, he is a person who has pursued the idea that AI should learn from experience for his entire career. So when he points out the limits of supervised learning, it is not a reaction against a trend but the continuation of a decades-long, consistent view.

2. The Bitter Lesson - A Prophecy He Left Years Ago

To understand Sutton's current argument, you first need his short 2019 essay, The Bitter Lesson. It identified a single pattern running through the history of AI research.

The pattern is this. Researchers often try to inscribe their own human knowledge and intuition directly into systems - the theory of chess openings, the grammar of language, the rules of vision. In the short run this works. But in the long run, what almost always wins is the general method that minimizes human knowledge and instead learns for itself through massive computation and data. Chess, Go, speech recognition, computer vision - all walked the same road.

The biggest lesson is that general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin.

The reason it is "bitter" is that the clever human insight researchers labored to insert ends up powerless before scalable learning. There is an important point here. Sutton's recent critique does not contradict the Bitter Lesson; it is its extension. He sees today's AI field quoting the Bitter Lesson while doing roughly the opposite of what it prescribes. That is, fitting models to vast human-made data is, in another form, yet another way of leaning on human knowledge.

3. Supervised vs. Reinforcement Learning - A Fundamental Difference

Now let us draw the core concepts sharply. Everything in Sutton's argument stands on this difference.

3.1 Supervised Learning - Copying the Answer

Supervised learning shows the system a large number of labeled examples and teaches it to reproduce those answers. Show it millions of cat photos tagged "cat," and the model recognizes cats in new photos too. Today's large language models are likewise, in essence, trained to predict the "next word" of human-written text. This is imitation at enormous scale.

3.2 Reinforcement Learning - Trying and Learning from Reward

In reinforcement learning, by contrast, no answer is given. In Sutton's framing, the system is not told "take this action" but is given only a reward to pursue. It must try actions itself and discover which ones bring the greatest reward. With no answer, there is nothing to copy. The only teacher is the outcome obtained from interacting with the world.

3.3 Imitation Is Unnatural

Sutton goes further and points out that pure imitation, as in supervised learning, is actually a rare way to learn in nature. Animals and infants do not learn because someone labels every answer for them. They predict, try, fail, and try again. There is no "answer label" in a baby learning to walk - only the experience of falling and the reward of taking one more step. For Sutton, real intelligence grows inside this stream of experience.

4. Why Imitation Has a Ceiling - The Problem of Discovery

This is the central point of the piece. However powerful supervised learning is, it is structurally hard for it to exceed the ceiling of knowledge contained in the data it imitates.

The logic is simple and forceful. Supervised learning learns from human-made data. But whatever humans have not yet figured out exists nowhere in that data. What is not in the data cannot be imitated. So a pure imitation system can quickly catch up to the frontier of knowledge humanity has already reached, yet cannot push itself beyond that frontier on its own.

And yet the true breakthroughs that built the history of science lay mostly beyond that frontier. A new mathematical theorem, an unexpected move in Go, a protein-folding structure no one had ever attempted - such discoveries do not come from neatly summarizing existing data, but from exploring an action space no one had visited. AlphaGo could play the famous "Move 37" - which it could never have made by imitating human game records alone - precisely because it crossed the ceiling of human data through the experience of playing against itself.

Imitation carries us to the edge of human knowledge. But taking one more step past that edge is something only experience can do.

This is exactly where Sutton aims when he speaks of the limits of LLMs. A system that imitates static human data can become a marvelous "summarizer" and "recombiner," but it faces a structural limit in becoming a "discoverer" that produces genuine novelty absent from the data.

5. Experience, Exploration, Reward - The Three Pillars That Enable Discovery

So what does discovery come from? Sutton's answer can be organized into three interlocking concepts.

5.1 Experience - Data from Interacting with the World

Sutton defines experience broadly: experience is the data you get when you interact with the world. Unlike static text written in a book, experience directly carries the trace of causation - "I did this action, and that result followed." This is decisive. Even if human data runs out, experience does not. As long as the system acts, new experience is generated endlessly.

5.2 Exploration - Deliberately Trying the Untried

Exploration is the heart of reinforcement learning. Repeating only the best action so far is safe, but you will never discover a better path. So the system must, at some rate, deliberately try actions it has not yet attempted. Discovery is, in essence, a product of exploration. Imitation has no room for this exploration, because a system that copies answers by definition does not "try something new."

5.3 Reward - A Signal of What Is Good

Reward is a measurable signal of which outcomes are desirable. Crucially, reward can be an actual outcome from the environment rather than a human preference or label - objective signals such as health metrics, test results, or whether an experiment succeeded. Reward gives direction to exploration. In the infinite space of actions, reward is the compass pointing "this way is better." If experience is the raw material, exploration is the act of seeking it, and reward is the criterion for sorting the good material from the bad.

6. The Era of Experience - Sutton and Silver's Manifesto

This vision becomes sharper in the essay Welcome to the Era of Experience, which Sutton wrote with his former student and AlphaGo lead David Silver. The two divide AI's progress into three eras.

Era Source of learning Representative example
Era of Simulation Reinforcement learning in controlled environments Atari, AlphaGo
Era of Human Data Imitation of internet text and human writing Today's large language models
Era of Experience Continuous interaction and feedback with the environment Self-learning agents (the goal)

Their diagnosis is incisive. The AI industry will eventually run out of high-quality human data. And some breakthroughs are simply beyond what humans have figured out at all. If so, the next leap must come not from scraping more static data but from agents that adapt and learn within "a never-ending stream of experiences" over months and years. In Sutton's words, large language models are learning from training data. They are not learning from experience.

This view connects to a larger debate about what actually drives AI progress. The question of whether intelligence will keep growing merely by scaling up computation is explored more deeply in our separate piece, the AI growth slowdown and the limits of compute.

7. Counterarguments and Tension - Can Imitation Really Not Discover?

For balance, we should hear the other side. Sutton's thesis is not decisive for everyone.

First, there is the observation that imitation learning itself causes new abilities to emerge. A sufficiently large language model often shows reasoning, translation, and coding abilities it was never explicitly taught. In the process of compressing and generalizing data, something beyond mere memorization appears. Some take this to argue that "imitation, if deep enough, approaches discovery."

Second, reinforcement and supervised learning may be not opponents but complements. Today's state-of-the-art systems first do imitation learning on human data, then layer reinforcement learning on top using verifiable rewards. Human imitation is an efficient starting point that injects a vast "prior" quickly, and on top of it, experience-based exploration pushes the data ceiling upward. Seen this way, imitation is not something to discard but a stepping stone toward experience.

Yet even these counterarguments do not topple Sutton's core. Whether through emergence or complementarity, the engine that truly crosses the data ceiling still comes from the experience of "trying new actions and observing the results." The issue is not "is imitation useless?" but "is imitation alone enough?" And Sutton's answer is clearly "no."

8. What Is Creativity - A Question That Cuts Across Humans and Machines

This debate ultimately carries us to a deeper question: what is creativity?

From one view, creativity is "a novel combination of existing elements." By this definition, even imitation-based models can be quite creative, because LLMs excel at absorbing vast data and recombining it in unexpected ways. But from another view, creativity is "a new discovery that escapes the existing frame itself" - like Copernicus's heliocentrism, not recombining data but changing the very perspective from which data is seen.

The discovery Sutton speaks of is closer to the latter. And in his view, the latter kind of creativity comes not from recombining static data but only from the experience of touching the world directly and observing its response. Just as you cannot discover a new law of physics without experiment, there is no genuine novelty without trying. For a machine to be truly creative, his implication runs, it must go beyond imitating human thought to become a subject that grapples with the world on its own.

Interestingly, this question also touches another debate - whether AI has consciousness or an inner life. The view that distinguishes fluency from real understanding is treated in our separate piece, Ted Chiang - No, AI Is Not Conscious; read alongside Sutton's discovery debate, the outline of "what AI cannot do" grows sharper.

9. The Outlook for AGI and Scientific Discovery

So what does all this imply for artificial general intelligence (AGI) and the future of science?

If we follow Sutton's vision, the road to AGI is not a linear path of stacking up bigger models and more human data. It is the direction of equipping agents with the ability to learn continually and improve their own way of learning (meta-learning) while interacting ceaselessly with their environment. Sutton goes so far as to say that once an era arrives in which systems hold these abilities, today's LLMs will be remembered as "a momentary fixation" in the history of AI.

On the side of scientific discovery, this opens enormous possibility. If AI can enter a cycle of experience that goes beyond imitating human data - forming hypotheses, designing experiments, observing results, and refining hypotheses again - then AI can become not a mere research assistant but a subject of discovery. New drug candidates, novel materials, unknown mathematical structures - AI might, through exploration, set foot first in territory humans have never reached.

Of course, big challenges accompany this outlook. Exploration in the real world is far costlier and riskier than in simulation, and the question of how to define a "good reward" remains thorny. But the direction is clear. If we want real discovery, AI must move beyond reading and act.

10. Conclusion - From Reading to Acting

I read Rich Sutton's message not as pessimism but as an invitation. He is not saying today's AI is trivial. On the contrary, he is pointing to a far higher place AI could reach. Only, to get there, we must re-imagine AI not as a tool that imitates the human shadow, but as a subject that learns for itself by grappling with the world.

The core compresses into a single sentence: imitation carries us to the edge, but it is experience that carries us beyond it. Supervised learning is a powerful way to absorb quickly what humanity already knows, but discovering what humanity does not know comes only from a stream of experience where trial and error, exploration, and reward interlock.

Finally, three things to take from this debate:

  1. Distinguish imitation from discovery. Just because AI produces impressive output does not mean it has crossed the ceiling of human knowledge into real discovery. They are different kinds of ability.
  2. Recognize the data ceiling. A system trained only on static human data can hardly generate, on its own, more than the knowledge contained in that data.
  3. Watch the direction toward experience. The next leap is likely to come not from more data but from agents that learn by interacting with their environment.
