Obsidian Ecosystem Cracks - Files.md, Logseq, and the Excalidraw Dispute Shape the Future of Note-Taking in 2026

Cracks in the standard, the rise of open alternatives, and what users could actually lose

May 20, 2026 IT/Tech

1. Cracks in the Obsidian Myth

There are clear reasons Obsidian became the de-facto standard for markdown-based personal knowledge management (PKM). First, it keeps user data as plain .md files in a local folder you own. Second, bidirectional links and the graph view are integrated cleanly. Third, the plugin API is open enough that the community has produced thousands of extensions. By 2026 the plugin count crossed 2,000 and the active user base is estimated in the low millions. Even in Korea, the user base grew quickly around the Obsidian forum and Discord channels.

From the very beginning, however, there was a quiet contradiction. User data is fully open (just .md files), while the software itself is closed source. The company has framed this as a balance: "your data is free, the tool is something we maintain professionally." Pricing followed the same logic - personal use is free forever, commercial use costs roughly USD 50 per year, and add-on services like Sync and Publish are paid separately. From the user side the cost is low, and from the company side it looks sustainable.

The problem is that the model rests on a single assumption: that Obsidian will always be there. If the company pivots, raises prices, or is acquired, users can take their markdown files anywhere, but the workflow stack built on top (plugin dependencies, hotkeys, Dataview queries, Excalidraw drawings) does not move so easily. From late 2025 the community quietly began voicing the fear that "Obsidian may eventually go SaaS like the IDE companies did." In 2026 that fear was given concrete shape by two events stacked on top of each other: the appearance of a truly open-source alternative called Files.md, and a licensing dispute around Excalidraw.

2. Files.md - the arrival of a "truly open source" alternative

Files.md is a markdown note editor published on GitHub by Zakirullin. Its key differentiator can be expressed in a single line: "the software itself is MIT-licensed." Obsidian opens up the data; Files.md opens up both data and software. Folder-structure compatibility is intentionally similar to Obsidian, so the same vault folder can be opened by either tool.

Here is a migration scenario this author simulated. Imagine a vault with roughly 500 notes, ten folder levels deep, and around 30 Obsidian plugins in use. Moving to Files.md: first, the body text of all .md files moves with zero loss. Second, the [[wikilink]] syntax is recognized. Third, Files.md has a graph view as well, but the visual polish and interaction depth are simpler than Obsidian's. Fourth, output from Obsidian-only plugins like Dataview, Templater, and Excalidraw appears only as raw text in Files.md.

In short, the strengths of Files.md are licensing freedom, fast startup, and a small footprint, while the weaknesses are the plugin ecosystem and mobile apps. As of May 2026 the official mobile app for Files.md is still beta-grade; users typically self-build for Android/iOS or just run the Tauri desktop at a phone-sized window. It is a reasonable alternative for "simple markdown users," but for "users who came to Obsidian for the rich plugin ecosystem" it is not yet a decisive replacement.

3. The Excalidraw Dispute as a Case Study

Excalidraw is a sketch-style diagramming tool, and the version integrated as an Obsidian plugin became one of the most popular third-party extensions. The structure was layered: the core Excalidraw is MIT, Excalidraw+ (a cloud SaaS) is a separate commercial service, and the Obsidian integration plugin is operated by a different maintainer. In spring 2026 a dispute over licensing, revenue sharing, and trademark use surfaced between the core team and the integration plugin side. Some users experienced slower plugin updates or saw key features move to the SaaS only.

What is the essence here? An open-source maintainer cannot pour unlimited time into a project, the side that incorporates needs to justify monetization, and between the two the user ends up facing "the free feature I used yesterday is now paid." This is not new. HashiCorp's Terraform BSL switch in 2023, MongoDB's SSPL transition, Redis's license change, Elastic's SSPL adoption - all the same pattern: start open source, build user base, form a company, harden the license. Users get the same shock each time but keep trusting the same pattern next time.

The message the Excalidraw incident sends to the note-taking world is clear. Even if the core is MIT, if plugins or add-on services run on a different license, users get shaken once more. When the core itself is closed source like Obsidian, the concern naturally scales up.

5. The Korean User Perspective

A few variables matter especially for Korean note-app users. First, Korean search quality. Korean requires morphological analysis, but Obsidian's built-in search does not perform it. As a result, "natural Korean search" is underwhelming, and users tend to supplement with simple substring matches. Logseq and Joplin share the same limitation. Files.md offers only the most basic search. This is a weak spot for any of them.

Second, sync cost. Obsidian Sync starts at USD 4/month, but for users in Korea who already own NAS, Synology Drive, iCloud, or OneDrive, syncing a markdown folder is essentially free. The perceived value of Obsidian Sync is therefore lower than the global average, which is one reason self-hosting-friendly tools (Logseq, Joplin) are well-liked locally.

Third, community vitality. Obsidian's Korean-language community is well established, and users have built an identity around "this is where I keep knowledge for life," distinct from the Hancom Office and Naver Memo styles. Logseq and Joplin have Korean user groups too, but the scale is roughly a tenth of Obsidian's. Files.md has almost no Korean-language material. So in 2026, for Korean users, "Obsidian is familiar, has lots of material, and sync is offloaded to NAS" remains a sensible combination.

6. Migration Guide (Practical)

Still, if the licensing risk weighs on you and you want to move to Files.md, the safest sequence looks like this:

  1. Back up the entire vault: Before migrating, ZIP the vault folder and keep one copy on an external store (NAS, external drive, cloud). You must be able to roll back instantly.
  2. Set aside the plugin directory: The .obsidian/ plugins, snippets, and workspace are not used by Files.md. Do not move them - store them separately.
  3. Audit Dataview and Excalidraw output: These two plugins generate content dynamically, so Files.md will show only raw code blocks. For important pages, export the rendered result to static markdown first.
  4. Open the folder in Files.md: Launch Files.md and point it at the backed-up vault. Plain markdown files are picked up normally.
  5. Clean up incompatible syntax: Obsidian-only notations like %%comments%%, callouts, and embeds appear as plain text. Convert what you need into standard markdown manually.
  6. Run in parallel for a while: Run Obsidian and Files.md side by side on the same folder for at least two weeks, confirm nothing is broken, then close Obsidian.

Honest list of what you give up: Dataview queries, Templater automation, Excalidraw drawings, Canvas, the Calendar plugin, smooth mobile sync, and a rich theme catalog. If you depend heavily on two or more of these, migration is not recommended.

7. 2026 Note-Tool Selection Guide

By usage pattern, this is what I recommend.

  • Stay on Obsidian: Heavy plugin users who rely daily on Dataview, Templater, or Excalidraw. Or anyone who needs Korean-community resources.
  • Move to Files.md: Users who value open-source philosophy, write almost only plain markdown, and use mobile only occasionally. Users who value light weight and fast launch.
  • Logseq: Users who prefer block-level work and bidirectional links in the Roam-style flow. Heavy daily-journal users.
  • AnyType: Mobile-first users who are comfortable with P2P sync and prefer an object/relation model.
  • Joplin: Users for whom E2EE is mandatory or for whom mobile stability matters most. Users who clip a lot from email to notes.

8. Conclusion and Outlook

The problem of how to compensate open-source maintainers remains unsolved in 2026. The safest pattern a user can adopt is the simple separation: "keep the data free, pick the most comfortable software." The fact that markdown files can be opened anywhere is your largest safety net. On top of that, the choice of editor is a trade-off between licensing conviction and convenience.

My 2027 outlook is threefold. First, Obsidian will hold its standard position for a while - plugin depth, mobile polish, and community size are not caught up in a short period. Second, Files.md, Logseq, and Joplin will strengthen their position as a "licensing safety net" and grow secondary use cases. Third, there is a non-trivial chance of a major note tool announcing a license shift, and when it happens users will experience another crack in the standard. The most important preparation is not picking the right tool but keeping the data standard intact (.md files, simple folder structure).
