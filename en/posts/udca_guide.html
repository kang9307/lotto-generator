What is UDCA: the ingredient and its link to Ursa

UDCA (ursodeoxycholic acid) is a type of bile acid. It is named after the bear (Latin Ursus), whose bile it was first found in, and the drug used today is synthetic. Ursa is the product name Daewoong Pharmaceutical uses for this ingredient in Korea.

  • 1902: recorded in the literature as first identified in polar bear bile
  • 1927: a researcher at Okayama University in Japan (Shoda) isolated and named it from bear bile
  • 1954: a synthesis method was invented
  • 1961: Ursa launched in Korea (per Korean press reports)

Even with the same ingredient, approved indications, dosing and dose forms differ by product, and both over-the-counter and prescription-only products exist. Products in the Ursa line checked on the MFDS drug safety site are shown as both OTC and prescription. Check the package and the product leaflet of the exact product first.

How it works: a summary of the usual explanation

The points below summarize what pharmacology sources commonly explain. They do not mean any particular effect will occur in an individual.

  • Bile acid composition: as hydrophilic UDCA takes a larger share of the bile acid pool, the share of toxic hydrophobic bile acids falls in relative terms.
  • Cholesterol stones: it acts to lower the cholesterol saturation of bile, and cholesterol stones may dissolve slowly, as the usual explanation goes.
  • Bile flow: Korean product information describes it as promoting bile flow in liver disease with cholestasis.

Korean approval and the US label are different

This is the first distinction to make. The phrase gallstone dissolution is an indication on the US FDA label; it does not appear in the efficacy wording of the Korean Ursa product information checked for this update.

Gallstone dissolution: who it is for and its limits

The US FDA label for ursodiol capsules states the following.

  • Target: radiolucent, non-calcified gallbladder stones smaller than 20 mm in patients who cannot undergo surgery
  • Complete dissolution: about 30% in unselected patients over up to 2 years, and 81% for stones up to 5 mm
  • Recurrence: up to 50% within 5 years even after successful dissolution
  • Duration: safety beyond 24 months has not been established
  • Use in Korea: the approved uses and treatment length of Korean products are decided by the treating doctor

Liver disease, mainly PBC

  • PBC indication: an indication on the US FDA label (URSO 250 and URSO Forte), and AASLD 2018 guidance recommends UDCA as first-line therapy.
  • Limits on survival evidence: the Cochrane review (CD000551) concluded that liver biochemistry and histological progression improved but improvements in total mortality, liver transplantation, itching and fatigue were not shown.
  • Response varies: some patients respond insufficiently, so the AASLD guidance also covers second-line therapy. A specialist decides the plan.
  • Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC): a Cochrane review found liver tests improved but death, transplantation and histological worsening were not significantly reduced, so it neither supports nor refutes benefit.
  • Other cholestatic diseases: LiverTox states that efficacy outside PBC and gallstones is not fully established.

What to check before taking it

  • Product leaflet first: even the same ingredient differs in indications and dosing by product, so check the package and leaflet.
  • Do not calculate doses yourself: per-kilogram doses are label figures for specific indications, and the actual dose is set by a doctor or pharmacist.
  • How to take it: the US PBC label says to take it in divided doses with food (quoted from approval). For Korean products, follow the product leaflet.
  • No self-adjustment: do not stop or increase it because symptoms improved or worsened; consult first.
  • Other medicines and supplements: tell your doctor or pharmacist what you take.

Tell a clinician immediately about the following instead of judging them yourself.

  • Severe diarrhea with signs of dehydration
  • Worsening jaundice
  • Severe abdominal pain
  • Allergic reactions such as hives or difficulty breathing

Interactions and special situations

  • Drugs that reduce absorption: the US PBC label says to avoid combining it with bile acid sequestrants (cholestyramine, colestipol) and aluminum-containing antacids.
  • Drugs that may reduce effect: the same label says to avoid estrogens, oral contraceptives and clofibrate. If you take any, discuss it with a doctor or pharmacist.
  • Pregnancy: the US label describes available data as showing no increased risk of major birth defects, but wording on Korean products differs by product, so do not take it on your own during pregnancy or if pregnancy is possible; consult first.
  • Breastfeeding: the US label says it is present in breast milk with no reported adverse effects in infants. Whether to take it is decided with a doctor.
  • Children: this article gives no pediatric dose. Use in children is for specialists to decide.

Checking response and monitoring

  • PBC: the US label says to check liver function monthly for the first 3 months after starting and every 6 months after that (quoted from approval).
  • Gallstones: the type and interval of follow-up tests such as ultrasound are set by the treating doctor.
  • Records: bringing test results and any symptom changes to visits helps the doctor decide.

Daily-life management

  • Self-management: the Korean patient medication guide stresses thorough self-management including diet, exercise, not smoking and abstaining from alcohol.
  • Diet and alcohol: instructions differ by condition, so follow your treating doctor's guidance.
  • Supplements: before adding a product said to be good for the liver or gallbladder, consult a doctor or pharmacist.

Research-stage topics: not clinical recommendations

  • NAFLD/NASH: the AASLD 2023 guidance states UDCA failed to show histological improvement in randomized trials in NASH and advises against using it as a treatment.
  • Preventing liver injury: use to prevent liver damage from chemotherapy, anti-tuberculosis drugs or alcohol is not established and may be off-label.
  • Other research: studies of other indications, such as neurodegenerative disease, could not be verified in this update and are not covered.

Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

Q1: Which gallstones is UDCA used for?

A: The US label targets radiolucent, non-calcified gallbladder stones smaller than 20 mm in patients who cannot undergo surgery. Complete dissolution was about 30% in unselected patients over up to 2 years. Approved uses of Korean products differ by product.

Q2: Is UDCA also used for liver disease?

A: PBC is a US-labeled indication and AASLD 2018 guidance recommends it first-line. The Cochrane review, however, did not show reduced total mortality or transplantation.

Q3: What are the common side effects?

A: On the US PBC label, abdominal discomfort, abdominal pain, alopecia, diarrhea, nausea, pruritus and rash occurred in 1% or more. Tell a clinician immediately about severe symptoms.

Q4: How long should it be taken?

A: A doctor decides. The US gallstone label says safety beyond 24 months has not been established. Do not stop or increase it on your own.

Q5: Can I drink alcohol while taking it?

A: The medication guide stresses self-management including abstaining from alcohol. If you have a diagnosed condition, ask your treating doctor.

Conclusion

Ursa (UDCA) is a drug whose Korean approved uses differ from its overseas label indications, and whose strength of evidence varies by disease. Gallstone dissolution has demanding conditions and a modest complete-dissolution rate, and in PBC guidance recommends it first-line while the survival evidence remains debated.

Whether to take it, the dose and the duration must be set by a doctor or pharmacist based on your diagnosis and the product leaflet, not by this article. This article is general information and does not replace diagnosis or treatment.

Do not obtain prescription-only drugs on your own. See a clinician if you have symptoms, and tell one immediately if anything goes wrong while taking it.
