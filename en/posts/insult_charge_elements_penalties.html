When Insult Becomes a Crime in South Korea - Article 311 Elements, Penalties and Procedure

Where publicity, identifiability and contemptuous expression divide, and what to know before filing

2026-09-01 Law & Legal

Being offended and the criminal law applying are not in the same place. Start from the two pressed together and the reading usually goes wrong in one of two directions. Something that should not be let go is let go, or a matter that never met what the statute asks is carried to the door of an investigating authority. Neither is cheap.

In South Korea the provision on insult is a single sentence. Inside it sit three requirements, and where real cases divide is almost always one of the three. What follows starts from the wording, sets out what each requirement asks, where matters tilt toward not being made out, and how the procedure runs, as of September 2026. It does not tell you the outcome of any particular case.

This article is general information on Article 311 of the Criminal Act of the Republic of Korea and is not legal advice. It describes Korean law only. Whether the offense is made out turns not on the words alone but on concrete facts such as the circumstances of the statement, the relationship, the place and the surrounding context, and the actual determination belongs to the investigating authorities and the courts. For your own matter, consult a lawyer or a body such as the Korea Legal Aid Corporation (132 within Korea). The account is current as of September 2026; statutes and case law change.

What the Statute Actually Requires

Article 311 of the Korean Criminal Act reads as follows. A person who publicly insults another shall be punished by imprisonment with or without labor for not more than one year, or by a fine not exceeding KRW 2 million. One sentence carries the requirements and the penalty range, and the statute says no more. Most of what is argued over comes not from the text but from the case law reading it.

Broken up, the sentence shows three requirements. Publicly is one, another is two, insults is three. In practice these are called publicity, identifiability and contemptuous expression. All three must be met for the elements to be satisfied, and if one is missing the provision does not attach however clear the rest may be.

  • Publicly — was it in a state where unspecified or many persons could perceive it? The point is that the number of listeners is not the test
  • Another — can the target be recognized? That does not mean a name has to appear
  • Insults — was the expression capable of lowering social evaluation? A different measure from how unpleasant it felt

The Constitutional Court of Korea examined this provision for vagueness and for infringement of freedom of expression and held that it does not violate the Constitution (Constitutional Court, 27 Jun 2013, 2012Hun-Ba37). Among the grounds it cited were that punishment requires a complaint, that the upper limit of the penalty is comparatively low, and that Article 20 on justifiable acts applies. All three return below.

Where It Divides from Defamation

What separates the two is not severity but whether a fact was alleged. The Supreme Court has settled on reading insult as expressing an abstract judgment or a feeling of contempt capable of lowering a person's social evaluation, without alleging any fact (Supreme Court, 2 Feb 2023, 2022Do4719). A quiet sentence carrying a concrete fact goes toward defamation; a heated expression carrying no fact goes toward Article 311.

The larger difference is the provision on justification. Article 310 states that where an act under Article 307(1) concerns a true fact and is solely for the public interest, it is not punishable. Because the text points only to Article 307(1), it does not apply to insult as such. What stands in its place is Article 20, and its requirements are not the same as those of Article 310.

The rows worth noticing are the last two. Article 70 of the Network Act covers defamation only and does not cover insult. Being online does not bring an aggravated provision into play. The defamation side is set out separately in Online Defamation Response Guide.

Publicity - Not a Head Count

Publicity means a state in which unspecified or many persons could perceive it. The most frequent misreading is arithmetic. Ten people present so it holds, one person so it does not, is not how the case law works.

The Supreme Court took this on directly in a 2020 en banc decision. It stated that it maintains the doctrine that even where a fact is alleged individually to a small number, publicity is satisfied where there is a possibility that the listener will pass it on to unspecified or many persons, and it set out two brakes alongside (Supreme Court en banc, 19 Nov 2020, 2020Do5813). One is that speaking only to specific few persons can be a strong circumstance negating publicity, so that in such a setting strict proof by the prosecutor is required. The other is that wilful negligence is required: the inner acceptance of that risk must also be established.

This reasoning is not reserved to defamation. The Supreme Court held that the same doctrine applies to publicity under the insult provision (Supreme Court, 16 Jun 2022, 2021Do15122).

Words Exchanged Between Two People

That the listener was one person does not settle the outcome, but neither does it drop out of the analysis. The 2021Do15122 decision carries the sense that publicity may be negated where the listener stands in a privately close relationship such as spouse, relative or friend, so that confidentiality is expected. Conversely, in 2020Do5813 the fact that the listener was a relative of the victim did not by itself negate the possibility of dissemination. What is looked at is not the label on the relationship but how far the words could have travelled from that spot.

Rooms With Several People and Group Chats

A board anyone can read rarely turns on publicity. Disputes arise in between, in places like a group chat with a fixed membership. The method the case law gives is not a checklist but an overall assessment. Examine the circumstances and situation of the statement, its content and manner, the intent, the attitudes, relationships and positions of those involved, then judge the possibility of dissemination objectively: that is the summary in 2020Do5813.

  • The number of listeners is one circumstance. Few tilts toward negation, but it does not end there
  • To find publicity through dissemination, the actor's awareness and acceptance must also be established

Identifiability - The Target Must Be Recognizable

The offense is made out by expressing contempt toward a specific person or an entity holding personality, so the victim must be identified: that is the Supreme Court's starting point (Supreme Court, 27 Mar 2014, 2011Do15631). It does not mean a name has to be written. If the surrounding circumstances taken together make clear who is meant, identification can be established.

The Constitutional Court held that where only an internet ID is known and, even taking the surrounding circumstances together, it is hard to tell that the holder of that ID is the complainant and there is nothing from which to infer it, the victim cannot be regarded as identified for defamation or insult (Constitutional Court, 26 Jun 2008, 2007Hun-Ma461). Turned around, even a bare ID changes the outcome if the surroundings make the person recognizable. The axis is not the form of the label but whether recognition is possible.

Expression aimed at a group has a clear rule and a clear exception. The 2011Do15631 decision holds that where criticism by group designation is diluted by the time it reaches individual members and does not rise to a level affecting each member's social evaluation, the principle is that insult against individual members is not made out, while it may exceptionally be made out where the criticism is not so diluted. The number of members, the character of the group and the victim's position within it enter the assessment.

  • Identification can hold without a name, and can fail with an ID attached. Both are common
  • The larger the group, the further the individual claim recedes; in a small group individuals surface without names
  • Identifiability looks not at the speaker's intent but at whether a third party could recognize the target

Contempt and Rudeness Are Not the Same

The third requirement is the most misread. What the statute asks for is expression capable of lowering social evaluation, not expression that made the listener uncomfortable. The two do not always overlap.

The Supreme Court held that where an expression is not capable of lowering the social evaluation of the other party's personal worth, it cannot be treated as meeting the elements of insult even if it was put in a somewhat rude manner (Supreme Court, 29 Nov 2018, 2017Do2661). The case concerned a statement made before some 140 labour and management participants, and the size of the audience did not translate straight into liability. Even where publicity is satisfied, an empty expression requirement returns the outcome to the same place.

There is an extension in the opposite direction. The 2022Do4719 decision held that insult may be made out even where only non-verbal, visual means are used, if what is conveyed is an abstract judgment or a feeling of contempt capable of lowering social evaluation. This requirement looks at what was conveyed, not the form of the words.

Expressions whose meaning is not fixed are common as well. The Supreme Court has said that for ambiguous expressions or newly coined terms the meaning is settled first, taking account of motive, intent and the context in which the words sat, and only then judged (Supreme Court, 15 Dec 2022, 2017Do19229). The same decision stated that where matters of public concern and personality rights collide, the two values must be weighed against each other.

  • The measure is not was it unpleasant but was it capable of lowering social evaluation
  • That is also why this article lists no sample expressions. Turned into a list, context is erased, and a list without context gives false confidence in either direction

Where It Does Not Hold - Opinion and Justifiable Acts

Meeting all three requirements is not the end. Conduct that satisfies the elements is still not punished where illegality is precluded. In insult cases that passage is Article 20 of the Criminal Act.

Opinion or Contempt

There is no clean line between criticism and insult. The way the case law stands in for that line is to look at where the expression sits. It separates whether personal attack looks like the purpose itself from whether the words were used in part while setting out a judgment on some matter.

What Article 20 Does

Article 20 provides that an act performed in accordance with statute, an act in the course of duty, or any other act which does not violate ordinary social rules, is not punishable. The Supreme Court has held that even a piece of writing carrying an opinion that includes contemptuous expression may exceptionally have its illegality precluded under Article 20 where, judged by the sound social conventions of the time, it can be regarded as not violating ordinary social rules (Supreme Court, 28 Nov 2003, 2003Do3972).

How that works shows more concretely in a later decision. The Supreme Court held that even where a piece of writing contains contemptuous expression, illegality may be precluded if the author, on the premise of objectively tenable facts, sets out an opinion and the contemptuous words appear only in part while stressing that the opinion is sound (Supreme Court, 25 Mar 2021, 2017Do17643). For short posts on internet boards it also indicated that the overall flow with the surrounding opinions, whether the words target the victim's conduct, and whether excessive malice appears, are considered together.

This passage is neither wide nor narrow. Where an opinion on a matter is carried and its premise is not floating in the air, it may open; where the expression itself was the purpose, it closes.

A Complaint-Required Offense - The Six-Month Clock

Article 312(1) of the Criminal Act provides that the offenses under Articles 308 and 311 may be prosecuted only where a complaint has been filed. Insult is a complaint-required offense. That is a different structure from Article 312(2), which makes the offenses under Articles 307 and 309 unprosecutable against the victim's express wish.

The difference is large. For the latter, investigation and indictment are possible without a complaint and are blocked when the victim states that punishment is not wanted. For the former, the complaint is itself the condition of prosecution, so without one no charge can be brought.

That complaint carries a deadline. Article 230(1) of the Criminal Procedure Act provides that for a complaint-required offense no complaint may be filed once six months have passed from the day the offender became known, with the period running from the day an unavoidable obstacle to filing ceased where one existed.

The starting point is what gets missed. The provision says not the day it happened but the day the offender became known. In anonymous online matters that difference decides outcomes. Six months is shorter than it sounds, and it is not rare for it to pass while a response is still being weighed.

There is a place to step back after filing. Article 232 provides that a complaint may be withdrawn until judgment is pronounced at first instance, and at the same time that a person who has withdrawn cannot file again.

  • Without a complaint no charge is brought. A report or a petition does not substitute for one
  • The deadline is six months from the day the offender became known, not from the day of the incident
  • Withdrawal is possible until judgment at first instance, but only once. It cannot be undone

From Complaint to Disposition

Filing the Complaint

Article 223 of the Criminal Procedure Act provides that a person injured by an offense may file a complaint. The manner is in Article 237: a complaint is made in writing or orally to a prosecutor or a judicial police officer, and where made orally a record is to be prepared.

What the statute does not require and what practice needs are different things. What has to be set down is not feeling but when, where, in what state, and what occurred. Assessment starts once it is visible which facts fill each of the three requirements.

Investigation and Disposition

Once filed, investigation proceeds and the judicial police officer decides whether to refer the case. The prosecutor then either brings a charge or issues a non-prosecution disposition. Non-prosecution itself has kinds — no suspicion, no offense established, no right to prosecute, suspension of indictment — so non-prosecution is not a finding that nothing happened.

On timing the statute fixes one thing. Article 257 provides that a prosecutor investigating on a complaint or accusation shall complete the investigation and decide whether to bring a charge within three months of receiving it. Real cases do not always end inside that, and what follows from exceeding it is left to the later section, since no basis for it was verified.

What Happens Inside the Statutory Range

A penalty range is a ceiling. Up to one year of imprisonment with or without labor, or a fine up to KRW 2 million, means a sentence may be set within that band, not that such a sentence is expected. One of the grounds the Constitutional Court gave for upholding the provision was that the upper limit is comparatively low (Constitutional Court, 27 Jun 2013, 2012Hun-Ba37).

One expectation deserves care: that filing means the other side will be punished in some form. A prosecutor's disposition includes several branches of non-prosecution besides a charge, and even where a charge is brought the court's judgment remains. A complaint is the start of a procedure, not a conclusion.

  • No suspicion and suspension of indictment mean entirely different things though both are non-prosecution
  • Sentencing is a separate exercise into which circumstances, degree of harm and remedy all enter, not the words alone

Civil Claims Run on a Separate Track

Criminal proceedings and a damages claim are different procedures with different purposes. The criminal side decides whether the state imposes punishment; the civil side decides who covers the loss that occurred.

The provisions are separate too. Article 750 of the Civil Act provides that a person who causes loss to another by an unlawful act done intentionally or negligently is bound to make compensation, and Article 751(1) provides that a person who has injured the body, liberty or reputation of another, or inflicted mental anguish, is bound to make compensation for non-pecuniary loss as well. That is the basis on which solatium is discussed.

Separate means both can proceed and also that one outcome does not automatically set the other. To what extent a criminal determination bears on the civil side is not set out here, as no basis for it was verified.

  • A criminal complaint and a civil claim are not the same procedure. One does not substitute for the other
  • A settlement in the criminal process sometimes resolves the civil part alongside. What is being waived has to be read in the document
  • Withdrawal of a complaint cannot be undone (Criminal Procedure Act art. 232(2)). That bites when settlement and withdrawal are decided in the same sitting

The Risk That Comes Back to the Complainant

A complaint does not run in one direction only. Article 156 of the Criminal Act punishes a person who, with intent to have another subjected to criminal or disciplinary punishment, reports a false fact to a public office or public official, by imprisonment for up to ten years or a fine of up to KRW 15 million. Far heavier than insult.

The wording has to be read as it stands. What Article 156 requires is reporting a false fact, and the outcome of non-prosecution does not by itself satisfy that. Setting down what actually happened and having it come out differently on legal assessment sits, under the text, in a different place from inventing something that did not occur. Where the line between them falls is again for the investigating authorities and the courts.

What happens more often is the other side of it. Once one party files, the structure invites the other to file as well. Where words went both ways, it is possible for both sides to meet the requirements.

  • False accusation requires reporting a false fact. Non-prosecution as an outcome does not amount to it
  • Still, the gap in penalty weight is large. That is why inflating the facts is dangerous
  • This section is not saying do not file. Filing is a proper right; what has to be known before starting is which directions the procedure opens in

What Changes Online

As seen above, Article 70 of the Network Act covers defamation only. So Article 311 of the Criminal Act applies as it stands to insult committed online. The provision does not change, which is not to say the shape of the case is the same. Three things shift.

First, the record. Words offline survive only in memory; words online survive with a form. This works both ways. It becomes material supporting the requirements, and it also brings out what the complaining side wrote.

Second, how publicity attaches. A board anyone can read is hard to dispute, but in a room with a fixed membership the dissemination doctrine attaches exactly as above. Being online does not fill the publicity requirement by itself.

Third, identifiability. Where only handles or IDs exist, whether the target can be recognized becomes its own issue. That is the ground the 2007Hun-Ma461 decision covered. The same problem arises in reverse, so establishing who made the statement sits near the front of the procedure.

  • Records can be deleted. Keeping them generally means capturing the whole screen so that the address, the date and the surrounding exchange are held together
  • Identifying the other party is not something an individual may do at will. It is handled inside the procedure, and this article covers no service-specific method
  • The deadline does not stretch because it happened online. But the starting point of the day the offender became known can attach differently in anonymous matters

Why the Same Words Are Judged Differently

Lists claiming to set out which words amount to insult circulate online. They diverge from actual determinations because the elements do not look at the words alone.

Gather what the decisions cited above ask for in common and a picture appears. Publicity is to be judged after examining circumstances and situation, content and manner, intent, relationships and positions (2020Do5813); the meaning of an expression is to be settled through motive and context before judgment (2017Do19229); and preclusion of illegality is to be weighed against the overall flow, the premised facts and any malice (2017Do17643). The string of words is one ingredient among several.

  • Same words, different setting, and publicity divides
  • Same words, and what stood before and after divides the meaning and the illegality

In Short

From one sentence of Article 311 come three requirements — publicity, identifiability, contemptuous expression — and all three must be met for the elements to be satisfied. Even then, room remains for illegality to be precluded as a justifiable act under Article 20.

The divide from defamation is whether a fact was alleged. Article 70 of the Network Act covers defamation only, so Article 311 applies to online insult too. Because it is complaint-required, no charge is brought without a complaint; that complaint must come within six months of learning who the offender is; and once withdrawn it cannot be filed again.

What remains is not a verdict but an order of work. Set down what happened in the language of the three requirements, check whether the deadline is still open, and hold your own words to the same measure. That answer can only be given by someone reading the record directly.
