Before You Say a Human Beat the AI - The Conditions Missing From a Two-Stone Handicap Game

Handicap as a measuring scale, visits as a budget, and how to read the place where a superhuman system broke

2026-09-05 Trends & Culture

A result travels furthest once it has been compressed into a single sentence, and the first thing that falls off during that compression is the set of conditions it was produced under. A win-loss sentence stripped of its conditions is not information; it is atmosphere.

This article uses one Go match as its material and rebuilds those conditions one at a time. The subject is the exact boundary inside which a system can be called superhuman, and what to do with a sentence that has dropped it. The reading transfers directly to model comparison tables and benchmark scores.

Everything below rests on published match conditions and published academic work. No judgement about any person is offered, and no private matter is discussed. Items that could not be established are marked as such in the body and collected with their reasons in the second-to-last section.

The trigger - a sentence travels and the conditions stay behind

In July 2026, three games of Go were played at two-day intervals. On one side sat a professional player. On the other ran KataGo, an open source Go engine. The human side finished two wins to one, and that result was quickly compressed into a single line: a human beat the AI.

Taken on its own, that line supports almost no conclusion. Adding the phrase two stones helps less than it seems, because it omits who placed them. If the human placed them, the human was the side receiving the handicap; if the engine placed them, the engine was. In the first case the result is an achievement built on the premise that the human is still the weaker side. In the second it would be an entirely different and far larger claim.

There is a bigger gap behind that one. KataGo is not a single fixed opponent. Strength under that one name varies enormously depending on which network file was loaded and how many times the engine was allowed to search per move. Without those two values, the sentence someone beat KataGo sits in a state that is neither true nor false, because what was beaten has not been specified.

The handicap direction is the most important row in that table, and two independent lines of evidence point the same way. The first is that reporting states the player set down two black stones at the start of every game. The second is a public remark he made himself: once he can win consistently at two stones, he would like to make the terms harder, either by giving komi or by playing an even game. Saying you want to make the terms harder means the present terms are the easier ones, and that is consistent only with the human being the side that received the handicap.

Where the sources disagreed with each other

Even among materials describing the same three games, some values do not line up. It is tempting to pick one and move on, but when there is no basis for choosing, the honest move is to record the disagreement itself.

  • The human clock appears both as 5 hours plus 30 second byo-yomi and as a flat 5 hours 30 minutes in a summarised retelling. The original notation reads 5h+30s, which favours the first form, but neither version states how many byo-yomi periods were allowed
  • The organising and sponsoring bodies are named differently from source to source. One describes a co-sponsorship between an economic daily and a backer; another gives a distinct event title. Which of these is the formal name could not be established
  • Whether this counted as an official fixture or a sponsored exhibition is also worded inconsistently. Several outlets used the word official, but whether the games meet the definition of an official fixture under tournament regulations could not be established
  • At least one record compresses the handicap into the phrase two stones for the AI, a shorthand that reads as though the engine was the one receiving stones. Checked against the article text and the player remark, the direction is the one given in the previous section

The last item is the instructive one. Nothing but a change in how a fact is abbreviated can flip its direction in the reader's mind. Most reversals in performance reporting happen exactly this way. They come from compression, not from dishonesty.

What is a handicap stone actually measuring?

The convention that one stone equals one rank

A handicap compensates a difference in strength on the board itself. The weaker side places stones before play begins and proceeds from that improved position. By convention, one rank of difference is worth one stone. Three ranks means three stones, five ranks means five. When a single rank separates the players, no stone is placed; instead the stronger side takes White and forgoes the compensation points.

What deserves attention here is that this scale is discrete. There is nothing between one stone and two. That makes the number of handicap stones less like a precision ruler and more like a staircase with wide steps. Saying two stones does not assert that the strength gap is exactly two ranks; it asserts that someone set the terms at roughly two steps in the hope that the contest would be close.

Where do two stones go?

With two stones, Black takes the star points at the upper right and the lower left, two positions facing each other diagonally. The arrangement is conventional and exists so that the advantage is spread across the board rather than concentrated in one corner. Move the stones elsewhere and the same nominal two stones produce a different practical balance. Komi in handicap games is normally set to zero or half a point, and the 0.5 used in these games matches that convention.

StonesConventional meaningPlacement
None, with komiSame rank. Even gameColours drawn, komi to White
None, without komiOne rank apartWeaker side takes Black, no compensation
TwoRoughly two ranks apartUpper right and lower left star points
ThreeRoughly three ranks apartThree star points
NineThe largest handicap in common useAll nine star points

What happens when that scale is laid between a human and a machine

The handicap scale was built on assumptions about the distribution of human strength. There are ranks, the gap between adjacent ranks is roughly uniform, and generations of experience have established how many stones close a given gap. The trouble is that laying this scale between a person and an engine breaks those assumptions one by one.

  • The machine has no rank. Engine strength is expressed through settings, not through a grade. Keep the same file and change only the amount of search and the playing strength moves substantially
  • The gap is not uniform across the board. One rank between two humans tends to show up fairly evenly in all phases of the game. The gap between a human and an engine varies sharply by position type, overwhelming in some regions and much less so in others
  • A handicap position sits outside the engine training distribution. Self-play training is overwhelmingly even-game play. An opening with two stones already on the board lies at the edge of that distribution
  • Only one side can study the other. The engine is public and can be run for as many practice games as anyone wants before the match. Preparation in the reverse direction does not exist
  • The clocks are asymmetric. One side has five hours for the whole game while the other has twenty seconds for each move. That asymmetry is part of the measurement conditions, not a detail outside them

So the number two is not a determination that the gap equals two steps. It is closer to a setting chosen so the contest would look competitive. The engine took the first game and the human took the other two, which tells us two stones landed near a balance point. It does not establish that the gap is precisely two stones.

The same network at a different visit count is a different player

A modern Go engine move has two components. One is the immediate output the neural network produces on seeing the position, the part that corresponds to intuition. The other is search, which extends candidate lines and checks them. The unit of one search expansion is called a visit. The visit count is the compute budget assigned to that move.

How much that budget governs strength shows up with unusual clarity in the adversarial policy research, almost as a side effect. That work trained an attacking policy against KataGo and then measured win rates holding the attacker fixed while changing only the victim visit count.

Measured evidence that changing visits changes the outcome

Victim search budgetAttacker win rateSample
No search100%1,000 games
4,096 visits97.3%1,000 games
10 to the 6th visits82%50 games
10 to the 7th visits72%50 games

The network is identical throughout. The only thing that changed is how many times each move was double checked. Yet the outcome moves from 100 percent down to 72 percent. Two things follow. First, more search genuinely helps. Second, multiplying search by ten thousand does not make the weakness disappear. Visits are a real axis of strength, but certain classes of defect shrink only gradually along that axis and never vanish.

Return to the match with that in hand and the problem becomes sharp. The published conditions contain only a clock, twenty seconds per move, and no visit count at all. Deriving visits from seconds would require the network size, the batching arrangement and the thread configuration, none of which were published either. The result is that nobody can say which row of the table above the opponent in those games corresponds to.

What twenty seconds and four graphics cards actually pin down

Twenty seconds per move on four graphics cards is better than nothing, but it is not a substitute for a visit count, because several variables sit in between.

  • Network size. More blocks and more channels make each evaluation slower, so fewer visits fit into the same twenty seconds, in exchange for each evaluation being more accurate. That trade is not settled in advance
  • Precision and batching. Whether evaluations run at half precision and whether they are grouped into batches changes throughput by multiples
  • Threads and card utilisation. Having four cards present is not the same as having a configuration that actually keeps four cards busy
  • Pondering. It is on the record that thinking during the opponent turn was switched off. Had it been on, the effective search per move would have been far larger than twenty seconds implies
  • Position difficulty. Even at a constant twenty seconds, the number of visits reached differs from position to position

The habit of separating latency and throughput by measurement window shows up in exactly the same shape when choosing models. The way one system can report wildly different numbers depending on which segment is being timed is laid out in the piece on splitting latency into four parts before picking a model.

Those last lines are the strongest sentence the current evidence supports. It still describes something remarkable. It is simply far narrower than a human beat the AI, and being narrower is what makes it checkable.

KataGo is not a fixed target

KataGo is open source, and its networks keep being produced on a public training server. New files appear on that server continuously; even in early September 2026 the newest entries are only days apart. In other words, the name KataGo does not identify a target without a version.

The rating numbers attached on that server come with a caveat written by the project itself: the ratings are approximate and are not necessarily comparable to ratings from any other bot or any other training run. The project has, in effect, nailed a warning to its own numbers telling readers not to compare across runs.

  • The repository distributes several networks of different sizes at once. The smaller ones are described as much weaker but still beyond professional level. Weaker and beyond professional level sit in the same sentence
  • So which file was loaded changes who the opponent was, even at an identical twenty seconds. Writing down the file name is not bureaucracy; it is the act that specifies the target
  • The engine version is a separate variable again. Search implementation and default settings change between releases
  • If none of this is recorded, nobody can repeat the games under the same terms later. An unrepeatable match survives as a record but not as evidence

Handicap play is a separate problem on the engine side too

In a handicap game the engine giving the stones begins from a position it is already losing. Search that maximises win probability alone behaves badly in that situation. When every candidate returns a low win rate, the differences between them shrink to noise, and the engine ends up unable to distinguish a move that limits the damage from a move that could turn the game around.

This is known on the engine side, and the KataGo repository names its response in its own description. It states that the engine cares about maximising score as well as win rate, which enables strong play in handicap games when far behind, and that it supports alternative komi values including integers along with good high-handicap game play.

Play in handicap games is therefore an area governed by settings. A separate parameter adjusts how aggressively the stone-giving side is pushed to play. How that parameter was configured for these three games was not published, and it bears directly on the outcome. With the same network and the same visit count, a different value here produces an opponent with a different temperament.

Where a superhuman system actually broke

From here the subject is not the match but the academic record. It is a separate matter from the July result, but it is the firmest support available for the proposition that superhuman is a conditional label, so it is worth stating precisely.

The 2022 paper Adversarial Policies Beat Superhuman Go AIs trained attacking policies aimed at KataGo and reports achieving a win rate above 97 percent against KataGo running at superhuman settings. In the authors' own words, these adversaries do not win by playing Go well; instead they trick the victim into making serious blunders.

What is it that breaks?

The central attack uses a cyclic shape. The adversary coaxes the victim into forming a large group of stones arranged in a loop, then strikes at the point where the victim misjudges whether that group lives. An accompanying observation captures the character of the defect well. The victim prediction loss stays elevated through most of the game, and the victim reports a win probability above 99 percent right up to the moment the group is captured. It does not lose by being ground down. It loses while still believing it is winning.

A human can carry it out unaided

What makes this defect unusual is that a person can understand and execute it. The paper states the attack is comprehensible to the extent that human experts can implement it without algorithmic assistance and consistently beat superhuman AIs, and reports humans reaching a win rate above 90 percent with the strategy against top-ranked bots. Reporting later described a strong amateur player winning 14 of 15 games against a KataGo-derived bot on an online server.

  • Training the attacker consumed 14.0 percent of the compute used to train the victim. What was needed was not the resources to surpass the opponent, only the resources to locate its blind spot
  • A simpler form of attack succeeded far more cheaply still. A win rate of 99.9 percent against a searchless opponent is reported alongside it
  • The attack partially transferred to other engines. Without any retraining, win rates of 6.1 percent and 3.5 percent were obtained against other superhuman systems. The transfer rate is low, but it is not zero
  • What the defect exposes is not average ability but worst-case behaviour. Average and worst case are different axes, and the label superhuman is earned only on the first of them

Does adding a defense close it?

The obvious follow-up question is whether it can simply be patched. The 2024 paper Can Go AIs be adversarially robust? addresses exactly that. The authors frame Go as a favourable setting in which to test robustness, because average capability is extraordinarily high, the domain is narrow, and the setting is innately adversarial to begin with.

Three defenses were tested: adversarial training on hand-constructed positions, iterated adversarial training, and changing the network architecture. The result reads as follows. Some of these protected against previously discovered attacks, but none withstood freshly trained adversaries. Moreover, most of the reliably effective attacks the new adversaries found were different realisations of the same overall class of cyclic attacks.

VictimVictim search budgetAttacker win rateSample
UndefendedNo search100%1,000 games
Undefended4,096 visits97.3%1,000 games
Adversarially trainedNo search100.0%1,048 games
Adversarially trained4,096 visits95.7%1,052 games

One row makes the defended victim look marginally worse than the undefended one. The samples and the attackers differ, so those cells are not directly comparable, but they are more than enough to establish that the existence of a defense is not by itself reassuring. The two gaps the paper names are efficient generalisation of defenses and diversity in training. Where evaluation systems manufacture their own blind spots is covered from another angle in the piece on evaluation blind spots.

Is this match the same story as the cyclic attack?

A line has to be drawn clearly here. There is no basis for treating the handicap result as the same phenomenon as the cyclic attack. The two differ along several axes.

AxisThe July 2026 handicap gamesThe adversarial policy research
NatureAn ordinary game starting from pre-placed stonesA policy trained specifically against a victim network
How it winsConventional play, as far as published records showInducing a specific shape that breaks life-and-death judgement
Opponent specificationNetwork and visit count not publishedCheckpoint and visit count stated explicitly
SampleThree gamesHundreds to a thousand games per cell
RepeatabilityNo settings log published, so not repeatableCode and conditions are public

Whether any cyclic life-and-death collapse decided the outcome in the published records was not established. The two events may therefore be unrelated, and treating them as unrelated is the reading more faithful to current evidence. The moment one is used as evidence for the other, both become weaker.

They belong in one article anyway because of a single shared structure. The label superhuman rests on conditions. Change the conditions and the label changes with them. In the first case the conditions were the handicap and the clocks. In the second they were the type of opposing policy and the amount of search. Erase the conditions and keep only the word superhuman, and both stories are read wrongly.

Six questions to ask before accepting a performance claim

This is the practical part. Reduced to six, the questions to ask on meeting a sentence of the form A beat B are these.

The six questions on one page

QuestionApplied to this matchApplied to a model comparison
1. Which way does it point?Who placed the two stones?Which side ran on the favourable configuration?
2. What exactly was the opponent?Which network file, which engine version?Which model, and which snapshot of it?
3. What was the budget?How many visits per move?Inference budget, attempts allowed, context length?
4. How large is the sample?Three games or a thousand?How many items, how many repetitions?
5. What was measured?Wins, or margin in points?Average or worst case, and on which metric?
6. Can it be repeated?Is a settings log published?Are prompts, seeds and versions published?

If any one of the six is blank, the sentence is not yet a proposition. That does not make it false; it means its truth value cannot currently be decided. Treat undecidable as false and good results get discarded; treat it as true and decisions get stacked on evidence that does not exist.

  • If exactly one field is blank, ask about that one field. Usually the original document contains it and the summary dropped it
  • If several are blank, treat the claim as an anecdote worth noting rather than as evidence to build on
  • If the direction field is blank, there is no point examining the rest. A reversed direction reverses the conclusion wholesale
  • If the sample is in single digits, whether the conditions are published matters more than the sample size. Anything repeatable can have its sample enlarged

Carrying the same reading into benchmarks and model comparisons

What visits are in Go appears elsewhere under other names. Only the vocabulary changes; the slot is the same one. The slot is how much compute was attached to this model for this measurement.

  • Where several answers are generated and one is selected, the number of attempts is the budget. Whether one correct answer out of a handful suffices moves the score dramatically
  • Where the model writes out extended reasoning, output length is the budget. Change the length cap and the same model produces a different score
  • Where tools are attached, the permitted number of calls is the budget. A score measured without tools and a score measured with them are different metrics wearing one name
  • The number of in-context examples is also a budget. Zero examples and several examples give two different numbers under the same heading
  • When the grader is itself a model, the grader configuration is a condition too. Change the instrument and you change the scale

So the first thing to check on a comparison table is not the score but whether the conditions printed beside each score are symmetric across the two systems. If one side got multiple attempts, or one side had tools attached, that table is not comparing two models; it is comparing two configurations. The same concern approached from the benchmark design side is set out in the piece taking a benchmark apart and in the piece on the conditions behind a coding benchmark.

The broader movement of turning games themselves into evaluation arenas is covered in the piece on humans and agents standing under identical rules. The principle that comparison requires identical rules occupies the same position there as it does here.

What could not be confirmed

  • The network file and engine version used could not be confirmed. No material covering the match records a file name or a release number. Without that value, the opponent is not specified
  • The actual visits per move could not be confirmed. Only the clock, twenty seconds, was published, and converting seconds into visits requires the network size and the runtime configuration
  • The handicap-related engine settings could not be confirmed. The repository description establishes that a parameter tuning the stone-giving side exists, but the value used in these games was not published
  • Whether all three games ran with identical settings could not be confirmed, because no per-game settings log was published. Various claims circulated online on this point, but none came with supporting material, so no judgement is possible
  • The move count and margin of game one could not be confirmed. Records for games two and three are public; game one is not
  • The detailed ruleset could not be confirmed. Territory scoring with 0.5 komi is established, but which national ruleset applied and what the fine adjudication provisions were is not written down anywhere
  • The reference date and rating system behind the world number one label could not be confirmed. Rankings depend on which system and which date are used, so this article does not rest any argument on that description
  • The precise scope of the word official could not be confirmed. Several outlets used it, but no material was found that distinguishes an official tournament fixture from a sponsored exhibition here
  • A visits-to-rating curve for the network used in these games could not be confirmed. The visit-related figures in this article are values the adversarial policy research reported for its own experimental subjects and do not transfer unchanged to another network
  • Whether any cyclic-shape position appears in the published records could not be confirmed. For that reason no sentence in this article connects the match to the adversarial vulnerability
