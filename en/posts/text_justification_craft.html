What It Takes to Square Off a Line - The Real Price of Justified Text

The slack has to go somewhere - into word spaces, letter spaces, hyphens, or the sentence itself

Aug 31, 2026 Trends & Culture

Introduction: Text With a Ruler-Straight Right Edge

Justify a block of body text and one of two things usually happens. If the column is generous, the paragraph looks tidier. If the column is narrow, a few lines go conspicuously loose and a pale scar appears down the middle of the paragraph. The eye works out where the cost of that straight right edge went before you consciously do. Switch back to left alignment and the scar vanishes, but now the right margin is serrated.

A piece doing the rounds again lately takes the problem head on. It concerns a Super Metroid walkthrough written in the late 1990s by someone going by rs1n. It runs to more than 17,000 words of plain text, and every single line ends exactly flush right. No justification tool was involved. The author rewrote sentences by choosing words that happened to fit. In his own words, he just chose words carefully so that everything lined up on the right hand side.

Let us be clear about what that is. It is one person's act of craft. It is not a method to recommend to a team, and it is not a tool problem waiting for a tool. What makes the record worth reading is something else: when a person takes over a job the composer normally does silently, the shape of that job becomes visible.

This piece does not decide whether justified text is good or bad. That answer moves with language, medium, and column width, and it does not settle on one side. Instead it follows where the leftover space goes. Word spaces, letter spaces, hyphens, or the sentence itself. One of those four always pays.

1. Why a Ragged Right Edge Is the Default

Two causes overlap to make line lengths uneven.

The first is that glyphs have different widths. In Latin script a lowercase i and a capital W occupy wildly different amounts of room. Two eight-letter words can measure quite differently depending on which letters they are made of, which is why counting characters will not get you a matched line length.

The second matters more. The break point is decided by the words, not by the compositor. The engine adds words to a line until the next one will not fit, and breaks there. The size of the remainder is set by how long that next word would have been. A run of short words leaves a small remainder; one long word coming up leaves a large one. Which words follow which is a decision the writer made, not the composer, so this remainder is a value nobody in the layout stage controls.

Worth pausing on one case. Set the text in a monospaced face and the first cause disappears, since every glyph has the same width and character count is line width. And yet the right edge stays ragged, because the second cause is untouched. In fact monospace is worse off: with fixed advance widths there is no such thing as stretching a space by three tenths of a character. A line is exactly N characters or it is not. That is precisely why the walkthrough's author had no option other than rewriting the prose.

So flush left with a ragged right becomes the default. Left alignment leaves the remainder sitting as empty space on the right, and in exchange the word space stays at one fixed value across the whole document. The reader's eye learns one rhythm and never has to guess where a word boundary is. The serrated right margin is a cosmetic loss, but it also gives each line a distinct silhouette that helps when returning to find the next line.

2. Where Does the Leftover Space Go?

Once you decide to square both edges, the remainder cannot be removed, only distributed. There are three places to put it, plus a fourth that abandons typesetting and solves the problem in prose.

Where it goesWhat growsSuitsWhat it costsCSS handle
Word spacesThe width of each spaceSpace-delimited languages, generous measureSpace width varies line to line; narrow columns grow vertical white streakstext-justify: inter-word
Letter spacesThe gap between glyphsLanguages that break at character levelIn Latin script the word stops reading as a single shapetext-justify: inter-character (legacy distribute)
HyphensNothing stretches; more text fits on the lineLanguages with a hyphenation dictionary and a correct langWords are cut; traces appear in copy, search, and speechhyphens: auto
The sentenceNothing at allFixed measure, fixed face, frozen textA person rewrites the prose; any change of width voids the whole thing(not a CSS matter)

The first row is what the web does by default. The text-justify property nominally lets you pick the method, with values auto, none, inter-word, inter-character, and distribute. MDN, however, marks it as not Baseline, meaning it does not work in some of the most widely used browsers, so no outcome should depend on it. What you can rely on is text-align: justify together with text-align-last, which aligns the final line separately and has been Baseline widely available since September 2022.

Why the word-space method is the conspicuous one comes down to one line of arithmetic. Say a line has 40 pixels to absorb. With twenty word gaps, each grows by 2 pixels and nobody sees it. With five gaps sharing the same 40 pixels, each grows by 8. Given that a normal word space is somewhere around 4 or 5 pixels, that is close to tripling it. The total being distributed is identical; the number of parties sharing it is not.

  • The fewer gaps a line holds, the more each one has to absorb, and gap count scales with measure, so narrower columns produce wider swings
  • Paragraphs full of long words hold fewer gaps at the same measure and fare worse for the same reason
  • The last line of a paragraph is normally excluded from justification; stretching it would scatter three words across the full measure
  • Letter spacing divides the same slack into far more pieces, but it blurs word shape in Latin script, which is the thing readers actually use to recognise words

3. When the White Space Lines Up Vertically

A single loose line is just a loose line. The trouble starts when consecutive lines happen to have their widened gaps at roughly the same horizontal position. The white marks join up and read as a channel running through the paragraph. English-language typesetters call it a river.

This is not treated purely as an aesthetic complaint. The W3C's WCAG understanding document cites it directly as the reason for its rule against justification.

The uneven spacing between words in fully justified text can cause 'rivers of white' space to run down the page making reading difficult and in some cases impossible.

Why it worsens in narrow columns is answered by the arithmetic above. The wider a single gap opens, the more visible it becomes, and once something visible repeats across two or three stacked lines, people perceive it as a line. In a wide measure each gap only grows a little, so nothing becomes distinct enough to connect.

Leading is involved too. Tight line spacing puts adjacent gaps physically closer, making them easier to join into a continuous shape. So if you have committed to justification, do not economise on leading. But recognise what that is: a measure that reduces rivers rather than removing them. As long as the total leftover is unchanged, it is still somewhere on the page.

4. One Line at a Time, or the Whole Paragraph?

The same paragraph, at the same measure, in the same face, can come out visibly differently. The dividing question is how much the composer looks at before it commits to a break.

The simplest approach fills each line greedily: take as much as fits, break, move to the next, never look back. It is fast, and its weakness is structural. A choice made early can ruin a later line with no opportunity to revise it. If one long word gets pushed wholesale onto the third line, the second line empties out, when taking one word fewer on the second line would have left all three acceptable. Making that judgement requires looking ahead, and this approach does not.

The alternative treats the paragraph as a single problem. Donald Knuth and Michael Plass published such a method in Software: Practice and Experience in 1981. It models the paragraph with three primitives, boxes, glue, and penalties, and uses dynamic programming to find the combination of break points that minimises total badness across the whole paragraph. The point of the approach is that the final appearance of a given line may be influenced by text on succeeding lines. TeX is built on it.

Browsers stood on the speed side for a long time, because re-optimising a whole paragraph every time someone drags a window edge is expensive. The CSS text-wrap property is now closing part of that gap, with two values that do quite different jobs.

  • balance — evens out the number of characters per line. MDN notes that because this is computationally expensive it is only supported for blocks spanning a limited number of lines, citing six or fewer for Chromium and ten or fewer for Firefox. It is for headings, captions, and pull quotes
  • pretty — behaves like wrap except that the user agent uses a slower algorithm that favours better layout over speed. The documentation frames it as being for body copy, giving minimising orphans as an example
  • stable — behaves like wrap except that while the user is editing, lines before the one being edited stay put rather than the whole block reflowing

Put plainly, what print does better is not taste but time and scope. It has the budget to consider the whole paragraph, a human can change one word and reflow if the result displeases, and above all the final measure is a single known number. On the web none of those three hold.

5. Korean, Japanese, and Chinese Play by Different Rules

Everything above assumes a language in which spaces separate words. Break that assumption and the shape of the problem changes entirely.

W3C internationalisation material describes Japanese and Chinese line breaking this way: individual characters are moved to the next line without regard to word or syllable boundaries. The leftover at the end of a line therefore never exceeds about one character width. There is barely anything to redistribute in the first place. Body text in Japanese and Chinese books sits flush on both sides not because the typesetting is more sophisticated but because there was never much of a gap to open.

Korean sits in between. The same material notes that words in modern Hangul text are usually separated by spaces, but that breaking by syllable is also common, particularly in justified text. In other words Korean can be set the English way or the Japanese and Chinese way, and choosing one means choosing its cost as well.

LanguageDefault break unitSize of the remainderProblem that replaces itRelevant CSS
English and other LatinSpace (word)As large as the next wordGaps open up; hyphenation becomes close to mandatoryhyphens, text-justify: inter-word
Japanese and ChineseCharacterAt most one character widthProhibition rules; you cannot break just anywhereline-break, hanging-punctuation
KoreanEither space or syllableDepends which you pickYou must first decide between cutting words and opening gapsword-break: normal vs keep-all

In CSS that choice lives in a single declaration. word-break: normal uses the default line break rule, while keep-all means, in MDN's words, that word breaks should not be used for Chinese, Japanese, and Korean text. That is why keep-all is so common on Korean pages: it stops a two-syllable word from being split across lines. The cost is predictable. Keep words whole and you have imported the English problem. Remainders grow, justification opens gaps again, and long words or URLs bring an overflow risk with them.

Breaking at character level is not free either. It attracts a different rule set, kinsoku shori, or line breaking prohibition. The W3C's requirements document for Japanese text layout separates characters that may not start a line from characters that may not end one.

  • Must not start a line — closing brackets, full stops, commas, and middle dots. No line should begin with a full stop
  • Must not end a line — opening brackets, and separating marks such as the question mark and exclamation mark
  • To honour the rules the composer either pushes a character down to the next line (oidashi) or pulls one up onto this one (oikomi)
  • That pushing and pulling is absorbed by adding or reducing inter-character spacing. Japanese is set solid by default, with no space between characters, so this adjustment is the line adjustment process
  • In CSS the strictness is selected with line-break: loose, normal, or strict. The value anywhere ignores line breaking prohibitions altogether, and no hyphens are applied with it

One more device from print composition is letting punctuation hang past the edge of the measure. CSS offers hanging-punctuation, whose allow-end value lets a stop or comma hang if it does not otherwise fit prior to justification. MDN marks this property as not Baseline as well, so treat it as an enhancement that improves the result where present and must not be load-bearing.

Finally, real documents are mixed. A Korean paragraph with English product names and URLs in it runs both regimes at once. The Hangul breaks finely while the Latin run moves as an indivisible block, so gaps blow open only on the lines where that block lands. Since this article ships in four languages, the same paragraph is composed four times, and assuming one stylesheet produces one result across all four is exactly where it comes apart.

6. What Hyphenation Actually Buys You

What makes justified English tolerable is, in practice, hyphenation. Being able to split a word mid-way means the remainder is rarely a whole word long, and there is correspondingly less reason for gaps to open. Justification without hyphenation is usually the worst available combination.

The web property is hyphens, and it takes three values.

  • manual — the default. Words break only where a character in the text presents an opportunity. Do nothing and this is what you have
  • none — no breaking inside words, and any marked opportunity is ignored
  • auto — the browser breaks words at appropriate points, based on the document's lang attribute, and only where a suitable hyphenation dictionary exists for the declared language

That last item is the one that bites in practice. If you set hyphens: auto and nothing happens, suspect a missing or wrong lang before anything else. Declaring the wrong language is worse than declaring none, because a dictionary for another language will split words in the wrong places.

Two characters mark break points by hand. U+00AD SOFT HYPHEN, written as &shy;, is invisible and only renders a hyphen if the browser actually breaks there. U+2010 HYPHEN is a hard hyphen: it renders whether or not the line breaks at that point. Mixing them leaves a document containing both invisible marks and visible glyphs doing similar jobs, so pick one and stay with it.

Hyphenation's costs are not confined to the page.

  • Meaning gets muddied. You cannot break just anywhere; breaks should follow morphological boundaries, which is exactly why a dictionary is required
  • Copying and searching drift apart. Results differ depending on whether the hyphen exists only on screen or as an actual character, and if it is a real character the original word no longer matches a search
  • Speech output can be affected. A rendered hyphen is harmless, but one baked into the text changes the unit being read
  • There is no counterpart in Korean, Japanese, or Chinese. These languages do not hyphenate; character-level breaking and prohibition rules occupy that role instead

The walkthrough author's reasons for avoiding hyphens are on this list: they are visually distracting and they interfere with copy and paste. In a plain text file a hyphen is not a rendering effect but a character actually written into the file, which makes that judgement weigh considerably more there.

7. Rewriting the Sentence to Fit

By now what the walkthrough actually did is precise. He did not abandon typesetting; he moved its burden into the writing. Rather than asking the composer to distribute leftover space, he selected words whose widths summed exactly to the target.

The gains are real.

  • No space was stretched, so a river cannot form
  • No hyphens, so words stay whole and survive copying intact
  • Density is identical line to line, so the paragraph's grey value is even
  • The constraint makes the prose shorter and sharper, since anything baggy fails to fit and gets cut on its own

The losses are equally real.

  • It holds at exactly one width. Narrow the window or enlarge the type and the entire premise collapses. It is only stable with a monospaced face at a fixed column count
  • Some sentences cannot be fixed. Quotations, proper nouns, code, figures, and legal text cannot trade wording for width
  • Maintenance compounds. Change one character early on and every line after it rebreaks, so fixing a typo becomes rewriting a paragraph
  • It does not survive translation. The property does not carry into another language; the work starts over each time
  • It evaporates when readers change settings. Someone reading at larger type, or listening, never receives the alignment at all. What reaches them is only prose that was compressed to fit

The interesting part is that this manoeuvre is already routine at small scale. Rewording a button label to fit its box. Trimming two characters off a table header so it stays on one line. Evening out the lengths of menu items so the set looks deliberate. All the same operation. The only difference is whether the target is one line or a whole document, and that difference is what separates the usable version from the unusable one. A line can be rewritten. Seventeen thousand words cannot.

8. Accessibility - What Is Actually Established

There is one standards clause that addresses justification: WCAG 2.2 Success Criterion 1.4.8 Visual Presentation. Two conditions tend to fall off when it is quoted, so they go first.

  • The level is AAA. Most sites targeting AA are not bound by this criterion at all
  • The criterion bundles five requirements. Fixing alignment alone does not satisfy it

The five are that foreground and background colours can be selected by the user; that width is no more than 80 characters or glyphs, 40 if CJK; that text is not justified; that line spacing is at least space-and-a-half within paragraphs with paragraph spacing at least 1.5 times the line spacing; and that text can be resized without assistive technology up to 200 percent without requiring horizontal scrolling to read a line. The alignment clause reads:

Text is not justified (aligned to both the left and the right margins).

The understanding document gives two reasons. First, people with certain cognitive disabilities have problems reading text that is both left and right justified, with the rivers explanation quoted earlier following as the mechanism. Second, and in the opposite direction, justification can also cause words to be spaced closely together, so that it is difficult for them to locate word boundaries. The benefits section frames the criterion as helping people with cognitive, language, and learning disabilities perceive text and track their location within blocks of text.

It matters not to overreach from there. What the standard supplies is a requirement and its rationale, not an effect size. There is no figure in it saying alignment costs some percentage of reading speed. And note that the width clause carves out CJK at 40 characters, which shows the criterion itself assumes conditions change with the writing system.

Translated into practice: do not force justification onto body text whose size the reader can change. If you use it anyway, hyphenation, a wider measure, and looser leading will reduce the swing between lines. They will not eliminate it.

9. What to Decide in Practice

Neither use justification nor never use justification is of any use. Ask four questions in order instead and the answer mostly settles itself.

QuestionCan carry justificationCannot
Is the final measure fixed?Print, PDF, fixed-width cards and labelsResponsive web, reader-controlled zoom
How many words fit on a line?A measure that holds plenty of words per lineNarrow columns, single-column mobile, sidebars
What is the language's break unit?Japanese and Chinese; Korean where syllable breaking is allowedEnglish without hyphenation; Korean using keep-all
Will the text change later?Documents printed once and doneFrequently edited text, anything that gets translated

The order has a reason. If the measure is not settled, no amount of tuning controls what the reader sees. Once it is settled, the next question is how much capacity a line has to share the remainder, and only with that capacity do language-specific measures mean anything. The last question is not about typesetting at all but about operations: hand-tuned line breaks survive only while the text stands still.

The places you actually touch come down to a short list.

  • Declare lang correctly. Hyphenation, break rules, and font fallback all branch on it
  • If you justify English body text, turn on hyphens: auto with it. Doing one without the other is usually a net loss
  • In Korean, settle word-break before alignment. That declaration, not text-align, is what actually shapes the right edge
  • Use text-wrap: balance for short blocks like headings while assuming its line-count limit, and consider text-wrap: pretty for body copy, remembering that it governs layout quality rather than alignment method
  • Do not make outcomes depend on non-Baseline properties (text-justify, hanging-punctuation). Use them as enhancements only
  • Keep one media query that turns justification off at narrow widths. A paragraph that looked fine on a desktop collapsing on a phone is the most common route to failure
  • Once decided, check with the worst paragraph you have: the one full of long words and URLs

Conclusion: What a Flush Right Edge Demands

Squaring off the right edge looks like a question of appearance, but at execution it turns into a question of allocation: who absorbs the leftover space. Give it to the spaces and rivers appear. Give it to the letters and word shapes blur. Give it to hyphens and words get cut and a dictionary becomes a dependency. And there is one last option: a person rewrites the sentence.

What the walkthrough demonstrates is what happens when that last option is pushed all the way. The result is beautiful and the conditions are brutal. One width, one face, no edits. Those three rarely hold at once in real work, which is why this belongs on the shelf as a record rather than a technique.

The practical conclusion it leaves is modest. Justification is not a decision but a conditional choice. Where the measure is fixed, where a line has capacity to share the remainder, where the language breaks finely, and where the text is not going to change, it is worth having. Miss any one of those and left alignment is the quieter answer. A serrated right margin is not a failure of typesetting; it is the visible trace of a decision to hold word spacing constant.

What Could Not Be Confirmed

  • The exact column width in characters used by the walkthrough, and the editor it was written in — not pinned down from the source
  • Any quantified effect of justification on reading speed or comprehension — WCAG states its rationale in prose without effect sizes, and no separate controlled study was located
  • What algorithm each browser actually uses for text-wrap: pretty — MDN goes no further than a slower algorithm and minimising orphans
  • The details of prohibition rules in a standards document for Korean text layout — the kinsoku material here rests on the Japanese requirements (JLREQ) and cannot be assumed to map directly onto Korean
  • Current per-browser support for hanging-punctuation and text-justify — MDN says only that they are not Baseline. The pixel figures used here are illustrative assumptions, not measurements
