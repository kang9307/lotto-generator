Noni: What Is Actually Established - Potassium, the Liver, and Where It Overlaps With Medication

The claim list gets neither longer nor shorter here. What is confirmed, what is not, and who should stay away

2026-09-01 Health & Wellness

Search for noni and most of what comes back was written by someone selling it. Those pages tend to repeat the same list. Antioxidant, immunity, pain, and a carefully worded gesture toward cancer. The list is long, but which study each item came from is rarely written down anywhere on the page.

This article does not lengthen that list and does not shorten it either. It looks at two things instead. How far the evidence in humans actually goes, and who should avoid this. The weight sits on the second question. The route by which noni has actually harmed people runs not through whether it works but through potassium and the liver.

This article is general information, and diagnosis and treatment follow the judgement of a clinician. It is not an assessment of any particular product or brand, and it does not instruct anyone on intake amounts or on adjusting medication. If you have a chronic condition or take any medicine, discuss it with your clinician before starting. The regulatory section describes the rules of the Republic of Korea.

What noni is, and where it sits legally

Noni is the fruit of a small tropical tree with the botanical name Morinda citrifolia. To take the description straight from LiverTox, the drug induced liver injury database run by the United States National Institutes of Health, it has been used for centuries in Polynesia and Southeast Asia as both a food and a herbal remedy, and it entered the United States market as a juice in 1996. The same document lists the known constituents as flavonoids, glycosides, vitamins, anthraquinones and polyunsaturated fatty acids.

Four forms are common on shelves. Juice, concentrate, powder, and compressed pills. That distinction looks trivial and it returns later, because how much of anything a single glass carries changes enormously with the form. What matters is not how many millilitres the label recommends but what is in those millilitres and in what quantity.

And there is a legal position. Noni products are food. They are not medicines. That sentence is not a seller's modesty or a legal hedge, it is the classification itself, and most of the rest of this article follows from it. Because it is food, it cannot be labelled or advertised as treating disease, and because it is food, it has not been through the process that establishes efficacy and safety in humans the way a medicine has.

  • Botanical name Morinda citrifolia. A tropical fruit native to Polynesia and Southeast Asia
  • Forms sold - juice, concentrate, powder, pills. The same volume does not carry the same amount
  • Legal class - food. Depending on the product it may be an ordinary food or a health functional food
  • What this article does not cover - the quality, origin or price of any particular brand

Why potassium comes first

If you order the noni story by risk, potassium comes before liver injury. Liver injury is rare and its mechanism is unknown, whereas potassium is a number that sits in the composition table, behaves predictably, and has a clearly identifiable group of people it affects.

Start with the figures. In the compositional data submitted to the European Food Safety Authority for its 2009 assessment of noni fruit puree and concentrate as a novel food ingredient, the puree contained 214.3 milligrams of potassium per 100 grams and the concentrate 2026 milligrams per 100 grams. Separately, a case report published in the American Journal of Kidney Diseases in 2000 analysed a commercial noni juice directly and found 56.3 milliequivalents per litre. The two numbers agree with each other. Converted to mass, 56.3 milliequivalents per litre comes to a little over 220 milligrams per 100 millilitres, which sits almost exactly on top of the European puree figure.

A misreading tends to start here. Those numbers do not say that noni is an unusually high potassium fruit. The authors of the case report themselves described the level as similar to orange juice or tomato juice. The real problem is not the concentration, it is that it drops out of the count.

That case report shows the point precisely. A patient with reduced kidney function arrived with a raised serum potassium and said he was adhering to a low potassium diet. He had removed orange juice from his diet. He had not removed noni juice, because health products do not get written on diet sheets. The paper's conclusion reduces to a single line. Herbal remedies and alternative medicine products can be surreptitious sources of potassium for patients with renal disease.

Concentrate takes this one step further. Divide the two European figures and the concentrate carries more than nine times the potassium concentration of the puree. The same glass therefore delivers a different amount of potassium depending on whether it holds puree or concentrate, which means that keeping to the millilitres printed on the label is not the same thing as having consumed the same amount.

Why reduced kidney function changes the outcome

The same amount of potassium going in produces different blood levels in different people, because what differs is not the way in but the way out. The patient information published by the United States National Library of Medicine opens its list of causes of high potassium with exactly this. If your kidneys are not working well, they may not be able to remove the proper amount of potassium.

Medication comes on the next line. The same source names, among blood pressure lowering drugs, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists such as spironolactone as common causes of high potassium. These are widely used. They are standard in hypertension, heart failure and chronic kidney disease, which means the population drinking noni and the population taking these drugs can overlap substantially.

The awkward part here is not the symptoms but the absence of them. The same source states plainly that high potassium often produces no symptoms. When symptoms do appear they arrive as chest pain, difficulty breathing, palpitations, a slow or weak or irregular pulse, and in severe cases a sudden collapse. In other words this is a risk that does not give you an early warning through how you feel, and so identifying who is in the affected group beats waiting to notice something.

  • Potassium is excreted mainly by the kidneys. When excretion falls, the same intake shows up as a different blood level
  • Some blood pressure drugs and diuretics act to hold potassium in the body. That adds to the potassium arriving in food
  • High potassium often has no symptoms. Feeling fine is not evidence of being fine
  • If you are on dialysis, the dietary review in a nephrology journal states that noni juice should be avoided because of its potassium content

How far up the evidence ladder the claims have climbed

Laboratory work on noni is not scarce. The herb monograph from Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center collects several strands. Animal model results in which glycosides inhibited cellular transformation, mechanistic work in which polysaccharides raised p53 and Bax while lowering the Bcl-2 family, and reports that a compound called damnacanthal inhibited a particular signalling axis in hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines.

Right beside that list, the same source sets down one sentence. There is no proof from clinical trials that the same effect occurs in humans. That sentence is the whole of this section. The rest is a question of how to read it.

Anyone who knows how drug development is structured will not find the gap surprising. Of the things that work in cells, some work in animals, and of the things that work in animals, some work in people. Most drop out at each stage. Which is why the mere presence of a citation is not human evidence. Whether the study cited in an advertisement was done in cells, in animals or in people is in practice the only line that matters.

  • An improved antioxidant marker does not by itself mean that any disease has been reduced
  • The conclusion of this section is not that there is no effect but that nothing has been confirmed in humans. Those are two different sentences

Where noni sits under Korean labelling rules

The system in the Republic of Korea splits food in two. To take the wording from the national health information portal run by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency, a health functional food is a food manufactured or processed from ingredients or components with a useful function for the human body, whose functionality and safety have been recognised by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety. Anything without that recognition is an ordinary food, and an ordinary food cannot carry functionality claims.

There are two routes to becoming a recognised functional ingredient. Listed ingredients, which appear in the ministry's notified Standards and Specifications for Health Functional Foods and may be used by anyone, and individually recognised ingredients, which may be used only by the business that obtained the recognition through review. The governing rule is the ministry's Regulation on Recognition of Functional Ingredients and Standards and Specifications for Health Functional Foods.

So the practical way to check is not to read the advertising copy but to look at the package. A health functional food carries the mark, carries a functionality information panel, and carries a statement that the product is not a medicine for the prevention or treatment of disease. That statement is a mandatory labelling item under the governing rules, so its presence is not a warning sign, it is normal labelling.

  • Does the package carry the health functional food mark and a functionality information panel - if not, the product is an ordinary food
  • The form 「may help with」 is how a physiological activity function is expressed. It does not denote a treatment effect
  • The statement that the product is not a medicine is a mandatory item under Article 4(1)3 of the Act on Labelling and Advertising of Foods and the Labelling Standards for Health Functional Foods
  • Whether noni itself appears on the functional ingredient lists is something this article could not verify directly. It is left in the section below

Liver injury - rare, but on record

LiverTox is the drug induced liver injury resource run by the United States National Institutes of Health, and it assigns a likelihood score to each substance. The noni entry carries a revision date of 28 March 2020, and the score assigned is C. The published definition reads: a probable rare cause of clinically apparent liver injury. That sentence is easy to misread in both directions. It is neither a declaration of danger nor a clean bill of health.

The reported cases share a fairly consistent shape. Onset falls between two and eight weeks after starting, serum enzyme elevations follow a hepatocellular pattern, immunoallergic features such as rash or fever are generally absent, and autoantibodies are raised in some patients. Most recovered fairly quickly after stopping. In rare cases the injury progressed to acute liver failure requiring emergency liver transplantation.

ReportSubjectCourse

The mechanism has not been established. Anthraquinones are the usual suspects, but LiverTox records that the levels are low and that in vitro and in vivo studies have not demonstrated intrinsic hepatotoxicity. The reported cases look idiosyncratic rather than like a direct dose dependent toxicity. And no rechallenge studies have been reported, because it is not the sort of question one can settle by giving the substance back to a person, which is the structural reason causality is hard to pin down here.

If the regulator called it safe, why do the warnings remain

In 2009 the European Food Safety Authority concluded that noni fruit puree and concentrate were safe for the general population under the specified conditions. It also ruled on the hepatitis cases. The wording is that the available data are not sufficient to establish a causal relationship between the consumption of noni juice and hepatotoxicity. The same document noted that the accumulating case reports might indicate that some individuals have a particular sensitivity.

So does that contradict the LiverTox score of C. It does not. The two bodies answered different questions. LiverTox answered whether this substance is plausibly a rare cause of liver injury. The European authority answered whether this food can be placed on the market for the general population. The first question is about individual risk, the second about population risk. Different answers are what you should expect.

It is worth noting that the exposure assumed in that assessment was not modest. The calculation used a 97.5th percentile adult intake of 624 millilitres a day and 548 millilitres for children. In other words, even assuming generous consumption, the conclusion for the general population came out on the safe side, which means the residual risk concentrates not on the average person but on people in specific circumstances. The next section is that list.

  • A population level safety conclusion and an individual level caution hold at the same time. Quoting only one of them distorts the picture
  • The sentence that causality has not been established is not proof of no relationship. It means there is not enough to judge on
  • As of September 2026 both judgements stand as written. These are sentences that would change if a new conclusion were published

What happens when it overlaps with medication

The most common misreading lives here. It is the idea that a health product has nothing to do with medicine. That idea does not hold. Interactions come from what a compound touches, not from where it came from. If a compound induces or inhibits a liver metabolising enzyme, the blood level of any drug cleared by that enzyme moves. If a compound arrives carrying an electrolyte, it adds to drugs that manage that electrolyte. Natural versus synthetic has no place in either sentence.

  • The rebuttal that products differ compositionally is not grounds for reassurance. It means prediction is harder
  • The action this requires is single. At a consultation, write the supplement on the same list as the medicines

If you have surgery coming up

Here the verified ground is narrow. No primary guideline naming noni specifically and setting a number of days to stop before surgery could be verified. So this article does not give a stopping point. That is for the treating team to decide.

What does need saying at the preoperative interview is fully explained by what came before. Electrolytes are managed during anaesthesia and surgery, coagulation values can move if warfarin is in use, and liver function bears on the metabolism of several agents. All three are items whose basis this article has already set out, and so the fact that you are drinking noni has no reason to be a blank on the preoperative form.

  • Filling in only the medication box and omitting supplements is common. Write it in the same box
  • State the form and the daily amount. Juice, concentrate or powder changes the content
  • Whether and when to stop is not yours to decide alone. This is as far as this article can go

Symptoms that mean stop and get checked

The list below has two branches, the liver side and the potassium side, and they wear different faces. Either way the order is the same: stop taking it and get checked. Setting yourself an observation period to work out the cause on your own is not a method worth recommending.

Possibly indicating liver injury
  • Yellowing of the whites of the eyes or the skin
  • Urine darkening to a cola or deep brown colour
  • Unexplained severe fatigue
  • A dull ache or discomfort in the upper right abdomen
  • Loss of appetite, nausea, itching
Possibly indicating high potassium
  • Chest pain
  • Difficulty breathing
  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Palpitations, or a slow, weak or irregular pulse
  • Sudden collapse - this one means emergency care without delay

Two things to add. First, high potassium often progresses without symptoms. None of the list applying to you is not evidence that things are fine, which is why, if kidney function is reduced or the relevant drugs are in use, membership of the affected group matters more than symptoms.

Second, at the consultation, say when you started. Because the reported liver injury cases had a latency of two to eight weeks, being within two months of starting noni is itself information that gets used. What, in what form, how much a day, and since when. Those four are enough.

How to read the label and the advertising

The sentences that recur in noni advertising fall into three types. Naming a disease directly, phrasing something so it reads like a medicine, and making an ordinary food look like a health functional food. Article 8(1) of the Korean Act on Labelling and Advertising of Foods prohibits these three separately, as subparagraphs 1, 2 and 3.

A consumer does not need to memorise the provision. What is worth remembering is the order in which to turn the package over. Advertising copy is chosen by the seller, labelling items are set by regulation. Where the two disagree, the labelling is closer to the facts.

  • Step 1 - is the health functional food mark there. If not, this is an ordinary food and not a product that may speak about functionality
  • Step 2 - what does the functionality information panel say. 「May help with」 is the prescribed form for a recognised function, not a treatment effect
  • Step 3 - is the statement that the product is not a medicine for prevention or treatment of disease there. This is a mandatory item and should be there
  • Step 4 - check whether the study cited in the advertisement was done in cells or in people. That distinction decides the rest

So is it all right to drink it

Nothing verified in this article supports prohibiting a healthy adult from taking it at the amount stated on the label. The European authority reached a safety conclusion for the general population even while assuming generous intake, and the LiverTox score says probable rare cause. Building a case on fear is the same category of error as claiming efficacy without evidence.

Within what this article verified, three conditions remain. First, if you take any medication, say so. Second, understand that the same volume carries very different amounts depending on the form. Third, remember that if something goes wrong within two months of starting, that timing is information. Keep those three and what remains falls outside what an individual can control anyway.

And one more. This is not a treatment. The Korean national health information portal states explicitly that the functionality of a health functional food is not the direct treatment or prevention of disease in the way a medicine is. Deciding to reduce or stop a prescribed medicine because you have started taking noni is not something this article supports in any way, and any such adjustment belongs in a conversation with a clinician.

Common misreadings

  • It is natural, so it is safe - safety is decided by constituents, not by origin. The title of the 2008 case paper says exactly that. Herbal does not at all mean innocuous
  • The more the better - potassium arrives in proportion to the amount consumed, and the liver side is idiosyncratic, so cutting the amount is not a reliable way to avoid it either. Neither direction gives a reason to drink more
  • It can replace medication - the epilepsy case is the precise counterexample. Anticonvulsant levels stayed below the therapeutic range while the juice continued
  • It is a food, so there are no interactions - the table in the previous section is the rebuttal by itself. Metabolising enzymes and electrolytes do not distinguish food from medicine
  • Liver injury is reported, so it is a dangerous food - that is the exaggeration in the other direction. The European authority said the data are insufficient to establish causality, and the LiverTox score reads probable rare cause
Stated again. This article is general information and does not substitute for diagnosis or treatment. Nothing written here is a basis for reducing or stopping any medicine you take. If you have symptoms or a chronic condition, follow the judgement of your clinician.

What I could not verify

  • Whether noni is recognised in Korea as a health functional food functional ingredient, on either the notified list or the individually recognised list, could not be checked directly. So this article does not assert that it is unrecognised, and describes how to check on the package instead
  • No primary guideline naming noni and setting a number of days to stop before surgery could be verified. General anaesthesia related recommendations on herbal supplements are mentioned in several places, but the source documents did not open, so no interval is given here
  • No safety study data for pregnancy or breastfeeding could be verified. That row in the table above rests only on the absence of data
  • Whether the warfarin case turned on noni's own vitamin K or on something else in that particular product could not be settled. What is verified is that a rebuttal letter in the same journal pointed to compositional differences between products, and so no vitamin K figure appears here
  • Tingling in the hands and feet, often listed clinically alongside high potassium, does not appear in the symptom list of the public health information cited here, so it was left out of the list above
  • Several news reports describe product testing and advertising enforcement against noni products in Korea, but the originating ministry press releases did not open, so no counts or threshold values are given
  • The total number of cases tallied by LiverTox could not be confirmed as a settled figure. That a 2008 paper was titled as the sixth case is as far as verification goes
