If We Could Design a Perfect World - The Pinker-VanderWeele Debate on Human Flourishing

From the MIT conversation between Steven Pinker and Tyler VanderWeele to Tyler Cowen's growth thesis - the core dilemmas of utopia design and the new variable AI adds

Jul 6, 2026 Trends & Culture

Intro: Why "Designing a Perfect World" Is Such a Compelling Thought Experiment

If you had to design a perfect world, where would you even start? The reason this question is hard to answer has less to do with the answer itself than with its premise. For "perfect" to mean anything, you first have to decide what standard of goodness you're measuring against - and the moment you ask the question, it becomes obvious that no such agreement exists. A conversation recently recirculating online captures this head-on: a talk titled "Can We Engineer a Perfect World?", held at MIT between Harvard cognitive psychologist Steven Pinker and Tyler VanderWeele, who directs Harvard's Human Flourishing Program. As of July 2026, this piece uses that exchange as a starting point to lay out the competing intellectual traditions around human flourishing, the core dilemmas of utopia design, and the new variable AI brings to this old question.

1. The MIT Conversation - Pinker and VanderWeele

The talk was organized by the MIT Veritas Forum and moderated by Rosalind Picard of the MIT Media Lab. The two speakers start from clearly different places. Pinker, best known for "Enlightenment Now," is a secular rationalist who argues, with data, that reason, science, and humanism have genuinely moved humanity toward a better state. VanderWeele, an epidemiologist and biostatistician who leads Harvard's Human Flourishing Program, brings a view in which faith and spirituality can also contribute to individual flourishing. In effect, secular rationalism met a more pluralistic, faith-inclusive concept of flourishing on the same stage. Both agree that expecting perfect rationality and goodness from this world is bound to disappoint - but they diverge sharply on what should actually count as "flourishing."

2. Pinker's Optimism About Reason and Progress

The argument Pinker makes in "Enlightenment Now" is, at its core, fairly simple: progress is long-run improvement across measures like life, health, wealth, safety, peace, knowledge, leisure, and happiness, and the engine behind that improvement is reason, science, and humanism. He points to society-level statistics - war deaths, extreme poverty, illiteracy, life expectancy - as evidence. From this vantage point, a "perfect world" isn't a blueprint completed on some future date; it's closer to a direction, one that keeps pushing along the trajectory of improvement the data already shows. The strength of Pinker's optimism lies in verifiable metrics, but whether those metrics fully capture the meaning or relational quality an individual actually experiences remains a separate question.

3. VanderWeele's Multidimensional Framework for Flourishing

In a 2017 paper, VanderWeele proposed a measurement framework built around five domains of flourishing: happiness and life satisfaction, physical and mental health, meaning and purpose, character and virtue, and close social relationships. His research team reports that across 22 countries, these five domains were rated as nearly universally important regardless of culture. Where Pinker tries to prove progress with society-level statistics, VanderWeele places at the center of flourishing exactly the things statistics tend to miss - an individual's sense of meaning, relationships, and character. He argues that faith and spirituality can specifically contribute to the meaning and character domains, which naturally creates tension with Pinker's secular progress narrative. This disagreement over what to measure ultimately becomes a disagreement over what "a perfect world" even means.

4. Another Axis - Tyler Cowen's Growth-Centered View

One point needs to be precise here: the counterpart in this specific MIT conversation was Tyler VanderWeele, not Tyler Cowen. That said, economist Tyler Cowen - a George Mason University professor and host of "Conversations with Tyler" - is frequently mentioned alongside Pinker in discussions of human flourishing and progress, and the two have in fact had separate conversations of their own on other topics. In his book "Stubborn Attachments," Cowen argues that maximizing the sustainable rate of economic growth, constrained by respect for human rights, should be society's overriding moral priority. His reasoning rests on the historical record: economic growth has substantially reduced poverty and improved lifespans, medicine, and stability. If Pinker is closer to a historian proving "how much things have improved" with data, Cowen is closer to an economist asking "how do we keep that improvement going." Line up Pinker, VanderWeele, and Cowen side by side, and it becomes clear that the answer to "what is a perfect world" changes entirely depending on whether you weight reason and data, meaning and relationships and faith, or growth and institutions.

5. The Core Dilemmas of Utopia Design

5.1 Values Are Plural and Often Conflict

Philosopher Isaiah Berlin's value pluralism holds that goods like liberty and equality, or individual talent and equal outcomes, are each legitimate in their own right yet do not fully reconcile with one another. Berlin warned that the monistic belief that human values converge on a single hierarchy carries a real risk of sliding into totalitarian projects. There is no single correct answer to "a perfect world" not because designers lack the skill, but because the values humans actually pursue never fully reconcile with each other in the first place.

5.2 Tradeoffs Don't Disappear

Tradeoffs between efficiency and equity, stability and freedom, growth and sustainability are not something design can eliminate - they are closer to a constant that forces you to pick a side every time you design. To "design a perfect world" means choosing one combination among these tradeoffs, not transcending the tradeoffs themselves.

5.3 Unintended Consequences and the Risk of Utopian Engineering

In "The Open Society and Its Enemies," philosopher Karl Popper distinguished between utopian engineering - redesigning an entire society around a single blueprint - and piecemeal engineering, which tests and adjusts existing institutions in small increments. He warned that the more totally an attempt tries to redesign society at once, the harder its consequences are to predict, and the more it tends to drift toward suppressing dissent against that blueprint. This warning is especially worth remembering whenever the thought experiment of "designing a perfect world" gets translated into actual policy.

6. The New Variable AI Adds to This Debate

6.1 "What Should Be Optimized" Is Now Code

A question that used to sit purely in philosophers' thought experiments now has to be implemented as an actual objective function in AI alignment design. 2026 research on "Positive Alignment," co-authored by researchers affiliated with Google DeepMind, OpenAI, and Anthropic, argues that the industry has invested heavily in preventing harm but not nearly enough in designing systems that actually help humans flourish.

6.2 Whose Flourishing Should Be the Benchmark

Proposals like the Human Flourishing Benchmark (HFB) suggest evaluating AI along axes such as cognitive autonomy, agency, capability development, and relational authenticity. But this is, in effect, translating Berlin's value-pluralism dilemma directly into code. Whether an AI optimizes for Pinker-style societal metrics, VanderWeele-style meaning-and-relationship metrics, or Cowen-style growth metrics produces an entirely different picture of the "better world" that same AI builds. Who makes that choice, and through what process, is itself becoming a new form of the utopian-engineering debate.

6.3 "Protopia" Instead of Utopia

Technology thinker Kevin Kelly's concept of "protopia" - not a perfect endpoint but a steady accumulation of tomorrows only slightly better than today - shares essentially the same spirit as Popper's piecemeal engineering. My own read is that "designing a perfect world" in the AI era is less about coding one final blueprint and more about building a flexible, polycentric system that can keep adjudicating between different visions of flourishing.

7. What This Means for Readers in Korea

7.1 The Familiar Dilemma of Growth Versus Happiness

Over the past few decades, Korea has made striking gains on Pinker-style progress indicators - income, life expectancy, education. Yet a very different story emerges from youth life-satisfaction and meaning indicators, and from a birth rate that is among the lowest in the world. This is precisely the gap between Pinker (societal-level progress) and VanderWeele (individual-level meaning and relationships) playing out in real statistics. Korean society has, in effect, demonstrated with its own recent numbers that societal-level indicators improving does not automatically translate into flourishing that individuals actually feel.

7.2 From "How Much Have We Grown" to "What Are We Growing For"

Even Cowen's growth-first framework rests on the premise that growth itself is not the goal - it is a means toward the quality of life growth is supposed to support. The implication for Korea's policy debates is that alongside growth-rate figures, we need to keep asking whether that growth actually translates into individual meaning, relationships, and happiness.

7.3 The Same Question Is Coming for AI Policy

As Korea designs its own AI adoption and regulation, it cannot avoid the question of what that AI is actually optimizing for. An AI optimized purely for efficiency and productivity, and one that also accounts for relationships, meaning, and autonomy, build entirely different societies. That is exactly why this thought experiment never stays confined to abstract philosophy.

Conclusion

A perfect world is less a blueprint completed once and for all than a process of continuous negotiation between competing visions of flourishing. Pinker, VanderWeele, and Cowen arrive at different answers not because one of them is wrong, but because "flourishing" itself is inherently multidimensional and plural. Now that AI has given this old question real executional force, what matters is not finding the single correct answer, but preserving the social and technical room to keep weighing different answers against each other and adjusting course.
