Jeonse-gwon Registration vs. Move-in Report & Fixed Date

Introduction: Two Paths to Protecting Your Deposit

When signing a jeonse (전세) lease in Korea — the country's distinctive lump-sum deposit lease system — tenants face one urgent, practical question: how do I legally protect my deposit? In Korean rental practice, the answer splits into two main paths. One is securing opposability and a priority repayment right through a move-in report and a fixed date. The other is registering a jeonse-gwon (전세권, a statutory lien right) directly on the property registry. These two paths are often discussed as if choosing one excludes the other, but they are legally different in kind. Move-in report and fixed date are statutory protections granted to tenants under the Housing Lease Protection Act — a contractual-style right. Jeonse-gwon is a real right (물권) under the Civil Act, recorded directly on the property registry. That distinction is what drives every practical difference: whether landlord consent is required, when the protection takes effect, and how a tenant can act if the property goes to auction.

This guide compares the two systems head-to-head as of July 2026, explains which situations call for which tool, and covers how jeonse deposit guarantee insurance (HUG/SGI) fits alongside them. This article provides general legal information only, not advice for any specific transaction — always consult a professional before signing a contract.

1. Move-in Report and Fixed Date — Opposability and Priority Repayment

1.1 What Is Opposability?

Opposability (대항력) is the tenant's ability to assert "I am the legal tenant of this property" against a new owner, even if ownership changes hands through sale or auction. Under the Housing Lease Protection Act, opposability arises once the tenant takes possession of the property (moves in) and completes the move-in report (전입신고) — but the effect begins at midnight of the following day, not the moment of filing. That one-day gap matters: if the landlord registers a new mortgage on the day the tenant pays the balance and moves in, that mortgage could rank ahead of the tenant. For this reason, even when the balance payment and move-in report happen on the same day, it is standard practice to check the property registry again on the day of the balance payment.

1.2 Fixed Date and the Priority Repayment Right

A fixed date (확정일자) is an officially certified date stamp applied to the lease contract, obtainable at the local community service center (읍面동 or 행정복지센터), a registry office, or online through the Korean Court Internet Registry Office, simply by presenting the original lease contract. The fee is typically around 600 KRW, and no landlord consent or cooperation is required. A fixed date is the key requirement for the priority repayment right (우선변제권), but it does not complete that right on its own. The priority repayment right takes effect on whichever is later: (1) the date the opposability requirements — possession and move-in report — are met, or (2) the date the fixed date is obtained. In other words, obtaining a fixed date early does not help if move-in and the move-in report happen later; your priority ranking is delayed to match.

1.3 Why Ranking Order Matters

When a property is sold through auction or public sale, the proceeds are distributed according to the ranking of registered rights. If a tenant's priority repayment right ranks behind a mortgage, the mortgage holder is paid first, and the tenant only recovers whatever remains. This is exactly why checking the property registry before signing — to see whether senior mortgages exist and whether the sum of the maximum secured amount and the jeonse deposit approaches a dangerous percentage of the property's value — is essential.

2. Jeonse-gwon Registration — Protecting Your Deposit With a Real Right

2.1 What Is Jeonse-gwon?

Jeonse-gwon is a real right defined under Article 303 of the Civil Act: the right to occupy another person's real estate after paying a jeonse deposit, and to use and benefit from it according to its purpose. Unlike move-in report and fixed date, which are contractual-style protections created by a special statute (the Housing Lease Protection Act), jeonse-gwon is recorded directly in the property registry itself. Because it is a real right, it is enforceable against third parties from the moment of registration, and it survives a change in ownership.

2.2 The Strengths of Jeonse-gwon Registration

Registering jeonse-gwon provides three advantages that move-in report and fixed date cannot match. First, timing: opposability under move-in report/fixed date begins at midnight of the following day, while jeonse-gwon takes effect on the same day the registration application is filed. Second, direct foreclosure rights: a tenant who only holds a fixed date must first win a lawsuit for return of the deposit before filing for compulsory auction. A jeonse-gwon holder can file for foreclosure directly, based on the jeonse-gwon itself, with no separate judgment required. Third, no move-in report or actual residence is required. Tenants who cannot actually live at the property — for example, a parent leasing on behalf of a child studying abroad, a corporate tenant, or someone who must keep resident registration at another address — can still secure their rights purely through jeonse-gwon registration.

2.3 The Limits of Jeonse-gwon Registration

Jeonse-gwon registration has significant practical limits. The biggest obstacle is landlord consent. Because it is a formal registration, it cannot proceed without the property owner's cooperation — providing the registration certificate, seal certificate, and other documents, and cooperating with the filing process. If the landlord refuses, the tenant has no way to register jeonse-gwon unilaterally. In multi-unit houses (다가구주택) where the entire building shares a single registry with no separate registration per unit, registering jeonse-gwon on a specific unit can be practically difficult or impossible. There is also a well-established practical limit for detached and multi-unit houses where land and building are registered separately: jeonse-gwon generally only attaches to the building, not the land, so the tenant cannot recover priority proceeds from a sale of the land itself. By contrast, in sectional-ownership buildings such as apartments or officetels, the "no separate disposal" principle links the land share to the unit, which largely avoids this problem.

4. Which One Fits Your Situation?

4.1 When Move-in Report + Fixed Date Is Enough

For the vast majority of ordinary jeonse or wolse (monthly rent) tenants, move-in report and a fixed date provide sufficient protection. The cost is negligible, no landlord consent is needed, and any tenant who actually resides at the property has to file a move-in report anyway. The baseline strategy is to complete both on the same day right after signing, and to check the property registry three times: before signing, on the day of signing, and on the day of the balance payment.

4.2 When to Consider Jeonse-gwon Registration

Jeonse-gwon registration is worth actively considering when: the tenant cannot file a move-in report because they will not actually reside at the property (for example, leasing on behalf of a child or parent, or needing to maintain resident registration elsewhere); the tenant is a corporation, for which the concept of a move-in report does not apply; the landlord is willing to consent and the property is a sectional-ownership building (apartment, officetel) where the land-proceeds limitation is less of a concern; or the deposit is large enough that the value of being able to file for foreclosure without a prior judgment outweighs the registration cost.

4.3 Doing Both

Jeonse-gwon registration and move-in report/fixed date are not mutually exclusive. Combining both lets each system cover the other's weak points. Because the combination doubles both cost and the burden of landlord cooperation, in practice it tends to be chosen for high-value jeonse deposits or when there are red flags about the landlord's creditworthiness.

5. Adding Jeonse Deposit Guarantee Insurance (HUG/SGI) — A Third Layer of Protection

5.1 What Deposit Guarantee Insurance Does

Jeonse deposit guarantee insurance protects tenants when a landlord fails to return the deposit at the end of the lease. A guarantee institution — HUG (Korea Housing and Urban Guarantee Corporation), SGI Seoul Guarantee, or HF (Korea Housing Finance Corporation) — pays the tenant first (subrogated payment) and then pursues reimbursement from the landlord. All three institutions offer this coverage, but eligibility conditions, covered property types, and premium rates differ. Premiums are generally calculated as deposit amount × premium rate × (contract days / 365), and the rate varies by institution, property type, and the landlord's debt ratio, so it's important to get an exact quote from each institution before enrolling.

5.2 How It Relates to Jeonse-gwon and Fixed Date

Jeonse deposit guarantee insurance does not replace jeonse-gwon registration or a fixed date — it is a separate, complementary safeguard. Most guarantee products require move-in report and a fixed date as eligibility conditions, and having jeonse-gwon registered generally does not interfere with enrollment. The accurate way to think about it is a two-layer structure: secure your legal ranking first (fixed date and move-in report, or jeonse-gwon), then use guarantee insurance to cover the scenario where the landlord ultimately never returns the deposit at all.

Guarantee insurance applications can be denied for properties with an excessively high jeonse-to-value ratio, multiple senior mortgages, or a trust registration. A denial itself is a warning sign, so checking guarantee eligibility before signing a contract is also a useful way to screen for jeonse fraud risk.

6. Jeonse-gwon Registration Procedure and Cost

6.1 Required Documents

Jeonse-gwon registration typically requires the landlord's registration certificate (or title deed), seal certificate, a power of attorney with the registered seal affixed, a copy of resident registration, the jeonse-gwon establishment contract, and the tenant's ID and resident registration copy. Since none of this is possible without the landlord's cooperation, it is safest to include a clause in the lease contract itself committing the landlord to cooperate with jeonse-gwon registration.

6.2 Procedure

Registration applications can be filed in person at the relevant registry office or electronically through the Korean Court Internet Registry Office. Most tenants delegate this to a certified law scrivener (beopmusa), since documents from both landlord and tenant are required — the general recommendation is to use a professional rather than attempting it alone.

6.3 Cost

For jeonse-gwon registration, the registration and license tax is 0.2% of the jeonse deposit amount, plus a local education tax equal to 20% of that tax amount. For example, on a 100 million KRW deposit, that's a registration and license tax of 200,000 KRW plus a local education tax of 40,000 KRW — 240,000 KRW in taxes alone. On top of that, a registration filing fee and a scrivener's fee apply separately; scrivener's fees are tiered by the taxable base (the deposit amount) and vary by office and region, so it's worth getting quotes from multiple sources in advance. Compared to the roughly 600 KRW fee for a fixed date, jeonse-gwon registration is considerably more expensive, so tenants should weigh deposit size and risk against that cost.

7. How This Connects to Jeonse Fraud Prevention — Small Tenant Priority Repayment

7.1 The Small Tenant Priority Repayment System

Separate from the ordinary priority repayment right tied to a fixed date, the Housing Lease Protection Act includes a "small tenant priority repayment" system that pays a fixed amount to tenants below a certain deposit threshold ahead of every other rights holder. Under the most recent amendment (the February 2023 enforcement decree), Seoul recognizes small-tenant status for deposits up to 165 million KRW, with priority repayment up to 55 million KRW; the Seoul metropolitan over-concentration control zone (excluding Seoul itself), Yongin, Hwaseong, and Gimpo recognize deposits up to 145 million KRW, with priority repayment up to 48 million KRW. These thresholds have been revised repeatedly over time, and — importantly — the applicable date is not when the tenant moved in, but when the property's first secured right (such as a senior mortgage) was registered. So confirming actual eligibility requires checking both the property registry and the current enforcement decree together.

7.2 How Jeonse-gwon Relates to Small Tenant Priority Repayment

Small tenant priority repayment applies to tenants who have met the opposability requirements (move-in report and possession) under the Housing Lease Protection Act — it is a separate system from jeonse-gwon registration itself. A tenant who registers jeonse-gwon but skips the move-in report can still receive ranked distribution as a jeonse-gwon holder, but whether they also qualify for small tenant priority repayment needs to be checked separately. This is why a tenant who actually resides at the property should complete the move-in report as well, even if they also register jeonse-gwon — doing both secures multiple layers of protection at once.

7.3 Checking the Property Registry Is the Baseline, Regardless

Whether you register jeonse-gwon or rely only on a fixed date, checking the property registry three times — before signing, on the day of signing, and on the balance payment date — to confirm mortgages, attachments, trust registrations, and ownership changes is a common baseline step. This document is available from the Korean Court Internet Registry Office (iros.go.kr).

8. Conclusion — A Checklist for Choosing

Jeonse-gwon registration and move-in report/fixed date are not a matter of "only one being correct" — they are tools to choose or combine based on the tenant's circumstances. For an ordinary tenant who actually lives at the property and would struggle to obtain landlord consent, move-in report and fixed date — supplemented by jeonse deposit guarantee insurance if needed — are often sufficient. Conversely, if actual residence isn't possible, the deposit is large, or the landlord is cooperative, jeonse-gwon registration is worth evaluating alongside the statutory protections. Whichever path you choose, the same fundamentals apply: check the property registry before signing, review the jeonse-to-value ratio, and complete the move-in report and fixed date on the balance payment date.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need both jeonse-gwon registration and a fixed date?

Legally, either one alone can protect a tenant, but each has different strengths, so some tenants combine both. Move-in report and fixed date cost almost nothing and require no landlord consent, making them the default choice for most tenants. Jeonse-gwon registration requires landlord consent but grants stronger rights, including the ability to file for foreclosure directly. Combining both is worth considering for large deposits or when the tenant cannot actually reside at the property.

What if the landlord refuses to allow jeonse-gwon registration?

Jeonse-gwon registration requires the property owner's consent and cooperation with paperwork, since it is a formal registration on the property registry. A tenant cannot register it unilaterally. If the landlord refuses, the realistic alternative is to focus on securing opposability and priority repayment rights through move-in report and fixed date, and to supplement that protection with jeonse deposit guarantee insurance (HUG, SGI, or HF) if needed.

I got a fixed date early, but my move-in report was delayed. What happens to my priority?

The priority repayment right takes effect on whichever date is later: the date the opposability requirements (occupying the property and completing the move-in report) are met, or the date the fixed date is obtained. Even if you obtain a fixed date on the day you sign the contract, if your move-in and move-in report are delayed, your priority ranking is delayed by the same amount. It is safest to complete the balance payment, move-in, and move-in report all on the same day.

Roughly how much does jeonse-gwon registration cost?

The registration and license tax is 0.2% of the jeonse deposit amount, plus a local education tax equal to 20% of that tax. For a deposit of 100 million KRW, taxes alone come to roughly 240,000 KRW. On top of that, a registration filing fee and a licensed law scrivener's (beopmusa) fee apply, which vary by office, region, and deposit size, so it is worth comparing quotes before proceeding.

If I have jeonse deposit guarantee insurance (HUG/SGI), do I still need a fixed date or jeonse-gwon registration?

No — jeonse deposit guarantee insurance does not replace a fixed date, move-in report, or jeonse-gwon registration. It is a separate, supplementary safeguard. Most guarantee products require move-in report and a fixed date as eligibility conditions. Securing your legal priority (fixed date and move-in report, or jeonse-gwon registration) and enrolling in guarantee insurance should be treated as complementary steps taken together.
