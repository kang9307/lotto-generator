Don't Build Your Own Monitoring — Zero-Cost Observability With Sentry and PostHog

A free-SaaS observability strategy for small teams and solo developers - scope, limits, and self-hosting trade-offs

Jul 4, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: Why "Don't Build It, Bolt It On" Matters Now

Any startup or solo developer who ships a new service quickly runs into the same question: how do I know when something breaks, and how do I see where users are dropping off? The instinctive answer for many developers is to reach for a self-hosted monitoring stack - Prometheus for metrics, Grafana for dashboards, a separate stack for logs. That instinct is usually a trap. The moment you stand up one more piece of infrastructure, that infrastructure itself becomes something you have to operate. You end up with the paradox of having to figure out, when an alert doesn't fire, whether that's a service outage or a monitoring outage.

I want to argue for a different answer, from the perspective of small teams and solo developers. The conclusion up front: observability is support infrastructure, and in the early stage it makes more sense to hand it to a combination of free SaaS tools and spend your limited engineering time on the product's actual domain features. As of July 2026, the leading combination for this is Sentry for error and performance tracking, and PostHog for product analytics and session replay. This piece breaks down how the two tools divide labor, how far their free tiers actually stretch, the trade-offs against self-hosting, and the privacy and data-sovereignty questions that come with "free."

1. How Sentry and PostHog Divide Labor - Errors vs. Behavior

"Monitoring" gets used as a catch-all term, but it really has to answer two separate questions: is the code broken, and what are users actually doing. Forcing both into a single tool tends to leave both half-served.

1.1 Sentry: Catching the Point Where Things Break

Sentry specializes in capturing exceptions, crashes, and server 5xx errors along with stack traces. A single SDK per language or framework gets you instant reports for frontend JS errors, backend exceptions, and mobile crashes. It also offers performance tracing, letting you inspect slow transactions or N+1 queries at the span level.

1.2 PostHog: Seeing What Users Actually Do

PostHog is a product-analytics tool that bundles event-based user behavior tracking, funnels, session replay, feature flags, and surveys into one product. Where Sentry looks at what broke, PostHog looks at where users get stuck and where they drop off. The two questions don't overlap.

1.3 Why You Should Run Both

Running both lets you cover incident response and product improvement, each with a dedicated tool, without taking on either as self-hosted infrastructure. The failure pattern I've seen most often in practice is the opposite: trying to make one general-purpose tool - a hand-built dashboard, say - do both jobs, and ending up with both done shallowly. Splitting the roles is already half the design.

2. How Far the Free Tiers Actually Go (as of July 2026, subject to change)

Exact free-tier numbers shift with vendor policy over time. What follows reflects the scope confirmed as of research in July 2026 - always re-check the current figures on each service's official pricing page before signing up.

2.1 Sentry's Developer Plan

Sentry's free Developer plan includes 5,000 errors per month, 1 user, 50 session replays per month, and 30-day data retention. Tracing (performance spans) is also available within a separate free allowance, but the exact span figure is reported inconsistently across sources, so this piece won't commit to a specific number. The 1-user limit is usually the first wall you hit on the free plan - the moment you become a team, you need the paid Team plan (starting at $26/month).

2.2 PostHog's Free Plan

PostHog offers 1 million events per month, 5,000 session replays (2,500 for mobile), 1 million feature-flag requests, 100,000 tracked errors, and 1,500 survey responses, all free. Data retention on the free plan is one year. A million events a month is generous enough that many early-stage startups reportedly run six months to a year without needing to upgrade.

2.3 Making the Free Tier Last - What Not to Send Matters More Than What to Send

Free quotas ultimately come down to deciding what not to send, not what to send. The original author describes sending only server 5xx errors to Sentry while treating 4xx client errors as noise to filter out, and turning trace sampling to zero when performance tracing isn't immediately needed. I agree with this direction. Especially in the early stage, holding to the single principle that "what you don't send matters more than what you do" can push back the point where you exhaust your free quota by several multiples. On the PostHog side, the same logic applies to sampling session replay conditionally around error sessions or specific funnel drop-off points, rather than recording every single session.

3. The Trade-off Against Self-Hosting (Prometheus/Grafana)

The question "if it's free, why doesn't everyone just self-host" only gets answered once you weigh what self-hosting gives you against what it takes away.

3.1 What Self-Hosting Gives You

A Prometheus-and-Grafana combination (often extended into the LGTM stack) carries no license cost, keeps telemetry from ever leaving your own infrastructure, and lets you design metrics, logs, and dashboards to any level of detail you want. If your Kubernetes environment already has platform engineers comfortable with Helm and PromQL, self-hosting is a natural extension of what you already run.

3.2 The Hidden Cost of Self-Hosting

The problem is that "free" only applies to the software license. In practice you're running and tuning four or five separate systems - collector, storage, query engine, dashboards, alerting - plus the learning curve for PromQL and LogQL, and the ongoing operational burden of managing object storage, compaction, and retention policy. That burden gets billed not in lines of code but in people's time. For a solo developer or a team of three or four, this operational cost can easily outgrow the actual product work.

3.3 Which Side Is Right - A Team-Size Judgment

My own criterion is simple. If your team already runs Kubernetes and has resident platform engineers, the marginal cost of self-hosting is low enough that a Grafana stack makes sense. If, on the other hand, you're an early-stage startup, a solo developer, or a side project that can't dedicate staff to observability, the free SaaS combination is far better. The core of this strategy is that if you outgrow the free tier later, you'll likely have revenue by then too, which gives you the grounds to absorb the cost of upgrading.

4. Privacy and Data Sovereignty - Free Comes With a Price

Observability data carries user behavior, IP addresses, and sometimes sensitive information buried inside error stacks. Where it's stored matters as much as the price tag.

4.1 Where the Data Actually Lives

PostHog lets you pick a US (Virginia) or EU (Frankfurt) region at signup, which helps for GDPR-conscious products serving European users. Sentry's SaaS also offers US and EU regions, but it's worth noting that picking a region doesn't automatically guarantee regulatory compliance on its own.

4.2 Open-Source Self-Hosting as a Third Option

A lesser-known card is that both tools ship open-source editions you can self-host. If keeping code or user data from ever leaving your own servers matters to your team, there's a middle ground between fully-managed SaaS and building from scratch: run the open-source edition yourself. That said, PostHog officially recommends self-hosting only up to roughly 300,000 events per month, and suggests moving to the cloud beyond that due to scaling complexity - meaning the "don't build it" principle only partially applies to the self-hosted open-source path.

4.3 What to Check in Regulated Industries

In heavily regulated domains like healthcare or finance, confirm the data processing agreement (DPA), the subprocessor list, and any region-pinning option before wiring in free SaaS. In my view, a team that has to worry about this level of detail is likely already close to outgrowing the free tier anyway.

5. Adoption Tips - How to Start Today

5.1 Start With Minimal Setup

Sentry starts collecting errors within minutes of installing the SDK and registering a DSN. PostHog attaches to a web app with a single snippet just as quickly. Notably, in a React Native or Expo environment, PostHog can be applied without an app rebuild, which is reported to lower the adoption barrier compared to alternatives like Firebase Analytics that require a native rebuild.

5.2 Managing Noise - Sampling and Filtering

Set filtering rules from day one. Send only server 5xx to Sentry, and turn on performance tracing only once you actually need it. Starting PostHog session replay with conditional sampling instead of recording every session pushes back the point where you exhaust your free quota. Build-time integrations like automatic source-map upload can conflict with your release build pipeline, so verify them in staging first.

5.3 When to Move to a Paid Plan

Exceeding the free tier isn't a failure - it's a signal. Filling 5,000 errors on Sentry or 1 million events on PostHog every month means real users are already attached to your product. At that point you likely have revenue too, so it makes more sense to treat the paid upgrade as the next stage of growth rather than as a cost.

Conclusion: A Realistic Picture of Zero-Cost Observability

"Don't build your own monitoring" doesn't mean never self-host. It's closer to a prioritization rule for the early stage: decide where your limited resources should go. Hand errors to Sentry and behavior to PostHog, learn to survive inside the free quota - which mostly means learning what not to send - and a small team can build a genuinely solid observability setup for zero dollars. But keep two things in mind from day one: your data lives in an external region, and growth will eventually force a real decision between upgrading to paid or reconsidering self-hosting. Free SaaS is a starting point, not a destination.
