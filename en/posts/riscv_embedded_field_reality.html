The RISC-V Argument, and Why the Same Design Produces Opposite Conclusions

Change the constraints and a different objection is the one that lands on your invoice

2026-08-17 Dev & Infra

Two people looking at the same design and reaching opposite verdicts

Open a bill of materials to pick an embedded board today and RISC-V is somewhere on the list. Search the name and you will run into two pieces of writing with opposite temperaments almost immediately. One goes through the instruction set point by point and argues it was designed badly. The other answers that those objections barely register in the world it works in. Both were written by people who actually handle silicon, and both bring specifics.

The usual reaction is to pick a winner. But read them side by side and something becomes obvious fast. The two authors are not giving different answers to one question. They are answering different questions. One stands where cycles, code size, and binary compatibility are the scarce resources. The other stands where getting the part into your hands at all is the scarce resource. The verdicts diverge not because of the design but because of the constraints underneath.

So this article does not crown a winner. It does not conclude that RISC-V is better, and it does not conclude the other camp is better. Instead it separates out what each piece assumes, then shows which objection turns into an actual cost when those assumptions change. At the end it looks at how the same argument reads differently inside Korea's embedded and fabless landscape. Investment judgments about specific companies are out of scope.

1. What the critique objected to, within the confirmed scope

Start with what the critical piece actually said. This article does not translate it and does not reproduce its phrasing; the points below are compressed in my own words and limited to items the original actually raises.

  • One design for everyone — a ten-cent microcontroller and a server-class core sharing one foundation leaves compromises that suit neither end well.
  • Too much is optional — multiply and divide, user and supervisor modes, and control status registers (CSRs) are all optional. Without the CSR extension there is no spec-compliant way to handle an interrupt at all. The misa register meant for feature detection is itself optional and unreadable from lower privilege levels.
  • Interrupt entry cost — the piece works out a minimum of 44 cycles before any real work begins, and sets it against 27 cycles on a Cortex-M0.
  • Missing instructions — with no test-a-bit-and-branch instruction, the author counted 35,393 places in one Linux kernel binary where the pattern expands into two instructions and clobbers a temporary register. Bitfield extract and insert expand into three to six.
  • Addressing modes — the base plus scaled index form common in array access does not exist, so it splits into shift, add, and load. The SHxADD family that closes this gap arrived as an extension more than two years after the fact.
  • Compressed instruction encoding — nine formats in the 16-bit set, with immediate encoding differing per instruction. Byte store offsets span only 0 through 3, with one bit hardwired to zero.
  • Scattered immediates — immediate bits are spread across the instruction word rather than kept contiguous. The piece lists the bit order of the J-type form to show how little pattern there is to find.
  • Encoding collision — the byte sequence 0xA002 decodes as a floating point store on a core with the double precision extension and as a jump table instruction on a core with a different compressed extension. Upgrading a processor changes what a valid binary means.

Two more items sit alongside those. The system timer is memory mapped at an implementation-defined address rather than exposed through CSRs, leaving programmers to guess where peripherals live. And on the profile offered as the remedy, the piece notes that several boards routinely recommended to buyers do not actually meet the newest profile.

Left as a flat list, every item reads with equal weight. So sort them by kind first. Some are about aesthetics, some are about code size and latency, and some are about whether a binary can be shipped at all. The third kind is the heavy one.

2. Where the response stands, in a world that starts when the part arrives

The response appeared on August 16, 2026. Its author is an embedded engineer and educator based in a Caribbean island nation, and he describes his own position as third world in both the title and the body. That phrasing is his own, so it stays intact only where quoted; the body of this article uses the neutral description of a region with heavy procurement constraints.

His premise is not instruction encoding. It is the conditions under which a part reaches your bench.

  • Shipping dwarfs the part — getting one dollar-priced chip delivered costs between 60 and 200 dollars. Cheap hardware availability, he writes, is worth more than encoding elegance.
  • Shipping has a floor — a clarification he added during discussion. A fixed cost of roughly 60 dollars sits underneath every order, so per-unit shipping collapses as order size grows. Cheaper parts let the same budget buy deeper, which is where the logic comes from.
  • The ladder is continuous — from ten-cent microcontrollers to Linux-capable single board computers, registers, calling conventions, and toolchains carry through.
  • The other camp splits by tier — Cortex-M0, M7, and A-series behave differently enough that moving between tiers means relearning the privilege model.
  • Licensing draws the feature boundary — having to buy a higher core grade to attach a memory management unit or supervisor mode is recast as a business boundary rather than a technical limit.
  • Total cost of entry — a learning stack covering a microcontroller, a dual-core part, a secure system on chip, and a Linux board comes in under 100 dollars delivered, against the observation that equivalent tooling elsewhere runs twice that before any hardware.

He answers the fragmentation charge too. Fragmentation and extensibility come out of the very same decision, and the point of that decision is that a small chip should not carry features meant for a large core.

The pushback belongs here as well, for balance. The most common objection in discussion was that if shipping runs 60 to 200 dollars, the gap between one dollar and ten cents is diluted, not amplified; the fixed-floor clarification is his answer to that. Another objection is blunter: parts from the other camp ship at the same rates. Some commenters compared cores with different extension mixes to two languages from the same root, mutually recognizable but useless for shipping binaries without recompilation. On performance, the observation was that the fastest boards available now sit around the level of a popular small board from the other camp, paired with the counterpoint that this gap reflects engineering investment rather than any intrinsic ceiling in the instruction set.

3. The assumptions underneath, put side by side

Here the two sets of premises meet, axis by axis. This table is the spine of the article. No column is marked correct. The same sentence can be true in one column and meaningless in the other.

Whether alternatives existA better core is always available to pickAvailable, yes, but at different arrival times and prices

The table exposes how much of the dispute comes from one word carrying two meanings. To the critic, compatibility is a property of binaries. To the responder, it is a property of people. One asks whether the same image runs on a different chip; the other asks whether someone who learned this chip can move to that one. Those two answers are independent of each other.

4. Where the better design and the usable design part ways

This is the center of the argument. Design quality is a closed problem. Put the specification and the encoding tables on a desk and you can usually settle which is tighter. Adoptability is an open problem. Price, stock, customs, documentation, teaching material, and the skill level of the people involved all enter as variables. The overlap between the two problems is narrower than it looks.

A practical way to hold this: the conditions under which encoding and instruction selection actually land on an invoice are narrow. The argument changes your conclusion only if one of the following applies.

  • Code size is pressing against the flash ceiling — a few percent of density moves you a part grade up or down, and compressed instruction details land straight on unit cost.
  • Deadlines are measured in microseconds — interrupt entry cycles go directly into the deadline budget. Numbers like 44 against 27 stop being a matter of taste.
  • One binary ships to many devices — the extension mix becomes the deployment target, and encoding collisions plus weak feature detection hit head on. Teams writing their own assembler or static analyzer also inherit format count and immediate scatter as work.

The conditions that put procurement logic in front are just as clear.

  • Lead times are long and unpredictable — the part that arrives beats the optimal part. That is a schedule fact, not a preference.
  • Low volume or prototype stage — entry cost outweighs unit cost. The price of one debugger competes with the whole prototype budget.
  • Training people is the goal — putting the same kit in twenty pairs of hands multiplies the unit price by twenty.
  • End-of-life risk is real — whether you have somewhere to port to comes before design elegance.

The intersection of those two lists is small. Most projects sit on one list only. Borrow someone else's conclusion without checking which list you are on and you end up selecting parts against a criterion that will never be billed to you. Half the reason this argument feels unproductive lives right there.

5. The ladder, and what one instruction set really carries upward

The real claim in the response is not about price. It is about the ladder: one instruction set from a ten-cent part all the way to a board running Linux. How far does that hold?

What carries over:

  • Base integer instructions and register names are the same, so assembly knowledge from a small core reads directly on a large one.
  • The calling convention is the same. Argument passing and return value rules do not need relearning.
  • The shape of the toolchain is the same: one compiler family, one debugger family, one way to specify a target.

What does not carry over is equally clear:

  • Privilege structure differs by tier. Supervisor mode and memory management units are absent from small parts, and the specification does not promise them.
  • Interrupt handling differs. Small cores and application processors use different interrupt controller structures, with vendor-specific arrangements mixed in.
  • Extension mixes differ. Code that uses vector or bit-manipulation extensions does not simply run one tier down.
  • Memory model and cache behavior differ. Code that was fine on a single core breaks quietly on multiple ones.

Put plainly: the ladder is true for people and false for binaries. The continuity of the learning path genuinely exists, and in education and workforce contexts it is worth real money. But read that continuity as deployment compatibility and the claim collapses. That misreading is exactly what the critic is angry about, and the responder's frustration is that he never made that claim. This one distinction explains half the argument.

6. What an open ISA changes and what it leaves alone

The word open generates more confusion than anything else here. Split it. What genuinely changes:

  • Reading and implementing the specification takes no contract. Deciding to build a core does not begin with a legal review.
  • The designer picks the feature mix. There is no structure forcing you up a product grade to attach a memory management unit. This is the point the response calls a business boundary.
  • Building and distributing teaching or research cores needs no permission. Lecture material and a working core can live in the same repository, and the barrier to entering the core design market drops with it.

The list of things it leaves alone is longer. An open instruction set does not mean a free chip.

  • Manufacturing cost is untouched. Masks, packaging, test, and yield loss have nothing to do with the instruction set.
  • Design cost is untouched. Automation licenses, process design kits, and verification staff still cost money. A free specification and cheap silicon are separate matters.
  • What sets the price does not change. A ten-cent part is cheap because the die is small and the volume is huge, not because a royalty was waived.
  • Software porting cost does not shrink. A larger menu of optional extensions can enlarge the set of combinations that must be validated.
  • Documentation and toolchain quality remain vendor-set. An open specification does not improve anyone's datasheet. In practice what stalls a project is usually the vendor reference code and examples, not the specification.

One thing worth naming. The licensing freedom the response emphasizes reaches the people building chips before it reaches the people buying them. For a developer purchasing finished parts, that freedom arrives only indirectly, through more vendors and more varieties producing cheaper options downstream. Leave the indirection out and say it is cheap because it is open, and the sentence becomes false.

7. Fragmentation and profiles: the flip side of extensibility, and how far the remedy reaches

The response calls fragmentation and extensibility two faces of the same decision. That is half right.

The right half first. Keeping a small part from carrying features meant for a large core is the legitimate purpose of optional extensions. Forcing a multiplier onto a control application that never multiplies wastes die area and power for nothing. So far, so intended.

The wrong half is the asymmetry of the cost. Gates saved on the hardware side are saved once, on that chip. Combinations added on the software side multiply across compilers, distributions, and test matrices. Saved once and paid many times do not belong on the same scale. And the size of the bill varies by layer. In embedded work that compiles for one device, fragmentation costs close to nothing. For anyone publishing a general-purpose distribution, it can decide whether the business works at all. The same fragmentation is billed at wildly different amounts depending on where you sit.

One item should not be filed under fragmentation at all. Encoding collision is a different category of problem. Choosing not to use an extension is under a developer's control; the same byte sequence decoding to a different valid instruction depending on the core is not. That cannot be justified by the argument that small chips stay lean. The freedom to choose extensions and the collision of the resulting choices are separate things. The response does not answer this item directly, and it remains the heaviest of the objections.

What the official remedy, profiles, aims at and what it misses

The formal answer to fragmentation is the profile. RISC-V International announced that the RVA23 profile was ratified on October 21, 2024. It targets 64-bit application processors running rich operating system stacks and makes the vector and hypervisor extensions mandatory, with the announcement noting that the vector extension serves as the baseline for the Android application binary interface.

What a profile does, stated precisely: it does not remove fragmentation, it declares the minimum set that software may assume. Mandatory extensions are used as present; optional ones are detected at run time and used for optimization. That is what pulls the deployment target back into one shape. The announcement states the goals plainly as reducing vendor lock-in and lowering software development cost.

Two things sit outside its reach. First, chips already sold. A profile organizes what comes next and does not apply backward. This is exactly the critic's point: boards widely recommended to newcomers do not meet the newest profile, so the market splits into an almost-compliant tier and a later one. That objection is not aimed at the profile's design but at the lag, and lag cannot be specified away. Second, the layer. The RVA family targets application processors, as the name says. The hottest part of this argument is the ten-cent microcontroller tier, and the cheap-part layer is not the layer these profiles answer. Citing ratification as proof that the fragmentation argument is over misaligns the layer of the dispute with the layer of the remedy. Calling profiles useless because of that misalignment overshoots in the other direction, since the upper layer really did get a single deployment target out of it.

Translated into practice, the axes look like this. Find your situation on the left and check the middle column first. This is not a table for picking a camp. It is a table for picking which metric to measure first.

One rule for using the table. If two or more rows apply, resolve the conflicting axes first. Hard real time combined with long lead times means narrowing to parts that meet the latency budget, then checking availability within that set. Reverse the order and you end up holding a part you can buy but cannot ship on time.

9. How the argument reads inside Korea's embedded and fabless landscape

Read the response from Korea and the first impression is usually the same. It is hard to feel why the shipping cost matters so much. Local distribution and delivery conditions differ from the premises the response is built on. So its central evidence fades into background for a Korean reader, leaving only the encoding and fragmentation dispute behind. That is not because the response is wrong. It is because its premise is invisible here. Lose the premise and only the conclusion survives, and a conclusion without its premise looks like a matter of taste.

The axes that genuinely bind here are different. Long-term supply guarantees, end-of-life handling, certification, and people. In an export-oriented product structure, one end-of-life notice can cascade into recertification, and that cost buries any difference in unit price several times over. Under those conditions the more important question is not which instruction set is better designed but whether an alternate vendor using the same instruction set actually exists. That is also where the value of an open specification shows up most clearly in the Korean context.

A note on the domestic picture, with its timing stated plainly. The national research institute has published a RISC-V-based chip design platform aimed at lowering the barrier for smaller fabless firms. Industry reporting has described a major electronics maker commercializing RISC-V-based communication modems and image sensors, another using it inside neural processing units, and a domestic startup shipping a storage controller built on RISC-V cores. These points rest on earlier reporting and were not confirmed against current product disclosures.

One pattern is visible across them. Where this instruction set has been adopted domestically, it usually sits in controllers, accelerators, and subsystems rather than in the processor that defines the product. Those positions share a trait: they do not host a general-purpose operating system, they do not ship binaries to outsiders, and they do not require a software ecosystem. In other words, adoption went to the places where fragmentation is barely billed at all. That is not a way of winning the argument but a way of choosing a layer where the argument does not arise, and it is the thesis of this article showing up in local practice.

To be explicit, this article carries no view on any company's prospects or investment merit. The examples above illustrate where adoption sits, nothing more.

10. What could not be confirmed

Everything left out or narrowed for lack of confirmable grounding, collected in one place.

  • Part prices and shipping figures cited in both pieces were not cross-checked against seller listings. They vary by region, date, quantity, and customs handling.
  • The measurement conditions behind the 44 and 27 cycle comparison were not reproduced outside the original. Interrupt entry cost depends heavily on core implementation and memory latency.
  • Market share figures for RISC-V against the other camp are not used here. Definitions differ by tracker, and quoting one number invites misreading.
  • Specific licensing terms and amounts are contractual and not publicly verifiable. The business boundary framing is presented strictly as the response's argument.
  • The status of profile work aimed at the microcontroller tier was not confirmed against ratification documents. Only the application processor profile family is described here.

Closing: the two pieces are not answering the same question

The most upvoted comment in discussion described the shape of this argument better than either piece did. One side is looking at the high-performance tier that climbs toward laptops and servers; the other is looking at the range from very cheap microcontrollers up to low-end application processors. Those tiers have different scarce resources, different definitions of failure, and even different meanings for the word compatibility. Divergent verdicts are the natural result.

What a practitioner keeps is this. Which instruction set is better has no answer; which cost lands on my invoice first does. Hold the first question and you end up borrowing someone else's premises. Hold the second and one table finishes the job. Design quality is settled on the specification. Adoption is settled in warehouses, budgets, and schedules. Both are real. They just do not happen in the same room.
