The Trigger — A Pen Name on a Cartoon He Did Not Draw

On October 5, 2026, Nieman Lab published an article titled “ChatGPT is adding real cartoonists’ signatures to fake New Yorker cartoons.” The byline is Andrew Deck, and the piece was introduced on GeekNews on October 6. The subheading says that OpenAI’s chatbot is not just imitating the magazine’s style but is also falsely attributing AI-generated images to real artists.

The first example in the article is a single-panel cartoon that went viral on Twitter in the days after two entertainers died in late August. According to the article, one tweet of the cartoon received 25,000 likes, while other versions of the image circulated on Facebook, Instagram, and Bluesky. In the bottom right corner of the cartoon was the pen name “BLOPER,” which is the signature of New Yorker cartoonist Brendan Loper. The article states that Loper did not draw the cartoon or sign it, and that the image was generated by ChatGPT and first posted to Facebook by a fan. In the comments section of her own post, the article says, the poster wrote that she had simply asked ChatGPT to make “a New Yorker-style cartoon.”

Loper told the reporter, “I had people writing to me about this cartoon, asking if it was mine.” According to the article, the first message was a text from his twin brother, whose friend had seen the AI-generated image. Emails and direct messages on social media from strangers followed. “It was very surreal,” he said.

Two things need to be read separately in this report. One is that the drawing style of a magazine is being imitated. The other is that the name of a specific person is being placed on a drawing. The cartoonists and the law professor quoted in the article treat these as distinct matters. The machine-readable marks that a generation tool attaches on its own, namely metadata and watermarks, belong to yet another layer, which is covered in The Marks on a Generated Image.

Whose Signatures Appeared — Forum Posts and the Reporter’s Tests

The article says Loper is not the only one. The figure “more than 15” belongs to the reporter. The original sentence reads: “In all, I documented more than 15 New Yorker cartoonists whose signatures have been used by OpenAI’s image generator without permission or compensation.” It is a count the reporter documented, and it rests on two kinds of evidence: images posted to online forums, and the reporter’s own tests. The article does not publish the full list of names, the number of test runs, or the prompts that were entered.

  • Images in the forums. The article says that the same online forums hold dozens of images carrying the signatures of other prominent contributors, and it names Harry Bliss, Emily Flake, Joe Dator, Pat Byrnes, Peter Vey, and Jason Adam Katzenstein.
  • The reporter’s tests. In the reporter’s own tests, ChatGPT output cartoons with the signatures of George Booth, Liza Donnelly, Ellis Rosen, and Saul Steinberg. The article describes them as among the magazine’s most established cartoonists, past and present.
  • Older contributors. The signatures also included those of older cartoonists such as Warren Miller, who published in the 1960s.
  • Emily Flake. She has been a New Yorker contributor since 2008. Several ChatGPT-generated cartoons posted online are signed with her pen name, “e. flake.”
  • Pat Byrnes. In 1998 he published his first cartoon in The New Yorker under the pen name “P.Byrnes.” Since he first began drawing cartoons as a teenager, he has always signed with a period at the end. Across the more than a dozen ChatGPT cartoons the reporter found signed with Byrnes’ name, every one ended with a period.
  • Joe Dator. He has been a New Yorker contributor for the past 20 years.
  • Jason Adam Katzenstein. He has been publishing cartoons in The New Yorker since 2014.

The reporter lists the following observations from his own tests.

  • House style. ChatGPT regularly generated cartoons with the hallmarks of the magazine’s house style: a black-and-white, single-panel illustration with a sparse background, clean linework, and a sardonic caption, or at least an approximation of one. The article refers to the publication as a 101-year-old magazine.
  • Quality of the captions. Some of ChatGPT’s punch lines do not make sense.
  • No signature at all. Not every image came with a signature.
  • Meaningless pen names. Some images included pen names that were gibberish.
  • Signatures of real contributors. The signatures that did appear were regularly those of real contributors to the magazine.

Flake’s remarks show that the drawing and the signature behave differently. “I can see the DNA of my work in there, but it still doesn’t look like something I drew,” she said, adding, “It’s kind of like seeing a distant cousin.” According to the article, the character stances and shading contained echoes of Flake’s visual identity, but she said that overall the images looked more like amalgamations of several different cartoonists’ illustration styles. Each signature, on the other hand, was undeniably hers. She put it this way: “It’s like somebody attributed a quote to me that I didn’t say.”

What a Signature Does — Separating Style Mimicry from Signature Reproduction

The article says that cartoon signatures today are more than a byline. In an era when cartoons are regularly stripped from their original context and circulated on blogs and social media without proper licensing, a signature can also act as a watermark that ties an illustration to its original creator.

Loper described it in these words: “The signature for me is the certificate of authenticity. I only put it on work that I’m putting on the internet that I feel needs to be recognized as from my hand.” He continued: “If I put my name on it, it’s official, it’s mine. It really is a brand. It’s the mark that I made.”

According to the article, several cartoonists the reporter spoke to differentiated ChatGPT’s ability to mimic their illustration style from the reproduction of their signature. “I’ve had people hack my credit card,” said Joe Dator, adding, “That feels like less of a violation than this. When they hacked my credit card, they didn’t dress up like me.” Katzenstein considers the reproduction of his signature to be more than a violation of intellectual property; in the article’s words, to him it is closer to false impersonation. “[ChatGPT] is attaching my name to work that I do not endorse or like,” he said.

The period in Byrnes’ signature is placed at the end of the article as an illustration of what a signature is. “It was like signing a letter,” he said of it. “Here’s my statement — my gift to you to accept or reject.” The statements in the article can be divided into two columns as follows.

OpenAI’s Response and What Changed Afterward

The article carries a statement from an OpenAI spokesperson. The statement, and the changes the article describes after the reporter raised the issue, are listed below.

  • First sentence of the statement. “We believe the future of creativity is one that is fundamentally human, and our focus is on building tools that empower human creativity and creators.”
  • Second sentence of the statement. “We very much appreciate the community flagging bugs and unintended behavior by our models, so we can address them.”
  • A new message. After the reporter notified OpenAI of the issue, ChatGPT began to output a new message when asked to generate New Yorker-style cartoons: “This prompt may violate our guardrails concerning similarity to third-party content.”
  • State at publication. Even so, the article says that as of its publication ChatGPT continues to sign some of the generic cartoons it generates with the names of real New Yorker cartoonists.
  • The unanswered question. OpenAI declined to answer the reporter’s questions about how its models might have ingested signatures from The New Yorker’s archives during the training process.

The two sentences of the statement quoted in the article do not contain the word signature. The statement expresses appreciation for reports of bugs and unintended behavior in general, and it does not explain why signatures are reproduced. On the cause, what the article offers is a single sentence from the reporter. It says, “It’s possible they were scraped from the internet,” and it cites, as background, court documents released last month in the ongoing New York Times v. OpenAI lawsuit. The sentence is framed as a possibility, and the article contains no statement from a technical expert explaining the path by which the signatures entered the model.

Elsewhere, the article uses OpenAI’s verification tool. According to OpenAI’s description, the tool checks whether an uploaded file contains provenance signals associated with OpenAI tools, and the supported signals are C2PA metadata and SynthID watermarks. The description says that a detected signal indicates the content likely originated from OpenAI tools, and that it does not determine whether the content is accurate or presented in the correct context. It also notes that signals relying on metadata can be removed. What can be read from this is a difference of layers (an interpretation). The verification tool looks at a signal that the generation tool placed in the file, whereas a signature drawn inside the picture is a mark read by people. The former answers the question of which tool made the file; the latter is read by a viewer as an answer to who drew it.

The New Yorker’s Account — The Licensing Deal and the Contributor Contracts

On the magazine’s side, the article describes two kinds of contract. One is the agreement between the parent company and OpenAI. The other is the contributor contract between the magazine and its cartoonists.

  • The 2024 deal. In 2024, Condé Nast, The New Yorker’s parent company, signed a multi-year licensing deal with OpenAI. The article says the deal allows OpenAI to use much of the publisher’s content in its products.
  • Terms. The terms of the deal are not public.
  • Training on cartoons. A spokesperson for The New Yorker told the reporter that Condé Nast has never granted an LLM developer permission to train models on its cartoons.
  • The logo. Some ChatGPT creations reproduced The New Yorker’s logo alongside cartoons. The spokesperson said that is also not allowed in any LLM deals.
  • How contributions work. The magazine’s two cartoon editors work with a roster of freelancers. The freelancers submit their work on spec and only get paid if a cartoon is selected for publication.
  • The contracts. The reporter writes that he reviewed several templatized contracts for New Yorker contributing cartoonists, none of which permit licensing for AI training purposes.
  • Copyright. While The New Yorker has the right to publish commissioned cartoons first, it is not a “work for hire” arrangement, and the cartoonist retains ownership of the copyright.

The article sums this up by saying that even if Condé Nast did want to license cartoons to OpenAI, the magazine’s standard cartoonist contract leaves little room for it.

The article also addresses livelihoods. According to it, The New Yorker has not taken a turn toward publishing AI-generated cartoons. None of the contributors the reporter spoke to, however, said they could support themselves solely on their commissions from the magazine. A New Yorker cartoon serves as a calling card, and that work opens doors to commercial work, book illustrations, and other jobs that together make up a livelihood. The cartoonists said it is this work that is most threatened by AI. In the same context, the article notes that The Baltimore Sun began regularly publishing AI-generated cartoons in its opinions section earlier this year.

A Law Professor’s View — Copyright and the Right of Publicity

The article says that U.S. intellectual property case law has yet to catch up with the rise of AI image generators, and that the courts are also likely to treat the two issues as distinct. Any copyright infringement claim, for example, would most likely hinge on ChatGPT’s reproduction of each cartoonist’s specific drawing style. Both sentences are the reporter’s forecast and not a definite conclusion. The only legal scholar quoted in the article is James Grimmelmann, a professor of digital and information law at Cornell Law School.

  • Attribution and fair use. “[The signature] is an indication of authenticity, but attribution is a very small part of the fair use inquiry.”
  • The difficulty of a copyright suit. He said the chief legal challenge in a copyright suit is that ChatGPT is not usually reproducing any specific cartoon in these examples; rather, it is mimicking a more general style.
  • Another route. He says New Yorker cartoonists could have grounds for a “right of publicity” case. The article describes this as a body of state-based law that protects individuals from the unauthorized use of their name or identity.
  • The condition. For that, there would need to be proof that the AI-generated cartoons were used for a commercial purpose, not just as a joke or gag. The reporter writes that he found no evidence that these cartoons are being bought or sold.

The INTA overview linked from the article defines the right of publicity as an intellectual property right that protects against the misappropriation of a person’s name, likeness, or other indicia of personal identity for commercial benefit. The examples of such indicia given in the overview include nickname, pseudonym, voice, signature, and photograph. The same overview states that in the United States no federal statute or case law recognizes the right of publicity, while a majority of states recognize it by statute, case law, or both. It adds that states diverge on whether the right survives posthumously and, if so, for how long.

The article mentions two other lawsuits. A class action led by the illustrator Sarah Andersen against Stability AI, DeviantArt, and Midjourney has been in the courts since 2023, and the article notes that there is no reference to signatures in that filing. The article also says Katzenstein was part of the class action filed against Anthropic in 2024 by members of the Authors Guild. The Authors Guild announced that on July 20, 2026, the court granted final approval of a 1.5 billion dollar settlement, and its announcement says that class members release only claims relating to the past acquisition and copying of their works, the “inputs” side, and that claims based on AI outputs are not released. The article does not describe any lawsuit filed over the reproduction of signatures. How copyright in AI images is viewed under Korean standards is discussed in the copyright section of Why AI Images Make a Post Less Believable.

What Could Not Be Verified

  • The path by which signatures entered the model. The article mentions only the possibility that they were scraped from the internet, and OpenAI did not answer the question. Technical explanations such as memorization or overfitting do not appear in the source material and are therefore not used in this post.
  • The full list behind “more than 15” and the test conditions. The people named in the article are a subset. The number of test runs, the prompts, and the model version used by the reporter are not given.
  • How often a signature appears. The article gives no figure for the share of generated cartoons that carry a signature, or for the share of those signatures that belong to real contributors.
  • Behavior after the new message. The article does not make clear whether generation is refused when the message appears or whether an image is produced along with it. It says only that the message began to appear and that some cartoons continue to be signed.
  • The terms of the Condé Nast and OpenAI deal. The agreement is not public. That cartoons fall outside it is known only through the New Yorker spokesperson’s account.
  • Whether anyone has sued. The article does not say whether the cartoonists it quotes have filed, or plan to file, a lawsuit over the reproduction of their signatures.
  • Assessment under Korean law. The source material consists of U.S. cases and commentary on U.S. law. How this situation would be assessed under Korean law is not addressed here.
