Why Pick C When AI Writes the Code

What decides a language choice once generation volume stops being the bottleneck

Aug 16, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: a combination that looks contradictory

Since generation speed went up, the same question keeps circling inside teams. Does picking a language even matter anymore? If nobody is typing the lines by hand, the reasons to choose a familiar language or a comfortable syntax both shrink. So what criteria are actually left?

There is an interesting case to reason from. antirez, the creator of Redis, has spent 2026 publishing repositories that are overwhelmingly inference engines written in pure C. Flux 2 image generation inference, Voxtral speech recognition, Qwen-family transcription, MiniMax H3, and DeepSeek 4 local inference are all C repositories. Meanwhile the same person publishes a blog post titled Control the ideas, not the code, arguing that inspecting individual lines is mostly pointless when AI produces thousands of them a day.

On the surface those pull in opposite directions. He picks the language that demands the most manual care, then reduces the manual care he spends on reading. This article is not a profile of him. It answers one question: once generation volume explodes, what becomes the axis that decides a language choice?

The direction is worth stating up front. This is not a question of which language is better. It is a question of whether the cost of reading and verifying lands on a human or on a compiler, and of which constraints make each answer hold. Different constraints, different answer. A team with different constraints that copies the same decision inherits the cost without the benefit.

1. What is actually confirmable

Before handling the argument, set down what can be checked. Three things are visible in his own blog and public repositories.

  • The output is C. The recent repository list is entirely C, and the targets span image generation, speech recognition, transcription and large language model inference. This is not one domain explored deeply; it is the same method applied repeatedly across several domains.
  • Local execution is the premise. Metal, CUDA and ROCm are handled as separate acceleration backends inside the same codebase. The goal is not inference sitting behind a service but inference running on the machine in front of you.
  • He states plainly that he demoted code review. He wrote that if you control the ideas of your software, looking at the code itself is suboptimal and often pointless. He adds that he still reviews Redis-generated code out of respect for users, while calling that work mostly pointless at the current level of models.

What matters is that the third point is broken into three separate grounds. They differ in kind, and different kinds are refutable in different ways.

GroundContentKindIf models improve
VolumeThousands of lines a day makes exhaustive review impossibleArithmetic — human hours do not growGets stronger, since volume grows
BiasModels are strong at local optimisation, weak on overall structureObservation — a claim about todayMay weaken
Opportunity costAn eight-hour day forces a choice between review and design or QAChoice — a matter of priorityStays as is

Splitting them this way makes the discussion tractable. The bias argument has an expiry date. As models get better at structure, it weakens. The volume argument has no expiry and in fact strengthens. The faster generation gets, the larger the share of code nobody reads. So the way to evaluate the claim is to ask whether it still holds with only the volume ground left standing. It does. And this is precisely where language choice enters.

2. What moving the unit of control really means

Taken at face value, control the ideas rather than the code is close to a slogan. To make it usable, you have to specify what the unit of control became instead.

His answer is the design document. Maintain, in writing, what the data structures are, what the implementation strategy is, and why that structure was chosen. Agents receive instructions inside that model, and the human maintains the model rather than the code. According to the Korean write-up on GeekNews, the practice started as an implementation-notes file on one project and settled into a DESIGN.md form.

There is one point practitioners should filter here. Having a design document does not mean the unit of control moved. Most organisations already have design documents, and a large share of them are summaries that only make sense once you have read the code. Those do not replace reading. The test is simple.

If the code vanished entirely, could someone rebuild the same thing from that document alone? Then control genuinely moved. If not, only the label moved.

A document that passes that test is not a summary but a regeneration spec. It records what is not supported, who owns which state, which invariants constitute a bug when broken, and how correctness is judged. Naturally the specification workload grows. What shrank is review labour; what grew is specification labour. There is no magic that reduces the total. The difference is that specification labour does not scale with generation volume. That is the crux of the scalability argument below.

3. Where the cost goes when generation scales

This section is the body of the argument. AI generation and language choice meet exactly here.

Start with the asymmetry. Writing code got dramatically cheaper; reading it did not. A generator emits hundreds of lines in minutes, but the rate at which a person understands them and judges them correct is what it always was. The day did not get longer, and the volume anyone can read attentively did not grow.

As a result the bottleneck moves from authoring to verification. Once it moves, there are only three responses.

  1. Reduce what must be read. Keep the codebase small and narrow the supported envelope.
  2. Hand reading to machines. Compilers, static analysis, sanitizers, fuzzers and regression gates read on your behalf.
  3. Reduce the cost of being wrong. Contain failures, make them reversible, limit the blast radius.

The third is largely a deployment and operations concern, mostly independent of language. Language choice intervenes directly in the first two. And that is where two camps diverge.

  • The reduce-what-is-read camp is favoured by languages with a thin execution model. Fewer layers means the same behaviour is expressed with fewer concepts, and what happens when is predictable, so the range a person can hold in their head widens.
  • The hand-reading-to-machines camp is favoured by languages with strong checkers. When type and ownership rules are dense, a class of defects cannot pass at all, even in code no human read.

antirez sits closer to the first. That choice carries a price: whatever the compiler does not catch must be covered by something else. That, in my reading, is why he wrote a separate post about software testing. In it he notes that automatically generated code falls short of the structural quality of the best hand-written software but often exceeds decently developed hand-written code, and says the area where LLMs are decisively strong is QA: reviewing recent commits to identify what might be affected, running targeted tests, and checking for performance regressions even without a predefined baseline.

That is the key to scalability. When generation volume grows, test generation grows with it. Human review does not. That is why moving verification toward machines is the only scaling path for the volume problem. Seen that way, choosing a language that puts verification in the compiler and putting verification into a QA agent are not different answers but different points on the same axis.

# Let machines read whatever people no longer do - dev and CI build
cc -std=c11 -O1 -g \
   -Wall -Wextra -Wconversion -Wshadow -Werror \
   -fsanitize=address,undefined \
   -fno-omit-frame-pointer \
   -c engine.c

# Keep the release build separate. Never ship sanitizers
cc -std=c11 -O2 -DNDEBUG -c engine.c

The important flag above is -Werror. Leave warnings as warnings and they multiply with generation volume, and nobody reads a growing pile of warnings. For a machine to do the reading, the machine has to be able to stop you.

4. What C gives - control and predictability

Strip out the sentiment and there are four reasons to choose C.

  • The execution model is thin. Little sits between source and machine code. No hidden allocations, no hidden destructor calls, no hidden exception paths. Performance is easy to explain, and when it is odd, you know where to look.
  • There is no runtime. Startup is short, the deployable artifact is small, and unpredictable pauses are not structurally injected during execution. In audio and real-time work, where tail latency rather than average is what counts, that property is hard to substitute.
  • It attaches to everything. Accelerator SDKs ship as C headers. When adding a new hardware target, C is the language that binds with no intermediate layer. For a project juggling Metal, CUDA and ROCm in one codebase, that is a requirement, not a convenience.
  • The language barely changes. Code from a decade ago still builds. When automatic generation piles code up quickly, a language that does not move holds maintenance cost down. If the language changed every year, the extra volume would convert directly into migration debt.

There is a tempting fifth item: that C dominates training data, so models write it better. That claim is not used here. The per-language composition of major models' training data is not published, and public coding benchmarks generally aggregate without controlling for per-language difficulty. There is no evidence in either direction, so it cannot be an argument.

What can be said with confidence is this. C's advantages arise not from what the language does for you but from what it declines to do. It does not intervene, so it is predictable; it does not intervene, so it binds easily; it does not intervene, so it does not change. And for exactly the same reason, it does not protect you either.

5. What C takes away - a human stands in for the safety net

The costs are unambiguous, and they get worse in contact with AI generation.

  • Memory safety is not guaranteed by the language. Use after free, out-of-bounds access and reading uninitialised values are all syntactically possible.
  • Failures do not surface where they occur. This is the real problem. Code that writes one slot past a boundary does not die there; it reappears much later as an unrelated data structure that looks corrupted.
  • Undefined behaviour interacts with optimisation. Change the optimisation level and symptoms vanish or new ones appear. Reproduction alone becomes a project.
  • The standard library is thin. Strings, containers and error handling get built by hand each time. If a generator writes them, the authoring cost falls but the amount to read and verify rises by the same measure.
  • The reuse ecosystem is shallow. Instead of pulling in an existing library you build one, and every library you built yourself is one whose bugs you own.

The worst combination forms right here: the second item overlapping with reading less code. When the share of unread code grows and the symptom is far from the cause, debugging turns from reading into searching. And search cost grows faster than code volume, not proportionally to it.

So the decision to cut review while on C logically requires runtime verification alongside it. Cut review without sanitizers, fuzzing and deterministic regression baselines and you do not have a new methodology, you have verification that was skipped. The benefit list in the previous section is valid only when that price is paid.

The last item also carries an internal tension. Keeping a small codebase and building the libraries you lack point in opposite directions. The way out is not to write frugally but to narrow the domain. That is the next section.

6. The other side - how Rust and Zig answer the same problem

Other camps answer the same question — how do you trust code no human fully read? — differently. Introducing those answers weakly would make this a partisan article.

Rust - put verification in the compiler

Rust's ownership and borrow checking reject a large class of memory errors and data races at compile time. In an AI-generation context the value of that property is direct. The existence of defects that cannot pass even unread is straightforwardly good when review bandwidth is the bottleneck. As generation grows, checking cost grows with it because it sits on the machine side.

The price is equally clear. Design pressure appears in order to satisfy the checker, unsafe boundaries still need human management, and build times and a learning curve must be absorbed. Whether the right move is a full rewrite or leaving existing C assets in place and hardening them is a substantial debate in its own right. This site already covers that argument in terms of threat model and code lifetime, so it will not be repeated here — see Memory Safety Absolutists - The Rust vs Fil-C Debate and What Is Actually Practical.

Zig - keep the control, remove the traps

Zig keeps manual memory management and explicit allocators, so it does not give up the control C provides. In exchange it removes hidden control flow, brings compile-time execution into the language, and lets you consume C headers directly. It sits between reduce-what-is-read and hand-it-to-machines.

The price is maturity. The official download page lists 0.16.0 (13 April 2026) as the latest release, and the project is still pre-1.0. The language and standard library keep moving, which collides head-on with the C advantage of a language that does not change. Conversely, for a project starting now that will be actively worked on for years, that motion may be a stream of improvements rather than a cost.

AxisCRustZig
Who carries reading costHumans plus runtime toolsThe compilerBetween humans and compiler
Memory error preventionNone at language level; tools compensateCompile-time within safe codeSome checks built in; not total
Locality of failureLow — symptom can be far from causeHigh — usually rejected or panicsMedium
Attaching a new hardware backendLeast frictionNeeds a binding layerFirst-class C interop
Rate of language changeVery slowSlow, though the ecosystem moves fastFast, being pre-1.0
Does verification scale with generationOnly if you wire up toolsLargely by defaultPartially
Fits whenFixed domain, long life, hardware-adjacentStrong safety requirements, larger teamsCoexisting with C assets, new projects

Read the table down its columns and no winner appears. You have to read it across the rows. Decide which row is a real constraint for your project first, then pick the column that is favourable in that row. Invert the order and you pick the language first and fit the constraints afterwards, at which point any choice can be justified.

7. A small codebase is constraint design, not taste

A phrase that recurs in his explanations is a codebase you can own. Controlling a small, narrowly focused codebase directly beats riding on a mature but difficult large one.

Left there it sounds like aesthetics. Translated into practitioner terms it reads: the goal is not fewer lines but fewer promises. Codebases usually grow not because there are many features but because there are many cases to prepare for. Promising to accept arbitrary models, to support arbitrary precisions, or to run on any machine each produce branches and abstraction layers.

Promise withdrawnCode that disappearsWhat you lose instead
We will load arbitrary model graphsGraph parsing, operator dispatch, shape inferenceA model change means a code change
We will support multiple precisionsConversion paths and per-precision kernel branchesPrecision experiments cannot happen outside the code
We will run on any hardwareFallback paths and runtime capability detectionDevices outside the list do not run at all
We will use external librariesBuild config, version alignment, init codeYou own the bugs in what you wrote yourself
We will leave room to extend laterPlugin points, config surface, abstract interfacesA changed requirement is rework, not extension

The right-hand column is the honest price list. This trade pays only when the domain is narrow and stays fixed. Inference engines, where input format and computation structure are settled at model release, fit well. Make the same trade in a line-of-business system whose requirements change quarterly and you buy back the withdrawn promises every quarter.

Having no dependencies is likewise a value rather than an aesthetic. Builds reproduce, supply chain audit surface shrinks, and the same artifact still gets produced years later. In organisations where intake review is the bottleneck, this single item can justify a low-level choice on its own. But to actually collect that value the reproducible build has to be documented. If it is written down nowhere, the absence of dependencies cannot be demonstrated during an audit.

8. If you cut reading, what goes in its place

Everything so far was judgement; this section is execution. Cutting human review is not a decision but an outcome. The decision is what you put in the vacated space.

a. Build the machine-reading layer first

  • Promote warnings to errors. A warning nobody reads is equivalent to no warning.
  • Wire address and undefined behaviour sanitizers permanently into dev and CI builds. Keep them out of the release build.
  • Fuzz input parsers and boundary handling. The more generated code there is, the more edges nobody points at by hand.
  • Put static analysis in CI, but gate only on new findings against a baseline. Gating on everything gets disabled on day one.

b. Build a baseline you can judge against

  • Fix the reference output and the tolerance as a number, in advance. Decide it later and you will decide it so that things pass.
  • Promote a regression gate only after deliberately breaking something and confirming it is caught. An unverified gate is just a rubber stamp.
  • Record performance relative to identical conditions, never as an absolute. Log the device, thread count and input length beside it.

c. Turn the design document into a regeneration spec

Applying the test from section 2 to a document skeleton produces something like this.

# DESIGN.md - this document alone must be enough to rebuild the same thing

## 1. What we decided NOT to do
- Supported models: exactly one, fixed. No arbitrary graph loading
- Supported precision: exactly one. No conversion at runtime
- Supported hardware: fail at init below the baseline instruction set

## 2. Data structures and ownership
- All state lives in one struct. No allocation after init
- The caller owns the buffers. The library never frees them

## 3. Invariants - breaking these is a bug
- At every frame boundary, write_pos < capacity holds
- Public functions never modify input buffers, even on failure

## 4. How correctness is judged
- Baseline: reference implementation output within tolerance 1e-4
- Regression gate: registered in CI only after fault injection confirms detection
- Performance: relative comparisons only, logged with device, threads, input length

The value of this document lives in section 1. Most design documents record what the system does and never record what it was decided not to do. Yet the latter is what you actually need when directing a generator. If the non-goals are not written down, the generator will helpfully add the fallback code, and the codebase you meant to keep small grows quietly.

9. A practitioner's view - when this judgement reproduces

Can a team copy the same decision? Conditionally. And the conditions sit in organisational structure rather than language skill.

SituationReproducibilityLook at first
One or two owners, narrow fixed domainHighIs the supported envelope written down
On-device or edge with known target hardwareHighBaseline instruction set and failure policy
Intake review or supply chain audit is the bottleneckMedium to highIs the reproducible build documented
High turnover, outsourced or contract staffingLowIs the handover object code or the design doc
Regulated procurement and security checklistsLow to mediumDo the checklists constrain language and tooling
Business systems whose requirements shift quarterlyLowHow often withdrawn promises must be bought back
Teams weighted toward junior engineersLowCan a model be built without reading code

The heaviest row is turnover. When the unit of control moves from code to design, the handover object moves with it. Yet handover in many organisations still centres on source and screens. In a place with no habit of maintaining documents, cutting review means the relocated control lands nowhere at all. There, the thing to fix before the language is the documentation practice.

The last row cannot be waved away either. antirez himself left a reservation on this point, to the effect that he is unsure whether programmers without enough experience can build a mental model of software without looking at the code directly. That connects straight to hiring structures. The path where a junior builds intuition by reading code is getting shorter, and no replacement path is established yet. There is no settled answer to this today.

The realistic compromise is not a full migration. Freeze one bottleneck path narrowly, lower that alone, and leave the rest on the existing stack. A failure is then reversible within a single module, and you can start without satisfying every row of the table at once. Practise the design document and the regression gate in that module first, and whether to expand becomes a decision you can make with evidence.

10. What could not be confirmed

Collected in one place: what this article did not use as evidence.

  • Specific productivity anecdotes. Accounts of a 700-line embedding library in five minutes, or a redesign reproduced in twenty minutes from design documents alone, come from the Korean write-up, and the original posts carrying those figures were not cross-checked. They are mentioned as directional colour, not as evidence.
  • Cross-language performance figures. No public comparison table with stated conditions between these repositories and existing inference frameworks was located, so no multipliers or throughput numbers appear here.
  • Per-language model strength. The language composition of major models' training data is not published. Neither AI writes C better nor writes it worse has support.
  • External reproduction of the design-document practice. No public case of another team relocating the unit of control this way and reporting results was located. Section 8 is a construction derived from principles, not a report of observed cases.
  • Repository popularity metrics. Figures such as star counts vary by the day and are not cited.

Conclusion: the choice was a constraint, not a language

What this case actually confirms is not that C is good. It confirms that this combination holds under a specific set of conditions: a narrowly fixed domain, known target hardware, few owners, code that must live a long time, and a readiness to move verification toward machines. Change the conditions and the same choice produces different outcomes.

The same applies to the other side. Languages with strong checkers structurally fit a world where the share of unread code keeps rising, and that benefit grows with team size and safety requirements. This article does not claim that answer is wrong. The two answers are different points on the same problem, and where you stand is decided by constraints.

Three things to take away.

  1. Do not pick the language first. Decide which row is your real constraint — hardware adjacency, safety requirements, code lifetime — then pick the favourable column in that row. Invert the order and any choice can be justified after the fact.
  2. Before cutting review, decide what replaces it. Warnings as errors, sanitizers, fuzzing, a regression gate verified by fault injection, and a design document you could rebuild from. Without those, cutting review is not a methodology.
  3. Translate keep it small into make fewer promises. If the non-goals are not in the document, the generator will always add the fallback code.

Compressed to one line: as the cost of writing code fell, what rose in value is reading and verification, and the language is the instrument that decides who gets billed for it. Whether that bill goes to a person, a compiler or a tool is decided by your project's constraints, not by fashion.
