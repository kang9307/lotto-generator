Why Compression Ratio and Prediction Accuracy Measure the Same Thing - From the Entropy Floor to Where Model-as-Compressor Breaks

Guess the next thing well and it gets written short, and the places where that bridge snaps

Aug 16, 2026 Tech & IT

Opening: two 10MB files, one becomes 30KB and one gets bigger

You compress a log file and it comes out forty times smaller. You compress the encrypted backup sitting next to it, same size, same compressor, same options, and the result is a few bytes larger than the original. Two files of identical length, treated identically, ending up on opposite sides of the ledger.

What separated them was not size but guessability. In a log file the next line is nearly determined: the timestamp format is fixed, there are only a handful of level strings, and the same message repeats thousands of times. In an encrypted byte stream there is nothing on which to base a guess about the next byte, because that is precisely what encryption is designed to achieve.

Push that observation all the way and the nature of compression as a technology comes into view. Every compressor has a predictor inside it. And the performance gap between compressors is almost entirely a gap between predictors. This is not a recent discovery, it was present from the founding of information theory, but the arrival of language models gave it sudden practical weight.

What this article covers is that bridge. How probability turns into bit length, what it actually means to say entropy sets a floor, where lossy compression becomes a different game, and where the view that a language model is a compressor breaks down. The last one is the center of gravity here. Equivalence is a strong word, but quote it without the conditions under which it holds and it turns into overstatement immediately.

No specific model name or this quarter's ratio table is the protagonist here. What matters is the structure that survives version changes. Practical comparison of individual codecs is covered in Zstandard in Rust 2026 - Memory-Safe Compression for the Modern Stack, and what next-token prediction looks like on a user's screen is covered in What Picking the Next Word Actually Means - The Traces You Can See on Your Own Screen, so neither is repeated below.

1. Why writing it short and guessing it right are the same thing

Take any lossless compressor apart and you always find two components.

  • A model — given everything seen so far, it produces a probability distribution over the next symbol. As in, the next letter is u with probability 0.7, e with 0.05, and so on
  • A coder — it takes that distribution and the symbol that actually arrived, and emits bits

Of the two, what the coder does is essentially settled. The way to turn probabilities into near-optimal lengths has been known for decades, and differences between implementations sit below the decimal point. Which means a contest between compressors is almost entirely a contest between models. When a new compression format is announced, the actual news is usually that a better predictor was bolted on.

Everything rests on one cost formula. The number of bits needed to write a symbol is the negative logarithm of the probability you assigned to it.

  • Symbol given probability 1/2 → 1 bit
  • Symbol given probability 1/4 → 2 bits
  • Symbol given probability 1/1024 → 10 bits
  • Symbol given probability 0 → cannot be written at all (infinite). This is why real implementations enforce a probability floor on every symbol

That is the whole formula. Well-predicted symbols get written short, badly predicted ones get written long. So once a file has been written out and you count the total bits, that number is the sum of how well that model predicted across that file. Compression ratio and prediction accuracy are not similar quantities. They are the same quantity in different units.

It helps to flip the sentence around. Rather than compression is prediction, the more precise direction is build a predictor and a compressor falls out of it. If something produces probabilities, the procedure for turning it into a compressor is mechanical. Conversely, if you have a compressor, you can extract a probability model from inside it. That round trip is the skeleton of this whole article.

2. The bridge from probability to length - arithmetic coding

A symbol with probability 0.7 costs about 0.51 bits. But bits do not split in half. Spend a minimum of one bit per symbol and you lose 0.49 bits every time, and on heavily skewed data that loss eats most of the available compression.

Arithmetic coding is exactly the fix for this. The idea is to stop assigning bits per symbol and instead represent the entire message as a single interval between 0 and 1. Each incoming symbol narrows the interval in proportion to its probability. When you are done narrowing, you just name any number inside what remains.

Alphabet and model probabilities:  A = 1/2,  B = 1/4,  C = 1/4
Message to encode:                 A  B  A

Start interval                     [0.0000, 1.0000)   width 1
 └ narrow on A                     [0.0000, 0.5000)   width 1/2
    └ narrow on B                  [0.2500, 0.3750)   width 1/8
       └ narrow on A               [0.2500, 0.3125)   width 1/16

Final interval width = 1/2 × 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/16
Bits needed to name this interval = log2(16) = 4 bits

Adding the per-symbol costs gives the same answer:
  1 bit (A) + 2 bits (B) + 1 bit (A) = 4 bits

Fixed-length encoding would have used: 2 bits × 3 symbols = 6 bits

What is worth noticing here is not the numbers but three properties.

  • The interval width is the probability of the message. Multiply the probabilities and you get the width, take the logarithm of the width and you get the bit count. Nothing else sits between probability and length
  • Costs simply add. Each symbol's cost is computed independently and summed. That lets you decompose the question of how many bits this model spent on this file down to individual symbols
  • The model is free to change every step. Arithmetic coding does not care if a different distribution arrives at each position. That property is what lets context-sensitive models plug in, and it is the same socket a language model plugs into

Decoding retraces the same steps backwards. You see which slice the received number falls into, that fixes the first symbol, then you subdivide within that slice for the next one. This carries a decisive precondition: the decoder must be able to produce exactly the same probability distribution the encoder used at every step. Section 6 returns to what happens to that precondition when a language model is involved.

3. Entropy sets the floor - and the floor moves with the model

Entropy is the cost formula above, averaged. Multiply each symbol's cost by its probability of occurring, sum it all, and you get the average number of bits per symbol under that distribution. That value is the floor. No amount of clever coding gets below it. Claude Shannon established this in his 1948 paper (A Mathematical Theory of Communication, Bell System Technical Journal, vol. 27), and all subsequent compression research has happened above that line.

The reason random data does not shrink follows immediately. If the next byte is equally likely to be any of 256 values, no model changes the fact that the probability is 1/256 and the cost is 8 bits. Eight bits in, eight bits out, nothing to save. Header and block framing overhead then make the output slightly larger than the input.

Checking this takes a few shell lines (Linux or macOS).

# Two 10MB files - one random, one the same sentence repeated
head -c 10000000 /dev/urandom > random.bin
yes 'the quick brown fox' | head -c 10000000 > repeat.txt

gzip -k random.bin repeat.txt
ls -l random.bin.gz repeat.txt.gz

# random.bin.gz  → slightly larger than the original
# repeat.txt.gz  → hundreds of times smaller
# The two originals have identical byte counts.
# The only difference is whether the next byte can be guessed.

Most explanations stop here, and that is where a misconception hardens. Entropy is not an intrinsic property of a file. Entropy is always a value relative to some model. Change the model and the floor for the same file moves.

  • Under a model that treats bytes as independent, the floor for a page of English prose is fairly high
  • Switch to a model that uses the preceding characters as context and the floor for the same file drops sharply, because what can follow a given letter is constrained
  • A model that knows the document's topic and register drops it further
  • The decimal expansion of pi is statistically indistinguishable from noise, yet it is nearly free for a model that knows the generating rule

So the sentence this file has an entropy of X is incomplete unless the model is named. Looking random means the model in use does not know the rule, not that no rule exists. Handling the absolute regularity limit of data itself requires moving to Kolmogorov complexity, which is laid out alongside the pigeonhole principle in πFS - The Filesystem That Stores Data in the Digits of Pi Instead of a Disk.

4. Lossy compression solves a different problem - deciding what to throw away

Everything so far has been lossless. The requirement was exact recovery of the original bit stream, and under that requirement probability prediction was the only lever available.

Lossy compression changes the requirement itself. What comes back is not the original but something a human cannot distinguish from it. So the core component of a lossy compressor is not the predictor but the discard rule. What can be thrown away without anyone noticing is not a probability question, it is a perceptual model question.

AspectLosslessLossy
What is recoveredThe original bit stream exactlyAn approximation humans cannot distinguish
Core componentNext-symbol predictorDiscard rule (perceptual model) plus a predictor
Performance ceilingSet by entropySet by how far you let quality fall
What the ratio meansHow well the model predictedNothing on its own. It needs a paired quality metric
Typical targetsText, code, binaries, archivesPhotos, music, video
Usable as a model metricYesNo

What gets thrown away differs by medium.

  • Images — the picture is converted into frequency components and the high-frequency coefficients are rounded coarsely. Because the eye is less sensitive to color change than to brightness change, color information is often stored at reduced resolution separately
  • Audio — masking. A quiet sound right beside a loud one is inaudible in the first place, so that component is discarded wholesale
  • Video — here prediction returns to the front. The next frame is predicted from the previous one and only the difference is written. This step, motion compensation, is the heart of video codec ratios

The important point is that prediction never disappears from lossy compression. Once the discardable parts are gone, the final step of writing down what remains is still lossless, and a probability model and coder go back to work there. Lossy compression does not replace lossless compression, it adds a stage in front of it. What each codec generation actually improves is covered in AV2 Video Codec Standard v1.0 Released 2026 - What the Next Open Codec Means.

One thing practitioners get wrong regularly: lossy ratios cannot be compared without pinning the quality metric. The claim that codec A is 30% smaller than B only means something if both outputs look equally good. Drop that premise and the contest is won by whoever degraded quality further.

5. What it means to treat a language model as a compressor

Write down plainly what a language model does: given the tokens so far, it emits a probability distribution over the next token. That is precisely the input arithmetic coding wants. No adaptation is needed to connect them. Feed the model's output straight into the coder and you have a lossless compressor.

This framing is not a conceptual amusement, it is a measurable claim, and it has been measured. The DeepMind paper Language Modeling Is Compression (arXiv:2309.10668, submitted September 2023, revised March 2024) is the canonical reference, and what makes the abstract's figures interesting is that they come from non-text data.

  • ImageNet patches: Chinchilla 70B compressed to 43.4%. PNG on the same target reached 58.5%
  • LibriSpeech samples: Chinchilla 70B compressed to 16.4%. FLAC on the same target reached 30.3%
  • Conditions: the patch and sample units defined in that paper, and raw compression rates that do not count model parameters toward the compressed size

The weight of the result is that a model trained mostly on text beat codecs purpose-built for those media. It reads as evidence that compression is not a per-medium technology but a prediction technology. If the ability to find regularity is general enough, it works to some degree on kinds of data it was never trained on.

The two condition lines matter. Parameters were not counted, and the units are the ones that paper defined. Carry the result without them and it becomes overstatement immediately. That is the next section.

6. Three places where that view breaks

A model is a compressor is true. It is when you try to draw a practical conclusion from that sentence that three things block the road.

6.1 Accounting - does model size go in the numerator

What you count when computing a ratio changes the number entirely.

[Case A] Compressor and decompressor already hold the same model
   bits to send = data bits
   → this is the ratio papers and benchmarks usually report

[Case B] The model has to travel too (self-extracting archive)
   bits to send = decompressor bits + model bits + data bits
   → this is what the Hutter Prize measures

Same compressor, same file, different number.
A ratio that does not state which rulebook it came from cannot be compared.

Case A is not dishonest arithmetic. Sharing a dictionary between browser and server ahead of time is a real web standard, and if you deploy a model once and exchange tens of millions of payloads, the parameter cost divides down to effectively zero per item. The problem appears when two numbers are placed side by side without saying which accounting produced them. Whether tens of gigabytes of parameters go into the numerator is not a decimal-place question, it is an orders-of-magnitude question.

6.2 Runtime cost - a good ratio alone does not get you shipped

General purpose codecs move hundreds of megabytes per second on one core. Neural compression runs one forward pass per symbol. That gap is not the kind tuning closes.

  • HTTP responses — if compression costs more time than the transfer it saves, it is a loss. The break-even is tight enough that production codecs default to speed-first levels
  • Backups and cold storage — one of the few places tolerant of slow compression. Heavy codecs survive here
  • Real-time streams — the latency budget is in milliseconds, leaving no room to call a model
  • Memory — parameters must stay resident, so embedded targets and browsers cannot even start

So the realistic use of this direction is not replacing gzip but serving as a yardstick that shows where the theoretical ratio ceiling sits. Making the model itself small is a different field, covered in Bonsai 27B: What It Takes to Run a 27B-Class Model on a Phone. Same word, compression, different problem.

6.3 Decoder symmetry - the most easily forgotten one

Lossless coding requires the decoder to reproduce a bit-identical probability distribution to the one the encoder used. A tiny divergence shifts an interval selection, and once it shifts, everything after it is garbage. Guaranteeing identical output from neural inference is harder than it sounds.

  • Floating point accumulation order — change the order of a parallel reduction and the last digits change
  • Kernel and library versions — the same operation with a different implementation can differ in the final bits
  • Batch size and padding — a different batch shape can route the computation down a different path
  • Hardware — bit-identical results across accelerators and precision modes are hard to promise

Real implementations therefore quantize probabilities onto an integer lattice and force deterministic execution paths, which costs performance all over again. Lossless has no 99.99%. It works or it does not, and this requirement is the practical reason using a model as a compressor is hard to move out of the lab.

7. Separating the layers of the claim

Getting from compression is prediction to intelligence is compression looks like a short walk, but the character of the claim changes twice along the way. Split the layers and the safe boundary becomes obvious.

LayerWhat it claimsKind of evidenceHow far it holdsWhere it becomes overstatement
1. Mathematical equivalence A probability model and a lossless coder are interchangeable A theorem. Arithmetic coding is a constructive procedure that doubles as the proof True without conditions. But it is an existence claim about a coder Reading it as therefore building good compressors produces intelligence
2. Engineering usefulness Compression ratio works as a model quality metric Measurement. Count the bits on the same data Only inside a comparison with data, preprocessing and accounting held fixed Placing ratios from different datasets or accounting rules side by side
3. Practical viability This approach can replace real compression tools Engineering. Speed, memory and determinism all count Only in the few places where compute is abundant Reading a ratio table and concluding gzip is finished
4. Philosophical claim Intelligence is compression A proposal to redefine a word. Refutation conditions unclear Up to a good predictive model is a good compressor Inverting it into a high ratio means it understood

Mistakes usually happen when a layer gets skipped. Layer 1 is unconditionally true but it is an existence claim. It says if something produces probabilities there is a corresponding coder. It does not say intelligence emerges from building good compressors. Layer 2 is the most useful one in practice, and it is exactly the one whose fixed-conditions caveat gets dropped in citation.

With layer 4 the thing to watch is that it is unclear what would count as showing it false. A claim with no refutation condition is not a wrong claim, it is a claim that is not up for testing. Using it as an interesting lens and citing it as if it were a measurement are different acts.

8. What actually differs between compression methods

If every lossless compressor is a model plus a coder, then the difference between real methods lies in how the model is built. Sorted on that axis, the family tree is legible at a glance.

MethodModel (how next-symbol probability is produced)CodingWhat decoding requiresSpeed feelTypical home
Run-length (RLE)Effectively none. Only sees runs of identical valuesLength plus valueThe rules onlyVery fastSimple bitmaps, fax
Dictionary (LZ family)Assumes strings seen before will recurMatch offset and length plus HuffmanThe rules onlyFastHTTP, logs, general archives
Dictionary with trained dictionarySame, but a domain dictionary is shared in advanceSameRules plus the same dictionaryFastSmall JSON, repetitive payloads
Context modeling (PPM, CM)Per-context statistics learned adaptively from the dataArithmetic codingRules plus an identical adaptation procedureSlowExtreme archiving, benchmarks
Neural network plus arithmetic codingA pretrained network emits the probabilitiesArithmetic codingRules plus identical weights and numerical reproducibilityVery slowResearch, model evaluation

Reading the table top to bottom shows a regular trade.

  • Heavier models buy ratio and pay speed. There are almost no exceptions
  • What the decoder must carry grows. Rules only, then a dictionary, then weights and numerical reproducibility
  • Deployment difficulty rises in step. Further down the table, the it-unpacks-anywhere property is lost
  • Production codecs clustering in the middle is not a coincidence. That row has to satisfy speed, portability and memory at once, not just ratio

Worth adding: the dictionary family held the standard slot for decades not because its ratios were outstanding but because the decoder only needs the rules. Requiring no shared state is a property you cannot trade for a few percentage points of ratio.

9. Where compression ratio gets used in practice

9.1 Storage and transfer

Here the ratio is not the goal but one of three axes. The decision usually sorts out like this.

  • Written once, read often — compression can be slow, decompression must be fast. Release artifacts, static assets
  • Written once, almost never read — push the ratio as high as it goes. Backups, audit logs
  • Written and read constantly — both directions must be fast. Log pipelines, database pages
  • Countless tiny payloads — a pre-shared dictionary decides the outcome. Short payloads are not long enough for regularity to surface on their own
  • Already-compressed input — do not compress it again. You spend the time and the size goes up

9.2 Compression ratio as a model evaluation metric

The standard language model metric and compression ratio are the same quantity written differently. Convert per-token loss into bits per byte and that is the ratio. Knowing this has immediate practical uses.

MetricWhat it measuresStrengthWatch out for
Per-token perplexityAverage difficulty of getting one token rightEasy to track during trainingIncomparable across tokenizers. Bigger token chunks look better
Bits per byteBits spent per original byteComparable even with different tokenizersHeavily dependent on data type
Compression ratio (data only)The same value as a percentageDirectly comparable with codecsYou must disclose that model size is excluded
Compression ratio (model included)Decompressor and parameters counted inWhat actually has to be sentExtremely unfavorable to large models

Starting with the strengths,

  • Relatively resistant to benchmark contamination. It is not a score you raise by memorizing answer choices
  • Continuous. Not right or wrong but how confident, folded into the value
  • Easy to swap domains. Measure on your own corpus and you get fit to that corpus directly
  • No human grading. Reproducible and automatable

The limits are equally clear.

  • Training data contamination destroys it. If the evaluation text was in training, the ratio measures memorization, not skill. Hence the rule of measuring on data created after the model's release date
  • It does not track usefulness. Whether a model follows instructions, calls tools correctly, or declines when it should is invisible here
  • It swings hard with data type. A value measured on code cannot be compared with one measured on prose
  • Interpretation gets murky after alignment. Post-training that reshapes the output distribution can worsen the ratio on raw text, which does not mean the model got worse

So the practical conclusion is not pick models by compression ratio but use compression ratio as one metric among several, with conditions pinned. On the training side, the fact that computing this loss itself becomes a GPU memory bottleneck is covered in Saving Memory on Cross Entropy Loss - Why the LM Head Eats GPU Memory in LLM Training.

10. What the Hutter Prize puts prize money on

The most explicit institutionalization of this equivalence is the Hutter Prize. Entrants compete to make enwik9, a one gigabyte slice of English Wikipedia, as small as possible, with cash for each improvement. What deserves attention in the rules is not the money but what gets measured.

  • The target file is enwik9, one gigabyte of text extracted from English Wikipedia
  • Entries are scored on the compressed output plus the decompressor executable, together, not the output alone
  • That total must be at most 99% of the previous winning entry to qualify
  • 5,000 euros per 1% improvement, against a fund of 500,000 euros
  • Execution time and memory limits apply, and since 2017 the source must be released under a free software license
  • A 30-day public comment period precedes any award
  • Per the winners list summarized on Wikipedia, the recent record is 110,793,128 bytes, set on September 3, 2024 by fx2-cmix from Kaido Orav and Byron Knoll

The hypothesis on offer is explicit: making text smaller requires understanding text better. Grammar, factual relations, register and world knowledge all contribute to prediction, so the ratio serves as a proxy for comprehension. The idea carried prize money long before large language models became a topic of general conversation.

But it is worth seeing that the rules narrow the range of that hypothesis themselves. The moment you count the decompressor and impose time and memory ceilings, approaches that arrive carrying enormous parameter counts are excluded from the start. So actual winning entries are sophisticated context-mixing engineering rather than the smartest model. The accounting discussion from section 6.1 is implemented here as a rulebook.

  • What it measures — the frontier of text prediction reachable inside one machine with bounded time and memory
  • What it does not — the prediction ceiling with unlimited resources, and whether that ability transfers to other tasks
  • What it still gives — accounting nailed down tightly enough to produce one of the few figures comparable across years

11. Seven common misconceptions

  1. Compressing well means intelligence — the arrow points the other way. Predicting well makes compression good. The reverse holds only inside that data's domain, and only partly
  2. Entropy is an intrinsic property of a file — it is not. It is always relative to a model, and changing the model lowers the floor for the same file
  3. Compressing an already-compressed file shrinks it further — it does not, and usually grows it. Well-compressed output resembles noise, and noise does not shrink
  4. You could build a compressor that shrinks every file — impossible. The pigeonhole principle refutes it immediately. Shorten some inputs and you must lengthen others
  5. LLM ratios beat codecs so codecs are obsolete — ratio is one axis of three. It stalls on speed and determinism, and changing the accounting flips the ranking itself
  6. Lower perplexity always means a better model — it depends on tokenizer and evaluation data. Comparison requires normalizing to bits per byte and fixing the corpus
  7. Lossy compression is degraded lossless — it solves a different problem. It decides what may be discarded, and that judgment rests on human perception, not probability

Of these seven, the ones that actually cost money in practice are 3 and 6. The third quietly burns CPU inside pipelines, and the sixth produces wrong conclusions in model selection meetings. What various context-reduction strategies actually save and lose is treated separately in Tokenmaxxing Is Dead — The Shift in LLM Context Efficiency Strategy.

12. What this article could not verify

Every figure above comes from a cited source. The items below fall outside what was confirmed here.

  • The enwik9 table in the DeepMind paper. The 43.4%, 16.4%, 58.5% and 30.3% used above are the values stated in the abstract. Text-data ratios and per-model comparisons from the body tables are not reproduced here
  • The adjusted ratio with parameters included. The same paper addresses this accounting, but those numbers were not carried over. Section 6.1 explains only the structure by which accounting splits in two
  • The exact time and memory ceilings in the Hutter Prize. That constraints exist is confirmed, but no specific figures are given. The official page did not open due to an expired certificate, so the figures above come from the Wikipedia winners list rather than the rules text
  • A small discrepancy in the record value. Sizes for fx2-cmix circulate as 110,793,128 bytes and as a slightly different figure. The gap appears to be a difference in what the total includes, but without checking the primary text only the first value is used above, with its conditions attached
  • The GPT-2 example in the source article. The original gives 434 bits for an order-1 model against 176 bits for GPT-2 on one sentence, without stating tokenizer handling or the byte length of the target. It illustrates a direction only, so it is not used to support any argument here
  • Measured figures for the shell example in section 3. The direction follows straight from theory, but no byte counts for a specific environment are given

Conclusion: a predictor gives you a compressor, the reverse is conditional

If one sentence survives from this article, it is this. If something produces probabilities, turning it into a compressor is a mechanical procedure. Arithmetic coding is that procedure, the logarithm of the probability becomes the bits, and the costs simply add. Nothing is conditional up to here.

Conditions start appearing when you walk the other direction. What can be concluded from the fact that a ratio is good? What can be said safely is the regularity in that data was captured well, and no more. That the regularity can be explained, or carried to another task, or amounts to understanding, are all additional claims requiring separate evidence.

So three questions are enough when this subject comes up at work. What data was it measured on. Was model size counted. Does decoding actually work. Check those three every time you see a compression ratio and most overstatement filters itself out on the spot.

And the real value of this lens is not the leaderboard, it is the thinking tool. The question why does this data not shrink is always the same question as what does my model not know. Why unifying log formats improves ratios, why columnar storage helps, why a pre-shared dictionary rescues short payloads — all variations on one question. Once you read compression as prediction, half of storage and transfer work turns into a data layout problem.
