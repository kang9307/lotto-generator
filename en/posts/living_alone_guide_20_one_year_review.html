6. What a Full Year Teaches You

Once you have a full year behind you, living alone stops feeling like an exception and starts feeling like the default. According to the Korean statistics office report "1-Person Households in Statistics 2025," published in December 2025, single-person households numbered 8.045 million as of 2024, making up 36.1% of all households in the country. More than one in three homes is a household of one, so living alone is no longer a minority choice but close to the most common household form there is.

People tend to picture solo living as something people in their twenties do, but the age spread is far wider. In the same report, those aged 70 and over made up the largest share at 19.8%, followed by people 29 and under at 17.8%, those in their 60s at 17.6%, and those in their 30s at 17.4%. It is not concentrated in one generation at all; it is spread fairly evenly across every age band. Knowing that the inconvenience you are dealing with is shared by eight million other households, rather than being your personal failing, takes some of the weight off on its own.

Looking at housing types, detached houses came first at 39.0%, followed by apartments at 35.9% and row houses or multi-unit dwellings at 11.7%. The home ownership rate among single-person households was 32.0%, which means close to seven in ten are renting. That is why one year is not just a lap around the calendar for a solo household. It is also the halfway mark of a standard two-year lease. This is exactly why a retrospective should not stop at nostalgia. If you turn the looking back into a check-up, the next year gets a lot easier.

A check-up does not have to be elaborate. Split it into five areas, housing, money, health, relationships, and safety, then sweep through each one on a set schedule. That alone prevents most of the serious problems. Write it on paper or in a notes app at first, and once the habit sticks, put it in your calendar as a repeating event. Lease expiry dates and health screening years in particular are hard to undo once missed, so set the reminders in advance.

The most important column in that table is actually the one on the right. You can write the perfect list of items, but if you never decide when to look at it, you end up never looking at it. Tie quarterly checks to the months when the season turns, half-yearly checks to the start of summer and winter, and the annual check to your move-in anniversary. Anchoring them to dates you already remember is what makes the habit survive.

  • Attach your check-up day to an existing habit, such as the weekend after payday
  • Do not try to do all five areas at once; give one area fifteen minutes
  • Note both what you fixed and what you postponed so the next round goes faster
  • Take photos, which makes slow-moving problems like mold or leaks easy to compare later
  • Anything the landlord or building office needs to know, text it the moment you find it so there is a record

7. Money - Reviewing a Year of Spending

The most useful part of a one-year review is, in the end, the money. The same 2025 report put average monthly consumption spending for single-person households in 2024 at 1.689 million won, about 58.4% of the 2.89 million won average across all households. By category, housing, water, and fuel came first at 18.4%, with food and lodging right behind at 18.2%. In other words, more than a third of a solo household's spending goes to the cost of having a home and the cost of eating.

The picture is only complete when you look at income too. Annual income for single-person households in 2024 was 34.23 million won, about 46.1% of the all-household average. Income is under half, but spending is over half. The reason living alone does not halve your cost of living is straightforward: rent, maintenance fees, phone bills, and insurance premiums do not scale with the number of people in the home. So a year-one spending review should not be a vow to spend less. It should be a rebuild of your fixed-cost list.

The method is simple. Download the last twelve months of card and bank statements and pull out only the items that left your account every single month. That list is the true shape of your fixed costs. Most people find one or two lines that make them think, is that still coming out? Once you sort each item by what to verify and where the savings are, next year's budget more or less writes itself.

Annual Fixed-Cost Review Table

Fixed CostWhat to VerifyWhere to Save
RentContract amount matches what actually leaves your account; size of any renewal increaseNegotiate at renewal, compare local market rates, consider a deposit-heavy structure

Once the fixed costs are sorted, the next step is a budget. For assigning every won a job before you spend it, see zero-based budgeting; for splitting income by ratio, see the 50/30/20 budget rule. If you want a target for your safety net, building an emergency fund from your salary covers it. To cut the utility bills themselves, Part 4: Utility Bills and the winter heating cost guide go into specifics, and the fundamentals are in Part 11: Money Management.

  • Pull only the monthly-recurring lines out of twelve months of statements to lock down your fixed costs
  • Divide that fixed-cost total by your monthly income to get a percentage
  • If that percentage is over 50%, the thing to review is not a line item but the home itself
  • Within what is left, check separately how much goes to delivery and eating out
  • Make three months of fixed costs your first emergency fund target
  • Do not leave savings sitting in your checking account; route them out by automatic transfer

8. Home - Renew or Move?

The one thing you must not overlook in year one is the contract calendar. On a two-year lease you are now halfway through, and the renew-or-move decision arrives sooner than you expect. Under Korea's Housing Lease Protection Act, a tenant can exercise the right to request contract renewal, but it has to be requested between six months and two months before the lease ends. Miss that window and you may lose the right entirely, so the safest move is to set a reminder for the date six months out.

The renewal right can be exercised once, and the renewed lease runs for two years. Any rent increase at renewal is capped at 5% of the existing amount. Remembering just those three things, the six-to-two-month window, the one-time limit, and the 5% ceiling, puts you in a far stronger position in the renewal conversation. That said, renewal can be refused for reasons set out in the law, such as the landlord moving in themselves, so it is safer to keep the possibility of moving open and prepare for both.

The lease reporting requirement is worth checking too. A residential lease with a deposit above 60 million won or monthly rent above 300,000 won must be reported within 30 days of signing. This applies not only to new contracts but also to renewals where the amount changes, and failing to report can bring a fine of up to one million won. The rule covers the entire capital region, the metropolitan cities, Sejong, Jeju City, and city-level areas within provinces, while county areas are excluded. Filing online through the real estate transaction management system (rtms.molit.go.kr) automatically grants the fixed date stamp, so there is no reason to put it off from a deposit-protection standpoint either.

So how do you choose between renewing and moving? There is no universal answer, but you can build a standard. Moving costs you agent fees, movers, new furniture and curtains, and several days of your time. Divide that total by the monthly rent difference to see how many months it takes to break even, and the decision becomes arithmetic instead of emotion.

  • Renew when: the increase was negotiated within 5% and recovering moving costs would take more than a year
  • Renew when: your commute works well and the place has no significant defects
  • Renew when: the landlord communicates well and repair requests are handled promptly
  • Renew when: local market rates have risen above what you are currently paying
  • Consider moving when: condensation, mold, or leaks persist no matter how often you report them
  • Consider moving when: noise or security problems are costing you sleep night after night
  • Consider moving when: housing takes up an unreasonable share of your income
  • Consider moving when: your daily geography changed and the commute has grown significantly
  • Either way: coordinate at least two months ahead so the deposit return and the new balance payment do not collide

If you want to reweigh the deposit-versus-monthly-rent question, see the jeonse vs monthly rent guide, and to go through the contract clauses line by line, use the lease contract checklist. If you are hunting for a place again, Part 1: Finding a Place still applies, and Part 2: Moving Checklist covers the preparation.

9. Health and Daily Habits Check

Health is the easiest thing to postpone when you live alone. With nobody around to drive you to a clinic, you end up telling yourself that a bit of rest will sort it out. The statistics put average monthly health spending for single-person households in 2024 at about 122,000 won, and the right way to read that number is not that illness is expensive but that routine maintenance matters that much.

The first thing to confirm is the national health screening. The general health check is provided once every two years as a rule, while non-office workers enrolled through their employer are eligible annually. Eligibility usually alternates by whether your birth year is odd or even, so check the national health insurance website or app once to see whether this is your year. It is surprisingly common to miss a free examination simply because nobody told you that you qualified.

Daily habits are better verified with numbers than with memory, because memory always bends in your own favor. Count each of the items below over the past month and it becomes obvious very quickly which part has quietly collapsed.

  • Your average weekday sleep, and how consistent your bedtime and wake time really are
  • How many meals a day you actually ate, and how many of those you cooked yourself
  • How many days a week you moved your body for thirty minutes or more
  • How many days in the month you did not leave the apartment at all
  • Which direction your weight has moved compared with a year ago, and by how much
  • Whether any expired medication is still sitting in your first-aid box
  • Whether you know which clinic and pharmacy are open at night or on holidays
  • Whether at least two people could be reached if you collapsed alone

What to do when illness actually hits is covered in Part 12: When You're Sick, and coping when your mood drops is in Part 14: Mental Health. The fastest way to rebuild your meals is to read Part 7: Simple Recipes alongside Part 8: Fridge Management.

After about a year, problems start appearing that were not there at the beginning. Most of them are not because the building suddenly aged, but because nobody told you the maintenance intervals. 11. Planning the Next Year

A retrospective ends with a plan. But if you write ten resolutions the way people do in January, most of them will not survive. In year one it works far better to cut the list to three goals and rewrite each one as a measurable sentence. Not "save money," but "cut fixed costs by 50,000 won a month and move that amount out by automatic transfer." A number and a deadline are what let you judge later whether you actually did it.

Pick those three from different areas: one about money, one about your body, one about your mind or your relationships. Spread that way, one area failing does not take the others down with it. If you make all three savings goals instead, a single month of unexpected spending makes the whole plan feel like a failure.

  • Money: cut fixed costs by 50,000 won a month and auto-transfer that amount to savings
  • Money: open the subscription list in June and December and cancel anything unused for three months
  • Body: walk or exercise thirty minutes three times a week and mark it on a calendar
  • Body: if this is your screening year, book it in the first half and keep the results
  • Mind: make one plan a month to eat with someone outside the apartment
  • Home: set a reminder six months before the lease ends and compare renewing against moving then

Making a plan is easier than revisiting one, so write the review date down at the same time you write the goals. Once a quarter, fifteen minutes, is enough. Read the three goals in order, mark each as kept or not, and for anything you missed, lower the target and write it again. The point is to shrink the goal rather than file it under failure.

Anyone who has made it through a year has already run into most of the ordinary problems at least once. Compare the blankness of moving-in day with where you are now and it is clear that possessions are not the only thing that grew. You gained a sense of where to look when something breaks, enough slack that an unexpected expense does not knock you over, and a working method for handling time spent alone. The next year starts from there.
