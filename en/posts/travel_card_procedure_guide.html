What Actually Gets Added to a Single Overseas Card Payment

Where the fee layers attach, what to press on the currency choice screen, and how ATMs, card loss and leftover balances work - as of August 2026

Aug 11, 2026 Finance & Economy

Read this first - scope and limits

This article is informational, based on official guidance current as of August 2026, and is not financial advice. It deliberately contains no issuer names, product names, fee percentages or preferential exchange rates. Those values differ by product and change often, so pinning them into an article turns them into wrong information. The fees, rate-setting method and ancillary benefits that actually apply are fixed only by the card issuer's terms and product disclosure. Nothing here forecasts exchange rates or encourages topping up to profit from currency movement. It is written around cards issued in Korea; the comparison section below notes how cards issued elsewhere differ.

What really happens when you tap a card abroad

Almost everyone has done the arithmetic at the till, noted the figure, and then been surprised a month later when the statement shows something else. The instinct is to blame the exchange rate, but the rate is rarely the whole story. Between the terminal abroad and the statement at home, the currency changes twice, and different parties take their share along the way.

The Credit Finance Association of Korea describes the route this way. The merchant authorises the transaction in the local currency, the international card network converts that amount into US dollars and bills the domestic issuer, and the domestic issuer converts it into Korean won and bills the cardholder. Euros, yen, baht - it makes no difference. Even where the dollar is not used, settlement passes through it.

That dollar detour is not merely a card-network habit. Korea's exchange rate publication system is itself built around the dollar. Seoul Money Brokerage Services publishes the market average rate each business morning, calculated as a volume-weighted average of spot transactions brokered in the market, and rates for other currencies are derived as cross rates from the dollar rates formed in international markets. There is no separately quoted market price for swapping won directly into Thai baht; it is computed through the dollar.

The line worth remembering: an overseas payment involves two conversions, not one. That is how many places a fee can attach.

The fee is not one layer - taking it apart

Reading a phrase such as no overseas fees as meaning nothing is added will throw off your arithmetic. The cost of an overseas payment is a stack of layers with different owners, and they differ in one crucial respect: whether you can do anything about them.

The column that actually decides anything is the fourth. Layers 1 and 3 attach to the act of converting currency, so switching products does not make them vanish. What a product describes as waived is usually layer 2. That is why two products both advertising a waiver still bill different amounts - they set the rate for layers 1 and 3 differently, and at different moments.

Layer 4 is the opposite case: it is the one cost you can delete at the terminal. Since it is the only layer where control sits entirely with the cardholder, it gets its own section below.

If you want the actual percentages, look them up per product. The disclosure site of the Credit Finance Association of Korea lets you compare fee rates by company and by product. Opening the disclosure for the card already in your wallet beats trusting a rate table circulating on a forum.

When is the exchange rate fixed - not on the day you paid

Most people assume billing uses the rate at the moment of payment. It does not. The Credit Finance Association of Korea states that the rate applied is the one on the day the overseas transaction record reaches the domestic issuer from the international card network, and that this handoff normally takes three to seven days.

Two things follow. First, a mismatch between the figure you worked out on holiday and the figure on the statement is normal. Second, that difference is a settlement lag, not a gain or a loss. Nobody knows which way the rate moves over those days, and this article does not pretend to.

The base rate itself is republished daily. Seoul Money Brokerage Services publishes the market average rate and the derived cross rates at around 8:30 each business morning and notifies the Ministry of Economy and Finance, the Bank of Korea and foreign exchange banks. Given that rates move within a single day too, the premise that today's rate can pin down tomorrow's bill does not hold in the first place.

If what you want is the mechanics of exchange rates themselves, that ground is covered separately in Understanding Exchange Rates. This article is not about how a rate is set, but about how an already-set rate gets applied inside card settlement.

Very different products hide under the name travel card

Travel card is not a legal term. It is closer to a marketing bracket for products aimed at overseas use, and inside that bracket sit three legally distinct things. Miss the difference and everything downstream changes: what to do when the card is lost, and what happens to money left on it.

Article 2 of Korea's Specialized Credit Finance Business Act defines a debit card as an instrument issued so that settlement occurs by electronic or magnetic transfer to a financial account between the holder and the merchant, excluding instruments that let the holder borrow funds. It defines a prepaid card as an instrument issued by a credit card business after receiving payment in advance and recording the corresponding amount on it. The dividing line is whether money leaves an account instantly or was received in advance and written onto the card.

Balances topped up inside an app split off once more. They are frequently treated as prepaid electronic payment means under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act. According to a Financial Services Commission release dated 3 September 2024, the amended act that took effect on the 15th of that month requires prepaid issuers to segregate the entire prepaid balance through trust, deposit or payment guarantee insurance. That segregation, however, is a different regime from deposit insurance, and issuers below certain thresholds for outstanding or annual issuance are exempt from registration altogether.

The cell worth staring at is when conversion happens for the prepaid row. Because the money is already in foreign currency, a payment merely draws down the balance. That makes spending easy to cap and removes the wait for a statement. In exchange, an insufficient balance means a decline on the spot, and places that pre-authorise a deposit - hotels, car rental - lock up part of your balance for the duration.

So the practical split is this. If the goal is a hard spending ceiling, prepaid fits the purpose. If the itinerary involves deposits, keep one credit card available. It is not a choice between them so much as a decision about which is primary and which is backup. The principles for combining cards day to day are set out in Maximizing Credit Card Benefits.

Would you like to pay in your home currency - the answer is already decided

Some terminals and ATMs abroad display an amount in your home currency and ask which currency you want to be charged in. This is dynamic currency conversion, or DCC. Seeing a familiar currency feels helpful, but what it actually does is insert one more conversion step into the route.

The Credit Finance Association of Korea puts the point plainly: paying in won rather than the local currency adds a step in which won is converted into the local currency before the local-currency settlement, so a conversion fee is charged one more time, which is why local-currency billing is the better choice abroad.

Visa's consumer guidance sets the rules around it. Merchants and ATMs must not choose on your behalf, and must clearly display the amounts in both the local and your home currency, both currency symbols, the exchange rate applied, and any additional fees or markup assessed, on screen and on receipts. They should also refrain from using devices such as different font size or colour to steer your decision. If those details are not visible, or you feel pressure to pick one currency, Visa's advice is to decline the conversion offer and report the incident to your card issuer. It also states plainly that declining will not affect your ability to make purchases or withdraw cash internationally.

This article does not quote a percentage for the DCC markup. It varies by provider and merchant, and it is a figure that is meant to be on the screen in front of you. When a home-currency amount appears, read the rate and the fee printed beside it. If there is nothing to read, that absence is itself a departure from the network rules.

Korean issuers operate a home-currency billing block. Once registered, won-denominated authorisations are declined, so there is no button left to press by mistake. The trade-off is that a handful of merchants abroad process only home-currency transactions, and there the transaction may fail outright - which means keeping another payment method or knowing how to lift the block temporarily.

ATM withdrawals work differently from payments

The same card behaves differently depending on whether you are buying something or taking out cash. A payment is merchant turnover; a withdrawal hands you money. In particular, drawing cash on a credit card at an overseas ATM is treated as a short-term card loan under some products, and interest accrues from the day of withdrawal, exactly as it does at home. Why that structure is dangerous is set out in Card Loans and Cash Advances.

Withdrawing on a debit or prepaid card is not borrowing, but other layers appear instead.

  • Local ATM operator fee: charged by the business running the machine, independent of your issuer, and different from machine to machine
  • Issuer overseas withdrawal fee: frequently a flat amount per transaction, which means splitting a withdrawal into several increases the total
  • Network currency conversion: applies exactly as it does to a payment
  • DCC on the ATM screen: the home-currency prompt appears at cash machines too, and the answer is still the local currency

Two principles collide here. On flat-fee arithmetic alone, one large withdrawal wins; but carrying more cash raises the risk of theft. Korea's Financial Supervisory Service advises choosing machines belonging to well-known financial institutions that many people use. Put the two together and the judgement resolves cleanly: withdraw at a machine attached to a bank branch, as few times as possible, in amounts sized to a few days of cash need. Pulling an entire trip's budget in one go buys a bigger risk to save a smaller fee.

What to check on the card before departure

Before asking whether you need a new card, check whether the one already in your wallet will work where you are going. The items below recur across the guidance from the Credit Finance Association of Korea and the Financial Supervisory Service.

  • An international network logo on the face of the card - domestic-only cards will not work abroad
  • Expiry date comfortably beyond the end of the trip
  • The romanised name on the card matches the passport - it matters wherever identification is requested
  • Whether it is an IC chip card - Europe runs largely on chip-based acceptance, and non-chip cards are refused in many places
  • Overseas use registration and overseas limits are switched on - some products require overseas use to be enabled separately
  • Billing date and account balance - if a billing date falls mid-trip, the domestic account still needs funds
  • The loss reporting number stored apart from the card - lose the wallet and you lose the number with it
  • Mobile transaction alerts enabled - the fastest way to notice unauthorised use
  • Immigration record consent service - a free service that screens out overseas authorisations arriving while there is no departure record
  • Home-currency billing block registered or not
  • Two cards on different networks - some merchants refuse a given brand, and a suspended card needs an alternative
  • For prepaid products, the top-up route and balance check method - if the app will not open abroad, you can neither reload nor check
Relying on a single card is the most common failure point. One primary card, one backup on a different network, plus a small amount of cash is the baseline, and the backup lives somewhere other than the primary.

Loss and theft - the time of the report sets the boundary

In a card incident the decisive variable is not the amount but the timing. How unauthorised use is treated turns on when the report was filed, so getting the order wrong is hard to undo.

  1. Report to your issuer's loss reporting centre immediately. Not the local branch, not the embassy - the issuer comes first. The Financial Supervisory Service also lists immediate reporting as the first step once a loss is confirmed
  2. File a police report locally. The receipt becomes supporting evidence in disputes and insurance claims
  3. Ask about an emergency replacement card. The Credit Finance Association of Korea notes that a temporary card can be issued within about two days while you are still travelling
  4. For a prepaid product, freeze it in the app and check the balance. A different instrument means a different handling route
  5. Log unauthorised transactions by time and amount. Keep the alert messages rather than deleting them
  6. Register an overseas use suspension after returning home. The Financial Supervisory Service lists this among post-trip measures

One misconception surfaces repeatedly. The idea that reporting from abroad achieves nothing is simply wrong. The clock that matters is when the report reached the domestic issuer, and loss reporting centres run around the clock regardless of local time or holidays. It is the opposite belief - that it can wait until you get home - that actually creates the loss.

Where travel insurance and card coverage overlap

Some cards attach travel-related protection as an ancillary benefit, which makes it easy to confuse with insurance. The two rest on different foundations.

  • Handling of unauthorised card use is not insurance; it follows the Specialized Credit Finance Business Act regime and the issuer's terms. It applies whether or not you bought a policy
  • Medical costs, liability and flight delay abroad are insurance coverage. Even when delivered as a card benefit, the substance is an insurance contract with its own limits and exclusions
  • Indemnity-type coverage such as baggage loss pays only within the actual loss even if you hold two policies. Holding a card benefit and a separate policy does not double the payout
  • Card benefits often carry conditions, such as having bought the flight with that card. An itinerary that misses the condition falls outside the cover

In short, a card benefit overlaps with insurance rather than replacing it. Coverage by peril and the duplication problem in indemnity policies are handled separately in the Complete Guide to Travel Insurance.

After the trip - balances, converting back, and the statement

Settlement continues for days after the trip ends. The final transactions take time to arrive, so an incomplete statement immediately after landing is normal.

  • Leftover foreign currency: converting a prepaid balance back to won triggers another conversion. Once on the way in, once on the way out - the count becomes two, and that is the only thing to factor in. Whether the rate will rise or fall is not a basis for the decision
  • Balance validity and expiry rules: they differ by product. How unused balances are handled and refunded is written into the terms, so check the issuer's guidance
  • Cancellations and refunds: a reversal is also processed at the rate on its own receipt date, so the won amount will not match the original exactly. That difference is not an error
  • Reconcile the statement: check that the number of items matches your alerts. Unfamiliar merchant names or duplicate charges are grounds for requesting the sales slip
  • Converting to instalments: the Credit Finance Association of Korea notes that a single-payment overseas transaction can be switched to instalments after returning. Instalments, though, are a credit transaction and carry a fee
  • Overseas use suspension: if no overseas spending is planned for a while, registering it is the safer default

How this compares with cards issued elsewhere

Everything above is written around cards issued in Korea. Change the country of issue and the same payment carries costs under different names in different places. For readers holding foreign-issued cards alongside, here is the character of the difference - without figures.

  • US-issued cards: the share taken on cross-border transactions is conventionally disclosed as a standalone foreign transaction fee line. What corresponds to layer 2 above is surfaced as a named item in the product disclosure, which makes comparison relatively easy
  • Japan-issued cards: prepaid foreign-currency cards for overseas use have circulated for a long time. Loading first and drawing down a balance makes them the same species as the Korean prepaid products. The governing statute and the method of protecting balances, however, differ by country
  • China-issued cards: the domestic network has broad acceptance, and cards holding two currency accounts on one piece of plastic are in use. Because acceptance by brand varies sharply by region, the network choice for a backup card matters more
  • What is the same everywhere: wherever the card was issued, the DCC prompt appears identically and the answer is identically the local currency. Network rules apply without regard to the country of issue
  • What is not the same: the loss reporting channel and the redress procedure follow the regime of the issuer's home country. A Korean-issued card is reported to the Korean issuer, a foreign-issued card to its own issuer
  • Not verified in this section: the actual fee rates of the products in those countries were outside the research scope. Only their character is described here; the numbers have to come from each issuer's own disclosure

Six places where assumptions go wrong

  1. Assuming no overseas fees removes every cost - the waiver usually covers the issuer layer, and the layers inherent to conversion remain
  2. Reading a home-currency display as a courtesy - the price of that convenience is an extra conversion step
  3. Assuming billing uses the rate on the day you paid - the receipt-date rate applies, three to seven days later
  4. Assuming one prepaid card is enough - insufficient balances and deposit holds will stop you. A backup is required
  5. Assuming frequent small withdrawals are safer - under a flat per-transaction fee, frequency is cost
  6. Assuming card benefits replace travel insurance - the perils covered and the conditions attached are different

What this article could not pin down

The structure is public; the numbers often are not. Here is what was left without a value, and where to find it.

  • Network conversion rates and issuer overseas fee rates - they vary by network, product and period. Look them up per product on the disclosure site of the Credit Finance Association of Korea
  • The size of the DCC markup - it varies by provider and merchant. Network rules require it on the screen and receipt, so reading it there is the accurate route
  • Local ATM operator fees - they differ by operator and there is no consolidated published standard. Beyond reading the on-screen notice, there is no reliable way to check in advance
  • The level of balance protection on prepaid products - the scope of the segregation duty depends on issuance size, and the method used by a given product is fixed only in its terms
  • The detailed card pages on the Financial Supervisory Service consumer portal - they appear in search results, but the paths have changed and the originals could not be opened. Frequently asked questions

Q. I chose the local currency but the receipt shows my home currency.

If a home-currency amount appears alongside an exchange rate, the transaction may have been processed as DCC. Visa's guidance is that the cardholder must be given the choice and the disclosure requirements must be met, so if you were not offered the choice, notifying your card issuer is the procedural step. Keep the sales slip rather than discarding it.

Q. Is a home-currency billing block always the better setting?

Usually, with one exception. A few merchants abroad process only home-currency transactions, and with the block in place the authorisation itself is declined. That situation calls for another payment method or a temporary lift, so it is worth remembering both that the block is on and how to lift it.

Q. Is it better to top up before the trip?

This article offers no view on which way rates will move. Choosing when to top up is a bet on currency movement, and the outcome is not predictable. One thing is structurally certain, though: topping up and later converting the remainder back means two conversions. Loading only what you expect to spend removes the question entirely.

Q. When people say carry two cards, does that mean two different networks?

Yes. Two cards on the same network fail together at a merchant that does not accept that network. Different networks mean one covers for the other. Keeping them in separate places is the other half of the point.

Q. My card was suddenly declined abroad.

Several causes are possible. Overseas use may not be enabled, the limit may be exhausted, or the fraud detection system may have flagged the transaction. A home-currency billing block meeting a merchant that only processes home currency is another. Calling the issuer for the reason is fastest, and the backup card covers the interval.

Q. Is money on a prepaid foreign currency card protected like a bank deposit?

It sits under a different regime from deposit insurance. The amended Electronic Financial Transactions Act, effective 15 September 2024, requires prepaid issuers to segregate the full prepaid balance through trust, deposit or payment guarantee insurance. Issuers below certain size thresholds are exempt from registration altogether, so how a specific product is protected has to be confirmed in the issuer's disclosure and terms.

Q. Can I withdraw cash on a credit card abroad?

You can, but know what it is. Depending on the product it is processed as a short-term card loan with interest accruing from the day of withdrawal. Unless it is urgent, withdrawing on a debit or prepaid card keeps the cost structure simpler.

In short - three things to settle before departure

Overseas payments look complicated, but the number of points a consumer actually controls is small. Concentrating on those is the efficient move.

First, always choose the local currency at the terminal. Of all the fee layers this is the only one you can delete, and the Credit Finance Association of Korea and Visa point the same way. To remove the possibility of a mistake, register the home-currency billing block.

Second, carry two cards on different networks plus a small amount of cash. A card failing is more common than people expect, and without an alternative the trouble outweighs any fee saved. Write the loss reporting number down somewhere other than the wallet.

Third, read the statement knowing it is based on the receipt date, not the payment date. There is a three to seven day lag and the rate over that period is not forecastable. Rather than treating a different number as an error, check first that the item count and merchant names line up.
