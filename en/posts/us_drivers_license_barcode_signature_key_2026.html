1. Hidden Signatures and Recovered Public Keys — Verification Mechanisms Concealed Behind Standards

Across the United States, physical driver's licenses serve as the foundational identity document for everyday civilian life. Beyond validating operating privileges on public roadways, these state-issued credentials facilitate banking transactions, commercial domestic flight boarding, age-restricted commercial purchases, and legal identity attestations. On the reverse side of nearly every card sits a large, high-density two-dimensional barcode. Commercial scanners at airport checkpoints, retail registers, and financial institutions regularly ingest this optical symbol to parse cardholder demographics in fractions of a second. Yet beneath this nationwide reliance on optical automation lies an astonishing cryptographic void: the overwhelming majority of identity barcodes store cardholder attributes in unencrypted, unauthenticated plaintext.

To mitigate the resulting surge in counterfeit credentials, select state vehicle licensing agencies and third-party security card printers quietly embedded digital signatures into their barcode payloads. However, digital signatures fundamentally require public verification keys to fulfill their security objective. Without published verification specifications, mathematical definitions, or public key certificates, these cryptographic fields remained entirely inaccessible to the general public for multiple years. Outside the issuing agency's proprietary, closed-circuit databases and law enforcement backends, commercial relying parties—including hospitality venues, retail staff, and corporate security officers—were left with no technical means of validating the authenticity of the signatures stamped onto the cards.

On September 16, 2026, security researcher Ryan Fahey published an extensive technical investigation titled Keys Not Included. His research dissected the proprietary barcode signatures implemented by Canadian Bank Note (CBN)—the specialized security printing firm responsible for producing driver's licenses across multiple US states—and mathematically recovered the dormant ECDSA public verification keys. Fahey's work did not compromise or leak private signing keys, which would enable card counterfeiting. Instead, it systematically reverse-engineered and recovered the public verification keys that ought to have been openly disseminated from the day of deployment. By decomposing the closed architecture through mathematical recovery and binary analysis, this research illuminated the friction between proprietary security implementations and the requirements of modern public-key identity infrastructure.

2. AAMVA Standard and the Plaintext Structure of Driver License Barcodes

Driver's license barcodes across all fifty US states, US territories, and Canadian provinces adhere to technical specifications defined by the American Association of Motor Vehicle Administrators (AAMVA). The standard specifies the high-density PDF417 stacked two-dimensional symbology, engineered to store hundreds of alphanumeric characters within a compact physical footprint readable by standard optical imaging hardware.

The standard AAMVA payload comprises a standardized file header followed by one or more data subfiles. The header specifies compliance designators, file type indicators, and a six-digit Issuer Identification Number (IIN) assigned to each issuing jurisdiction. The imaging scanner uses this IIN to determine which state Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) issued the credential. Following the header, individual identity elements are delineated by designated three-character element identifiers followed by raw text values.

Crucially, the standard AAMVA specification defines these elements as unencrypted plaintext strings without mandatory cryptographic signatures or tamper-evident checksums. The core identity elements defined by the specification include the following:

  • DCS: Identifies the legal family surname of the credential holder.
  • DAC: Designates the legal first or given name of the credential holder.
  • DAD: Records the middle name or middle initial of the credential holder.
  • DBB: Stores the credential holder's date of birth encoded as an eight-digit date string.
  • DBA: Reflects the expiration date of the physical driver's license document.
  • DBD: Specifies the exact calendar date on which the physical credential was issued.
  • DAQ: Represents the jurisdiction's unique driver's license or identification card number.
  • DCF: Denotes the document discriminator number assigned to track the specific card manufacturing batch.

Because the AAMVA standard is an open, publicly available document, fabricating a syntactically valid barcode requires nothing more than string concatenation formatted to specification and passed into an open-source PDF417 barcode rendering library. Conventional retail scanning applications merely check syntax and date ranges; they lack any mathematical mechanism to determine whether the encoded text was stamped by a legitimate state government or generated by an illicit software script. As Fahey previously demonstrated in his essay Insecure by Design, the baseline AAMVA standard was architecturally insecure by design because it omitted asymmetric cryptographic verification from its inception.

3. Two Design Philosophies — California Open Standard vs Canadian Bank Note Proprietary Signatures

Faced with escalating barcode fabrication, state governments began integrating digital signatures into physical card barcodes. However, the resulting implementations diverged into two completely antithetical engineering philosophies based on vendor selection: California's open, standards-based verification model versus Canadian Bank Note's proprietary, closed-box design.

California announced that starting October 1, 2025, digital signatures would be embedded in all newly issued driver's licenses. The manufacturing contract for California's physical cards is held by IDEMIA, a global identity vendor that produces driver's licenses for thirty-one of fifty-one US jurisdictions under a twelve-year agreement awarded in 2022. For its signature architecture, California selected the W3C Verifiable Credential Barcode specification. This open standard compresses credential assertions using CBOR-LD (Concise Binary Object Representation for Linked Data) and cryptographically signs them using the ecdsa-xi-2023 cryptosuite.

The paramount feature of California's deployment was its transparency and commitment to open verification. The California DMV published its cryptographic public keys at a publicly resolvable Decentralized Identifier (DID) endpoint at did:web:credentials.dmv.ca.gov. Furthermore, the agency provided an official, open-source verification application that enables commercial establishments, developers, and citizens to verify credential signatures offline or online without paying proprietary toll gates. This open architecture established an authentic public-key trust ecosystem where anyone can independently verify official state provenance.

In sharp contrast, Canadian Bank Note (CBN)—a venerable Canadian security printing firm—developed a closed signature mechanism for its clients. CBN manufactures physical driver's licenses for five US states: New York, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, and Wisconsin. In his research, Fahey confirmed that all five of these states possessed dedicated digital signature fields within their barcode data. However, neither CBN nor the respective state DMVs published technical documentation, data framing specifications, or public verification keys. As a consequence, the cryptographic signatures embedded on millions of active driver's licenses were rendered completely useless to the commercial relying parties who inspect IDs every day.

The contrast between California's IDEMIA deployment and CBN's multi-state architecture highlights fundamental divergences in engineering philosophy:

  • Trust Model Philosophy: California embraced an open-verification model where third-party trust is fostered through transparency, whereas CBN implemented a closed security-by-obscurity paradigm that limited verification exclusively to internal government hardware.
  • Standardization Compliance: California adopted international web standards (W3C Verifiable Credentials and CBOR-LD serialization), while CBN appended vendor-defined binary payloads onto legacy AAMVA string envelopes.
  • Key Distribution Channels: California deployed automated web-based DID document resolution allowing instant public key retrieval, whereas CBN maintained complete silence, failing to publish public verification keys for several consecutive years.
  • Cryptographic Failure Isolation: CBN designed each state with an independent asymmetric key pair, ensuring that a cryptographic compromise or key rotation in one state would not cascade into neighboring jurisdictions.

4. Comparing State Subfields and Barcode Signature Architectures

The AAMVA specification accommodates jurisdiction-specific needs by permitting custom subfiles prefixed with the letter Z followed by state identifier characters. Third-party vendors and state licensing administrators leverage these custom subfiles to append non-standard data blocks. An examination of Issuer Identification Numbers (IIN) and proprietary subfield tags across jurisdictions illustrates how signature implementations fragmented across different manufacturing contracts.

5. Reverse Engineering — Signature Field Structure and DER Encoding Analysis

To unravel CBN's undocumented signature system, Ryan Fahey's initial analytical step focused on the character composition of the opaque text strings occupying the custom subfields. Because PDF417 barcode scanners translate optical bars into ASCII text streams, raw binary bytes containing null values or parser control codes cannot be directly embedded. Printers must therefore encode binary cryptographic tokens into printable text.

Frequency analysis of the character distribution demonstrated that CBN did not utilize Base64. Instead, the vendor implemented Ascii85 (also known as Base85). Ascii85 packs four bytes of binary data into five printable ASCII characters, incurring only a 25 percent size expansion compared to Base64's 33 percent penalty. This efficiency is critical when packing dense signatures into the physically bounded square area of a PDF417 printed symbol.

Decoding the Ascii85 strings revealed a distinct, unambiguous binary payload. Every decoded sequence opened with the byte value 0x30, the standard ASN.1 Distinguished Encoding Rules (DER) identifier for a constructed SEQUENCE. Within this outer wrapper lay two 256-bit arbitrary-precision integers, denoted mathematically as r and s, representing a canonical on-wire Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA) signature.

  • Byte 0x30: Represents the standard ASN.1 DER tag identifying the start of a constructed SEQUENCE container.
  • Length Octet: Defines the cumulative byte length of the enclosed integer elements within the DER sequence.
  • Tag 0x02: Designates the first enclosed ASN.1 primitive integer, corresponding to the 256-bit ECDSA parameter r.
  • Tag 0x02: Designates the second enclosed ASN.1 primitive integer, corresponding to the 256-bit ECDSA parameter s, with dynamic leading zero padding depending on the sign bit.

Under strict DER encoding rules, if the highest-order bit of an integer is set to one, a leading 0x00 padding byte must be prefixed to denote a positive integer. Consequently, the decoded binary signature length varied predictably across sample cards, measuring exactly 70, 71, or 72 bytes. This variation provided undeniable mathematical proof that the underlying cryptographic primitive was an authentic ECDSA signature computed over the NIST P-256 (secp256r1) elliptic curve.

6. Signed Message Reconstruction and the 4-Step Verification Algorithm

Establishing the signature primitive as ECDSA P-256 was only half the battle. In order to verify an asymmetric signature, a verifier must possess the exact original message byte sequence that was hashed by the signer. Standard digital signature protocols compute an asymmetric signature across a complete document and append the resulting signature to the footer, cleanly excluding the signature bytes from the digest input.

In CBN's barcode architecture, hashing the payload with the signature field stripped produced constant verification failures. Through meticulous byte slicing and reconstruction, Fahey discovered an idiosyncratic framing pattern: the signature field itself was embedded inside the signed message envelope. During card personalization, the issuing system populates the signature field with an ASCII placeholder composed entirely of zero characters (ASCII '0', byte value 0x30). The system then computes a cryptographic signature over the entire barcode string containing this zeroed placeholder. Finally, the generated Ascii85 signature is stamped directly over the placeholder span.

On an authentic New York State driver's license, the total barcode payload measures exactly 484 bytes. Within this stream, the ZNB signature string occupies the 90-byte span extending from byte index 393 through byte index 483. To reconstruct the exact pre-image message digested by the state's signing key, a verification scanner must slice the 484-byte payload, replace the 90 bytes between offsets 393 and 483 with ninety ASCII zero characters, and pass the resulting string to the hashing engine.

Stage 1 Raw Barcode Extraction Read raw decoded text stream from PDF417 optical symbol (484 bytes for NY) Raw composite alphanumeric payload
Stage 2 Zero-Mask Reconstruction Overwrite bytes 393 to 483 with 90 ASCII zero chars (payload[:393] + b"0"*90 + payload[483:]) Original signed message pre-image

This four-step algorithmic pipeline completely demystifies CBN's proprietary data formatting. Any scanner executing this sequence can reproduce the exact SHA-256 hash digest generated by the state DMV at the moment of card issuance. Any alteration to demographic text or expiration dates alters the digest, causing the mathematical verification equation to fail instantly.

7. Mathematical Recovery of Public Keys and Multi-Sample Cross-Verification

Even with the four-step parsing algorithm established, signature verification remained impossible without the issuing authority's public key. Because the state licensing agencies refused to publish their keys, Fahey leveraged the algebraic properties of elliptic curves to execute mathematical public key recovery.

Given an arbitrary message digest e and an ECDSA signature pair (r, s), it is mathematically possible to reconstruct the elliptic curve points that could have generated that signature. Because the elliptic curve equation y^2 = x^3 + ax + b allows two possible y coordinates for a given x coordinate, each valid signature yields up to two (and under rare curve configurations, four) candidate public keys. When analyzing multiple distinct signatures originating from the same signing authority, computing the intersection of the candidate key sets across multiple cards isolates the single authentic public key common to all issued credentials.

Fahey gathered multiple physical driver's licenses across several jurisdictions and executed cross-sample mathematical recovery. The empirical results demonstrated complete convergence on unique, immutable public keys for each state:

  • New York State: Analysis of three distinct physical cards yielded three pairwise comparisons, all converging on a single, identical compressed P-256 public key: 02851d63a281796be0ca11189f03028abf80e032838f83215889b9e708eac16482
  • Commonwealth of Virginia: Analysis of six distinct physical cards produced fifteen pairwise combinations, all converging without deviation upon a single compressed P-256 public key: 02d0f2823d63c854566c5da2cb07e114dbad16f874c2422f74806fe3e2f4775f1d
  • State of North Carolina: Analysis of two distinct physical cards provided the minimum required sample pair, which converged perfectly upon a single compressed P-256 public key: 0398956191b3fd2704362fdd8500dc0d4419c9379c5187cefd223dd4ee566da9e8
  • Cross-State Key Isolation: Attempting to verify a New York barcode signature against the Virginia public key, or vice versa, failed unconditionally, proving that each jurisdiction retains an entirely separate private signing key.

The recovered public keys conform to the standard 33-byte compressed elliptic curve representation, designated by the leading 02 or 03 parity octet. The fact that multiple independent cards issued across different dates converged mathematically onto single public keys confirms that these are the true production signing keys actively utilized by state DMVs.

8. Counterfeit Card Testing and Security Limits — What Signatures Prove and Do Not Prove

To demonstrate practical utility, Fahey implemented a browser-based optical verification proof-of-concept integrating the recovered public keys alongside California's official public keys. The application utilizes client-side optical character and barcode scanning within the browser, explicitly processing all image data on the local device without transmitting biometric or personal identity artifacts to remote cloud infrastructure.

The verifier was evaluated against commercial counterfeit driver's licenses procured from sophisticated illicit forgery networks. The counterfeit New York credential exhibited exceptional physical fidelity: microprinting, ultraviolet luminescence, optical variable ink, and tactile engraving were meticulously cloned. Furthermore, the barcode mirrored the exact 484-byte length, structural subfield syntax, and padding parameters of an authentic document. Conventional barcode readers and age-verification apps passed the card without flagging discrepancies.

However, when scanned by the cryptographic verifier, the counterfeit card failed immediately. The counterfeit manufacturers had recognized the presence of the ZNB subfield and populated it with an Ascii85-encoded ECDSA signature generated using their own arbitrary private key. Because the counterfeiters did not possess the genuine, highly guarded private key held inside New York's secure issuance infrastructure, verifying the signature against the recovered authentic public key caused the elliptic curve verification equation to fail catastrophically. This test established that mathematical signature verification provides an infallible defense against arbitrary data tampering and fabricated credentials.

Nevertheless, it is technically imperative to define the precise trust boundaries of barcode cryptography. Understanding what digital signatures prove and what they leave unverified is critical for evaluating real-world identity security:

  • What the Signature Proves: The signature proves with mathematical certainty that an authorized state vehicle licensing authority signed the exact alphanumeric strings—name, birth date, document identifier, and license number—encoded in that barcode symbol.
  • What the Signature Does Not Prove: The signature does not prove that the individual holding the card is the person described by the data, nor does it authenticate the physical photograph affixed to the card surface.
  • Vulnerability to Photographic Transposition: Because driver's license barcodes contain no facial images, biometric vectors, or cryptographic facial hashes, an attacker can copy a valid barcode from a legitimate adult card and print it onto a counterfeit card bearing an underage individual's photo. That fraudulent credential will achieve a mathematically perfect signature verification score.
  • The Indispensability of Physical Cross-Referencing: Barcode cryptographic validation cannot operate as a standalone identity check. It must be paired with physical inspection ensuring that the decoded plaintext fields match the raised tactile text on the front of the plastic card, alongside facial comparison verifying cardholder presence.

These boundaries demonstrate that while barcode cryptography definitively eliminates amateur data manipulation, it cannot independently resolve the broader challenge of credential binding without complementary physical security controls.

9. The Cost of Security Through Obscurity and the Future of Identity Verification

The broader implications of Ryan Fahey's investigation transcend binary reverse engineering, spotlighting systemic shortcomings in identity governance. Canadian Bank Note and its state government clients invested substantial taxpayer resources to engineer and deploy robust, standard-compliant ECDSA P-256 digital signatures. The underlying mathematics were sound, and the signature implementation effectively thwarted sophisticated counterfeits.

Yet by withholding verification specifications and public keys, these agencies neutralized the societal utility of their own security investments. The fundamental principle of asymmetric public-key cryptography dictates that while signing keys must remain strictly confidential, verification keys must be disseminated as widely as possible to establish decentralized trust. By concealing public keys inside an administrative silo, state agencies degraded their own signatures into ornamental data, leaving hospitality staff, banking tellers, and commercial vendors defenseless against fabricated IDs.

This closed approach represents a textbook failure of security through obscurity. While public agencies hid their verification keys, illicit card manufacturers continued exploiting commercial blindness. Had these states followed California's lead by publishing verification keys at standardized DID endpoints and providing open-source verification libraries, commercial mobile devices nationwide could have been validating authentic credentials for years.

While Fahey's mathematical recovery grants relying parties the immediate capability to verify driver's licenses across New York, Virginia, and North Carolina, relying on independent reverse engineering is not a sustainable model for national identity infrastructure. Long-term credential integrity requires state licensing agencies and contracting vendors to abandon proprietary obscurity, standardize on open specifications such as W3C Verifiable Credentials, and publish cryptographic public keys through transparent public channels.

10. What Was Not Verified

  • The author was unable to directly procure physical driver's license specimens from South Carolina and Wisconsin to execute multi-sample public key recovery; while third-party decoded payloads confirmed the presence of signature subfields in both states, their exact public verification keys were not directly recovered.
  • The specific administrative, contractual, or policy factors that led IDEMIA to deploy W3C Verifiable Credential Barcodes and public keys in California while not actively expanding open verification keys across its thirty other contracted US jurisdictions were not publicly documented.
  • No official public statements have been released by Canadian Bank Note or the licensing agencies of New York, Virginia, and North Carolina explaining why their verification keys were historically withheld from the public or whether they intend to transition toward standardized public DID verification endpoints in the future.
