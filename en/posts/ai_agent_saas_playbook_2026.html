The New SaaS Playbook for the AI Agent Era - From Seat Pricing to Outcome Pricing

A critical read of The VC Corner's new playbook, viewed through the lens of founders, SaaS operators, investors, and the Korean market

Jul 14, 2026 Finance & Economy

Intro: Why the SaaS Playbook Needs Rewriting Right Now

Over a few weeks in 2026, the software sector shed a startling amount of market value, and reports noted that leading SaaS companies' price-to-earnings multiples slipped below the S&P 500 average for the first time. An oddly paradoxical scene unfolded alongside it. Salesforce's agent product Agentforce reached a revenue scale that made it one of the fastest-growing products in the company's history - yet Salesforce's own stock fell. Revenue up, market discounting it anyway: that gap captures the confusion the SaaS industry is living through right now.

Into this moment came an analysis piece from The VC Corner, surfaced on GeekNews (hada), titled "The New SaaS Playbook for the AI Agent Era." The piece argues that the premium SaaS has captured for two decades of software scarcity is migrating toward new assets - data, trust, action rights, and whichever tool an agent chooses. In its own framing, SaaS is being demoted "from prize to wrapper."

This article does not simply relay that claim. I first lay out what the piece argues and on what basis ("the piece argues that..."), then cross-check it against other surveys and industry data to separate what holds up from what looks overstated. I close by working through what actually changes for founders, SaaS operators, and investors - and finally, what it means for the Korean SaaS ecosystem. This is current as of July 2026.

1. Cracking Traditional SaaS - From Seat Pricing to Outcome Pricing

1.1 The Built-In Flaw of Seat-Based Pricing

The classic SaaS pricing structure sells a monthly license per user. The problem has long been noted: if a company buys 500 licenses but only 300 people actually use the product, the cost of the other 200 keeps getting billed regardless of the value actually delivered. The piece frames this as a structure that "shifted risk onto the customer." It was convenient for vendors, but it also meant the link between what a customer paid and what a customer got was often loose.

1.2 The Vanishing "Moat of Time" as Production Cost Falls Toward Zero

As AI coding tools spread, the time needed to copy a feature has collapsed. The piece's argument here is blunt: if a competitor can rebuild your core feature over a weekend, that feature was never a moat - it was a product with a marketing budget attached. In the past, simply having built something first, and shipped it first, acted as a defense on its own; that defense is thinning fast under AI. Agentforce's revenue-versus-stock-price paradox reads as an early sign that the market has already started voting that a feature, by itself, is not defensibility.

1.3 Outcome Pricing - From Selling Software to Underwriting Risk

The alternative the piece proposes is charging per outcome: per ticket resolved, per lead qualified, per review completed. Intercom's AI support agent Fin is often cited as a real example, charging per fully resolved inquiry. The catch is that risk flips direction. Under access-based pricing, the customer owned the outcome; under outcome-based pricing, the vendor is effectively guaranteeing it. If the agent fails, the vendor absorbs the cost - which is the piece's central metaphor for why this pushes margin structure closer to an insurance-underwriting business than a software sales business. Multiple industry forecasts likewise project that a substantial share of enterprise SaaS spend will shift toward usage-, agent-, or outcome-based pricing by 2030.

2. When the Agent Becomes the User - The End of PLG and the Return of Top-Down Sales

2.1 Users Who Don't Form Habits

For roughly fifteen years, the standard SaaS growth playbook was product-led growth (PLG): one human tries a tool, likes it, and tells colleagues. That strategy leans on human habit and word of mouth. The piece points out that agents don't form habits. An agent chooses tools on capability, reliability, cost, and reachability at the moment of need - the human path of "I liked it, so I told a coworker" simply doesn't apply.

2.2 When the Product Is the Approval Layer, Not the Dashboard

The party who decides whether an agent even gets access to a tool is usually not an individual practitioner but an enterprise buyer overseeing platform, security, and architecture. Citing Salesforce's "Headless" line of products, the piece argues that the dashboard itself is no longer the product - a system's position within the agent-approval stack is. The result, in the piece's view, is that top-down enterprise sales - sidelined for years by PLG - could regain real weight.

2.3 The GTM Principle Inverted - Agent Callability Instead of Search Rank

The piece argues that going forward, what matters for go-to-market is whether an agent can call your tool at all - a status it equates with ranking on page one of search results in the old world. The rapid spread of open connectivity standards like the Model Context Protocol (MCP) lends this claim some support; multiple industry surveys report a fast-rising share of software organizations running MCP servers in limited or full production. I'd attach one caveat of my own here, though: if the agent-orchestration layer ends up concentrated among a handful of Big Tech platforms, the competition for "being discoverable to agents" could simply reproduce the old SEO gatekeeper problem in a new form, tied to even fewer chokepoints.

3. New Moats - What Actually Can't Be Copied

3.1 Proprietary Data Loops

The first moat the piece names is not data you bought, but a data loop that keeps regenerating from product usage itself and improving the next outcome. A competitor cannot replicate this loop from a standing start, because it isn't built overnight - it only accumulates on top of a long usage history.

3.2 The Right to Act - Being Trusted to Write, Not Just Read

The second is action rights: moving money, signing contracts, altering medical records, deploying code. The piece notes that such rights are earned through years of compliance work and can be revoked in seconds, arguing that "twenty years of enterprise compliance cannot be reproduced by a few days of vibe-coded software." The most valuable position in the agent era, in this framing, is not the position that reads information but the one trusted to act on it.

3.3 Orchestration Distribution and the System of Record

The third is control of distribution within the orchestration layer that coordinates agents and tools; the fourth is the system of record - ERP, ledgers, identity, and compliance platforms - that an agent needs as a ground truth for action. The piece argues that in domains carrying legal or financial consequences, even a fairly accurate model cannot be trusted without an anchor point, so these heavier systems actually become safer in the agent era, not more exposed. Conversely, it flags that the most vulnerable companies are the ones whose entire business was a pretty interface sitting on top of someone else's data.

4. Reading It Critically - Overstated, or Just Early?

The direction itself shows up repeatedly across independent sources. Usage-based pricing has already reached mainstream adoption in SaaS, and outcome-based pricing, while still early, is growing fast according to multiple market studies. Major research firms similarly project that a meaningful share of enterprise software spend will shift toward usage-, agent-, or outcome-based pricing over the coming years.

But there is also real evidence, which the original author acknowledges, that this shift could unfold far more slowly than the framing suggests. First, surveys show that while many IT leaders say they trust agents, only a small share of companies actually let agents run core business processes fully autonomously - a large gap between stated trust and delegated action. Second, recent quarterly results from large SaaS vendors like Salesforce show most big deals are still closed on a seat-adding basis. Third, enterprise procurement cycles typically run months to over a year, a structural bottleneck that keeps contract structures from moving as fast as the technology underneath them.

My own read: framing this as "SaaS is dying" is an overstatement. What's actually happening looks more like a gradual migration from pure subscription pricing toward hybrid pricing that blends in usage and outcome components. The share of SaaS companies adopting hybrid pricing has grown substantially in recent years, and some of these companies report higher revenue growth and net revenue retention than pure-subscription peers. In other words, outcome pricing looks more likely to become a new line added to the SaaS price sheet than a replacement for SaaS itself - though that doesn't undercut the piece's core insight that which assets a company holds will still decide who wins and who loses inside that price sheet.

5. What It Means for Founders, SaaS Operators, and Investors

5.1 Founders - Design Assets, Not Just Features

A roadmap built around adding more features is weaker, from a defensibility standpoint, than one built around deciding which data loop to accumulate, which action rights to earn, and which orchestration layer to plug into. Experimenting early with usage- and outcome-linked pricing components, and narrowing the gap between what customers actually get and what they're billed for, builds durable trust over time.

5.2 SaaS Operators - A Reshuffling of Sales, CS, and Pricing Roles

Growth-marketing talent built for PLG motion may matter less going forward than people who can navigate enterprise procurement, security review, and compliance. At the same time, demand should rise for pricing and data-analytics roles that can design outcome-based pricing and quantify its risk.

5.3 Investors - Redefining the Valuation Yardstick

Judging SaaS companies purely on revenue growth is no longer enough; it now matters whether that revenue came from added seats or from outcome billing, and how much of the company's value sits in hard-to-copy assets - data loops, action rights, system-of-record status. The recent valuation compression across the SaaS sector reads as an early sign the market has started making exactly this distinction.

6. Mapping This Onto the Korean SaaS Ecosystem

Korean media have reported that enterprise application spending well above a hundred billion dollars in scale could be affected by agent-driven pricing disruption. Applying this discussion to Korea's SaaS industry directly, though, requires a few adjustments. First, most Korean SaaS vendors are unlikely to match global Big Tech's agent-orchestration infrastructure on a short timeline; rather than fighting that battle head-on, building a solid track record first in regulated verticals - public sector, finance - where data access itself is a barrier to entry looks like the more realistic play. Second, adoption of agent-connectivity standards like MCP is still at an early stage domestically, so the race to be "discoverable to agents" should be read as just beginning, not already lost. Third, given how accustomed Korean enterprise buyers are to seat-based pricing, the shift toward outcome pricing is likely to be more gradual here than in the West, and to arrive by way of hybrid models rather than a clean break.

Conclusion: What to Prepare For, as of July 2026

The piece's core question ultimately comes down to one thing: does a company's value today come from features that are easy to sell, or from assets that are hard to copy? Seat pricing isn't going to disappear overnight, but it's unmistakably true that outcome-based pricing is being layered on top of it, fast. As agents rise to become the actual users of software, the growth formula that leaned almost entirely on PLG is likely to tilt back toward enterprise motion.

If you're a founder, it's worth adding one question to this quarter's roadmap review: how many hard-to-copy assets did we actually add? If you're a SaaS operator, it's worth checking whether your role is shifting from adding seats toward guaranteeing outcomes and building trust. If you're an investor, it's worth looking one layer beneath the revenue growth curve at what assets actually sit underneath it. And all of this should be held alongside a caveat the original author states plainly: if agent trust plateaus below a critical threshold, or enterprise procurement drags, this could all arrive years later than the framing implies.
