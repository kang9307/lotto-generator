The Word Intelligence Blurs Accountability

What is left when you read the Vatican AI documents as accountability design rather than theology

2026-07-29 Tech & IT

Introduction: what this article does and does not do

Let me draw the boundary first. This article neither defends nor attacks any religion. It opens a handful of documents published by one institution and extracts only the parts a developer or a product manager can put to work. The theological premises of those documents carry no authority over a reader who does not share them, and nothing here argues that they should. The reason they are still worth reading is narrow and specific: these texts take direct aim at the one thing our industry is most consistently vague about.

Here is that thing. We call our systems intelligent. The moment we do, the subject of the sentence quietly changes. Our team chose to reject applications below this threshold becomes the model rejected it. It happens in meeting notes, then in design documents, and eventually in the postmortem written after something goes wrong. And once the line the AI made that call starts to function as an explanation, that organisation has already lost the ability to establish cause.

This is an accountability design problem long before it is a theological one. Audits, disputes and regulatory reviews all arrive with the same three questions. Who set it? Who approved it? Who could have overturned it? If no name appears in the answers to those three, the system is not in an operable state. What follows below starts from the verified facts, then translates the perspective into code review, model cards, incident reports and automated decision notices. It does not explain statutory text. Domestic compliance work is covered separately in the piece on preparing for Korea's AI Framework Act; here we only look at how the approaches differ.

1. Verified facts first - which document, when, at what rank

The most common error in writing about this area is mixing up document ranks. The Vatican issued an encyclical and a department issued a note carry different weight, and that difference goes straight to the credibility of whoever is doing the citing. Within what could be verified, the picture is as follows.

Document or sourceDateIssued byRank and character
Rome Call for AI EthicsSigned 2020-02-28Convened by the Pontifical Academy for Life, co-signed by companies and bodiesNot a magisterial text but a joint declaration and pledge
Message for the 57th World Day of Peace2024-01-01The reigning pope at the time (Francis)Message, eight numbered sections
Address to the G7 session on AI2024-06-14, Borgo EgnaziaThe reigning pope at the time (Francis)Address
Antiqua et Nova2025-01-28Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith + Dicastery for Culture and Education, jointlyNote, 117 paragraphs. Not an encyclical
Magnifica HumanitasSigned 2026-05-15 / presented 2026-05-25Leo XIVEncyclical, five chapters, 245 numbered sections

Two things in that table matter for practitioners. First, Antiqua et Nova is a Note, not an encyclical. Its subtitle is the Note on the Relationship Between Artificial Intelligence and Human Intelligence, and it carries the signatures of the prefects and secretaries of two departments. According to the text at the close of the document, the reigning pope approved the Note at an audience on 14 January 2025 and ordered its publication. Cite it as an encyclical written by a pope and the citation is wrong on its face.

Second, the rank went up a step in 2026. Magnifica Humanitas, presented on 25 May 2026, is an encyclical subtitled On Safeguarding the Human Person in the Time of Artificial Intelligence, and it was signed on 15 May. Reporting indicates the signing date was chosen to fall on the 135th anniversary of Rerum Novarum from 1891. Aligning an AI document with the date of a text written while industrialisation was rearranging labour is itself a signal: the document frames its own subject as a question about work and power rather than a commentary on technology.

  • Always cite rank and issuer together — a note, a message, an address and an encyclical are different objects. This matters most when the citation is being attached to an internal document as supporting evidence.
  • Always cite the paragraph number — both documents are numbered. A citation without one cannot be verified, and an unverifiable citation should be cut in review.
  • Signing date and release date can differ — the encyclical appeared ten days after it was signed. When you write down when something came out, say which of the two you mean.
  • The Rome Call is not a magisterial text — it is a signed pledge whose list of signatories keeps growing. It is handled separately below.

2. What the label intelligence actually does

Much of the early portion of Antiqua et Nova is spent on terminology. Paragraph 35 records that the very use of the word intelligence in connection with AI can prove misleading. Paragraph 12 is more concrete: advanced features give AI sophisticated abilities to perform tasks, but not the ability to think. The document treats intelligence in the AI context as a functional and therefore narrow usage.

Set the theology aside for a moment. That distinction translates cleanly into a language convention. Which verb you use in software documentation is not a matter of style. The verb summons a subject, and the subject summons a responsible party. The slippage most often seen in practice looks like this.

Sentence people actually writeWhat the sentence hidesCorrected sentence
The model rejected this applicationWho set the threshold, and the policy that permitted automatic rejectionA score of 0.31 fell below the internal cutoff of 0.35, so the auto-reject policy applied
The AI judged this user to be abusiveThe label definition, the labelling standard in the training data, the decision to skip reviewThe classifier produced an abuse label at 0.88 and the block rule fired with no manual review
The agent decided on its own and executedThe configuration that granted the permission, the deployment that allowed execution without approvalThe agent was shipped to execute without an approval step, inside the permissions it had been granted
The incident was caused by an AI misjudgmentThe threshold change history, the approver, the missing rollbackA threshold change shipped without review and no rollback path existed
The model summarised the document, so the basis lives in the modelThe source verification step, the person who approved publicationThe summary was published without verification and the publishing approver field was blank

Look at the right column and the pattern is obvious. Every corrected sentence brings a person or a procedure back into the frame. And each of those sentences immediately invites the next question: when was that standard set, and by whom, and where is the change history? The left column invites nothing, because those sentences are already closed.

None of this observation is new. Human factors research has studied the same phenomenon for decades under the name automation bias, and aviation and medicine already have investigative conventions built around it. The contribution of these documents is less a discovery than a different label on the same shelf. They treat the issue as a category problem rather than a performance problem: not an accuracy defect that better engineering removes, but what happens when a predicate that only attaches to persons gets attached to a tool. That angle earns its keep in practice, because it lets you separate out the class of risk that does not shrink as the model improves.

3. What the documents actually say about responsibility

Now let me narrow the citations to sentences that were actually verified, kept short, with paragraph numbers attached. The English editions were used as the reference throughout.

Paragraph 39 of Antiqua et Nova states that between a machine and a human being, only the latter is truly a moral agent, and follows it with a definition of that agent as a subject who exercises freedom in decisions and accepts their consequences. In the passages on attribution beginning at paragraph 44, the text holds that ultimate responsibility for decisions made using AI rests with the human decision-maker, together with a requirement that accountability be identifiable at each stage of the decision-making process. The same passage attaches conditions to the algorithms themselves: where AI assists human decisions, they should be trustworthy, secure, robust enough to handle inconsistencies, and transparent.

Weapons get a dedicated paragraph. Paragraph 100 treats lethal autonomous weapon systems as a grave ethical concern, on the ground that such systems lack the uniquely human capacity for moral judgment and ethical decision-making. The same paragraph quotes the line that no machine should ever choose to take the life of a human being. That sentence originates in the address delivered to the G7 session on 14 June 2024, and the Note is quoting it. Section 6 of the message dated 1 January 2024 makes a parallel point: autonomous weapon systems can never be morally responsible subjects, with the observation that the human capacity for moral judgment is more than a complex collection of algorithms.

The passages on labour also land close to practice. Paragraph 67 warns that current approaches can paradoxically deskill workers and place them under automated surveillance, and paragraph 68 notes the risk that benefits concentrate among a few if AI is used to replace workers rather than complement them.

From the 2026 encyclical, the verified sentence is paragraph 9. It states that technology is never neutral, on the ground that it takes on the characteristics of those who devise, finance, regulate and use it. The address delivered on the day of presentation, 25 May 2026, contains the statement that no one can be reduced to productivity, to cognitive performance, or to mere data. This article did not compare the full body of the encyclical paragraph by paragraph, so no citation is extended beyond those two sentences for material after chapter three.

  • Read moral agent as the party to whom responsibility attaches and the passage becomes an audit requirement directly: every decision needs a person identifier on it.
  • The phrase at each stage is the operative one — recording a single final approver is not enough. Data selection, label definition, threshold setting, deployment and exception handling each need a name.
  • The four algorithmic conditions are a familiar list — trustworthy, secure, robust, transparent. They overlap almost entirely with the characteristic lists in secular frameworks, which the next section puts side by side.
  • The deskilling point converts into an adoption metric — track how the share of judgments made directly by people changed before and after rollout, and the concern becomes measurable rather than rhetorical.

4. The axis where approaches diverge - risk based or dignity based

This is the core contrast of the article. Line the three kinds of document up and the difference in where the judgment starts becomes visible.

The EU AI Act is formally Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 and entered into force on 1 August 2024. Its approach is explicitly risk based. It sorts uses into unacceptable risk, high risk, transparency risk, and minimal or no risk, and attaches obligations according to that tier. What triggers an obligation is not who the provider is but what the system is used for. Application is staged: prohibited practices and AI literacy obligations from 2 February 2025, general purpose AI obligations from 2 August 2025, and full applicability from 2 August 2026, with high-risk areas listed in the annex from 2 December 2027 and high-risk systems embedded in products from 2 August 2028.

The NIST AI RMF 1.0 has a different character again. It was published on 26 January 2023 and is intended for voluntary use. Its core is the four functions GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE and MANAGE, and it lists seven characteristics of trustworthy AI: valid and reliable, safe, secure and resilient, accountable and transparent, explainable and interpretable, privacy-enhanced, and fair with harmful bias managed. The Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) followed on 26 July 2024, a revision of 1.0 is under way, and a concept note for a critical infrastructure profile was released on 7 April 2026.

AxisEU AI ActNIST AI RMFVatican textsCorporate AI principles
Starting pointRisk tier by useOrganisational risk capabilityHuman dignity and moral agencyBrand and product risk
Binding forceLegal obligationVoluntaryNorms internal to that communityInternal policy
Unit of judgmentSystem and useOrganisation and lifecycleThe act and its actorProduct and feature
Definition of failureRequirements not metUnmanaged riskErasure of the responsible partyIncident and reputational damage
Main artefactsConformity assessment, technical documentationProfiles, measurement metricsNormative prose, no templatesGuidelines and reviews
When the tier is lowAlmost no obligationsLower management intensitySame demand regardless of tierReview can be skipped

That last row is the practical difference. Under a risk based approach, a low tier means fewer obligations. That is by design and it is sensible as a way to allocate scarce resources. Yet in the field, incidents happen less often in the systems classified as high risk than in the internal tools nobody thought were high risk at all: the ticket auto-classifier, the resume pre-sorting script, the batch job that closes customer enquiries automatically. They do not appear in the tier table, so no documentation exists, and because no documentation exists nobody knows who changed the threshold or when.

Dignity based prose responds to exactly that gap, because it does not grade anything. The cost is real, though. You cannot use it the way you use a regulation. There is no template for what to submit or what test to pass. So the right reading treats the three not as substitutes but as different layers. Regulation sets the floor of what must be done, the management framework supplies the procedure that makes it repeatable, and the dignity based text fills in why a person should still be in the loop on a system whose tier came out low.

5. Where corporate AI principles overlap, and where they part

The Rome Call for AI Ethics, signed on 28 February 2020, is useful material for this comparison. It was convened by the Pontifical Academy for Life, and the original signatories included Microsoft and IBM along with the Food and Agriculture Organization and the Italian ministry responsible for innovation. Signatures continued afterwards; official material shows Cisco in 2024, Qualcomm on 13 June 2025 and Salesforce in October 2025, and an event held in Hiroshima from 9 to 10 July 2024 at which representatives of multiple world religions signed.

The declaration sets out six principles: transparency, inclusion, accountability, impartiality, reliability, and security and privacy. Put that list next to the NIST characteristics and the overlap is stark.

Rome Call principleCorresponding NIST characteristicOverlapWhere they part in practice
TransparencyAccountable and transparent / explainableNearly identicalTransparent to whom, the auditor or the end user
InclusionFair with harmful bias managedPartial overlapInclusion also covers access, bias management focuses on output distribution
AccountabilityAccountable and transparentSame wordInstitutional or individual accountability — this is where they split
ImpartialityFairnessNearly identicalNeither side settles which definition of fairness governs
ReliabilityValid and reliable / safeNearly identicalNIST is far more concrete about how to measure it
Security and privacySecure and resilient / privacy-enhancedNearly identicalAlmost no difference

At the level of vocabulary the two lists overlap to a surprising degree. That is not strange. Principle lists largely converged in the same period and out of the same set of conversations. Hence the common reaction, that this is no different from what our own company already publishes. The difference is not in the list but in the order in which the list is applied.

  • In corporate principles, accountability usually attaches to the organisation — we are committed to responsible AI. The subject of the sentence is a legal entity and no individual is named.
  • In the Vatican prose, responsibility attaches to a person — the premise is a subject who decides and bears the consequences, which does not dissolve into a corporate unit.
  • That difference changes the artefact — organisational accountability ends with a one-page policy. Individual accountability requires an identifier on every decision. The second is far more expensive and far more useful.
  • Overlapping principles do not need rewriting — there is no reason to tear up an existing internal principles document. What is missing is not principles but the bridge that lowers principles into artefacts.
  • Do not cite a pledge with the weight of a regulation — the Rome Call is a signed commitment, not a requirements list. It should not be used as a compliance basis.

6. Translation one - putting the subject back in the sentence

The cheapest place to start is the language convention. It costs almost nothing and the payoff shows up months later during an investigation. The rule is simple: ban judgment verbs whose subject is the system from written artefacts.

  • Keep the banned list short — judged, decided, approved, rejected, determined. Five is enough. Longer lists get ignored.
  • Supply the permitted phrasing alongside — produced, scored, proposed a candidate, ranked, assigned a label. Banning without offering an alternative fails every time.
  • Scope it to documents and commit messages — policing chat threads only breeds resentment. Apply it where the record persists.
  • Apply it to UI copy too — a screen that says the AI has reviewed your case teaches the user the same misconception. Automated review result is enough.
  • State the exceptions — this does not mean rewriting established technical terms or product names. A rule has to look reasonable to be followed.
  • Raise it as a one-line review comment — ask for the subject to be changed to a person or a policy and move on. It is not a topic for debate.

The real effect of the rule is not stylistic. Changing the subject to a person exposes the blanks. The fact that nobody knows who set this threshold gets discovered mid-sentence, while the document is being written, and that discovery becomes the work item in the next section.

7. Translation two - the decision record as an artefact

Any path where an automated determination affects a person needs a decision record. It is not the same as a log. A log captures what the system did; a decision record captures who could have overturned it. A minimal schema looks roughly like this.

{
  "decision_id": "dec_2026_07_29_0001",
  "subject_ref": "user_hash_9f2c",
  "outcome": "auto_reject",
  "model": { "name": "risk-scorer", "version": "3.4.1", "score": 0.31 },
  "policy": { "id": "risk-policy", "version": "2026-06-11", "threshold": 0.35 },
  "threshold_owner": "team_risk_ops",
  "threshold_changed_at": "2026-06-11",
  "threshold_approved_by": "approver_id_0142",
  "human_review": { "required": false, "reason": "score_below_review_band" },
  "override": { "available": true, "channel": "manual_review_queue", "sla_hours": 24 },
  "final_actor": null,
  "notice_shown": true
}

Each field maps onto a citation from the previous section. threshold_approved_by answers the requirement that accountability be identifiable at each stage, override answers the requirement that a person be able to reverse the outcome, and final_actor answers the statement that ultimate responsibility rests with the human decision-maker. The percentage of decisions that remain with final_actor set to null is that system's accountability gap rate.

  • Keep personal data out of it — record the subject as a hash or an internal reference. Decision records are retained for a long time, so raw values become a liability.
  • Separate model version from policy version — by far the most common real-world case is an outcome flipping because only the threshold moved under the same model. Collapse them into one field and the cause cannot be isolated.
  • Record approvers as individuals, not teams — a team name alone means nobody answers afterwards. Use identifiers that survive a reorganisation.
  • Test the override path on a schedule — it is common for a path to exist only on paper while being blocked in reality. Actually press the button once a quarter.
  • Match retention to the dispute window — inherit the ordinary log retention policy and the data will be gone precisely when it is needed.
  • Do not sample — decisions that impose a detriment on a person should be recorded exhaustively. Sampling is for statistics, not for tracing responsibility.

8. Translation three - stopping responsibility from evaporating in incident reports

The incident report is where this perspective bites hardest. The problem sentence always takes the same shape: cause: model misjudgment. Once those two words appear, the investigation stops on that line. Blocking it at the template level is more reliable than trying to educate people.

incident:
  id: INC-2026-0714
  impact: "412 automatic rejections, 37 of which were appealed"
  # forbidden: a cause field containing only a model name
  cause_chain:
    - what_changed: "threshold 0.30 to 0.35"
      when: "2026-06-11"
      who_changed: "eng_0091"
      who_approved: "approver_0142"
      review_record: "not performed"      # the real cause is here
    - what_changed: "added batch job auto-closing the review queue"
      when: "2026-06-18"
      who_changed: "eng_0113"
      who_approved: null                  # approval gap
  detection:
    how: "alert on a spike in appeals"
    lag_days: 26
  human_in_loop:
    designed: true
    effective: false
    why: "the queue auto-closed, so no person ever had the chance to look"
  actions:
    - "require two-person approval for threshold changes"
    - "remove the auto-close batch, escalate to a person on expiry"
    - "make final_actor mandatory in the decision record"

The point of this template is that who_approved is allowed to be null but that null is itself recorded as a finding. The structure surfaces gaps instead of hiding them. The second point is splitting human_in_loop into designed and effective. Saying a person was designed into the loop and saying a person actually intervened are completely different claims, and most incidents live in the space between them. For failure tracing in agent-style systems, reading this alongside enforcing permissions below the application layer separates the two layers cleanly.

9. Translation four - the automated decision notice

What to tell the user overlaps with regulated territory. Rather than explaining statutory text, this section sets out only the criteria to apply when writing notice copy. Bad notices share one trait: they put the system on stage as the actor.

  • Do not open with the AI has decided — that sentence tells the user there is nobody to appeal to. In a healthy system there is.
  • State the scope of what was automated — end to end, or only pre-sorting with a human making the final call. This is what users most want to know.
  • Put the review path on the same screen — send them to another page and it effectively does not exist. Include the expected turnaround.
  • List the main information categories used — this does not mean explaining the model architecture. The names of the inputs considered are enough.
  • Think twice before exposing raw probability scores — publish a number and gaming behaviour aimed at that number follows. Bands or reason codes are often better.
  • Version-control the notice copy — you must be able to reconstruct which wording was shown at the time of a dispute.
  • Do not let translations rot — in multilingual services it is common for the source copy to be fixed while the translations are not.

11. What to ask in review - a checklist

Here it is in a form you can drop into a design or code review. Not everything has to pass; the items you cannot answer are the next work item.

  1. Does this change create an automated decision that can impose a detriment on a person? If so, is a decision record produced?
  2. Is the identifier of whoever set the threshold or rule traceable from code or configuration?
  3. Can you find the date that threshold last changed, and its approver, within thirty seconds?
  4. Is there a path for a person to overturn it? Is there a record of that path actually being used in the last three months?
  5. Did you verify separately whether human intervention exists by design and whether it works in practice?
  6. Is there any place where a queue auto-closes on timeout? If so, that is where intervention is neutralised.
  7. Do documents and commit messages use the system as the subject of judging or deciding?
  8. Does the UI copy stage the system as the actor?
  9. Does the model card have a decision authority field, and is it non-empty?
  10. Are model version and policy version recorded separately?
  11. Was this feature classified as low risk and thereby exempted from documentation requirements? Is the basis for that exemption written down?
  12. Is the retention period for the data needed to investigate an incident at least as long as the dispute window?
  13. Does the user notice carry both the scope of automation and the review path?
  14. Do you measure how the share of judgments made directly by people changed after adoption?
  15. Does this system hold execution authority that directly affects a person's life or body? If so, can that authority be reduced?

12. Counterarguments and limits - where this perspective becomes excess

For balance, here is the other side. Expand this perspective without limits and cost goes up while safety does not.

  • You cannot put a person behind every automation — keeping exhaustive decision records for a spam filter handling thousands of events per second is unrealistic. The criterion is whether a detriment attaches to an identifiable person, not whether the step is automated.
  • Ceremonial approval is worse than none — add approval buttons and you reach a state where nobody reads before clicking. If the approver lacks real power and real time to overturn, that approval becomes a responsibility-laundering device.
  • Fixing vocabulary is not a cure-all — rewrite the sentences and nothing changes if the permission structure stays the same. The language convention is a starting point, not a solution.
  • Dignity based prose cannot set priorities — in an organisation with finite resources, what to do first will still be decided on a risk basis. Setting the two approaches against each other is a practical loss.
  • Most organisations do not share the premises — there are many settings where citing a religious document as internal supporting evidence is simply inappropriate. When that is the case, restate the same demand in the vocabulary of the secular frameworks. The tables above are that translation key.
  • Do not overstate the convergence — overlapping principle lists do not make the documents citable as authority for one another. They are different kinds of text.

13. What was left out because it could not be verified

Stating the boundary of the research. The items below were not covered, or were narrowed, because verification was incomplete.

  • The body of the 2026 encyclical after chapter three — only the chapter titles and some paragraphs were verified. No paragraph-level citation was made for the later material on work, freedom and power.
  • The exact paragraph numbers on autonomous weapons in the encyclical — the topic is covered, but the paragraph numbers could not be pinned down, so no citation was included.
  • The complete list of Rome Call signatories and their signing dates — only the original signatories and a few later ones were verified. No claim resting on the full list was made.
  • Any further documents after May 2026 — nothing newer than the encyclical was found. Rather than asserting that none exists, this simply records that none was found as of the verification date.
  • Differences between the language editions — citations follow the English editions. Fine differences against the Latin or Italian originals were not compared.
  • The final content of the revised NIST AI RMF — only the fact that a revision is under way and the April 2026 concept note release were verified.

Conclusion: fix the word and the structure follows

Reduced to one sentence, the claim repeated throughout is this. The moment you call a system intelligent, the subject of decision-making slides from the person to the tool, and that slide then hardens into your documents, your logs and your approval structure. Reversing it after an incident is close to impossible, because the records have already accumulated in that shape.

What the Vatican texts contribute is a different angle, in that they treat the issue as a matter of category rather than accuracy. That separates the risk that disappears as models improve from the risk that does not disappear no matter how good the model gets. Whether to accept the premises behind that framing is each reader's own business, and this article takes no position on it. The practical demands that fall out of it, however, work independently of the premises. Does a person's name remain attached to every decision? An organisation that can answer that question is in a far better position in an audit, in a dispute, and above all in an investigation.

So there are three things worth doing this week. First, list the automated decision paths that can impose a detriment on a person. Second, pick one of them and try to find who last changed the threshold and when. Third, if you cannot find it within thirty seconds, that is your first backlog item. That much holds regardless of belief, and it is the whole of what this article set out to cover.
