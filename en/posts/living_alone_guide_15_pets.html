Living Alone Guide Part 15: Pet Considerations

Think Before You Get One

Jan 25, 2026Health & Wellness
⚠️ Health information notice — This article is for general information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Consult a clinician if you have symptoms or need a decision.

Lonely? Want a Pet?

When you live alone, sometimes you wish there was someone at home waiting for you. Seeing cute dogs or cats makes you think "Maybe I should get one?"

But wait! There are realistic things to consider before getting a pet.

This article is not here to talk you out of it. It is also not here to push you into bringing an animal home today. The goal is to help you price out the problems that solo households actually run into — the hours the apartment sits empty, the money that leaves every month, friction with neighbours, and what happens when the animal gets sick — so that you start from conditions you can actually sustain.

Order matters. If your heart commits first and you try to bend your circumstances around it afterwards, something usually breaks. Check the conditions first, then choose an animal that fits them, and life gets easier for both of you. Let's walk through it step by step.

1. Think Hard Before Adopting

Adoption is less like "getting a pet" and more like "taking responsibility for the rest of an animal's life." So the first thing to check is how many years that contract runs.

1.1 What a Full Lifespan Commitment Means

Lifespans vary enormously by species. The ranges commonly cited in veterinary and pet-care references are below. Individual animals, breeds, and quality of care shift these numbers, so treat them as a baseline rather than a promise.

  • Dogs: roughly 10-15 years. Smaller breeds generally outlive larger ones, and breed-to-breed variation is wide.
  • Cats: indoor cats average around 13-17 years, and some are reported to live past 20.
  • Hamsters: 2-4 years, which is short. That means less commitment, but it also means goodbye comes quickly.
  • Rabbits: 5-8 years, and with good care some live past 10 — so "small animal" does not mean "small responsibility."

The one most people get wrong is fish. Goldfish have a reputation for dying within months, but with an adequately sized tank and proper filtration and water management, living past 10 years is not unusual, and some reach far beyond that. They are not naturally short-lived so much as commonly kept in conditions that kill them early. "Easy to care for" and "a short commitment" are completely different claims.

The reason to check lifespan first is simple arithmetic. If you are 25 and bring home a dog or a cat, you are designing a life together that runs to age 35 or 40. In that window you will move house, change jobs, possibly marry, maybe study abroad, maybe care for a family member — several times over. The question is whether your plan survives those changes.

1.2 Your Next Five Years of Variables

For some people the right answer is to wait; for others now is genuinely fine. The deciding question is "how much is my life about to move around?" Put these five items on a calendar and look at them honestly.

  • Moving or renewing a lease: pet-friendly places are a much smaller pool, and deposits or rent are sometimes higher.
  • New job or long business trips: a change of workplace or travel schedule rewrites your daily hours away from home entirely.
  • Marriage or moving in with someone: if your partner has allergies or does not want animals, that becomes a conflict immediately.
  • Studying or relocating abroad: moving an animal across borders involves quarantine procedures, cost, and lead time, so it cannot be decided on impulse.
  • Income changes: when income drops, vet bills are the first thing people cut — and they are exactly the thing you must not cut.

If two or more of these are likely within the next two years, you are better off building experience through fostering or volunteer care and postponing adoption until things settle. Undoing an adoption hurts the human, but it hits the animal far harder.

2. Check Your Living Situation

The first thing to check is not your feelings but your paperwork. If the housing does not permit it, every other plan is moot.

Lease Agreement

  • Pet policy: Many apartments prohibit pets
  • Violations: Could mean eviction or losing deposit
  • Landlord approval: Get permission first
  • Special clauses: If the lease contains a "no animals" clause, verbal permission is risky. Get it in writing — a text message counts.
  • Building rules: Apartments and officetels are governed by building management rules separately from your lease, so check with the management office too.

In Korea, multi-unit housing law requires residents to obtain the management body's consent when keeping animals in a way that affects communal living, with the specifics laid out in each complex's management rules. In other words, "the landlord said yes" may not be the end of the story. A five-minute phone call to the management office before you sign can save you the cost of an emergency move later.

Space Issues

  • Studios are small - large pets struggle
  • Noise issues - barking dogs cause complaints
  • Odor management - smells spread quickly in small spaces
  • Flooring - slippery laminate and hard flooring strain a dog's joints, so non-slip mats are needed.
  • Layout - keep the litter or potty area, food bowls, and sleeping spot separated from each other.

A small home is not automatically a disqualifier. Cats care more about vertical space than floor area, so a cat tree or a few shelves that let them climb raises quality of life considerably even in a studio. Dogs, by contrast, need time outside rather than height, which is why a spacious home without walks still produces problems. Judge space by "how does this animal spend its day," not by square metres.

If your lease says "no pets," getting caught with one can actually get you evicted.

3. Time Reality

You can earn money later; you cannot earn time later. In solo households, time is the first thing that collapses.

For Working People

  • Away from home 8-10 hours daily
  • Even longer with overtime or travel
  • Dogs can develop separation anxiety
  • Whether remote or flexible work is available changes the difficulty completely.
  • Count weekend plans and trips too — calculate "total hours away per month" to see the real picture.

The RSPCA, the UK animal welfare organisation, advises not leaving a dog alone for more than four hours, and much less than that for puppies. That is guidance rather than a hard rule, and individual dogs and training levels differ — but it makes clear that a routine of ten hours alone per day is not a comfortable arrangement for a dog.

Time Required

  • Dogs: Daily walks 1-2 times, playtime, training
  • Cats: More independent but still need attention
  • Cleaning: Daily fur cleanup and litter management
  • Small animals: no walks needed, but cage cleaning and bedding changes are a regular obligation.
  • Fish: little daily handling, but scheduled water changes and water-quality checks are non-negotiable.

The common misconception here is "cats are low maintenance." Cats simply do not need walks. The litter box still needs scooping once or twice a day, and they still need hunting-style interactive play. It is less "no time required" and more "walk time is replaced by cleaning and play time."

4.1 Ten Minutes Before Work, Thirty Minutes After

If you cannot shorten the hours away, design for density in the hours you are together. Predictability settles animals more than sheer quantity of time does.

  • A short activity before leaving: five to ten minutes of play or a brief walk burns a little energy and makes the day much calmer.
  • Keep greetings low-key: handling departures and arrivals calmly stops "being alone" from becoming a dramatic event.
  • Feed at the same time: consistent meal times create a daily rhythm and make toilet timing predictable too.
  • Protect focused time after work: twenty to thirty minutes with your phone down beats three hours of being in the same room distracted.
  • Make a window spot: for cats especially, one place to watch the outside world cuts a huge amount of daily boredom.

Adding a home camera lets you check what is happening and reduces your own anxiety. But a camera is an observation tool, not a substitute for care. If you find yourself repeatedly watching a problem on screen without being able to do anything about it, that is a signal to reduce the hours away, not to buy a better camera.

4.2 Signs of Separation Anxiety and What to Do

An animal struggling with alone time will signal it. The problem is that the signals mostly appear when no human is present, so they are easy to miss.

  • Sustained barking or howling: ask a neighbour or check a camera recording. This is also the single most common source of complaints.
  • Destructive behaviour: chewing concentrated around the front door or windows is strongly associated with being left alone.
  • Toilet accidents: an animal that is normally reliable but only has accidents when alone may be signalling stress.
  • Over-grooming or appetite changes: common in cats, and it can progress to visible skin problems.

The core of the fix is starting with very short absences and extending them gradually so the animal habituates. It takes weeks of consistent sessions, and punishment makes anxiety worse rather than better. Note also that every sign above can be caused by illness or pain, so before concluding it is behavioural you must have a veterinarian rule out physical causes first. If a behaviour professional is needed, ask your vet for a referral. Never give sedatives or supplements on your own initiative.

5. Cost Reality

Costs make sense when you look at them per year and per decade, not per month. Let's break them down by category first.

Initial Costs

  • Adoption/purchase fee: Free to thousands
  • Supplies (crate, litter box, bowls): $100-300
  • First vet visit/vaccines: $100-200
  • Spay or neuter surgery: timing and suitability depend on health, so decide after consulting your veterinarian.
  • Carrier and safety gear: essential for vet visits, moving house, and emergency evacuation.

Monthly Costs

  • Food: $30-100
  • Treats: $10-30
  • Litter/pads: $10-30
  • Toys, supplies: $10-20
  • Budget at least $50-200/month

Unexpected Costs

  • Vet bills: $50+ for visits, surgeries can be thousands
  • Pet insurance: $20-50/month
  • Pet sitting/boarding: $30-50/day when traveling
  • Housing costs: pet-friendly rentals are a limited pool, so your housing spend can rise.
  • Restoration costs: damaged wallpaper or flooring comes back as a deduction from your deposit when you move out.

Pets aren't something you buy when you want and discard when inconvenient. It's a 10-20 year commitment.

6. Costs That Run Bigger Than Expected

According to the "2025 Korea Pet Report" published by the KB Financial Group Research Institute, the average monthly cost of keeping a pet was 194,000 won, up 40,000 won from the 2023 survey. The same report put average adoption costs at 380,000 won and funeral costs at 463,000 won, and notably found treatment costs of 1,027,000 won over the previous two years — roughly double the prior survey. In short, vet bills are growing faster than food bills.

6.1 When Vet Bills Spike

Routine costs are predictable, but the situations below produce a large bill all at once. Knowing about them in advance at least keeps you from panicking.

  • Entering old age: check-up panels expand and chronic disease management begins. This is the most predictable large expense of all.
  • Dental work: often requires anaesthesia, which raises the cost, and delaying it reliably makes it more expensive.
  • Swallowed objects: especially common in small apartments. Thread, plastic wrap, or a chunk of an earbud becomes an emergency instantly.
  • Skin and ear conditions: these rarely resolve in one visit; they recur and accumulate.
  • After-hours and holiday visits: many clinics price the same procedure differently outside normal hours.

For context, Korean veterinary law requires clinics to inform owners of estimated costs in advance of major procedures such as surgery, and a scheme requires clinics to post the prices of key items — initial and follow-up consultation fees, hospitalisation, dog and cat vaccinations — where clients can see them. Checking those posted prices makes comparing clinics much easier.

6.2 Understanding Pet Insurance Before You Buy

No specific insurer can be recommended here, but the structure you need to examine is the same across products. Every item below appears in the policy documents, so read them side by side.

  • Renewal structure: since the May 2025 regulatory changes in Korea, new products are written as one-year renewable contracts by default. Budget on the assumption that premiums rise with age.
  • Deductible and reimbursement rate: these two numbers decide what you actually get back. A cheap premium usually means unfavourable terms here.
  • Exclusions: pre-existing conditions, congenital and hereditary disorders, and preventive procedures are frequently excluded.
  • Age limits on enrolment: past a certain age, new policies become hard to obtain. That is why "I will buy it when they get sick" does not work.
  • Saving instead of insuring: compare against putting the same amount into a dedicated account each month, and pick whichever fits your spending habits.

Insurance is not a way to spend less overall; it is a way to avoid being wiped out when a large bill lands at once. If the monthly premium feels heavy, at minimum start setting aside an emergency treatment fund. If you want to compare products in detail, read our complete guide to choosing pet insurance alongside this article.

Recommended

  • Cats: Independent, no walks needed
  • Fish: Quiet, low-cost, easy care
  • Hamsters: Small space OK, shorter lifespan
  • Adult cats and dogs: their temperament is already visible, which makes it far easier to judge the fit with your life.
  • Quieter shelter animals: ask staff to help you find a lower-energy individual and a studio suits them much better.

"Recommended" is not an absolute ranking — it is a ranking for people who are away from home a lot. If you work from home and can walk every day, a dog may actually suit you better; if you travel constantly, even a cat is hard. Put your own schedule on the table before you read the chart.

Not Recommended

  • Large dogs: Need lots of space and time
  • Barky breeds: Neighbor complaints
  • Exotic animals: Require specialized knowledge
  • Puppies and kittens: toilet training and socialisation demand a lot of hands-on time, which is punishing if you are often out.
  • Breeds designated as dangerous: these carry extra obligations such as keeping permits, training, and insurance, so check with your local authority first.

Exotic animals such as reptiles and birds usually require temperature and humidity control equipment, and there may be no clinic in your area able to treat them at all. Before bringing one home, confirm that a clinic within reachable distance can actually see that species. This is not a matter of taste; it decides whether the animal survives being ill.

8. Living Considerately in Shared Housing

Pet problems in apartments and shared buildings escalate along three predictable paths: noise, shared spaces, and toilet habits. Managing relationships with neighbours also connects to Part 13: Neighbor Relations.

8.1 Noise

  • Identify the cause of barking: demand barking, alert barking, and separation anxiety all call for completely different responses.
  • Reduce door and window triggers: if hallway footsteps or movement outside sets it off, blocking the sightline alone helps enormously.
  • Manage night-time activity: late-night zoomies and exercise wheels carry to neighbours far more than you would expect.
  • Introduce yourself early: tell the neighbours above, below, and beside you and leave a contact number, so a complaint starts as a conversation instead of a report.

Note that animal noise is different in character from the human-activity noise covered by inter-floor noise standards, so in practice these disputes are resolved through building rules, neighbour-to-neighbour agreement, and local authority complaints. Arguing over which statute applies is almost always slower than sorting it out through conversation early.

8.2 Elevators, Corridors, and Shared Areas

  • Restraint obligation: inside the shared areas of multi-unit buildings, Korean rules require you to carry the dog or hold the leash near the collar or the harness handle so movement is restricted.
  • Carry them in elevators: it is a small space with no escape route, which puts both people and animals on edge. Carrying is safer for everyone.
  • Where to wait: keep the lead short and stand to one side so nobody bolts when the doors open.
  • Yield the ride: if a neighbour is afraid of animals, waiting for the next elevator is the most reliable solution there is.

This is a matter of courtesy and a legal obligation at the same time. Failing to take required safety measures can result in a fine, so "my dog doesn't bite" is not an argument that covers this ground.

8.3 Waste and Odour

  • Clean up immediately: waste must be picked up on the spot when you are out, and urine is included when it lands in indoor shared areas or on seating.
  • Carry spare bags: keep at least two on the lead handle or in your bag. "I didn't have a bag" is the most common failure.
  • Ventilation routine: odour accumulates fastest in small homes, and two short airings a day beat any air freshener.
  • Litter box placement: avoid spots near the entrance, or the smell escapes into the corridor every time the door opens.

Odour is the thing you notice last in your own home. Use the first three seconds after walking in from outside as your test — if you can smell it then, assume the neighbours already can.

9.1 Animal Registration

  • Who must register: dogs aged two months or older kept as companion animals are subject to mandatory registration.
  • Cats are voluntary: cat registration is currently voluntary rather than mandatory, though it meaningfully improves the odds of recovering a lost cat.
  • Microchip versus tag: external tags can fall off or be lost, so implanted microchips are widely regarded as more effective in practice.
  • Voluntary reporting periods: the government periodically runs amnesty windows for unregistered owners, during which the fine is waived if you register.
  • Online handling: address and phone changes and death notifications can be filed online through the national animal protection information system (animal.go.kr).

Registration matters especially for people living alone because of what happens when an animal goes missing. In a larger household someone can stay behind and search; alone, you lose entire working hours during which nothing can be done. If the animal is registered, a finder or a shelter can identify you immediately from the registration number, which changes recovery odds substantially.

9.2 Safety Measures When Out

  • Leash length: use a leash or harness of two metres or less, or a carrier with a latch the animal cannot escape.
  • Exception: carrying a registered animal under three months of age in your arms is treated as an exception.
  • Carrier use: a carrier is effectively mandatory for clinic visits and public transport, so build the habit early through training.
  • ID tag: independent of registration, a tag with your phone number makes the first hour of a disappearance far more productive.

Carrier training is the item people most reliably regret skipping. If the first time you produce the carrier is on a day when the animal is sick and you are in a hurry, the animal is already frightened and you are already behind schedule. Simply leaving it open and feeding treats inside for a few weeks buys you half an hour in an emergency.

10. Preparing for Illness

The most exposed moment for someone living alone is weeknights and weekends — nobody to help, and the clinic is closed. This is not something to arrange after the animal gets sick; it belongs on the day you bring them home.

10.1 Decide These in Advance

  • One primary clinic: a clinic that holds the medical history makes emergency decisions faster. Walking distance is better still.
  • One after-hours clinic: save the location and phone number of a 24-hour clinic. Many charge differently outside normal hours, so ask ahead.
  • Transport: confirm in advance whether you can take a taxi at night with a carrier and whether animals are allowed.
  • Two emergency contacts: name two people who could care for the animal if you are injured or hospitalised, and get their agreement beforehand.
  • Keep records: a phone note with vaccination history, current medications, and known allergies saves real time at a first visit.

This preparation is not only for the animal. As covered in Part 12: When You're Sick, people living alone face the problem that if they collapse, nobody is there to look after what is left at home. The moment you bring an animal in, your emergency plan has to cover two lives.

10.2 After-Hours and Holiday Emergencies

  • Signs that warrant an immediate call: laboured breathing, repeated vomiting, seizures, blood in urine or inability to urinate, and severe lethargy can all be time-critical.
  • Suspected swallowed object: write down what, when, and how much as best you can remember and tell the clinic. It is often decisive.
  • Never medicate on your own: some human painkillers and fever medicines cause severe poisoning in dogs and cats. Follow veterinary direction only.
  • Do not induce vomiting: depending on what was swallowed this can make things worse, so call the clinic before doing anything.
  • Summer caution: heat-sensitive animals can deteriorate quickly, so see our pet summer care guide for seasonal handling.

Everything listed here is a guide for deciding when to call the clinic, nothing more. Self-diagnosing from the internet or sharing your own medication can put the animal somewhere you cannot bring them back from. When you are unsure, calling the clinic to ask is always the cheapest option available.

11. Alternatives

Conditions not fitting right now does not mean never. Filling the waiting period with experience makes you a considerably better owner later.

Try Before Committing

  • Foster: Temporarily house shelter animals
  • Pet sit for friends: Watch their pets while traveling
  • Volunteer: Help at animal shelters
  • Drop-in care for someone you know: start by visiting once or twice a day to handle food and the litter box.
  • Run a one-month budget simulation: actually move the pet budget into a separate account each month and you will know immediately whether it works.

Fostering in particular is the most accurate rehearsal because you live the real thing. You find out how often your sleep is interrupted, how many days you can put off cleaning, and how it feels when you are running late from work. If your mind is unchanged after all that, you are genuinely close to ready.

Combat Loneliness Without Pets

  • Grow plants
  • Watch pet videos
  • Join hobbies and social groups
  • Join a neighbourhood walking group or running crew so you meet people on a regular schedule
  • Build a routine of calling family or friends at a fixed time once a week

Loneliness is not itself a bad reason to adopt. It becomes risky when it is the only reason. Feelings shift within months; the responsibility lasts more than a decade. If you try the other approaches to loneliness and still want to share your home with an animal, that is a far sturdier motivation.

12. If You Decide to Get One

Once you have decided, sequence matters more than speed. There is more to do in the two weeks beforehand than on the day itself.

Preparation

  • Research before adopting (temperament, care needs)
  • Get supplies in advance
  • Find a vet beforehand
  • Arrange emergency pet care
  • Safety-proof the home - deal with cables, small swallowable objects, toxic plants, and loose window screens ahead of time.

The windows and insect screens deserve special attention, as they are the most common accident point in small apartments. Cats pushing through a screen happens more often than people expect, and the higher the floor, the worse the outcome. Check window locks and screen fittings before the animal arrives, not after.

Where to Adopt

  • Recommended: Shelters, rescue organizations
  • Caution: Avoid puppy mills and shady pet shops
  • What to ask: ask whether you can see the parent animals and the housing conditions. A refusal is itself the answer.
  • Health records: get vaccination history, deworming status, and recent treatment records in writing.
  • Return policy: confirm what happens if things go wrong and whether follow-up support exists. It protects both sides.

Shelter adoption is not recommended because it is cheap. The real advantage is that temperament and energy level are already visible, and staff can help you match an individual to a solo-household environment. Say you are looking for a quiet, low-energy animal and they will narrow the candidates for you.

13.2 The First Week After They Arrive

  • First clinic visit: get a health check and discuss the vaccination schedule as early as you can.
  • Register the animal: if it is a dog, registration is mandatory, so handle it rather than putting it off.
  • Introduce the space gradually: starting in one room and expanding slowly settles them better than opening the whole home at once.
  • Log food and toilet habits: noting intake and stool condition in the first week helps you spot problems far earlier.
  • Greet the neighbours: a short heads-up to those above, below, and beside you, plus a contact number, prevents most later friction.

Wrap-up

Pets can ease loneliness, but getting one unprepared hurts both you and the animal. Think it through, and when you're ready, it won't be too late.

To summarise, there are three tests. First, are your daily hours away within a range the animal can handle? Second, can you carry both the fixed monthly costs and a sudden treatment bill? Third, can you take this animal with you through whatever the next ten years bring? If the answer to all three is yes, you are ready; if any one wobbles, solve that item first.

And remember that deciding to wait is also a good decision. Starting once the conditions are in place beats forcing it and having to undo it, for you and for the animal. Life with a companion animal is not the kind of thing you lose by starting late.
