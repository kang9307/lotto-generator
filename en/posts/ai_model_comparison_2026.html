What matters here is not any individual number but the structure.

  • The output-to-input multiple differs by vendor. The Anthropic line is generally 5x, OpenAI's gpt-5.6 line is 6x, DeepSeek is 2x. The longer your outputs, the more that multiple dominates the total.
  • Vendors split on whether they publish a cache rate. Anthropic, OpenAI, DeepSeek and Google did; it does not appear on the xAI or Zhipu pricing pages.
  • xAI quotes a range rather than a single rate. The rate shifts by prompt-length band, so budgeting at the short-prompt rate will be wrong.
  • Google raises the rate at a 200k-token prompt boundary. On Gemini 3.1 Pro input goes from $2.00 to $4.00 and output from $12.00 to $18.00. If you plan to use long context, that boundary is your real rate.
  • Anthropic states in its documentation that the full one-million-token context is billed at the standard rate. The phrasing is that a 900k-token request is billed at the same per-token rate as a 9k-token one. That is the exact opposite of Google's structure.
  • Some models are listed as free. Zhipu's GLM-4.7-Flash and GLM-4.5-Flash are. The usage limits attached to that label were not verified.

The blanks here do not mean the feature is absent. They mean the item was not on the pricing page we opened. Still, the distribution of blanks tells you one thing: the more of the billing structure a vendor writes on one page, the earlier you can build a budget.

  • DeepSeek's 2x peak surcharge was not found at any other vendor. Pushing batch work outside the peak halves the cost, but a real-time service cannot avoid it.
  • Anthropic's cache adds a premium on writes and cuts reads sharply. The documentation states that with the five-minute cache a single read already pays for the write.
  • Google attaches an hourly storage charge to caching, $4.50 per hour on Gemini 3.1 Pro. If your design keeps caches alive, that line can exceed the token line.
  • Anthropic's documentation says batch and cache discounts stack. Using both widens the gap between the list rate and the effective rate.
  • xAI's image, video and voice models are billed in units that are not tokens, such as $0.02 per image or $0.050 per second, so they do not slot into a token budget model.

How to read a benchmark

Score tables are hard to trust not because the scores are wrong but because they are not comparable. Five things prevent a leaderboard from being copied across.

  • The measuring party differs. A vendor's own measurement and a third party's are different kinds of claim. Merge them into one table and you lose which row is which.
  • The configuration differs. Current models expose dials for reasoning depth. Anthropic documents that effort defaults to high, and Google documents a thinking_level that runs from minimal to high. The same model produces different scores depending on where the dial sits.
  • Contamination cannot be checked from outside. Whether a public problem set leaked into training data cannot be judged without the weights and the data. For closed models it is impossible in principle.
  • Only the average survives; the variance disappears. A model that collapses one run in five and a model that is steady can share an average. In production they are entirely different.
  • The measurement point drifts. The serving configuration behind an unchanged model ID can change. Anthropic states that IDs from the 4.6 generation are pinned snapshots, but not every vendor says so.

This article therefore does not copy score tables. It builds tables from the axes documentation verifies (price, limits, lifespan, availability) and replaces the unverifiable axis with a procedure the reader runs on their own work.

The Anthropic family

Anthropic published more information in documentation than any other vendor in this survey. Pricing, cache multipliers, batch discounts, retirement dates, supported regions and even per-tool token consumption each had their own page.

  • The line is four tiers: top-capability Fable 5, workhorse Opus 5, balanced Sonnet 5, lightweight Haiku 4.5.
  • Every current model except Haiku 4.5 is one million tokens in, 128 thousand tokens out.
  • Latency is documented only as relative wording: Fable 5 slower, Opus 5 moderate, Sonnet 5 fast, Haiku 4.5 fastest. There was no absolute figure such as tokens per second.
  • From the 4.7 generation the tokenizer changed, making the same text about 30 percent more tokens, as the documentation states. Choosing a vendor on rate alone misses that 30 percent.
  • The documentation states that temperature, top_p and top_k return a 400 error on 4.7 and later models when set to a non-default value. Code that tuned diversity through temperature does not port over.
  • Previous-generation models down to Opus 4.8 remain available. Opus 4.1 is the exception, being marked for retirement.

Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5

The vendor describes it as built for the most demanding reasoning and long-horizon agentic work. What defines this line is not a performance claim but the fact that its behavioural contract differs.

  • Status and availability: available from 9 June 2026. Fable 5 is general availability, Mythos 5 is invitation-only limited availability. (Anthropic launch page, checked 2026-08-02)
  • Price: $10.00 per million input tokens and $50.00 output. Cache hit $1.00. Batch $5.00 / $25.00. (checked 2026-08-02)
  • Context: one million input tokens as both the default and the maximum, 128 thousand output tokens.
  • Design intent (vendor-stated): agents that run autonomously for long stretches, and the most demanding reasoning tasks.
  • Documented constraints: first, thinking cannot be turned off; passing a disabled thinking configuration is not supported. Second, the raw chain of thought is never returned; you get a summary or an empty field. Third, safety classifiers can decline a request, and a decline arrives as an HTTP 200 success response. Fourth, 30-day data retention is required and the model is unavailable under zero data retention.
  • Choose it when: a single call may run for minutes in an asynchronous job, overnight runs happen without a human watching, and the cost of an error greatly exceeds the token cost.
  • Do not choose it when: your organisation enforces zero data retention, your regulatory environment requires reasoning traces in audit logs, response time is directly visible to a user in a conversational surface, or your integration cannot afford to add a refusal-handling branch.

The notable design point is that a refusal arrives as a success rather than an error. Code that unconditionally reads the first content block breaks on this model. The documentation also describes three retry paths on another model (server-side fallback, client middleware, manual retry) and states that a request declined before any output is generated is not billed.

Claude Opus 5

The vendor describes it as built for complex agentic coding and enterprise work. It sits in the position most likely to be used broadly across this family.

  • Status: general availability. The published retirement date is not sooner than 24 July 2027. (Anthropic deprecations, checked 2026-08-02)
  • Price: $5.00 input / $25.00 output. Cache hit $0.50. Batch $2.50 / $12.50. Fast mode $10.00 / $50.00.
  • Context: one million input tokens, 128 thousand output tokens. Its knowledge cutoff of May 2026 is the most recent of any model verified here.

Turning thinking off is permitted only at effort level high or below, and combining it with the two highest effort levels returns a 400 error. The raw chain of thought is not returned.
  • Choose it when: automation is heavy on coding and tool calls, a recent knowledge cutoff matters, or you need one million input tokens at a standard rate.
  • Do not choose it when: output tokens are very high and the budget is tight, since output costs five times input; you cannot construct a prompt cache because every input differs; or you need very low latency in a real-time response path.
  • Cost reference: $688.75 per month across the three assumed workloads in this article. With an 80 percent cache hit rate, the long-summary job alone fell by 62.6 percent.

  • Choose Haiku 4.5 when: the work is high-volume classification, extraction or routing with short input and short output.

The lowest rates in this survey, and simultaneously the most unusual billing structure.

Speed and latency when no official figure exists

No vendor documentation carries an absolute latency figure such as tokens per second. Only Anthropic published relative grades.

  • Latency grades in the Anthropic models overview: Fable 5 slower, Opus 5 moderate, Sonnet 5 fast, Haiku 4.5 fastest. An ordering, not a number.
  • Anthropic documents a fast mode on Opus 5 and Opus 4.8 billed at $10.00 / $50.00. Latency can be bought with money, but how much faster it is was not verified in that document.
  • Google implies latency through tier names: standard, batch, flex and priority, with the priority tier at 1.8x the standard rate. Latency itself is not quantified.
  • No latency notation appears in the xAI, DeepSeek or Zhipu documentation.

So this axis is presented as a measurement method only. Vendors do not give numbers, so there is nothing to do but measure your own.

  • Send the same prompt at least fifty times and record the difference between request time and completion time.
  • Look at the median and the maximum, not the mean. Users experience the maximum.
  • Measure time-to-first-token and total completion time separately. The former matters in a streaming interface, the latter in batch.
  • Measure across reasoning-effort settings. Higher effort increasing latency is by design.
  • Measure at different times of day, especially on vendors with a peak surcharge where time of day hits both cost and latency.
  • Record latency and cost in the same table. Measuring one alone gives you half a conclusion.

Coding, long documents and multilingual work

For these three uses, only what can be said from verified evidence is said. Where there is no evidence, that is stated.

  • Coding, verified: models exist with a coding purpose in the name (gpt-5.3-codex, $1.75 / $14.00). Anthropic describes Opus 5 as for complex agentic coding and enterprise work. xAI has a separate grok-build-0.1.
  • Coding, unverified: there was no official figure showing that these models actually do coding work better. The name and the description are a statement of intent, not evidence of performance.
  • Coding, practical judgement: coding workloads are output-heavy, so the output rate dominates the total. In this article's Job C (8k in, 4k out) the models with low output rates came out ahead. Tool-level comparison is covered separately in the coding assistant comparison.
  • Long documents, verified: several models accept one million input tokens, while output caps span 64 thousand to 384 thousand, a factor of six. Anthropic states the full million is billed at the standard rate; Google raises its rate at 200 thousand.
  • Long documents, unverified: no vendor published a figure showing that quality holds at long input. A context limit is how much is accepted, not how much is used well.
  • Long documents, practical judgement: the only real check is to ask the same question at half, three quarters and the full context limit and compare accuracy. That procedure is in the section 39 document.
  • Multilingual, verified: DeepSeek's documented ratio of about 0.3 tokens per English character and about 0.6 per Chinese character, and Anthropic's note that one million tokens is roughly 555,000 words or about 2.5 million Unicode characters.
  • Multilingual, unverified: no vendor published per-language quality figures. This article can say nothing about whether Korean, Chinese or Japanese is handled better.
  • Multilingual, practical judgement: the verified ratios alone support one statement. A Chinese document of the same length costs roughly twice the tokens of an English one. Chinese workloads therefore cost more in practice at the same rate. That is pure accounting, unrelated to quality.

Insight 1: the pressure pushing prices down

The distribution of verified rates has a clear shape. Within a single generation, the top and the bottom differ by more than a hundredfold. The $30.00 input of gpt-5.5-pro and the $0.07 of GLM-4.7-FlashX are 428 times apart.

Three pressures produce that distribution.

  • Competitive pressure through tier splitting. Vendors now publish three or more price points rather than one. gpt-5.6 has three (sol, terra, luna), Claude has four (Fable, Opus, Sonnet, Haiku), Gemini has three (Pro, Flash, Flash-Lite). Tier splitting protects the top price while competing at the bottom.
  • Same specification, different price. gpt-5.6-sol and terra matched on context, output cap, cutoff and modalities yet differ 2.5x in price. That is a signal that price is being set on something other than specification.
  • Old generations persist. gpt-3.5-turbo ($0.50 / $1.50) remains on OpenAI's list, Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite ($0.10 / $0.40) on Google's, GLM-4.5 ($0.60 / $2.20) on Zhipu's. Old generations do not disappear, they descend into the cheap band. Every new release thickens the floor.
  • The floor effect of published weights. The fact that DeepSeek's weights are published under MIT places a ceiling on API prices at that capability level. It is hard to charge a high rate for performance anyone can self-host.
  • Introductory pricing as a temporary cut. Claude Sonnet 5 is $2 / $10 through 31 August 2026 and $3 / $15 from 1 September. It is a documented case showing that a price cut is not necessarily permanent.

At the same time pressures pushing prices up are also in the documentation. Fast mode at $10 / $50, priority handling at 1.8x standard, United States-only processing at 1.1x, the band above 200 thousand tokens at 2x, peak hours at 2x. Base rates fall while option multipliers multiply. That is why a list price can look cheap while the invoice does not.

Insight 2: why context length is not performance

Laying out only the verified facts already produces the conclusion.

  • Models with one-million-token input appear across five vendors in this survey alone. One million is no longer a differentiator.
  • By contrast output caps range from 64 thousand to 384 thousand, a factor of six. The ability to read a long document and the ability to write one move independently.
  • Google raises its rate at a 200-thousand-token boundary. The vendor is saying through price that long context costs more.
  • Anthropic states the opposite, that the full million is billed at the standard rate. Two vendors have exactly opposite pricing policies for the same feature.
  • Google's documentation warns that rising default media resolution can push a request past the context window. There is a path by which context is consumed silently.
  • Anthropic's documentation shows that tool definitions occupy hundreds of tokens on every request. Context does not fill only with what the user put in.

The judgement that follows is this. A context limit is how much is accepted; performance is the ability to find what matters inside it. The former is in the documentation and the latter is not. And measuring the latter means asking the same question at several points along the limit. Context design itself is covered further in the context engineering article.

Insight 3: why vendors emphasize different things

Why do documentation structures differ so much in one market? It can be reverse-engineered from the verified facts.

  • Anthropic documents predictability. Retirement dates, a 60-day notice policy, per-tool token consumption, cache multipliers, region lists, even the downsides of its own deprecation policy. That is what an organisation evaluating a long contract needs.
  • OpenAI documents optionality. Thirty-plus models on one page, each with a cache rate. Lifespan and legal conditions were not verifiable by this method. That is what a developer wiring something up today needs.
  • Google documents tiers. Four service grades of standard, batch, flex and priority, plus per-modality rates and hourly cache storage. That is the grammar of cloud billing: AI sold the same way as every other cloud service.
  • xAI documents composition. Text, image, video and voice in one table with different billing units each. That is how a product bundle is sold.
  • DeepSeek documents the rate and its conditions. Cache hit and miss, peak hours, per-language token ratios. The documentation is short but everything needed for a cost calculation is there.
  • Zhipu documents model count. Fourteen rates in one table with almost no specifications, plus two free tiers.

These differences are not taste but a reflection of revenue model and distribution channel. Enterprise-contract vendors lead with lifespan and policy, self-service vendors with price and model count, cloud divisions with service grades. Read a vendor's documentation structure and you can see who they are selling to. And that tells you whether you are in their intended customer base.

Insight 4: where the open and closed boundary falls

The word open points at at least three different things. Verified cases separate them.

  • Weights published under an unrestrictive license. DeepSeek-V3.2's MIT license is this case. Use, modification, redistribution and sale are all permitted with only a copyright notice to preserve. This is openness in its widest sense.
  • Weights published under a custom license. The Llama line is understood to sit here, but this survey could not verify the license text, so its conditions are not quoted.
  • Weights closed and only an API offered. Claude, GPT, Gemini and Grok belong here.

The boundary really falls on who carries the failure risk.

  • With an API the vendor carries availability, scaling and updates while you carry retirement dates and policy changes. The 5 August 2026 retirement verified in this article is exactly that.
  • With weights you carry serving, scaling and security, and in exchange there is no retirement. Nobody switches your model off.
  • So the deciding criterion is not performance but whether your service has to outlive the vendor's retirement schedule. If the contract runs three years and the guarantee runs one, the answer is already written.
  • On cost there is a crossover. The cheapest API in this article's assumed workload was $12.67 a month. That figure cannot cover the hardware and staffing of self-hosting. Self-hosting only makes sense at far larger volume or under a data-control requirement.
  • Hardware requirements and quantization trade-offs for local execution are covered separately in the local LLM guide. The regulatory debate around openness sits in the open-weight regulation article.

How to measure it yourself

The honest conclusion of this article is one line. Public benchmarks do not transfer to your work, so measure it yourself. Writing that and then withholding the method would be empty, so the full procedure document is embedded below. Copy it and use it as-is.

  • A golden set of 30 to 50 tasks drawn from real work: 40 percent easy, 40 percent medium, 20 percent hard.
  • Write the pass condition first. Deciding the criteria after seeing the output lets the criteria drift toward the output.
  • Freeze the prompt template and change only the model. One variable at a time. Change two and you cannot separate cause.
  • Run the same input five times. Four of five and five of five are entirely different stories in production.
  • Record only items you can count: satisfied format clauses, factual error count, median and maximum latency, output token count, number of runs needing human touch-up.
  • Put tokens and time in the same table as quality. Measuring one alone gives you half a conclusion.

## 0. The premise — why measure it yourself

| What a public benchmark tells you | What it does not tell you |
|---|---|
| Accuracy on a specific public problem set | How it handles your in-house vocabulary, formats, and edge cases |
| The items the vendor chose to publish | The items the vendor chose not to publish |
| A value at one point in time, under one configuration | The value under your configuration (effort level, temperature, tools) |
| An average | The variance. Whether it collapses one time in five |

- Whether benchmark items leaked into training data **cannot be verified from outside**
- Vendor self-measurement and third-party measurement are **different kinds of claim**. Do not blend them into one ranking
- Fifty of your own tasks are more accurate **for you** than five thousand public benchmark items

## 1. The harness — step by step

1. **Build a golden set.** Thirty to fifty real tasks. Forty percent easy, forty percent medium, twenty percent hard
2. **Write the correct answer or pass condition first.** If you decide the criteria after seeing the output, the criteria drift toward the output
3. **Freeze the prompt template.** Use the template in section 2 and change only the blanks per task
4. **Record the configuration.** Model name, version string, effort or reasoning level, output cap, whether tools were enabled
5. **Send the same input to each model five times.** Record each run separately
6. **Grade the output only.** Grading blind, without knowing which model produced which output, is better still
7. **Write it into the table.** Use the form in section 6
8. **Record tokens and time alongside quality.** Measuring quality without cost gives you half a conclusion

## 2. The prompt template — drop your own work into the blanks

```
[ROLE]

[INPUT]

---
(paste the real material here)
---

[TASKS]

[OUTPUT FORMAT]
- One line of (summary format) at the very top
- Then (number) items, each ordered as (field A) / (field B)
- Total within (length limit)

[CONSTRAINTS]
- Do not state anything absent from (the source material)
- Where you are not certain, mark the item as NEEDS CHECK
- Do not use (list of forbidden phrasings)

[Output the format above only. No preamble, no closing remarks.]
```

- **Change only the blanks; keep the template fixed.** If the template changes, you cannot tell a model difference from a prompt difference
- Put one deliberately easy-to-break condition in the constraints. It becomes your ruler for instruction following
- The NEEDS CHECK clause is an instrument for observing **avoidance behavior**

## 3. One variable at a time

| What you changed | What you held fixed | What you can learn | What you cannot learn |
|---|---|---|---|
| Model only | Prompt, input, configuration | Differences between models | Whether a prompt fix would reverse it |
| Prompt only | Model, input, configuration | Prompt sensitivity | Whether the same holds on other models |
| Effort level only | Model, prompt, input | The quality and cost curve against effort | Whether the curve is the same on other tasks |
| Input length only | Everything else | The point where behavior degrades | Whether the cause is length or content |

- Change two things at once and **you cannot separate cause**. Even if results improve, you will not know why
- The more urgent it feels, the more strictly you should hold to one variable. A conclusion drawn from two moving parts has to be re-measured next month

## 4. Why repeat the same run

- There is **no guarantee** the same input yields the same output. One run is a sample of one
- **Five runs minimum.** Four-of-five and five-of-five are entirely different stories in production
- If even one failure appears, **keep that failure**. Failure modes are the real basis for model choice
- A model with high run-to-run variance is hard to put in an automated pipeline even if its average score is high

## 5. The evaluation checklist — with how to count each item

| Item | How to count it | Unit of record |
|---|---|---|
| Instruction following | Score each required format, length, and prohibition item pass or fail | satisfied clauses / total clauses |
| Output format stability | How many of five runs kept the structure intact | N of 5 |
| Length variance | Character count of the five outputs. Maximum divided by minimum | ratio (closer to 1.0 is more stable) |
| Factual errors | Plant items whose answers you know, then count the wrong ones | count / run |
| Unsupported assertions | Sentences stated as fact that are absent from the source | count / run |
| Refusal and avoidance | How many items were refused or deferred as NEEDS CHECK, and where | count / run plus the stage |
| Latency | Difference between request time and completion time | seconds (median of five) |
| Token consumption | Input and output token counts reported in the response | tokens / run |
| Human touch-up needed | How often the output could not be used as-is | N of 5 |

- **Drop any criterion you cannot count.** Reads naturally and feels smart do not reproduce next week
- Recording a single good-or-bad cell means you will not know later why you judged that way
- For latency, look at the **median and the maximum**, not the mean. Users experience the maximum

## 7. Measuring your own cost

Unit prices come from the pricing tables in the article. This section holds only **the counting procedure and the formula**.

1. Pick three representative jobs. For example: short query / long-document summary / code generation
2. **Actually run each one once** and write down the input and output token counts reported in the response
3. If the vendor offers a token counter, cross-check with it
4. Count **how many times per month** you run each job. Count it from logs, not from memory
5. Put the numbers into the formula

```
monthly input tokens per job  = input tokens per run times runs per month
monthly output tokens per job = output tokens per run times runs per month

monthly cost = (monthly input tokens divided by 1000000 times input unit price USD)
             + (monthly output tokens divided by 1000000 times output unit price USD)

total monthly cost = the sum across all three jobs
```

6. If caching applies, split **cache hits from cache misses** before computing

```
monthly input cost with caching
 = (miss tokens divided by 1000000 times base input price)
 + (hit tokens divided by 1000000 times cache read price)
 + (cache write tokens divided by 1000000 times cache write price)
```

7. The following do **not** appear in the headline unit price and must be added separately

- Server-side tool fees such as web search or code execution
- The portion of every request consumed by tool definitions and the system prompt
- Retries. A request that failed and was re-sent is still billed
- The share of traffic on real-time paths where batch or async discounts do not apply
- Multipliers tied to time of day or region option

8. **Different tokenizers count the same document differently.** When comparing vendors, do not compare unit prices alone. First check **how many tokens each one counts for the same document**

## 9. Do not

- **Do not ask a model which model is best.** It is circular, and the model has no basis for ranking itself
- Do not build your table by copying vendor-published scores. The measurement conditions differ between them
- Do not generalize a win on one task into a win on all tasks
- Do not conclude from a single run
- Do not tune the prompt separately for each model and then call it a model comparison
- Do not choose on price alone. A cheap model gets expensive once retries multiply
- Do not choose on quality alone. Latency drives user abandonment

- When you substantially rewrite your prompt
- Once a quarter, even when nothing has happened

Limits of this article

Not a formal disclaimer but a record of what was actually not done.

  • The research budget was 30 official URLs. Mistral, Cohere and others were never attempted. They are missing, not excluded.
  • No model was executed. This is a documentation comparison, not a performance evaluation. Every judgement about quality has to be made by the reader using the section 39 procedure.
  • A rendering failure cannot be blamed on the vendor. It may be a limitation of automated retrieval. A table visible in a browser may simply have been invisible to us.
  • Every token count in the cost simulation is an assumption, not a measurement. And because tokenizers differ, the same document produces different counts, so the multiples in the table should be read as magnitude rather than ranking.
  • Region lists were not compared exhaustively. Only a few regions of interest were checked. A full comparison could reveal further differences.
  • No negotiated contract pricing is reflected at all. A large customer's real rate will differ from this table.
  • There is only one check date. This is a snapshot of 2 August 2026. Yesterday's and tomorrow's values may differ.
  • This site has no relationship with any vendor, but the choice of URLs and research order can still carry bias. Vendors with better-organised documentation received more space. That is a reflection of documentation quality, not a quality evaluation.
