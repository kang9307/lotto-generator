Microsoft Teams for Beginners — Where Your Messages and Files Actually Live

Where your messages and files actually live

Aug 16, 2026 Tech & IT

Teams is a front desk, not a filing cabinet

You get a Teams account at a new job and the left rail already has a few teams and a list of channels in it. Nobody tells you what goes where. So most people do the familiar thing: search for a person's name, open a private chat, and drop the file there. That is almost always where the first button goes into the wrong hole.

Nearly every question that trips up new Teams users comes from not knowing one fact. Teams is not a program that holds your messages and files inside itself. Behind it sit storage services your company already uses, and Teams is closer to a front desk sitting on top of them. A file posted in a channel goes to the SharePoint site connected to that team. A file posted in a private chat goes to the sender's OneDrive. Conversations are journaled as compliance copies into mail mailboxes, and meeting recordings usually land in the storage of whoever created the meeting.

Once you have that one line, scattered puzzles resolve in the same place. Why a file you deleted from a Teams chat is still sitting in your OneDrive. Why files in one channel of your own team refuse to open. Whether a private channel is really private. Why some of a departed colleague's files are fine and others have dead links. All the same question wearing different faces: which storage was it actually in.

This article is not about memorizing button positions. The interface changes often and you will pick it up by clicking around anyway. It covers the part that does not move much — what goes where, who can see it, and when it disappears. Where admin settings make the answer differ by organization, that is flagged as it comes up, and the behavior described is based on Microsoft's official documentation as of August 2026.

Team, channel, chat — what each unit actually holds

Conversations in Teams split into three units. The names look interchangeable, but the storage and permission structure behind each one is different, which makes them genuinely different objects.

  • Team — a bundle of people. Creating a team also creates the group that holds its membership, plus a SharePoint site to hold files. A team is not a chat room, it is a permission unit.
  • Channel — a subject-based space inside a team. Every team starts with a general channel that cannot be deleted. Channels are rooms within the team, but the storage behind them depends on the type.
  • Chat — a person-to-person conversation outside any team. Whether it is one-on-one or several people, it does not belong to a team. What happens there is treated as the participants' own material, not the team's.

On top of those sits the meeting, which is a calendar entry that also produces a chat of its own, and where the recording lands depends on whether it was held in a channel. But the boundary that confuses beginners most is the one between channel and chat. On screen they are the same window of messages scrolling upward. One is an organizational space and the other is a personal one. That boundary is the root of every file, permission, and offboarding problem below.

Three kinds of channel — similar names, different backends

There are three channel types, and per Microsoft's documentation their file storage splits like this.

  • Standard channels — every team member has access. All standard channels in a team share a single team site, with one folder per channel inside it. Joining the team means access to every standard channel's files.
  • Private channels — only some members of the team are in it. This type gets its own separate SharePoint site, and only channel members can reach it. Even a team owner cannot see those files without being a channel member.
  • Shared channels — you can add people who are not team members, and in some configurations people outside the organization. These also use a separate SharePoint site.
  • Permissions on private and shared channel sites are inherited from channel membership. On the SharePoint side they show as read-only and cannot be edited there, so adding and removing people has to happen in the channel. The address usually follows the pattern [SharePoint domain]/sites/[parent team name]-[channel name], so if a link opens somewhere other than the familiar team site, you are probably looking at a private or shared channel file.

A practical conclusion falls out of this. Every private channel you add is another storage location to manage. One channel is one site. Spin up a private channel for a short-lived conversation and abandon it, and the organization accumulates sites nobody remembers the contents of.

Channel conversation vs private chat — the record lands elsewhere

Writing the same sentence in a channel versus a private chat is not a question of tone. It is a question of that sentence's lifespan and ownership. Split three ways, the difference is obvious.

First, who can see it. A post in a standard channel is visible to everyone on the team now and everyone who joins later, because channel conversations attach to the room rather than to people. A private chat is visible only to those invited. A new teammate can read yesterday's channel discussion on their own, but a decision two people closed out in a chat is something they cannot even know exists.

Second, is it findable later. Channel posts are the team's record and can be found within the team, while private chats can only be found from each participant's own side. This is where newcomers lose the most. Six months later you go looking for why a decision was made, and the reasoning lives in a two-person chat while the channel holds a single line of outcome.

Third, where the record accumulates. Separately from what you see on screen, Teams messages are stored as compliance copies in mail mailboxes. Per Microsoft's documentation, the destination differs by conversation type.

  • One-on-one and group chats — copies go to each participant's own mailbox.
  • Standard channel messages — copies go to the group mailbox representing that team.
  • Private channel messages — copies go to the mailboxes of every member of that channel.
  • Shared channel messages — copies go to a system mailbox associated with the parent team.

What is retained is not only message bodies. Reaction emoji, inline images, code snippets, in-meeting conversation, and meeting metadata such as start and end times and each participant's join and leave events are all in scope. The implication is simple. Making something disappear from your screen and making the record disappear are two different acts. Deleting a message should not be assumed to take a retained compliance copy with it.

Where to write what — a decision table

Do not memorize rules; decide with one question. Six months from now, will somebody other than me need to find this? If yes it is a channel, if no it is a chat. Common situations sorted on that basis look like this.

SituationWhere it goesWhy
Progress, decisions, and reasoning on live workStandard channelWhoever joins later has to be able to read it unaided
Documents the team will keep referring toThe channel Files tabOnly team storage survives independently of a personal account
An urgent but trivial one-line checkPrivate chatLog everything in the channel and nobody reads the channel
Compensation, reviews, anything personal to one personPrivate chatThis conversation should attach to a person, not to a room
Sensitive work only a subset should handlePrivate channelSeparate storage keeps it away from the rest of the team
Working with an outside vendor or partnerShared channel or guest accessWhich of the two is open depends on organizational policy
The final wrap-up of a settled discussionSummarize back into the channelA decision finished in chat never enters the team's record

That last row is the least familiar and the most important for a newcomer. Storage on a work account is convenient, but it is not yours. The offboarding section spells out exactly why.

Files are not stored in Teams

Upload a file in the Teams interface and it looks like it went somewhere inside Teams. In fact it uploads to another service's storage and Teams merely reflects it. Per Microsoft's documentation the destinations split like this.

What you uploadedActual storage locationHow access is decided
Standard channel filesChannel folder in the team SharePoint siteTeam owners and members are added to the site permission groups automatically
Private channel filesThat channel's own SharePoint siteChannel owners and members only. The site cannot be shared separately
Shared channel filesThat channel's own SharePoint siteChannel members including external participants. Not shareable with outsiders who are not members
One-on-one and group chat filesMicrosoft Teams Chat Files folder in the sender's OneDriveOpened by the participants of that conversation through a share link
Recording and transcript of a regular meetingRecordings folder in the organizer's OneDriveInvited attendees receive an individually shared link
Recording and transcript of a channel meetingRecordings folder in the channel SharePoint siteInherits channel owners and members permissions directly

Row four causes the most accidents in practice. The owner of a file posted in a chat is the person who posted it, not the team. In the conversation window it looks like everybody's file, but from the storage side it is closer to a file in your personal folder that a few people were handed a link to. If the uploader deletes or moves the original, the link stays in the conversation but the others cannot open it. Conversely, deleting the file message from the conversation leaves the original in your storage untouched. They are two separate acts.

Permissions follow the place, not the file

This is the core of understanding Teams. Rather than attaching permissions to files one by one, which storage the file sits in determines who can reach it. The consequences look like this.

  • Add someone to a channel and the channel's past files open up to them. There is no separate step to share files one at a time. Membership is access.
  • Remove someone from a channel and their access to that storage ends with it. That said, if they previously copied a file into their own storage, that copy is not recalled.
  • Move a file between channels and permissions move with it. Drag a document from a private channel into a standard one and the whole team can see it. In the other direction, personal material parked in a channel folder is readable by anyone with site permissions, and it stays with the team after you leave.
  • Share links are a separate layer from storage permissions. Links that open to the whole organization, links limited to named people, and links that reach outside are each allowed or blocked, and which is the default is up to admin settings.

The test is one question. Should this file attach to a person or to a room? Put room material in a chat and it eventually disappears; put person material in a channel and people who never needed to see it keep seeing it.

Notifications — why the defaults exhaust you

Three or four days in, the notifications become unmanageable. The cause is structural, not a personal failure of configuration. Teams notifications are decided by organizational policy, app-wide settings, team and channel settings, and per-conversation settings stacked on top of each other, and the defaults lean toward not missing anything. Joining a team can also surface some channels automatically. Your starting state is not something you chose; it is what the organization's defaults produced.

The common mistake here is muting the app outright. That buys silence at the cost of burying the calls aimed directly at you. There are three axes worth touching.

  • Reduce how many channels are visible first. There is no reason to keep every channel of every team in the list. Channels unrelated to current work drop out of the notification math when you remove them from the list, and you can bring them back when needed.
  • Give the survivors different intensities. Channel notifications generally offer a tier for every new post, a tier for only what mentions you, and off. Two or three channels at full volume is plenty for one day.
  • Block by time. Using a setting that stops notifications outside working hours means you do not have to resist by willpower. Some organizations already have a working-hours policy applied.

The most effective way to reduce notifications is not a setting but a habit. Use the everyone-call only when everyone genuinely has to see it. One person paging the whole team over something non-urgent is enough to send everyone else running toward mute, and then the genuinely urgent announcement does not land. Designing notifications in layers transfers between tools — the same approach applied elsewhere is in Slack Basics — The Places You Get Stuck in Your First Week.

Meetings — whose storage keeps the recording and transcript

Meetings leave more record than anywhere else in Teams. Where that record goes depends on the meeting type and who pressed record. Organized from Microsoft's documentation, it reads as follows.

  • Scheduled meetings and events — the recording uploads to the organizer's OneDrive, into a Recordings folder, even if the organizer never joined. Another attendee pressing record does not change the destination.
  • One-on-one and group calls — these go to the OneDrive of whoever pressed record. In a one-on-one, that person holds full permissions and the other side gets read-only. If the other party is outside the organization they have no access at all and must be shared in.
  • Channel meetings — saved into a Recordings folder in that channel's SharePoint site, with permissions following the channel owners and members list. Channel members who never attended can watch it.
  • Viewing rights for non-channel meetings — invited attendees receive an individually shared link. External attendees are not added automatically and must be added by the organizer, and in meetings above 250 participants some attendees are left out and have to ask the owner.
  • Transcripts are separate files, and recordings surface in many places. Even in a meeting where only transcription is on and nothing is recorded, what was said remains as text. Recordings get linked in the meeting chat, listed in the recordings and transcripts view on the calendar, and indexed by organizational search.

Telling participants is a procedural matter, not a courtesy. An indicator appears when recording starts, but an indicator appearing and participants understanding and agreeing to what it means are different things. Microsoft lets an organization point the recording and transcription notice at its own privacy policy URL, and shows a default policy document when none is set. An organization can also configure meetings so that recording proceeds only with participants' explicit consent. The line to hold in practice is short.

  • With external participants or people you are meeting for the first time, say out loud at the start that it is being recorded and get acknowledgment. Do not lean on the on-screen indicator alone.
  • If a recording or transcript will exist, know how far it will be visible in advance. A channel meeting means the whole channel; a scheduled meeting means the invited list.
  • Whether recording is allowed at all is an organizational policy. Where an admin has blocked it the button simply does nothing, and that is not a malfunction. Blocking downloads of channel meeting recordings so members can only view, and expiring recordings automatically after a period, are likewise on the admin side.

What administrators can see

This topic is best handled as plain fact. Neither inflating nor minimizing it helps anyone at work. Teams messages are stored as compliance copies in mailboxes, and someone granted separate investigative permissions can search, preserve, and export those copies under a regulatory or legal process. The scope includes chat and channel messages, prior versions of edited messages when a hold is in place, reactions, inline images, in-meeting conversation, metadata such as join and leave times, and recordings and transcripts. Private channels are no exception, because the copies go to channel members' mailboxes.

The following is equally true.

  • Not every administrator can automatically read conversations. Using the investigative tooling requires permissions granted separately. Managing accounts and reading content are different layers.
  • Which circumstances trigger a search is set by the organization's policy and procedure. What is technically possible and what an organization actually does are separate things, and employment and privacy regulations apply on top.
  • Anything done on a work account lives inside the company's record system. That is not a surveillance question, it is a basic property of business systems. At the same time there are exclusions — anonymous meeting participants fall outside search scope, for instance — so assuming the coverage is exhaustive is also inaccurate.

The practical conclusion compresses to one line. On a work account, write only sentences that would not embarrass you if someone else read them later. And keep personal things on a personal account.

Leaving a job — what survives when the account goes

This is the article's real conclusion. The storage split laid out above becomes the outcome directly. What sat in team storage stays; what sat in your own account's storage is cleaned up alongside it.

MaterialActual locationAfter account deletion
Files posted in channelsTeam SharePoint siteStays. It is the team's asset
Channel conversation recordCompliance copies in the team group mailboxStays with the team
Files posted in private chatsYour OneDriveEnters cleanup along with the account
Recordings of meetings you organizedYour OneDriveCleaned up too. Channel meeting recordings stay
Private chat conversation recordEach participant's own mailboxGone with your mailbox unless a hold applies
Personal files kept on the work accountYour OneDriveRecovery authority sits with company admins

Microsoft documents publicly what happens to a OneDrive after account deletion. The points worth knowing are these.

  • The default retention period is 30 days. Admins can lengthen or shorten it, so it varies by company.
  • By default the deleted user's manager is automatically delegated access, and the notice about pending deletion goes to that person. If no manager is set, a secondary owner designated by the organization receives it.
  • If delegation is off and neither a manager nor a secondary owner exists, nobody gets automatic access and nobody gets warned. This is the classic path by which a handover fails quietly.
  • A second notice arrives seven days before the retention period ends, and afterward the OneDrive moves to the site recycle bin and is kept for 93 days. From that point shared links stop opening, and recycle bin contents are not indexed for search, which makes them hard to reach even with investigative tooling.
  • Organizational retention policies and legal holds always take precedence over this process. Content can be deleted sooner than 30 days or kept far longer.
  • Mailboxes work slightly differently. Delete an account while a hold is in place and it remains as an inactive mailbox that permitted staff can search; with no hold applied, it simply goes away.

Whether you just started or are about to move on, two things hold.

  • Put material the team will need into a channel from the start. Even if it came through a chat, upload anything that has to survive into the channel files as well. This is not tidiness, it is changing which storage it lives in.
  • Do not keep personal things on the work account. Passport scans, family photos, side-project files in a work OneDrive are not yours to control after you leave. Under default settings the first person to reach that material is your former manager.
  • Clean up before you go. On the way out the account is locked first and deleted after. Asking once access is cut means borrowing someone else's hands, and the process then follows company rules.

What this article does not settle

The following vary by organizational setting, subscription, and point in time, so this article does not state them definitively. Check with your own admins or the official documentation.

  • Exact menu names and locations are not given. The Teams interface differs by client version and organizational setting and is revised often. This article goes only as far as which setting to look for.
  • Feature boundaries and prices by subscription are not covered. Which capability belongs to which subscription is settled by the official pricing pages and your organization's contract.
  • How far external guests and shared channels are permitted differs everywhere. Some organizations leave them open and others block them entirely.
  • Retention periods for messages and files cannot be stated. Organizational retention policy overrides default behavior, so there is no substitute for checking your own rules.
  • The availability of keyword alerts that catch your name or a specific word was not confirmed. Channel-level tuning works anywhere, but word-level alerting may differ. AI features that produce transcripts and summaries are left out for the same reason, along with how they handle data.

Frequently asked questions

Q1. Where are files uploaded to a Teams channel stored?

Not in Teams but in the SharePoint site connected to that team. Standard channels share one team site with a folder each, while private and shared channels get separate sites. Permissions come from that site's member list, not from the file.

Q2. Can the other person always open a file I shared in a private chat?

It is stored in the Microsoft Teams Chat Files folder in the sender's OneDrive and opened by participants through a link. The owner is the uploader, not the team, so deleting or moving the original breaks it for everyone else. Conversely, deleting the message leaves the original in place.

Q3. Who owns a Teams meeting recording?

Scheduled meetings go to the organizer's OneDrive, one-on-one and group calls to the recorder's OneDrive, and channel meetings to that channel's SharePoint site. Only channel meetings inherit permissions from the channel list; the rest hand individual links to invitees. Whether recording is allowed is an admin policy.

Q4. Are private channel conversations invisible to administrators?

Private means other members of the same team cannot see it, not that organizational search is impossible. Compliance copies are stored in the channel members' mailboxes, and someone with separate permissions can search them under a regulatory or legal process. When and by whom is set by organizational policy and procedure.

Q5. What happens to my Teams material when I leave the company?

It splits by storage. Channel files sit in the team site and stay, while chat files and recordings of meetings you organized sit in your OneDrive and are cleaned up with the account. After deletion the OneDrive is retained for 30 days by default, admins can change that, and by default access is delegated automatically to your manager.

Wrapping up

There is not much to memorize in Teams. If only one thing survives, make it this. Whatever you do in Teams, it is an act that places something into a specific storage location. Write in a channel and it lands in the team's storage; write in a chat and it lands in yours. Access rights, searchability, and whether it survives your departure all follow from that.

Three things for the first week. First, notice every time whether you are writing in a channel or a chat. If someone else will need it in six months, it is a channel. Second, cut how many channels are visible and give intensity only to what remains. Muting the whole app buries the calls meant for you. Third, keep personal things off the work account. One file uploaded for convenience becomes a file you cannot retrieve without someone else's approval.

The rest you can learn by clicking. The interface moves; the shape of the storage behind it does not move much.
