HOP, an Open-Source Desktop App for HWP and HWPX - And the Wider Map of Alternatives

For anyone who has to handle Korean office documents on macOS, on Linux, or inside an automation script, here is what actually works today and where each option stops

Aug 16, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: when one attachment stops the whole day

A grant announcement lands in your inbox. The extension is .hwp. Your laptop is a MacBook, your office server runs Linux, and the device in your hand right now is a tablet. You spend a few minutes trying to open it and end up asking a colleague for a screenshot. Anyone who works in or with Korea has hit this scene at least once, and remarkably it still happens in 2026.

Since 18 May 2026, newly created electronic documents at Korean administrative agencies have to use an open document format. The direction is now clear. But a regulation does not retroactively open the files already sitting on shared drives, and documents moving between schools, associations and business partners are outside its scope entirely. What is useful right now is therefore not another policy explainer but a map: which tool reaches how far, and where it stops.

This article draws that map. It covers the structure of the formats briefly, sorts the alternatives by what kind of thing they are, and then places HOP — a recently published open-source desktop app — on that map, stating only what could be verified. It closes on the practical question: when you archive a document or send it to somebody else, what should you pick?

1. HWP and HWPX only look like siblings

Many people treat the two extensions as an old and a new version of the same thing. They are actually different containers. Without that distinction you cannot explain either complaint you will hear: we switched to HWPX and it still will not open, and this is HWPX and it opened instantly.

1.1 HWP 5.0 — the binary container line

HWP 5.0 sits on a container called CFB, Compound File Binary. Think of a tiny file system packed inside a single file: several streams arranged in a directory structure, each holding binary records, with compression and encryption available as options. For a desktop word processor of that era this was a sensible engineering choice. The consequence is that you need code implementing those binary rules before you can reach the content at all. Open it in a text editor and you see nothing usable.

1.2 HWPX — the XML package line

HWPX follows OWPML, the Open WordProcessor Markup Language defined by the Korean national standard KS X 6101. Physically it is a ZIP archive containing XML documents. Rename it to .zip, unpack it, and the body text is right there as XML. It is the same idea as ODF (.odt) or OOXML (.docx).

One clarification worth fixing early. An open format and identical rendering across tools are two different problems. Pulling text and tables out of XML is not hard; placing that text at the same position with the same line breaks as the original program is the job of a layout engine. Publishing the format solves the first half. The second half is fought separately by every implementation. Everything in the later sections about how far a tool gets rests on this split.

2. Why this format persisted in public documents

Viewed purely as a technical question this makes no sense. More open formats existed, so why not switch? The answer lives not in the file but in forms and procedures.

Administrative documents are not free-form writing. The number of cells in an approval block, the numbering rules for attachment lists, tables nested inside tables, page numbering and seal placement are all frozen into templates, and those templates were designed on top of one word processor's typesetting features. Changing the format therefore does not mean changing a file. It means changing decades of accumulated template assets together with the document management systems that were built assuming them. That is not a scale one staff member decides on.

None of this is a peculiarly Korean oddity. In Japan, Ichitaro (一太郎) was deeply embedded in government offices and schools for a long time; in China, the WPS line has held a comparable position. Wherever the local script made typesetting demanding, a domestic office suite got ahead early, and the template inertia that followed is similarly heavy everywhere. Lock-in is not created by a format. It is created by the work procedures built on top of it.

  • Template assets — every agency's approval, dispatch and register forms depend on specific typesetting behaviour.
  • System coupling — document management and electronic approval systems were wired to a particular format.
  • Training and habit — the keyboard shortcuts and workflows already in people's hands are themselves a switching cost.
  • External submission — whatever the receiving office demands becomes the standard. The submitter has no choice.
  • Legacy archives — switching the new format leaves the existing archive exactly where it was.

3. What the 18 May 2026 rule actually changed

An amendment to the Regulation on the Operation and Innovation of Administrative Affairs passed the State Council on 12 May 2026 and took effect on 18 May 2026. Two things matter. Administrative agencies must follow an open document format when creating electronic documents, and attachments in the central and local On-Nara document system are restricted to open formats. The stated goal includes letting AI extract and analyse the data directly, and the same amendment carries a provision about making efforts to supply foreign-language versions of forms and documents for foreign residents.

That is the change. Knowing what did not change is more useful in practice.

  • Decades of already-produced documents stay in their original format. There is no retroactive conversion duty.
  • Documents exchanged among schools, associations, companies and individuals fall outside the rule.
  • Even in XML, the format switch does not mean your MacBook renders the file. You still need a reader.
  • Rollout speed varies by agency, so checking the receiving office's guidance before submitting remains the safe move.

The policy background and the AI-processing angle are covered separately in Korean document formats in the AI era and the open standard challenge. This article builds on top of that and concentrates on the tooling landscape.

4. What a closed format actually costs

Calling it inconvenient understates it. The cost splits four ways, and each branch is billed to a different person.

4.1 Cross-platform — it excludes people, not devices

A macOS, Linux or mobile user unable to open a document is not a matter of taste. If you cannot read a grant announcement, you lose the chance to apply. If a parent cannot open a school notice, information simply stops. It looks like one developer's annoyance and is in fact an information access gap.

4.2 Long-term preservation — will anyone be able to open it later

Preservation always reduces to a single question: will code that reads this file still exist in twenty years? A format with a published specification and several independent implementations answers closer to yes. A format that depends on one particular version of one particular product ties the lifetime of the document to the lifetime of that product. This is exactly why records-keeping organisations maintain PDF/A, the archival profile for fixed layout, as a separate deliverable.

4.3 Accessibility — is there structured text to hand to a screen reader

A screen reader can only read what it receives as structured text. Whether table headers are marked as headers, and whether headings carry an actual heading level, decides the quality of the reading. Scanned image documents are the obvious failure, but text that exists and cannot be extracted by any available tool produces the same result.

4.4 Automation — someone ends up retyping it by hand

Pulling dates and amounts out of several hundred official letters is five minutes of work for a program, provided the program can read the file. When it cannot, a person opens each one and retypes it, and typos enter the data. Lately one more item joins the list: handing documents to a conversational AI for summary or analysis, which likewise only works if the text can be extracted.

5. The landscape of alternatives — how far each one gets

There are five branches. They differ in kind, so the question is not which is best but which column your situation is in.

5.1 Official viewers and office products

The highest fidelity comes from the product line that defined the format. That is simply true and is not a criticism of anyone: whoever defined the typesetting rules draws them most accurately. Distribution policy and supported platforms have shifted over time, though, so what is offered today is best checked directly at the source of distribution. This article does not pin down a product lineup at any given moment.

5.2 Web office suites and cloud viewers

Nothing to install, a browser is enough — that is the whole appeal. The trade is that the file leaves your machine. Confidential correspondence, rosters containing personal data and contracts uploaded this way easily breach organisational policy, and in an air-gapped environment the option does not exist at all. The convenience comes with a hard limit on which documents you may use it for.

5.3 Import filters in office suites

This is where people most often waste an afternoon. LibreOffice really does ship an HWP import filter named hwpfilter. Its effective coverage, however, is the Hangul 97 generation, that is the 3.x line. The HWP 5.0 family from 2002 onward, whose document structure changed substantially, is not its target. A request for versions above 3.x is open as tdf#90513, and the discussion about detecting and rejecting incompatible versions instead of quietly misreading them is tracked as tdf#70097. Assuming most files you receive today will not be solved here saves time.

5.4 Conversion and extraction libraries

When the goal is not a human reading the file but a program consuming it, this is often the right answer. On the Python side there is pyhwp for the HWP line and python-hwpx for HWPX; on the Rust side there are implementations such as hwp-rs and openhwp. That HWPX is ZIP plus XML matters enormously here, because standard XML tooling already covers a large part of the job. Layout will not be reproduced, but for pulling text, tables and metadata that is frequently all you need.

5.5 Open-source viewers and editors

This branch aims past reading and into drawing on screen and editing. It is the technically hardest one. Parsing is only the beginning; line breaking, letter spacing, table splitting and pagination all have to be rebuilt before the screen resembles the original. HOP and its engine rhwp live in this column.

6. What HOP is attempting — and why a desktop app

HOP is an open-source desktop application whose goal is viewing and editing HWP and HWPX documents on macOS, Windows and Linux. It is published under the MIT licence, and as checked on 16 August 2026 the latest release is v0.4.1, dated 21 July 2026.

If a browser-based viewer already exists, why build a separate desktop app? The answer is the contact surface with the operating system. There are things a web viewer structurally cannot do.

  • File associations — double-clicking a .hwp or .hwpx and having it open. A browser cannot occupy that slot.
  • OS preview integration — glancing at a file without opening it, the way Quick Look works on macOS. Community discussion singled this out as a benefit.
  • Drag and drop, multiple windows, printing — comparing documents side by side and using the operating system's own print stack.
  • Air-gapped environments — where files may not leave the network, a local application is the only option there is.

Installation paths are laid out as well. macOS has brew install hop, Arch-family distributions have an AUR package, and direct downloads are provided as .dmg, .msi, .deb, .rpm and .AppImage. macOS covers both Apple Silicon and Intel, and Linux has x64 and arm64 builds.

7. Look at the engine and the app separately — rhwp

The common mistake when evaluating HOP is treating the app and the engine as one lump. The actual work of interpreting and drawing the document is done by a separate project called rhwp, which HOP wraps and adds operating system integration to. You need that distinction to tell which side a problem belongs to.

7.1 What rhwp covers

rhwp is an HWP and HWPX viewer and editor written in Rust and WebAssembly. It is MIT licensed and stood at v0.8.4 when checked. On the reading side it handles the HWP 5.0 binary format, HWPX, and HML (HWPML 2.9/2.91) within a verified structural range. On the writing side it supports HWP, HWPX and HML with loss-safe saving as the principle: if the document contains elements it does not support, it warns and blocks the lossy save. That design matters to users. Saying it cannot and stopping protects a document better than quietly flattening it.

Editing features listed include text insertion and deletion, undo and redo, character and paragraph formatting, table creation and manipulation, and cell formulas. Rendering covers line spacing, indentation, alignment and tab stops, cell merging and border styles, multi-column splitting and row-level page splitting of tables. Export targets are SVG, PNG and PDF. Because it builds to WebAssembly, the same engine also runs in a browser.

7.2 The versions move independently

HOP v0.4.0 integrated rhwp 0.7.19. The engine has moved on since. In other words, something already fixed in the engine may not have reached the app yet. That is why, when you hit a problem, you should check the engine repository's current state and not only the app's.

7.3 One honest line

The rhwp documentation states, in effect, that a particular product's PDF output is not treated as the correct answer, because output varies with tool version and export path. It does not set byte-for-byte equivalence as a goal. I regard that stance as the most trustworthy signal in this field — far more accurate than advertising a compatibility percentage. Fidelity differs per document, and the number that applies to the documents you handle can only be learned from your own files.

8. What works and what does not (as checked 2026-08-16)

The 0.x version line already tells you the character of the project. The usable stretch and the not-yet stretch are clearly separated.

8.1 Listed as working in the repository

  • Opening HWP and HWPX documents
  • Save and Save As in HWP
  • PDF export
  • Print dialog
  • Opening files by drag and drop
  • File associations for .hwp and .hwpx
  • Multi-window document viewing

8.2 Absent from the list, or conditional

  • Saving to HWPX — not present in the list of working save features. Reading and writing are not symmetric.
  • Linux variability — AppImage is portable, run-when-needed distribution, and the notes warn that some Wayland and IME environments may show unstable language switching or window display.
  • Complex document fidelity — approval documents with overlapping text boxes, mixed equations and charts, and seal images are the hardest stretch for any independent implementation.
  • What 0.x means — a release note mentions fixing desktop editing regressions. That signals active repair, and equally that things have not settled.

8.3 So this is how you use it

  • Always keep a separate copy of the original before editing and saving. This is basic hygiene independent of any tool's reliability.
  • For important submissions, do the final check in the environment the receiving office uses.
  • For reading content, printing and producing a PDF, this is already a practical option today.
  • If you must edit a heavily formatted document and send it back, the original product line is still the safer route.
  • When you hit a document that breaks, filing a repository issue is the most effective contribution — but do not upload a real document containing personal data; build a minimal reproducible sample instead.

9. What could not be verified

Everything deliberately left unasserted, collected in one place.

  • Fidelity or compatibility figures for any of these tools. No published measurement was confirmed, and no estimate has been written in as a substitute.
  • Supported platforms and distribution policy for official viewers and office products as of August 2026. This has shifted over time, so the source of distribution is the accurate reference.
  • HOP's roadmap and timing for HWPX saving and autosave. Only its absence from the working feature list was confirmed.
  • Exception and grace provisions in the amended regulation, and per-agency rollout progress.
  • Legal judgements about copyright and licensing. What legal standing an independent implementation holds is not something this article decides. Where it matters commercially, get professional review.

10. Archiving or sending a document — how to choose

All of the tooling discussion exists to answer this one question, and the criterion collapses to a single line: who will open this document later, with what, and to do what?

10.1 Habits that never cost you

  • Never delete the original. If only the conversion survives, whatever was lost is lost permanently.
  • Give conversions their own filename. Putting the format and the date in the name rescues your future self.
  • Right after converting, eyeball the tables, equations, text boxes and seal images. The breakage tends to appear in the same places.
  • Before emailing, ask what the recipient runs. One line of asking is cheaper than resending.
  • For documents that circulate among several people, separate the editable copy from the distribution copy. PDF for distribution prevents accidents.
  • When zipping files, check filename encoding once — that trap is covered in the ZIP encoding problem that mangles Korean filenames — and if a server has to generate documents, designing the generation pipeline itself is easier to maintain than converting formats.

11. Conclusion: a format is a promise, not a tool

Choosing a document format is not like choosing an editor. You can swap an editor whenever you like, but a format is a promise made to everyone who will ever open that file. How many of them can keep it is what determines the format's value.

What open-source implementations change is not the format itself. It is the set of options. You can open an official letter on a Mac, batch-extract text on a Linux server, process files locally inside an air-gapped network. What they cannot change is equally clear: the fine detail of a layout engine, template habits frozen into institutions, and the plain reality of maintenance capacity. Handing an entire workflow to a 0.x project is still premature.

Three things are worth doing today. First, take one document type you handle often and confirm for yourself that it opens in your environment. Second, add a PDF or PDF/A copy to the important documents you are storing. Third, choose the format of anything you send based on the recipient's environment. Those three alone sharply reduce the odds that one attachment stops your day again.
