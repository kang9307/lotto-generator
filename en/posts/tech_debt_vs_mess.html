What Earns the Name Technical Debt - Telling Borrowed Time From Plain Mess

A four-question test built from Cunningham’s original definition, and the treatments that work on each side of the line

Aug 16, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: What the Words Technical Debt Do in a Planning Meeting

We have a lot of technical debt built up. If you have said that sentence in a quarterly planning meeting, you also know what happens next. Usually nothing. People nod, and the schedule comes out unchanged.

The standard explanation is that the decision-makers do not understand engineering. I read it the other way. The sentence borrows the form of an accounting term while filling in none of the fields that accounting requires. No principal. No interest rate. No maturity date. If a finance lead walked into a room, said we have liabilities, and walked out, nobody would take them seriously. Engineering organizations do exactly that every quarter.

The harder problem sits underneath. A large share of what gets called debt in that room was never borrowed at all. Nobody decided it, nobody knew the alternative, nobody wrote a repayment plan. That is not a loan. That is a mess. And loans and messes are handled differently. Collapse the names into one and the treatment collapses into one too.

What this article does is draw that line. It goes back to the original conditions of the metaphor, builds a four-question test, and separates what works from what does not on each side. The arithmetic of when cleanup pays for itself is a different subject, covered separately in When Does Refactoring Pay Back.

One boundary first. In this article, mess describes the state of code. It says nothing about the skill or the care of whoever wrote it. Most mess is produced by conditions rather than by individuals: a team that left without a handover, requirements reversed three times, a module whose owner disappeared. Naming a state accurately and judging a person are separate activities, and this article only does the first. No specific company or team is used as an example either.

1. The Original Metaphor - The Sentences Actually Written in 1992

Ward Cunningham was the first to attach the word debt to software. The source is a 1992 OOPSLA experience report titled The WyCash Portfolio Management System, a short document about building a bond portfolio management system in Smalltalk. The debt passage is one paragraph of it.

Shipping first time code is like going into debt. A little debt speeds development so long as it is paid back promptly with a rewrite. (…) The danger occurs when the debt is not repaid.
— Ward Cunningham, The WyCash Portfolio Management System (OOPSLA ’92 Experience Report)

Read the paragraph end to end and the center of gravity is obvious. It sits on repayment, not on borrowing. There is a conditional clause: so long as it is paid back promptly with a rewrite. Strip that clause and the sentence becomes debt makes you faster, which is precisely the reading the original was written to warn against.

One thing is already settled here. The debt metaphor is a metaphor that only holds if repayment is assumed. Calling a state debt when no repayment plan exists is importing half of the metaphor. And the half that gets imported is the convenient half: the borrower's responsibility disappears, and all that remains is the impression that this is something we can pay off someday.

2. The Misreading Cunningham Corrected Himself

In February 2009 Cunningham published a short video explaining the metaphor again. He recorded it after watching bloggers read it as a license to write code carelessly on purpose. In it he said this.

I’m never in favor of writing code poorly, but I am in favor of writing code to reflect your current understanding of a problem, even if that understanding is partial.
— Ward Cunningham, Debt Metaphor (2009)

In the same video he explained why the word came up at WyCash in the first place. Building an object model for financial instruments meant learning the domain while doing the work, and when what they learned did not make it into the code, they slowed down. That slowdown was the interest. In other words, the act he called repayment was not fixing mistakes. It was getting newly acquired understanding into the code.

This is where the common reading inverts. The debt Cunningham described is not badly written code. It is clean code that honestly reflected the understanding available at the time. As understanding grows, that code ages. The gap between the code and the current understanding is the interest.

Which flips the sequence. Code written as a mess is not debt; it is a state in which repayment is impossible. The instrument of repayment is refactoring, refactoring requires structure, and mess has no structure or contract to preserve. Write it badly now and pay it back later is not merely a misreading of the metaphor — it stands at the exact point where the metaphor stops working.

3. What Debt Requires - Borrowing, Interest, a Repayment Plan, and a Record

Financial debt is debt because the fields are filled in. How much was borrowed, what was pulled forward in exchange, what the rate is, when it comes due — all of it written down. Demand the same form of software and the fields map like this.

  • What was borrowed - what did the choice pull forward? A ship date, a window to validate the market, a contractual delivery. If nothing was pulled forward, nothing was borrowed
  • Interest - the extra cost paid on every change until it is repaid. Added time per item, files that must be opened together, manual checks bolted onto each release
  • A repayment plan - who undoes what, and by when. Leave one of the three blank and it is a hope, not a plan
  • A record - were the three above captured at the moment of the decision? A narrative reconstructed afterwards is not a record

In practice the fourth field is the strongest discriminator. Debt leaves a record. What was deferred, when, and why is sitting in an issue comment or a decision log. Mess leaves none, because nobody decided anything and there was nothing to write. Checking only this, on a codebase you have just inherited, gives you a rough ratio between the two states.

One more condition belongs here. Interest has to be countable. A cost you cannot count never reaches a negotiating table, and an item that never reaches the table is not actually being managed as debt. Because of this condition, much of what gets filed as code that will probably become a problem someday belongs on a watch list rather than a debt list.

4. What Is Not Debt

Several states get called debt without meeting the conditions. Each has a different cause, so each has a different treatment.

  • Ignorance - nobody knew a better design existed. Nothing was borrowed, nothing was pulled forward, and the cost arrived anyway. This is not a loan, it is a gap in learning, and the treatment is design review and reading habits rather than repayment
  • Carelessness - it was known and disregarded. In Fowler's terms this lands on the reckless side. What is missing here is the repayment plan
  • Abandonment - it was debt once. Then the person who decided it left, the record was lost, and the maturity date passed. What remains is not debt but the wreckage of debt. Nobody knows what the principal was
  • Absence of design - a boundary was never drawn. Having modules is not the same as having boundaries. If anything can call anything from anywhere, there is no boundary. This was not deferred, it never happened
  • Exploration - an attempt in territory where the answer is not yet known. This is not debt, it is research cost. Failing is not a fault, and the equivalent of repayment is leaving the result written down
  • Taste - written differently, not written wrongly. Zero interest means no debt. The moment this category enters a list, the credibility of the whole list drops

Building the list usually produces a surprise. In a backlog labelled technical debt, only a minority of items satisfy all four conditions. The rest is a blend of ignorance, abandonment, missing design and taste. That is why the list has not shrunk for several quarters. Different diseases were filed under one name, and one medicine was requested for all of them.

5. The Test - Four Questions

Take one item and ask four questions in order. The order matters: if the first answer is no, the rest are moot.

QuestionWhat it asksIf yesIf no
1. Was there intentWhen the choice was made, did anyone know another path existed and pick this oneGo to the nextNot debt — mess or exploration
2. Was the alternative knownWas the better design known at that momentGo to the nextIgnorance family — the treatment is learning and review, not repayment
3. Was there a plan to repayWas it fixed who undoes what, and by whenGo to the nextReckless debt — attach a maturity date first
4. Is the interest measurableCan you count today what each change costs extra until it is repaidThis is debtMove to the watch list — no negotiation before measurement

Run it for real and the verdicts fall into roughly four groups.

  • Yes to all four - this is debt. Manage the maturity date and report the balance each quarter. It is the easiest of all these states to handle
  • Yes to 1 and 2, no to 3 and 4 - borrowed, but with no plan. Attaching a maturity date now converts it back into debt. Leave it longer and it slides into abandonment
  • No to 1, but the code is clean - this is exactly the state Cunningham described. Right for the understanding of the time, outdated for the understanding of today. The treatment is refactoring
  • No to 1, and there is no structure - this is mess. It needs a different instrument than refactoring, which the next section covers

The third group is listed separately for a reason. It is the one case where cost accrues without anybody having done anything wrong. Every system built while learning its domain passes through it. In organizations that treat it as failure, nobody touches the early model any more, and then it really does rot.

6. What Disappears When You Use the Wrong Name

If this were only about semantic precision it would not be worth an article. It is worth one because the wrong name costs money. Here is what goes missing when mess gets called debt.

  • Ownership - debt has a borrower. Call mess debt and you get a debtor with no borrower behind it. When no debtor is identified, nobody repays
  • The repayment plan - the word debt implies this can be paid off eventually, so not having a plan does not feel alarming. In reality the principal was never even defined
  • The choice of treatment - refactoring works on debt and does not work on mess. One name yields one treatment, and you repeat a medicine that does not work
  • The estimate - debt has a defined scope, so it can be estimated. Mess has to be investigated first, which means the estimate is the investigation result. Request both in the same meeting in the same format and the second one overruns every time
  • The credibility of the word - if debt is raised every quarter and nothing ever changes, the phrase becomes that topic again to whoever approves budgets. You get the same reaction on the day you bring an item that genuinely came due

The last one is the most expensive. Vocabulary is consumable: once a word has lost its credit, you cannot win the argument again with the same word.

7. Mess Does Not Yield to Refactoring

Refactoring is changing internal structure while preserving observable behavior. The word preserving is doing the real work in that definition. To preserve something, you have to know what it is.

Code classified as debt usually carries that contract. It was deferred knowingly, so the line between intent and stopgap is visible, and some tests survive. Mess carries none of it. Nobody knows which behaviors are specification and which are accidents. Tests are missing, or too flaky to act as a safety net. Starting a refactor in that state means changing structure without knowing what to protect. That is why attempts to tidy up a mess keep ending as incidents.

7.1 Change the order

What works on mess is not cleaning but manufacturing a boundary. The sequence usually looks like this.

  1. Attach observability - use logs, traces and metrics to see what actually happens first. With no specification available, observation becomes the specification
  2. Draw a boundary - define what this lump exchanges with the outside world. One interface or one schema is often enough
  3. Isolate - make the outside stop knowing the inside. A translation layer keeps the outer model and the inner model apart
  4. Replace - move traffic onto the new path a slice at a time. Delete the old path after replacement finishes, and not before
  5. Freeze - for areas that are not replacement targets, decide explicitly not to touch them. For code with a short remaining life this is the cheapest available move

Cleanup arrives last in this sequence, and half the time it never arrives at all, because once replacement completes there is nothing left to clean. This is the most practical difference between the two states. Debt is eliminated by repaying it; mess is neutralized by surrounding it.

7.2 The temptation to rewrite

Faced with mess, a full rewrite looks like the only answer. The logic is not wrong: with no structure to preserve, starting over can genuinely be faster. The problem is exposure. A full rewrite produces no benefit at all until it completes, and if priorities shift or people leave in the meantime, everything invested vanishes. That is why incremental replacement stays the default even though it gives up total return. The arithmetic of that trade is in the earlier article.

9. How to Say It Inside an Organization

Give us time to pay down technical debt fails for three reasons.

  • The form is empty - an accounting word arrives with no principal, no rate, no maturity. The listener has nothing to decide with
  • Nothing shows on screen - from the approver's seat it is a period producing no visible output. The cost is certain and now, the benefit is later and probabilistic
  • There is no end - paying back implies an end, and the work has none. Credibility erodes starting with the second request

There is one direction to move in. Stop describing a state with adjectives and start describing flow with counts. In the right column below, the capital letters are values your own organization has to count and fill in. Borrow another organization's averages and the sentence turns back into an adjective.

What does not landWhat turns into a budget conversation
Our technical debt is seriousThis module takes N changes per quarter, and an average of M files open together per change
The code is a messThis area has no owner, and J of the last K incidents originated here
We need time to refactorDrawing one boundary before the next feature shortens that work by D days
This will blow up eventuallyChange fail rate is X in this module and Y elsewhere, and the gap has widened for three quarters
We need a full rewriteAll at once exposes A person-days at peak; split up, it is B person-days

One more thing. Attaching the request to the next feature has the highest approval rate by a wide margin. A standalone cleanup window is a period with no deliverable, while cleanup attached to a feature becomes a line item that reduces that feature's estimate. It is also checkable: if you said it saves days, look at the number again next quarter.

10. Measurement - What Reveals the Difference

Debt and mess differ in the shape of the metrics, not in their absolute levels. What differs is distribution and trend. The first three rows below are delivery metrics as DORA defines them; the last two are counted inside your own organization.

MetricDefinition / sourceIf it is debtIf it is mess
Change lead timeTime from a commit entering version control to being deployed to production (DORA)Long only in specific areasLong regardless of area
Change fail rateShare of deployments requiring immediate intervention right after deploy (DORA)Clusters in modules you already know aboutBreaks in places nobody touched
Deployment rework rateShare of unplanned deployments resulting from a production incident (DORA)Rises near maturity datesConstantly high with scattered causes
Files changed togetherNumber of files opened for a single change (counted from version control history)Broadly stableGrows quarter over quarter
Presence of a decision recordShare of items where the reason for deferring is still written downPresentAlmost never present

The fourth row is the most practically useful metric in this article. The trend in files changed together is the earliest signal that boundaries are eroding. If the number of files you open to change one value keeps rising, the code did not get dirtier — the boundaries are disappearing. And boundary problems do not respond to cleanup.

Two cautions. First, DORA explicitly names goal-setting on metrics and Goodhart's law as risks. Set them as targets and the work of manufacturing numbers begins. Second, files changed together is not a DORA metric. It is counted directly from version control history, and a single bulk formatting commit will distort the whole ranking, so filter those out first.

Measurement exists for conversation, not evaluation. Measure the same way before and after an intervention and confirm the direction, nothing more. For the regime where understanding itself becomes the dominant cost, see The Shifting Economics of Code.

11. The Counterargument, and What I Could Not Verify

There is a position pointing the other way, and it has support behind it.

Martin Fowler published the technical debt quadrant in 2009. One axis runs reckless to prudent, the other deliberate to inadvertent. Messy code produced by people who did not know better (reckless and inadvertent) and designs whose flaws only became visible after building them (prudent and inadvertent) both sit inside the quadrant. Rather than litigating what counts as debt, he put the weight on the metaphor's usefulness for discussing design problems between technical and non-technical people.

Nat Bennett's piece, the starting point for this discussion, stands on the other side. He argues that the phrase technical debt creates the false impression that the cost was known in advance, and proposes that comparing software to a workspace — something you keep tidying and reorganizing — is more accurate.

The two positions actually serve different goals. Fowler's concern is a tool for conversation; Bennett's is diagnostic accuracy. So the judgement here is to separate the layers. Split the names when you diagnose, and drop the word debt entirely when you talk budget, replacing it with frequency and failure rate. What an approver needs is a number rather than a metaphor, so choosing the right metaphor and getting the work approved were never the same problem.

What I could not verify

  • Figures claiming technical debt consumes some share of development time - these are widely quoted, but tracing them usually lands on vendor surveys. I could not confirm a primary source with published sample and definitions, so no such figure appears here
  • The real distribution across the quadrant - I found no trustworthy survey of how field items actually split between debt and mess. Where the text says a minority, that is a judgement rather than a measured value
  • A verbatim transcript of the Cunningham video - the quoted sentence is limited to the portion that appears identically across multiple written records of the talk. For sentence order and surrounding context, the original video is the better reference
  • How much drawing a boundary reduces files changed together - it varies by organization and I could not find published comparative data. That is why the example sentences leave placeholders rather than numbers

Conclusion: The Name Is a Diagnosis

The word debt is not the problem. The problem is that it gets used as an adjective in a place where a diagnosis belongs. A diagnosis determines a treatment. Collapse the names and the treatments collapse too, and you spend several quarters requesting a medicine that does not work.

Reduced to three sentences.

  • Debt requires something borrowed, interest, a repayment plan and a record. Without them it needs a different name
  • What Cunningham called debt is not bad code but good code carrying outdated understanding, which is why refactoring repays it
  • Mess is not repaid but surrounded. Observe, bound, isolate, replace — and cleanup comes last, or never

There is one thing to do today: open your team's technical debt backlog and run the four questions against each item. Usually more than half of the list moves into a different column. And the items that move change what you should be asking for. Not time to clean up, but an owner, a boundary, or observability. All three are cheaper than cleanup, and they get approved faster.
