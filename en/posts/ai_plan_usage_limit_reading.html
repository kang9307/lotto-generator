The Same 20x Is Measuring Different Things - How to Read AI Plan Usage Limits

A multiplier never prints its denominator. How to check whether the five-hour window or the weekly total got multiplied

Sep 2, 2026 Tech & IT

One pricing table says 20x. The pricing table next to it also says 20x. But one of those twenties multiplies how much you can use in five hours, and the other multiplies how much you can use in a week. The two numbers occupy the same cell on the page and are not measuring the same quantity at all.

This is usually where the complaint comes from after an upgrade: it runs out sooner than expected. The price went up fivefold, so the available volume must have gone up fivefold. In practice only one class of limit moved, and the other class stayed where it was or moved much less. And when two limits are live at once, the effective limit is whichever one you reach first.

This piece does not argue that any service is the better deal. Per-million-token pricing is covered in the 2026 AI API pricing comparison, the reason unit prices fall while the invoice does not is in token prices keep falling but your bill does not, and organisation-level control lives in controlling AI coding costs. What is covered here is one thing only: how to read a usage limit.

Every figure below was read directly from each vendor's own pricing page or help documentation on 2 September 2026. Anything that could not be confirmed there was left out. Plans and limits change without notice, and the value that actually applies to an account is the one shown in the product's own usage screen. Nothing here recommends a service or claims one is superior.

The trigger - two 20x claims saying different things

In late August 2026 a public remark by someone at an AI company circulated on a Korean developer community. The gist was that their own top tier's 20x really is twenty times on the weekly allowance too, whereas a competitor's 20x attaches only to a five-hour limit while the weekly cap rises far less. The follow-on point was that reading the price sheet alone makes it easy to assume weekly volume scales by the same ratio.

Adjudicating that claim is not this article's job. A remark from inside one company about a competitor is by nature one side of a ledger, and more importantly these numbers keep moving after the remark is made. So rather than repeating it, what follows is only what the vendors' own documentation actually said on 2 September 2026.

The nerve it touched, though, is real. A multiplier never prints its denominator. The string 20x does not say what got multiplied by twenty, and that information usually lives not in the pricing table but somewhere inside the help documentation.

Twenty times what, exactly

Open the official pages and the divergence is right there in plain text. Everything below was read on 2 September 2026.

Anthropic's Max plan help page describes Max 5x as five times more usage per session than the Pro plan, and Max 20x as twenty times more usage per session than the Pro plan. The same page states that the session-based usage limit resets every five hours. That is the full reach of the multiplier. Below it sits a separate sentence: Max plans also have a weekly usage limit that applies across all models, and that weekly limit resets at a fixed time each week assigned to the account. So the thing multiplied by twenty is the session, and the weekly cap is written as its own item rather than as part of that multiplication.

OpenAI's Codex pricing document is built differently. The plan columns are literally named Plus, Pro 5x, Pro 20x and Business, and the values in the table are estimated messages per five-hour window. Across three model tiers the Plus column reads 10 to 100, 25 to 200 and 250 to 2,000, while the Pro 20x column reads 200 to 2,000, 500 to 4,000 and 5,000 to 40,000. Work the arithmetic and the Pro 20x row is exactly twenty times the Plus row, with Pro 5x at five times. So in this table the twenty multiplies messages per five-hour window. One sentence sits under the table: local messages and cloud chats share a five-hour window, and additional weekly limits may apply. The Business column carries the same values as Plus.

Cursor's pricing page puts twenty times the Pro agent limits on the description line of its top individual tier. The denominator there is the Pro tier's agent limit, and the Pro tier's actual limit is not printed on the same card. The tier below reads three times Pro, and the upper team seat reads five times the standard seat. There is a chain of multipliers, and no absolute number at the end of the chain.

GitHub Copilot does not use multiplier language at all. It prints a monthly credit allowance as an amount of money. That is a third species entirely: not a multiple, not a window, but how much you are allowed to burn in a month.

The conclusion here is not that one of these is better. It is that the same string is measuring different quantities, so setting two multipliers side by side is not a comparison at all. To compare 20x against 20x you would first have to convert both to a common denominator, and the values needed for that conversion are usually not published.

The practically important part is the sentence from earlier. When two classes of limit are live at once, the effective limit is whichever you reach first. If the window limit becomes twenty times larger and the weekly cap does not move, then what you feel after upgrading is not the limit that grew but the one that did not. Across a whole week the bottleneck has simply migrated from the window to the weekly cap.

The units limits are measured in

To find the denominator you first have to know the unit. Roughly five are in circulation, and each one feels different from the user's seat.

Message counts

The oldest and most intuitive unit. The problem is that one message can be anything. A one-line question and a request with ten attached files cannot honestly count as the same single message, so the documentation tends to print ranges instead. The 10 to 100 style figures seen earlier are exactly that. Planning against a number whose floor and ceiling differ by a factor of ten is difficult, which makes this unit easy to understand and close to useless for forecasting.

Tokens and credits

Tokens are the unit closest to actual cost, and the one a person cannot count in advance. So vendors wrap tokens in a layer called credits. The OpenAI pricing document states that credits translate token usage into a simpler unit and that the credit cost varies by model, context, reasoning and tools. GitHub goes one step further and pins credits to money: the documentation states that one AI credit equals 0.01 US dollars, and explains that input tokens, output tokens and cached tokens are priced per model and converted into credits.

Time windows, concurrency and model weights

A time window is not a quantity but an interval. A five-hour window only looks at consumption inside those five hours. A model weight is a coefficient that makes one identical request deduct a different amount depending on which model handled it. GitHub's older request-based billing documentation states that each model carries a multiplier and that the premium request allowance is deducted according to that multiplier, and notes that from 1 June 2026 the code review feature carries a multiplier of 13. One request counting as thirteen.

What a time window really does - burst and you hit it, spread and you do not

A window limit is the place where the felt experience runs opposite to a total cap. Miss that property and you cannot pick the right tier for yourself.

A five-hour window only cares how much you pushed into those five hours. Sit at the tool for more than ten hours a day and you may never touch it if the work is spread out. Cram a large job into three afternoon hours and you can hit it on a modest daily total. The same volume produces different outcomes depending on when it was placed.

A weekly total is precisely the reverse. It does not care when. Burst at dawn or trickle across seven days, the same sum is deducted. Which means the two limits are aimed at different kinds of person.

  • Short violent bursts — one or two large jobs a day, run in one block. This profile hits the window first. The weekly allowance is sitting untouched while the afternoon is blocked
  • Long steady daily use — frequent queries all day. This profile hits the weekly cap first. The week ends abruptly around Thursday
  • Long agent runs — one start, then tens of minutes of automated reading and editing. This profile hits both at once, and it is the hardest to forecast
  • Occasional use — neither limit is ever touched. Here the upper tier's multiplier is simply a number that never gets spent

One misreading comes up constantly. Hitting a window limit does not mean you have used up the plan's volume. It means you have used up those five hours. Hitting a weekly cap does mean you have genuinely used the week. The on-screen messages look similar, but the two events are different in kind and call for different responses. The first is solved by spreading work out; the second is not solved by spreading anything.

The documentation splits them too. Anthropic's usage guidance lists, as separate readouts, the current session, meaning how much of the five-hour session limit has been spent and how much time remains in it, and the weekly limits, meaning when the weekly allowance resets. And the weekly readout itself splits again into one for a specific upper model and one for all other models. Three live limits on a single account, at minimum.

Why each company measures differently

Not a difference of labelling habit. Instantaneous load and total consumption are two different costs.

Requests arriving at the same moment are a question of how much hardware has to be standing by. If everyone shows up at two in the afternoon, there must be enough capacity to survive that moment, and that capacity is paid for during the hours it sits idle. Tokens burned over a month are a question of electricity and time actually spent. The first is solved by flattening the peak, the second by capping the total. A time window handles the front, a total cap handles the back. Which is why one company often imposes both, and indeed both of the vendors above impose both.

The choice of unit splits along the same line. Message counts are easy for a person to picture and drift from real cost. Tokens and credits track cost and are hard to forecast. Either choice gives something up, which is why the same company sometimes runs both notations at once.

Money-pinned credits have one more reason behind them. GitHub's individual-plan documentation puts a flex allotment on top of the base credits and states that it is designed to adapt as the economics of AI evolve, including model pricing, new models and improvements in efficiency. In other words, a mechanism that lets the underlying cost move without rewriting the number on the pricing page. Seen from the user's side, that also means the label can stay identical while the amount you can actually buy shifts quietly.

Published numbers and unpublished ones

Open a handful of limit documents and something becomes obvious quickly. Far fewer cells carry absolute values than you would expect.

Anthropic's Max plan page prints the relative multipliers of five and twenty, states that the session resets every five hours, and states that a weekly limit exists. It does not say how many messages a session holds or what the weekly limit is. Instead the page carries a sentence of a familiar kind: to manage capacity and ensure fair access for all users, usage may be limited in other ways, such as weekly and monthly caps or model and feature usage, at the vendor's discretion. A discretion clause.

There is a settled way to read such wording. A sentence saying heavy usage may be limited does not mean there is no ceiling. It means there is one and it will not be published, and further that it is not a fixed number but something adjustable according to capacity conditions at the time. Anthropic did in fact announce on 6 May 2026 that it had doubled the five-hour limits on its coding tool and removed the peak-hours reduction. The vendor demonstrating from its own side that these values move.

The opposite case exists too. GitHub's individual-plan documentation prints each tier's monthly credits split into base and flex allotments, as plain numbers. With credits pinned to money, one multiplication gives you the monthly ceiling. What this side does not tell you is how long that will last. The same credits stretch a long way across light queries and vanish in a large agent session, and the documentation says so itself.

  • Only a ratio published — the denominator is unknown, so no cross-vendor comparison is possible. Usable only for comparing tiers within the same vendor
  • Published as a range — plan against the floor. The ceiling is the figure for the lightest possible work
  • Published as money — the cap is unambiguous but does not convert into time. Change the kind of work and the burn rate changes wholesale
  • Not published — check for a discretion clause, and if one is there, treat that tier's ceiling as provisional

What happens when you hit the limit

Hitting a limit is not one event. It splits at least four ways, and the four are entirely different experiences. Before choosing a tier, reading this column is worth more than reading the multiplier.

At the limitWhat happensWhat the user needsConfirmed instance
WaitThe feature is unavailable until the reset timeTo know the reset time, and something else to do until thenAnthropic's help centre offers waiting for the reset, upgrading, or buying usage credits
Grace on the running turnThe turn currently executing is allowed to finishConfidence that work will not be cut in halfThe OpenAI pricing document says the agent can keep working on that turn, subject to fair use limits

Metered overageNothing blocks; usage is billedA spending cap. Without one it is unboundedAnthropic states that with usage credits enabled, subsequent usage bills at standard API rates

The overage row deserves particular care. Half the reason for buying a subscription is that the invoice is predictable, and switching on metered overage removes exactly that property. Which is why the documentation pairs it with guard rails. Anthropic describes a prepaid arrangement with a ceiling on how much can be spent per day, and GitHub describes budgets with alerts at 75, 90 and 100 percent. Leave those switches off and choosing a tier stops meaning anything.

Downgrading is the quiet trap. Blocking is noticed instantly; downgrading is not. Output quality drops from some point onward and the reason can stay unidentified for days. Which model your tool falls back to at the limit, and whether it says so on screen, is something to check in advance.

Measuring your own usage

No amount of staring at a pricing table produces the right tier, because half the inputs are on your side of the table. Do you burst or spread, long sessions or short queries. Without those two facts you cannot tell whether the window or the total is your bottleneck.

Measuring is easy. One week of records is enough, and precision is not required. Two ratios do most of the work.

  • Peak share — what fraction of a day's consumption falls in that day's busiest five hours. High means the window is your bottleneck
  • Heaviest-day share — what fraction of the week's consumption falls on the heaviest single day. Low and flat means the weekly cap is your bottleneck
  • Session length spread — how many minutes a sitting lasts. Mostly under ten, or with hour-plus blocks mixed in
  • Task mix — the split between short queries, long document work and autonomous agent runs. The three burn at rates that differ by orders of magnitude
  • Model mix — how much of the work goes to the upper model. Where model weights apply, this single value overturns all the others

Record it by hand, or just screenshot the tool's usage panel twice a day. This much format is enough.

Date        Hours       Task type        Model     Session len  Notes
2026-09-01  09-14       short queries    light      ~5min x12   meeting prep
2026-09-01  14-19       agent run        upper      ~90min x2   refactor, 1 limit warning
2026-09-02  09-14       long doc work    upper      ~40min x3   -
2026-09-02  19-24       short queries    light      ~5min x6    -

To compute
  peak share          = (busiest 5 hours) / (that day total)
  heaviest-day share  = (heaviest day)    / (that week total)
  upper-model share   = (upper model use) / (total use)

Once a week of this exists the decision makes itself. High peak share means a tier that enlarges the window is worth paying for; low and flat means no window multiplier will be felt. And if both values are high, that means every tier will hit a limit, and the faster fix is not the tier but work scheduling and model routing.

Most services provide a usage panel. Anthropic states that the screen shows current session consumption, time remaining in the session, and the weekly reset time, and lists the factors affecting usage as message length, attachment size, current conversation length, tool use, model choice, effort level and artifact creation. Conversation length being on that list is worth dwelling on, because the same question asked at the end of a long thread carries everything before it along for the ride.

What has to line up in a comparison table

The five axes

Sit down to compare plans and the table usually breaks in its first row, because one cell holds messages per five-hour window, the next holds monthly credits, and the one after that holds a bare multiplier. Without aligning five axes to a common basis, the result is a table with numbers in it and no comparison.

  • Period — five hours, a day, a week, a month. The temptation is to convert window values into weekly ones, and that conversion is usually wrong, because when a separate weekly cap exists the window figure multiplied out is not what you can actually spend
  • Unit — messages, tokens, credits, money. They do not convert automatically. If the conversion factor is unpublished, leaving the cell empty is the accurate move
  • Model — limits split by model tier inside a single plan. Align upper with upper and light with light
  • Scope — do agent runs, code review, web search and attachments draw on the same allowance. Several products sometimes share one pool: Anthropic states that Pro and Max limits are shared across the conversational product and the coding tool, with all activity in both counting against the same limits
  • Behaviour at the limit — waiting, downgrading or billing. Where this cell differs, the four cells above can match and the products still differ

There is a useful check for the period axis. Write down the theoretical weekly figure derived from the window limit alone, next to the published weekly cap. The smaller of the two is the effective limit, and if the theoretical figure is far larger it means that tier's window multiplier is used less than advertised.

Theoretical weekly total from the window alone
  = (per-window limit) x (windows opened per day) x 7
  7 days x 24 hours / 5 hours = 33.6 windows   (continuous use; not realistic)

Effective weekly limit
  = min( theoretical window total , published weekly cap )

Reading it
  window total >> weekly cap   -> weekly cap is the bottleneck; a bigger window barely shows
  window total <= weekly cap   -> window is the bottleneck; spreading work out helps
  weekly cap not published      -> not computable; only measurement will tell you

The unit itself gets swapped out

This is the part of the article with the longest shelf life. It is not only the values that change. The unit changes.

GitHub Copilot is the worked example. It used to run on a unit called premium requests. Each plan included a monthly count, each model carried a multiplier, and once you ran out you could buy more at a set price per request. Then on 1 June 2026 request-based billing became usage-based billing. The current documentation states that the cost of an interaction is set by two things, the model and the number of tokens consumed. A rider notes that customers on an existing annual plan may stay on the old scheme until that plan ends.

What that shift means is simple. Every comparison table built before it became meaningless that day. The axis of so-many-requests-per-month vanished, so there is nothing left to set beside the next column. Here is what the current documentation prints.

The documentation puts one credit at 0.01 dollars. The allowance resets at midnight UTC on the first of each month, fixed regardless of the subscription billing date, and unused credits do not roll over. The detail worth noticing is that the ratio of price to credit value differs by tier: 15 dollars of credits on a 10 dollar plan, 70 on 39, 200 on 100. That is a multiplier of a sort as well, and nobody calls it 20x.

Values moved elsewhere in the same period. Anthropic announced on 6 May 2026 that for Pro, Max, Team and seat-based Enterprise it had doubled the coding tool's five-hour limits and removed the peak-hours reduction. Which is to say the multipliers and window lengths quoted in this article were different numbers a few months ago.

Where to look for the current numbers

Since the figures move like this, what the article can leave behind is not values but the places values live. In priority order.

  1. The product's own usage panel — the most accurate, because it is not the documented number but the number actually applied to your account. Session remainder, weekly reset time and credit balance are here
  2. The help centre or docs page about usage — not the pricing page. Pricing pages exist to sell, so they carry the multiplier; the denominator is in the help centre
  3. Announcements and release notes — when limits change this is usually where it surfaces first. The pricing page can lag by days
  4. Terms and fair use policy — home of the discretion clause. If a sentence there reserves the right to set ceilings at the vendor's discretion, every number above it is provisional
  5. Public remarks by staff and insiders — the fastest and the least reliable. It is a snapshot of a moment, and where a competitor is mentioned it is one side's view. Where it conflicts with documentation, documentation wins
  6. Community reports — the only route to felt experience, but measured on someone else's usage pattern. Read them for which profile hit which wall rather than for absolute numbers

And record the date you read it. That is why 2 September 2026 keeps reappearing through this piece. An undated limit figure is more dangerous than a wrong one. Wrong numbers get caught; undated ones get quoted indefinitely because nobody can tell when they expired.

Summary

The same 20x multiplies a five-hour window in one place, a weekly total in another, and an unnamed agent limit in a third. Whatever the multiplication left alone stays where it was, so wherever two limits are live, the one that did not grow becomes the effective limit. That is the usual mechanism behind an upgrade that fails to feel proportional.

So the thing to read off the table is not the multiplier but four facts: what the denominator is, what unit it is measured in, whether another limit runs alongside, and what happens when you reach it. With those four cells filled the multiplier becomes incidental information. Without them, no multiplier is large enough to support a decision.

And measuring your own pattern before choosing a tier is the correct order of operations, because the same plan behaves in opposite ways for a burster and a spreader. A week of records is enough, and those records survive every plan change. What changes is the vendor's numbers; your own usage pattern changes far more slowly. If you also want a sense of the underlying spend, running the same workload through the LLM API price calculator on a metered basis is one way to see it.
