How to Back Up Naver Blog and Tistory Posts with ExitPress: A Complete Guide

Every Post Deserves an Exit: Breaking Free from Vendor Lock-in and Owning Your Content in Markdown and MDX

1. Vendor Lock-in and the Crisis of Digital Content Ownership

Anyone who has maintained a technical blog on commercial blogging platforms for several years has likely experienced a lingering sense of vulnerability. Whenever a major portal adjusts its internal search ranking algorithms, hundreds or thousands of meticulously crafted technical articles can suddenly vanish from search results. Furthermore, arbitrary alterations to platform terms of service, layout templates, ad placement rules, and mobile rendering engines frequently disrupt the presentation of authors' intellectual work. In the Korean web ecosystem, Naver Blog and Kakao Tistory have served as the foundational digital homes where countless software engineers, domain experts, and prolific writers have deposited their knowledge. Paradoxically, however, the vast volumes of content accumulated on these services remain tightly locked inside closed, proprietary databases.

In software architecture, this dilemma is formally characterized as Vendor Lock-in. While individual creators retain legal copyright over their writings, they lack genuine technical ownership if the host platform fails to provide automated export APIs or machine-readable backup facilities. Without standardized data portability, authors lose effective sovereignty over how their writing is formatted, migrated, or permanently archived. When faced with the daunting prospect of manually copying thousands of individual articles, re-uploading embedded screenshots, and reformatting code blocks by hand, most bloggers abandon the idea of migrating to an independent platform and reluctantly settle within the platform's walled garden.

The Barrier of Isolated Data and Fragmented Markup

Articles published on legacy portal platforms are typically rendered using non-standard HTML markup, proprietary CSS classes, and idiosyncratic JSON structures generated by platform-specific rich text editors. While these formats function smoothly within the host service's web reader, extracting them into generic external tools inevitably triggers severe layout breakages, missing image references, and lost typographical hierarchies. Software developers and technical writers, in particular, face immense friction when attempting to consolidate legacy troubleshooting logs, architectural sketches, and code snippets into modern personal knowledge bases such as Obsidian or documentation frameworks built on GitHub Pages.

Genuine content ownership requires far more than merely having one's name displayed as the post's author on a corporate webpage. It demands the uncompromised ability to extract complete raw texts, embedded graphics, publication timestamps, category hierarchies, and cross-document hyperlink graphs into open standards, storing them safely on local disks. True data sovereignty ensures that even if a commercial service terminates operations tomorrow or unilaterally amends account policies, the creator's intellectual portfolio remains intact, fully portable, and easily reconstructible anywhere.

Thousands of Articles in Need of an Exit Strategy

Technical knowledge compounds in value over time. An engineering log or in-depth post-mortem drafted five or ten years ago often continues to provide critical insights today. In the emerging era of personalized artificial intelligence, historical blog archives can serve as rich domain corpora for Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) engines or fine-tuning datasets. However, when these valuable assets are trapped within closed silos—accessible only through limited RSS feeds capping output at fifty recent entries—creators face an existential risk where years of intellectual effort could become inaccessible at the whim of corporate decisions.

To confront this structural vulnerability, the open-source community created ExitPress. Built around the unambiguous principle that "Every post deserves an exit," this utility delivers a robust, automated pipeline designed to liberate trapped articles from proprietary Korean blogging platforms, transforming them into clean, standardized Markdown and MDX documents ready for modern publishing stacks.

2. ExitPress Overview and Development Philosophy

ExitPress is an open-source data liberation tool that systematically scans public blog entries and exports them as standardized Markdown or MDX documents, complete with YAML frontmatter, localized media assets, and a fully recoverable manifest.json metadata catalog. Unlike third-party cloud aggregators or paid subscription scrapers that require users to surrender account credentials, ExitPress operates entirely as a local utility executing directly on the user's workstation. This architecture ensures complete privacy and zero data interception throughout the migration process.

The genesis of ExitPress lies in the firsthand frustration of its creator, open-source engineer mym0404. Having authored over 2,000 comprehensive posts on Naver Blog over many years, the developer realized with growing alarm that the platform provided no native mechanism to aggregate, structure, or archive those historical records locally. Faced with the impossible hurdle of manually preserving more than two thousand articles, the author chose to build an engineering solution from scratch, giving rise to an enterprise-grade migration pipeline for the broader community.

Structural AST-Like Block Abstraction

Basic HTML-to-Markdown scrapers and simple Python script converters have existed for years, but they invariably fail when confronted with complex portal ecosystems. Naver Blog and Tistory do not serve simple static text; their output is an intricate mixture of nested inline spans, dynamic component wrappers, photo grids, interactive code frames, sticker embeds, and callout containers. Generic parsers inevitably mangle indentation, erase code syntax formatting, or separate image captions from their parent pictures, rendering the exported markdown practically unusable without extensive manual editing.

ExitPress solves this fundamental parsing problem through an intermediate Block Abstraction Layer. Regardless of whether the source material originates from Naver Blog's proprietary editors or Tistory's HTML containers, the ingestion engine decomposes raw markup into standardized, semantic building blocks. These intermediate elements are subsequently assembled into chosen output specifications (GFM, Fumadocs, Docusaurus, or Nextra) via modular template engines. This N×M decoupling architecture guarantees that expanding support for new input formats or output generators does not degrade core parsing reliability.

Open Source Transparency and Reconstructible Archives

Released under the permissive MIT License, ExitPress guarantees total transparency, inviting community members to review its codebase, audit security parameters, and adapt its parser logic for specific blogging quirks. A standout technical feature of its export cycle is the automated generation of manifest.json. This machine-readable file meticulously records source post identifiers, canonical URLs, original publication dates, category mappings, and cryptographic hashes linking online media assets to their local disk paths.

This design elevates the export process from a fragile one-way text dump into a durable, reconstructible archive. Should a developer later wish to ingest these historical articles into a SQL database, a headless CMS, or a newly released static site engine, the structured manifest.json catalog provides an immediate foundation for automated transformation scripts without needing to re-parse the raw markdown files.

3. Supported Input Platforms and Core Constraints

ExitPress offers dedicated, fine-tuned parsing support for the two dominant platforms representing the bulk of the Korean blogosphere: Naver Blog and Tistory. Because both services span nearly two decades of operational history, each maintains articles written across multiple generations of rich text editors. A viable migration tool must accurately identify and handle these historical markup divergences.

For Naver Blog, ExitPress supports legacy SmartEditor 2.0 (SE2), the mobile-first SmartEditor 3.0 (SE3), and the modern flagship SmartEditor ONE (SE4). For Tistory, it parses all publicly accessible web posts, accommodating both classic HTML styling modes and newer block-based editor layouts.

Bridging the Gap Across Editor Generations

The markup divergence across Naver Blog's editor versions is profound. Articles created under SE2 in the late 2000s are characterized by heavily nested <p>, <font>, and inline <span> tags, often embedding images inside complex multi-column table layouts. In contrast, SE3 and SmartEditor ONE rely on structured class-based components such as se-component and se-text-paragraph, separating captions, alignment attributes, and image groupings into discrete DOM subtrees.

ExitPress accommodates these disparities by employing specialized parser pipelines tailored to each editor family. It strips away nested presentation wrappers, extracts semantic headers (H1 through H4), normalizes unordered and ordered lists, and standardizes blockquote elements. Consequently, an engineering author whose blogging journey spans fifteen years across different editor releases obtains unified, cohesive Markdown outputs across their entire catalog.

★Critical Technical Constraint: Public Posts Only

Before deploying ExitPress, users must understand its most essential architectural boundary: ExitPress currently collects publicly accessible blog posts only.

The utility deliberately avoids intercepting browser session cookies, storing user passwords, or emulating authenticated OAuth sessions. It strictly operates by fetching and analyzing publicly reachable HTTP endpoints accessible to standard web clients. As a direct consequence, several categories of content cannot be retrieved by the scanner:

  • Private posts configured to be visible only to the blog owner
  • Restricted posts accessible solely to mutual neighbors or designated friend groups
  • Password-protected or draft entries stored on Tistory
  • Articles gated behind portal authentication or mandatory adult age-verification checks
  • Content situated within hidden categories whose index endpoints are inaccessible to unauthenticated visitors

Users seeking to back up personal diaries, financial records, or private notes must recognize that ExitPress will not see or download those entries. To migrate private entries using ExitPress, authors must temporarily change their visibility to public during the scan and export window, or alternatively resort to manual copy-paste workflows for sensitive entries.

4. Comparing the Four Markdown and MDX Output Adapters

A frequent pitfall in static site migrations occurs when exported files fail to adhere to the routing or metadata requirements of modern publishing frameworks. If folder layouts, frontmatter keys, or asset references deviate from framework conventions, the site will fail to compile. ExitPress directly mitigates this friction by supplying four purpose-built output adapters conforming to leading web documentation systems.

During the export stage, users select their desired target adapter via the graphical interface, allowing the engine to organize directories, generate navigation configuration files, and structure frontmatter metadata automatically.

Local Archiving via GFM vs. Modern Documentation Stacks

The primary adapter, GFM (GitHub Flavored Markdown), outputs classic index.md files nested inside clean category and post folders. Because GFM avoids custom JSX components or specialized React runtime imports, it represents the purest archival format. It is ideally suited for local knowledge managers such as Obsidian or Logseq, bare GitHub repository wikis, or bulk imports into Notion workspaces.

The remaining three adapters generate MDX (Markdown with JSX), a format enabling dynamic component embedding within static content. Crucially, these adapters do not just rename file extensions to .mdx; they automatically generate the configuration files required by each framework's routing and sidebar engines:

  • GFM: Emits category/post-folder/index.md, optimized for clean text readability and universal local tool compatibility
  • Fumadocs: Generates content/docs/**/index.mdx alongside category configuration files named meta.json, directing static assets to public/ for Next.js documentation setups
  • Docusaurus: Emits docs/**/index.mdx accompanied by hierarchical _category_.json manifests, saving images into the framework's standard static/ directory
  • Nextra: Generates content/**/index.mdx with matching _meta.js routing descriptors, placing assets in public/ for modern App Router deployments

Non-Destructive Output Isolation

A notable architectural virtue of ExitPress is its non-destructive isolation. The tool never overwrites an existing codebase or alters project configuration files such as docusaurus.config.js or next.config.mjs. Instead, all outputs are neatly segregated within a dedicated output/ directory on the user's filesystem.

When migrating into an existing Docusaurus or Nextra repository, developers simply copy the generated docs/ or content/ folder and the companion asset directories (static/ or public/) directly into their project tree. To avoid web routing issues with special characters, file paths and directory slugs can be ASCII-encoded, while display titles, category breadcrumbs, and frontmatter metadata retain their original Korean characters without distortion.

5. Asset Preservation and Link Management: Media and Backlinks

Seasoned engineers who have undertaken manual blog migrations know that preserving body text is only half the battle. The most severe technical failures typically center around broken media hotlinks and severed internal backlink graphs. If exported markdown documents continue pointing to original portal CDN URLs, any future anti-hotlinking policy enforced by the portal host will immediately cause all images to break. Furthermore, if cross-references within series articles continue pointing to old portal URLs, visitors arriving at the new blog will be routed back to the old platform upon clicking an internal reference.

To eliminate these hazards, ExitPress incorporates a dedicated media management pipeline alongside an automated backlink rewrite engine.

Three Flexible Image Processing Strategies

Because storage budgets and infrastructure preferences differ across engineering teams, ExitPress offers three distinct strategies for handling embedded media:

  1. Retain Remote URLs: Leaves original Naver or Tistory image URLs untouched within markdown documents. While this requires zero local disk storage and executes rapidly, it leaves the archive vulnerable to external CDN blocking or image deletion.
  2. Local Download and Compression: Downloads all embedded media to the local disk, compresses files for web efficiency, and rewrites markdown image tags to relative local paths (e.g., ./assets/screenshot.webp). This achieves complete, self-contained offline preservation.
  3. PicList Custom Storage Upload: Integrates with the open-source PicList ecosystem (powered by the PicGo engine) to upload compressed assets directly to custom object storage—such as AWS S3, Cloudflare R2, or GitHub Releases—automatically rewriting markdown image tags to point to the new CDN endpoints.

Intelligent Media Deduplication

Across extensive blog archives, authors routinely reuse identical visual assets, such as series header banners, author signatures, or category thumbnail graphics. Standard crawling utilities often download these identical images repeatedly for every single article, resulting in thousands of duplicate files consuming gigabytes of unnecessary disk space and inflating transfer bandwidth.

ExitPress addresses this inefficiency by hashing downloaded binary assets and checking them against a local deduplication registry. If an incoming image matches an asset already stored during the migration run, the utility skips redundant network downloads and storage uploads, linking the markdown reference directly to the existing local asset. This optimization significantly reduces disk consumption and dramatically accelerates migration times for extensive archives.

Preserving Internal Backlink Topology

Prolific technical bloggers frequently reference their own earlier publications (e.g., "See Part 1 for asynchronous fundamentals"). If internal URLs formatted as blog.naver.com/user/postNumber remain unaltered, the coherence of the author's internal knowledge graph is destroyed upon moving to an independent site.

ExitPress maintains an in-memory mapping table cataloging each post's original ID, publication date, title, and target slug throughout the scanning cycle. When the parser encounters an internal hyperlink pointing to another article within the same blog, it dynamically translates the URL into the new site's address format (such as https://myblog.dev/docs/...) or a relative filesystem path (../category/post-slug.md). The intricate web of contextual knowledge accumulated over years remains fully functional on the new domain.

6. Installation and Execution: From mise to Local Web UI

Unlike single-file Python scripts or compiled binary executables, ExitPress is structured as a TypeScript monorepo operating within the Node.js ecosystem. For creators without extensive terminal experience, setting up runtime prerequisites may initially appear slightly intimidating.

However, because the project standardizes its runtime dependencies using mise, following the official setup instructions allows users on Windows, macOS, or Linux to establish a working local environment in minutes.

Prerequisites and Environment Provisioning with mise

The primary prerequisite for building and executing ExitPress is the mise development environment tool. Configured via the repository's mise.toml file, mise automatically provisions the required versions of the Node.js runtime and the pnpm package manager without polluting global operating system paths.

Open your operating system terminal (PowerShell on Windows, or zsh/bash on macOS and Linux) and run the following command sequence to clone the codebase and prepare dependencies:

git clone https://github.com/mym0404/exitpress.git
cd exitpress
mise trust
mise install
pnpm install

Here, mise trust approves the repository's local configuration, mise install sets up the exact Node.js and tool versions specified by the project, and pnpm install retrieves all monorepo package dependencies across core parsers, UI modules, and output adapters.

Launching the Local Web Server and the Six-Step Migration Workflow

Once installation finishes, launch the local migration dashboard with a single command:

pnpm start

Upon launch, the terminal displays the active local server address. Open your web browser and navigate to http://localhost:4173. You will be greeted by a polished graphical dashboard that replaces complicated CLI flags with an intuitive six-step migration workflow:

  1. Select Blog Platform: Choose either Naver Blog or Tistory using the platform selector.
  2. Enter Blog ID or URL: Type your public blog identifier (username) or the full blog domain address into the input field.
  3. Scan Public Posts: Click the scan button to asynchronously fetch the index of published articles and category hierarchies.
  4. Select Categories and Date Ranges: Review discovered categories, select specific topics for export, or filter posts by date range.
  5. Execute Export: Select your preferred output adapter (GFM, Fumadocs, Docusaurus, or Nextra), configure media handling preferences, and trigger the export process.
  6. Inspect Output Directory: Once the progress bar finishes, navigate to the output/ folder inside the project root to access your organized Markdown documents and assets.

For contributors interested in inspecting the documentation website or exploring the component parsing demo, ExitPress provides auxiliary Vite preview commands:

mise exec -- pnpm --filter @exitpress/web build:pages
mise exec -- pnpm --filter @exitpress/web exec vite preview --config vite.website.config.ts

By abstracting asynchronous networking, DOM manipulation, and file serialization behind a local web UI, ExitPress makes industrial-strength data extraction accessible to users of all technical backgrounds.

7. Rule-Based Parser and AI Agent Continuous Improvement

The greatest challenge in developing web scrapers is the infinite variety of messy markup found in the wild. Bloggers employ idiosyncratic font formatting, legacy portal widgets that were phased out years ago, or raw HTML snippets pasted from external editors. An unexpected DOM structure can cause naive parsers to throw unhandled exceptions or silently truncate content.

ExitPress overcomes this barrier through an innovative development loop that pairs a deterministic rule-based parser with automated AI coding agent workflows.

Deterministic Precision Across Hundreds of Parsing Rules

Rather than relying on non-deterministic LLM text generation to rewrite raw web pages into markdown, ExitPress utilizes a rigorous, rule-based parsing core. This ensures zero API token costs, blazingly fast throughput handling dozens of articles per second, and absolute fidelity to the source text.

Every HTML block—from multi-column image carousels to syntax-highlighted code containers—is evaluated against comprehensive rule sets. Table cell line breaks, blockquote styles, callout banners, and mathematical notations are systematically translated into clean semantic markdown without hallucinated words or corrupted punctuation.

Contributing Parsing Fixes via AI Agent Workflows

If an author encounters a post featuring an uncommon widget or custom portal sticker that fails to parse cleanly, ExitPress provides an automated contribution mechanism leveraging modern AI agents. As detailed in the project's official README, the contribution workflow proceeds as follows:

  1. Fork the official ExitPress GitHub repository to your personal account.
  2. Install the high-speed JavaScript runtime bun and verify its availability:
bun --version
  1. Instruct your coding AI agent to execute the ingest skill by providing your blog identifier:
$ingest-blog {내 블로그 id}

This command activates the agent skill located at .agents/skills/ingest-blog. The AI agent scans the specified blog, isolates the DOM elements that the current parser failed to translate, and structures those failure cases into reproducible test fixtures. The agent then writes the necessary parsing logic, validates code coverage to prevent regressions, and automatically submits a well-documented Pull Request (PR) to the upstream repository.

Human maintainers review the generated PRs, fine-tune export options, and merge verified improvements into main releases. Through this feedback loop, every edge case encountered by an individual user continuously strengthens the parser engine for the entire community.

8. Technical Limitations and Migration Best Practices

Successfully extracting an archive into pristine Markdown and local assets with ExitPress is an essential milestone, but deploying those files to an independent platform (such as GitHub Pages, Vercel, or Cloudflare Pages) requires careful attention to ongoing operational realities.

Failing to account for search engine dynamics, canonical indexing, and social engagement data can result in severe traffic loss or search penalties.

Mitigating Search Engine Duplicate Content Penalties

The foremost technical risk during blog migration is search engine duplicate content penalties. If thousands of legacy articles remain actively indexed on Naver Blog while identical texts are uploaded simultaneously to a brand-new independent domain, search crawlers like Googlebot may flag the new domain as an unauthorized scraper or low-quality mirror, suppressing its visibility.

In standard web publishing, authors resolve this by inserting a <link rel="canonical" href="new-url"> tag into the legacy page header, instructing search crawlers that authority has transferred to the new location. However, closed portal platforms like Naver Blog strictly forbid users from modifying raw HTML <head> tags, rendering canonical link injection technically impossible.

To navigate this constraint, creators should adopt a phased migration strategy:

  • Publish articles to the new independent blog, monitor search indexation, and progressively replace old portal post bodies with brief summary abstracts directing visitors to the new site via clean outbound links
  • Begin migrating older, evergreen reference articles that receive minimal real-time search traffic, switching the original portal entries to private mode as new pages index successfully
  • Maintain the legacy blog as a broad discovery funnel while treating the new independent site as the primary authoritative repository for technical documentation

The Inevitable Severing of Social Engagement Data

While ExitPress faithfully reconstructs editorial content, images, dates, and categories, it cannot migrate proprietary social engagement metrics. Years of reader comments, threaded discussions, portal likes, scrap counts, and cumulative view counters reside exclusively in proprietary database tables and cannot be exported into markdown frontmatter.

Furthermore, creators unaccustomed to modern web tooling must be prepared to manage custom domain DNS records, configure static site deployment pipelines, and integrate independent commenting solutions (such as Giscus, Utterances, or Disqus). Despite these operational overheads, the ability to store one's intellectual output as portable text files on a local drive provides an invaluable foundation for enduring digital independence.
