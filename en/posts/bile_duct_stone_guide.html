Bile Duct Stones (Choledocholithiasis): A Complete Guide

Symptoms, Causes, Diagnosis, Treatment & Recurrence Prevention — How It Differs From Gallstones, Emergency Signs, and ERCP (As of July 2026)

2026-07-09 Health & Wellness

Introduction: A Stone in the Duct, Not the Gallbladder

Bile duct stones, medically known as choledocholithiasis, refer to stones that form in — or migrate into and lodge within — the bile duct itself, particularly the common bile duct that connects the liver to the small intestine, rather than the gallbladder. The name resembles 'gallstones' and the two are easily confused, but the location of the stone changes everything about the risk involved. Gallstones can sit quietly inside the gallbladder for years without incident, but a bile duct stone blocks the single channel through which all bile must flow, making it far more likely to progress to jaundice, fever, and acute cholangitis — a genuine medical emergency.

According to NIH StatPearls and related sources, roughly 1–15% of patients with gallstones also have bile duct stones. Most are 'secondary' stones that formed in the gallbladder and migrated into the duct, while a smaller subset are 'primary' stones that form directly within the bile duct itself. This guide, updated as of July 2026, draws on international clinical guidelines and reputable medical sources to cover the definition, causes, symptoms, complications, diagnosis, treatment, and recurrence prevention of bile duct stones. This article is for general information only — individual diagnosis and treatment decisions must be made with a qualified gastroenterologist.

1. What Are Bile Duct Stones — How Do They Differ From Gallstones?

1.1 Definition and Location

A bile duct stone is a stone lodged within the bile duct — the channel through which bile made in the liver flows toward the small intestine — most commonly the common bile duct. Like gallstones, these stones are composed mainly of cholesterol or bilirubin (pigment). When a stone blocks the duct, bile backs up toward the liver, causing jaundice and abnormal liver function.

1.2 Secondary vs. Primary Bile Duct Stones

Clinically, bile duct stones fall into two categories. Secondary bile duct stones — the vast majority — form in the gallbladder and travel through the cystic duct into the common bile duct. Primary bile duct stones are a rarer category that form directly within the duct itself, typically in settings of long-standing bile duct strictures, biliary infection, or chronic bile stasis, where a structural problem in the duct is often the underlying cause.

1.3 The Key Difference From Gallstones

Gallstones that remain inside the gallbladder are usually asymptomatic, and even when biliary colic occurs, it is typically a self-limited attack of pain that resolves within hours. Bile duct stones, in contrast, obstruct the single pathway through which bile must pass, so if left untreated they carry a real risk of rapidly progressing to jaundice, cholangitis, or pancreatitis. In short: the same word 'stone' carries a very different level of urgency depending on where it sits.

2. Causes and Risk Factors

2.1 A History of Gallstones

The single largest and most common risk factor is pre-existing gallstone disease. Stones that form in the gallbladder can travel through the cystic duct into the common bile duct, becoming secondary bile duct stones. Even patients who have already had a cholecystectomy can develop bile duct stones later — small stones missed during surgery can remain (so-called 'retained stones') and surface as bile duct stones afterward.

2.2 Older Age

Bile duct stones are reported more frequently with advancing age. This is thought to reflect the cumulative opportunity, over many years, for stones to gradually migrate into the duct, along with an age-related decline in bile duct motility and drainage function.

2.3 Bile Duct Abnormalities — Strictures, Diverticula, and Anatomical Changes

Bile duct strictures, periampullary diverticula near the duodenum, and anatomical changes from prior biliary surgery are cited as factors that slow bile flow and promote stone formation or stasis. When such structural issues are present, the risk of primary or recurrent bile duct stones is reported to be relatively higher.

3. Symptoms — From No Symptoms to Jaundice

3.1 Many Cases Are Asymptomatic

Bile duct stones, too, can exist without symptoms as long as the duct is not completely blocked. It is not uncommon for them to be discovered incidentally during a routine health check or imaging study performed for another reason.

3.2 Typical Symptoms

When symptoms do occur, they typically include the following.

  • Right upper quadrant or epigastric pain: ranging from mild to severe, and either persistent or intermittent.
  • Jaundice: as bile flow is blocked, bilirubin accumulates in the blood, turning the skin and whites of the eyes yellow.
  • Dark urine and pale stools: bilirubin excreted in urine darkens it, while stools lose their normal color.
  • Nausea and vomiting, a rapid heartbeat, and in severe cases a drop in blood pressure can accompany these symptoms.
  • Fever and chills accompanying these symptoms should raise suspicion of superimposed bacterial infection — cholangitis.

3.3 How the Pain Differs From Ordinary Biliary Colic

Biliary colic from gallstones classically appears suddenly after a fatty meal and then subsides within hours, whereas pain from bile duct stones tends to be more persistent, or to occur together with jaundice and fever, depending on the degree of obstruction. That said, symptoms alone cannot reliably distinguish the two conditions, so imaging confirmation is necessary.

4. Complications — Cholangitis and Pancreatitis, a Race Against Time

4.1 Acute Cholangitis — Charcot's Triad and Reynolds' Pentad

The most serious complication of bile duct stones is acute cholangitis. When the duct is obstructed, bacteria can multiply and spread infection and inflammation throughout the biliary tree; NIH sources describe this as a 'life-threatening ascending bacterial infection' and identify bile duct stones as its most common cause. Charcot's triad — fever, right upper quadrant pain, and jaundice — is the classic sign of cholangitis, but in practice its specificity (95.9%) is high while its sensitivity (26.4%) is low, meaning cholangitis cannot be ruled out just because all three are not present. When altered mental status and septic shock are added to the triad, it is called Reynolds' pentad, signaling that the condition has already become critical.

4.2 Gallstone Pancreatitis

If a bile duct stone obstructs the ampulla of Vater — where the bile duct and pancreatic duct meet — pancreatic fluid can reflux and trigger acute pancreatitis. This presents with severe upper abdominal and back pain along with markedly elevated serum amylase and lipase, and requires hospitalization.

4.3 When Treatment Is Delayed

The longer treatment for acute cholangitis is delayed, the greater the risk of progression to septic shock, and mortality can rise substantially depending on the degree of delay. Initial management centers on fluid resuscitation and antibiotics, with endoscopic, percutaneous, or surgical biliary drainage required depending on the patient's condition.

Emergency Warning Signs — Go to the ER Without Delay
  • High fever (above 38.5°C / 101.3°F) with severe chills
  • Severe abdominal pain that does not subside
  • Jaundice — yellowing of the skin or whites of the eyes
  • Dark brown urine with pale, clay-colored stools
  • Confusion, dizziness, or a sensation of dropping blood pressure

If any combination of these signs appears, it may indicate an emergency complication such as cholangitis or gallstone pancreatitis. Do not attempt self-diagnosis or wait it out — go to the emergency room immediately.

5. Diagnosis — From Blood Tests to ERCP

5.1 Blood Tests

Elevated bilirubin and liver enzymes (AST, ALT, ALP, GGT) are checked, along with inflammatory markers such as white blood cell count and CRP when cholangitis is suspected, and amylase/lipase when pancreatitis is a concern.

5.2 Abdominal Ultrasound

Ultrasound is the first-line test for detecting gallstones and duct dilation, is widely accessible, and involves no radiation. However, overlying bowel gas can obscure the lower common bile duct, making it difficult to confirm stones by ultrasound alone in some cases.

5.3 MRCP (Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography)

MRCP provides a non-invasive image of the entire bile duct and pancreatic duct system without radiation exposure. Reported sensitivity ranges roughly from 77% to 93%, with specificity above 90% in many studies, and it is widely used as a confirmatory test before ERCP when bile duct stones are suspected.

5.4 EUS (Endoscopic Ultrasound)

EUS uses an ultrasound probe mounted on an endoscope to examine the bile duct at close range through the stomach or duodenal wall. Multiple studies report EUS sensitivity of roughly 89–95%, comparable to or exceeding MRCP, and it is particularly useful for confirmation when CT or MRCP findings are inconclusive.

5.5 ERCP — Diagnosis and Treatment in One Procedure

ERCP (endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography) involves passing an endoscope into the duodenum, injecting contrast dye, and imaging the bile and pancreatic ducts by X-ray. It is distinct from other imaging tests because it can confirm the diagnosis of bile duct stones and, in the same session, remove them. Because it is invasive and carries its own risks, ERCP is generally reserved for cases where the clinical probability of bile duct stones is high, with MRCP or EUS used first to confirm the diagnosis in more uncertain cases before deciding whether to proceed to ERCP.

6. Treatment — Removing the Stone With ERCP, Preventing Recurrence With Cholecystectomy

6.1 ERCP With Endoscopic Sphincterotomy

The current standard treatment for bile duct stones is ERCP-based stone removal. The endoscope identifies the opening of the bile duct (the papilla), a sphincterotomy widens that opening, and the stone is extracted using a balloon catheter or a wire basket. ERCP combined with endoscopic sphincterotomy has become the established, high-success standard approach. However, reported complications include pancreatitis (about 3.5–9.7%), cholangitis (about 0.5–3.0%), bleeding (about 0.3–9.6%), and perforation (about 0.08–0.6%), so it is important to fully discuss the risks and benefits with your medical team before the procedure.

6.2 Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy — Whether and When

Because most bile duct stones are secondary — having migrated from the gallbladder — laparoscopic removal of the remaining gallbladder after ERCP stone clearance is generally recommended to prevent recurrence. International guidelines tend to recommend cholecystectomy during the same hospital admission, or within about two weeks after ERCP clears the duct and the patient is discharged. For gallstone pancreatitis specifically, however, some reports suggest cholecystectomy offers limited benefit purely for preventing pancreatitis recurrence, so the decision is made individually, taking into account whether active gallbladder disease is also present.

6.3 Biliary Drainage in Severe or Emergency Cases

When a patient is hemodynamically unstable due to acute cholangitis, or ERCP is not immediately feasible, a staged approach is sometimes used: endoscopic or percutaneous biliary drainage first to relieve pressure and control infection, followed by stone removal once the patient's condition has stabilized.

7. Recurrence Prevention and Long-Term Management

7.1 Recurrence Rates and Risk Factors

Even after ERCP fully clears the bile duct, bile duct stones can recur. Reported figures vary, but recurrence of the stones themselves has been reported at up to about 30%, and recurrence of any biliary event (recurrent pain, cholangitis, etc.) at up to about 47% when the gallbladder is left in place, with one study reporting a median time to first recurrent event of a little over a month after the procedure. Bile duct strictures, diverticula, and incomplete stone clearance are cited as factors that raise the risk of recurrence.

7.2 Lifestyle Management and Follow-Up

After the gallbladder is removed, bile flows continuously from the liver directly into the small intestine, so there is generally no major dietary restriction, though a gradual transition through a low-fat diet is recommended immediately after surgery. If the gallbladder is left in place, regular ultrasound follow-up, along with regular meals, maintaining a healthy weight, and avoiding rapid weight loss, can help reduce the risk of new stone formation. Above all, knowing the warning signs of recurrence — jaundice, fever, and severe abdominal pain — and seeking care promptly if they appear is the key to minimizing recurrent complications.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Are bile duct stones the same as gallstones?

Both are stones, but their location differs. Gallstones sit inside the gallbladder, while bile duct stones sit inside the bile duct itself, usually the common bile duct. Because bile duct stones block the only channel through which bile can drain, they carry a relatively higher risk of complications such as jaundice and cholangitis.

Can bile duct stones be confirmed with ultrasound alone?

Abdominal ultrasound can show duct dilation, but overlying bowel gas often obscures the lower common bile duct, so stones can be missed. When bile duct stones are suspected, MRCP or EUS is typically used for confirmation, and ERCP is performed when both diagnosis and treatment are needed.

Is ERCP a risky procedure?

ERCP is valuable because it can diagnose and treat bile duct stones in one session, but being invasive it carries reported risks including pancreatitis, bleeding, and perforation. These risks should be weighed against the dangers of leaving bile duct stones untreated, in discussion with your care team.

If ERCP removes the stone, does the gallbladder always need to be removed too?

Because most bile duct stones migrated from the gallbladder, leaving it in place carries a risk of recurrence. For this reason, cholecystectomy is generally recommended after ERCP clears the duct, though the final decision depends on individual health status and should be discussed with your physician.

Does having bile duct stones always mean a medical emergency?

Not necessarily. A small stone that does not fully block the duct may cause no symptoms at all. However, if high fever, severe abdominal pain, and jaundice appear together, this strongly suggests an emergency complication such as cholangitis, and you should go to the emergency room immediately.
