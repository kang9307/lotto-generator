When You Stop Writing Code and Start Delegating It - What Gets Cheaper and What Gets More Expensive

The weight that moved to verification, the bill concurrency sends, and where the team analogy breaks

2026-09-06 Dev & Infra

When your mouth starts doing what your hands used to do, your hands go idle. They do not stay idle for long. Something else moves into the vacated space. That is precisely the shape of what happens when the work shifts from writing code to asking for code.

Typing shrinks. So does the time spent recalling syntax, and the time spent hunting through documentation for the right call signature. What grows in their place is the work of judging whether the result is correct. And one ingredient that judgment depends on quietly disappears along the way. When you write the code yourself, understanding arrives with it. When you ask for the code, only the code arrives.

What follows is not a forecast. It is a balance sheet. It sorts out what gets cheaper, what gets more expensive, and where new machinery has to be built to carry the side that got more expensive.

Every statement attributed below comes from a secondary source. The item that prompted this piece is a long-form video interview, and the video page returned no captions and no transcript. So the remarks are carried only as far as a community summary reported them, and nothing beyond that summary is asserted. No assessment of the speaker as a person, and none of his other remarks, appears here.

The prompt - going from player to coach

In early September 2026 a Korean developer community carried an item about a long-form interview with a software developer. The item is a summary of that video. According to the summary, over the previous three months he handed almost all code writing on his own project to agents. His own role changed to setting the structure and the vision and deciding the final merge, which he likened to moving from being a player on the field to a coach directing several people.

The same summary carries a qualification. A large organisation will not get ten times faster overnight, and the reason given is not implementation capacity but human communication and approval structures. There is also a line about good products still requiring ideas, taste and a coherent vision.

What is worth attention here is not whether the forecast is right. Take the sentence about player and coach as given, and a question remains: what does the coach now have to hold that the player did not? A player knows the situation by running in it. The grass underfoot, the way the defence is drifting, the fatigue in the other team, all of it arrives through the body before it arrives through analysis. A coach does not run. Knowing the same things without running requires separate instrumentation. That instrumentation is what this piece inventories.

What moved was not the work, it was the verification

The most commonly dropped line item when people compute the return on delegation is this: they assume that total time falls by however much writing time fell. What actually happens is a relocation of cost. The weight taken off the writing side accumulates in the verification queue.

The same relocation shows up in organisation-level statistics. The 2025 report from Google's DORA research programme found that the relationship between AI adoption and software delivery throughput had flipped positive, unlike the previous year. But the relationship with stability is still negative. Change failures rise, rework rises, and the time it takes to resolve problems rises. The report's framing is concise: AI does not fix a team, it amplifies what the team already is. And what the amplification exposes is the downstream bottleneck in testing, review and quality assurance.

Producing more and producing it faster means having more to judge. If judgment capacity stays flat, either the pass rate falls or the judgment becomes ceremonial and the failures simply move further downstream. The diagnosis that comprehension itself has become the constraint is treated separately in Understanding Is the New Bottleneck. Here that constraint is taken as given, and the question is only what has to be built in response.

Understanding does not arrive with the code

While you write code yourself, understanding accrues as a by-product. Agonising over a variable name surfaces an ambiguity in the domain. Writing a conditional runs you into a boundary case you had not considered. Splitting a function reveals that a dependency points the wrong way. None of these discoveries is the goal of the activity. They are incidental, which is exactly why no separate invoice ever arrived for them.

How large a share reading occupies has been measured. A 2018 field study published in IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering collected 3,148 working hours from 78 professional developers and reported that, on average, roughly 58 percent of working time goes to program comprehension activities. The same study noted that senior developers spend a significantly lower share of their time on comprehension than junior developers do.

Carry that number over into the delegation case and the direction becomes obvious. Even people who write the code spent more than half their time reading. Stop writing and that 58 percent does not shrink, it approaches 100 percent, because reading and judging are all that is left. And the reading now happens under worse conditions than before. Code you did not write is code with no memory attached to it, no record in your head of which alternatives were rejected and why.

There is one more instrument problem stacked on top. A randomised controlled trial published by METR in 2025 took 16 experienced developers working in mature open-source repositories, assigned them 246 real tasks, and randomised whether AI tooling was permitted on each one. The result was that average task completion time rose by 19 percent. The same developers had predicted a 24 percent speed-up beforehand, and even after being slower they estimated they had been 20 percent faster. METR itself labels the finding as a historical observation bound to the tooling landscape between February and June of 2025.

You cannot transplant that study's conclusion onto today's tools. But one thing does transplant. Perception cannot serve as the instrument panel. The feeling of going faster is generated in the writing segment, while the segment that slows down sits in verification and rework, where the feeling never reaches.

Review capacity has a measured ceiling

Saying verification grows sounds abstract, but review has a concrete ceiling that was measured a long time ago. The SmartBear study of a Cisco engineering team analysed 2,500 reviews covering 3.2 million lines of code.

ObservationMeasured valueWhat it means once you delegate
Amount per sitting200 to 400 linesOutput size has to be cut at the instruction stage, before anything is produced
Time spent on that amount60 to 90 minutesReview is not work that can be batched up and cleared in one sweep
Defects surfaced then70 to 90 percentA figure that holds only when the conditions above are respected
Reviews over 250 linesNever yielded more than 37 defects per 1,000 linesLarge batches are not defect-free, they are defect-invisible
Reading rateBest under 300 lines per hour, substantial misses above 500Generation rate can climb, this rate cannot climb with it
Above 450 lines per hourDefect density below average in 87 percent of casesA fast review is closer to an unmeasured one than to a passed one

The reason this table bites hard in the delegation case is asymmetry. Throughput on the generation side can be raised. Throughput on the review side is pinned to a human reading rate. When an 800-line change arrives in one piece, it does not become two 400-line reviews. It becomes one review with a degraded detection rate.

Which means the machinery you now need is not a better review tool. It is work decomposition. A person has to cut what gets built and how far it goes, in advance, and put those cuts into the instruction. Delegate the decomposition and you have given away the only lever that controls output size.

Acceptance criteria - if you cannot write down done, you cannot delegate

When you write the code yourself, the standard for done can live entirely in your head and everything still works. The writer and the judge are the same person, so the standard never has to be externalised. Delegation removes that convenience instantly. A standard that lives outside the instruction may as well not exist.

Work whose acceptance criteria cannot be written down is work that cannot be delegated. That is not a limitation of any tool, it is a matter of definition. If you cannot say what finished means, you cannot judge whether something is finished. The minimum usable form has four layers.

  • Behaviour — which inputs must produce which outputs. At least one concrete input and output pair belongs in the instruction itself
  • Boundaries — what must happen on empty values, maximum lengths, duplicates, out-of-order arrival, and unauthorised callers
  • Non-functional — response time, memory, concurrent request count, and what must and must not end up in the logs
  • Prohibitions — files that must not be touched, dependencies that must not be added, public interfaces that must not change

That last layer is the one that is genuinely new to the delegation case. When you hand work to a person you do not have to enumerate the prohibitions. A colleague knows the conventions of the repository, and asks when they do not. In delegation, asking is not the default behaviour. A prohibition that is not written down is not observed.

[TASK]   Add partial cancellation to the order cancel API
[SCOPE]  api/orders/*.py and tests/orders/* only. Payment module untouched
[BEHAVE] Cancel quantity at or below remaining quantity partially cancels, above returns 409
[EDGES]  quantity 0 / negative / already fully cancelled order / two concurrent requests
[NONFUN] Response time of the existing full-cancel path must not increase
[FORBID] No schema migration. No new dependency
[DONE]   Tests exist for all four edge cases, and the diff is under 400 lines

The 400 lines on the final line is where the ceiling from section four gets pulled inside the instruction. Unless size is nailed down as part of the acceptance criteria, what comes back will not come back at a reviewable size.

Concurrency is not free

The appeal of delegation usually comes from the fact that several things can run at once. While one is in flight another can start, so wall-clock time falls. What is missing from that calculation is the bill that lands on the human.

Research on interrupted programming is instructive. An analysis of 10,000 recorded sessions from 86 programmers, alongside a survey of 414 more, found that only 10 percent of sessions resumed editing within one minute of an interruption. And only 7 percent went straight to an edit without navigating somewhere else first. The rest wandered through the codebase rebuilding context before they touched anything.

That research concerns human work interruption, and there is no measured value establishing that switching between agent branches carries the same recovery cost. The shape, however, is the same. Move from watching branch A to branch B and B's context has to be rebuilt, and that context is read rather than authored, which makes reconstruction more expensive rather than less.

Broken into line items, concurrency charges the following.

  • Re-entry — every branch switch means rebuilding that branch's state and the reasoning behind its current shape
  • Tracking — someone has to remember or record how far each branch got and what it is blocked on
  • Merging — colliding changes need an ordering decision, and the ordering changes the result
  • Attribution — when a test breaks, the time to locate which branch caused it grows with branch count
  • Rework — discovering late that a direction was wrong discards everything that branch produced

Branch collisions and isolation patterns at the repository level are covered in What Happens When You Unleash Multiple AI Agents on One Repo, the total hidden cost of orchestration in The Orchestration Tax, and the break-even point for parallelism in Where Parallel Coding Agents Actually Pay Off. What is counted here is only the share billed to one person.

Where half-finished work piles up

The most underestimated part of the concurrency bill is partial completion. If three branches each reached 80 percent, the total is not 240 percent. It is zero, because there is nothing shippable.

What makes that state particularly bad is the character of the remaining 20 percent. What is left over is usually boundary conditions, error handling, and the seams where the new work meets the existing code. All of it hard to judge and prone to rework. You have tripled the easy 80 percent and tripled the hard 20 percent along with it.

So the first thing to fix before adding branches is keeping the definition of done identical across them. When the definitions differ, merging stops being a technical operation and becomes a negotiation. And state belongs outside your head, not inside it. The minimum form looks roughly like this.

BRANCH  SCOPE                        CRITERIA  STATE          BLOCKED ON
A       Order partial-cancel API      4/4      awaiting review  -
B       Cancellation history schema   2/3      in progress      needs A's status enum fixed
C       Admin cancel button           0/3      queued           starts after B merges

Merge order: A -> B -> C   (B depends on A's status enum, C on B's schema)

What that board does is simple. It takes tracking out of human memory and puts it on paper. With two branches it is unnecessary. Past three, nothing runs without it.

Where the team analogy holds and where it breaks

The metaphor of coaching, or of running a team, is useful. Splitting work into parcels, briefing quality dominating the outcome, nothing merging until it clears a review gate: all of that is the exact shape of a human team. The question is how far the metaphor can be pushed. The distinctions below are not measured values; they follow structurally from the difference in the nature of the two things being compared.

Property of a human teamDoes it hold under delegationWhat has to replace it
Work is split and progresses in parallelHoldsNothing extra required, use it as is
Briefing quality dominates the outcomeHolds, and bites harder than beforeManage instructions as reusable documents, not as chat
Nothing merges before a review gateHoldsPut the size ceiling into the gate condition itself
Accountability for the resultBreaks, there is no point of attributionThe delegator carries all of it. There is no substitute
A correction sticks for next timeBreaks, it disappears when the session endsAccumulate corrections as rule documents in the repository
Refusal on the grounds that it cannot work hereMostly breaksFront-load design review and state prohibitions explicitly
Context persists inside the personBreaks, closing the window ends itRecord decisions and their reasoning outside the code
Colleagues check one anotherBreaks, branches do not know each otherA person fixes the interfaces between branches in advance

The most expensive row in that table is refusal. An experienced colleague pushes back when an instruction is wrong. A single sentence such as this would break the existing transaction boundary saves days of rework. In a structure where that objection never arrives, design errors only surface after implementation is complete. So the more you delegate, the further forward design review has to move. Whatever used to be caught at the back now has to be caught at the front.

The second most expensive row is learning. On a human team you do not have to make the same correction twice. Under delegation the same correction keeps being necessary. The only way to reduce that repetition is to write the correction into a file rather than into a conversation. The view that the codebase itself becomes the context for all later work is developed in Write Code Like a Human Will Maintain It.

Four things that stay in human hands

What to hand over and what to keep is not a matter of taste. It is settled by asking what collapses if you hand it over.

  • Problem definition — hand it over and a well-built wrong thing comes back. It is especially bad because the higher the implementation quality, the harder it is to notice the direction was wrong
  • Acceptance criteria — hand them over and the thing being inspected sets the standard for its own inspection. In that arrangement a pass carries no information
  • Architectural boundaries — hand them over and each branch looks locally reasonable while the assembled system has collapsed layers, with caches in different places and transactions opened at different depths
  • Final accountability — there is no recipient for it in the first place. A person gets paged when something breaks, and delegation does not alter that fact by one degree

In the summary that prompted this piece, the roles the speaker claimed for himself were setting structure and vision, and deciding the final merge. Those two map onto architectural boundaries and final accountability respectively. Whether the summary addressed the other two, problem definition and acceptance criteria, cannot be determined from the summary alone.

The view of delegation as a graded scale of autonomy is developed in AI Agent Autonomy Levels, and the question of what the execution layer decides rather than the model in The Model Only Emits Text.

Writing versus delegating - what gets cheaper, what gets costlier

Folded onto a single sheet, the argument so far looks like this. The rightmost column is the point of the whole piece. Every item that got more expensive requires a corresponding piece of machinery.

Read that table vertically and the cheaper column is conspicuously empty. Only three items genuinely get cheaper: typing, writing test code, and wall-clock time. The other six rows all land on the expensive side. Delegation still pays in some cases because work does exist where those three cheap items are large — mechanical transformations, boilerplate, and feature additions whose pattern is already settled. Conversely, in work dominated by the other six, raising the delegated share raises total cost.

Why a larger organisation does not speed up proportionally

The most restrained line in the summary that prompted this piece is here: the reason a large organisation will not immediately get ten times faster is not implementation capacity but human communication and approval structures.

Unpacked structurally, that gets clearer. What delegation unblocks is the individual's generation bottleneck. What sets an organisation's pace is not generation but the number of agreement points. Review approvers, deployment gates, security review, stakeholder sign-off, release windows. Generation can get ten times faster and the count of those points stays exactly the same. If anything the queue at each point lengthens, because more volume now has to pass through them.

The stability decline DORA observed points at the same location. Change volume rose while the control systems — automated testing, version control maturity, fast feedback loops — stayed where they were, so failure concentrated downstream. Which means that when an organisation raises its delegated share, the first thing to work on is not the generation tooling but the number of agreement points and the capacity of each one.

This is why the multiplier one person achieves on their own project does not transfer to an organisation. A one-person project has exactly one agreement point. It is the person.

The environment decides half of what can be delegated

The summary also contains a passage about environment: that Linux, with its configuration files, command-line interfaces and specific error messages, is a good environment for an agent to diagnose and change, and that after roughly forty years the computing platform has become competitive again.

There is no need to take sides on operating systems, but the property that passage points at generalises cleanly. For delegation to work, the target system has to offer three things.

  • Readable state — current configuration and status must surface as files or as command output. State that exists only behind a screen cannot become material for a decision
  • Reversible operations — attempts have to be cheap enough to make repeatedly. Autonomy cannot be raised in front of an operation that cannot be undone
  • Interpretable failure — the message has to say what failed and why, or no next attempt can be constructed. The phrase unknown error terminates the loop

All three are other names for observability. Which is why an organisation trying to raise its delegated share often finds that the real work is not switching tools but cleaning up logs, error messages and rollback paths. Those things were always good for people, but a team could limp along without them. Under delegation they change character: without them, nothing runs.

The order in which to move

There is an order to raising the delegated share, and it falls straight out of inverting the cost structure laid out above.

  • Reversible work first — start where the cost of failure is low. Data migrations and payment paths come last, not first
  • Only work with writable acceptance criteria — if done cannot be put into a sentence, that work is not yet at the delegation stage
  • Put the size ceiling in the instruction — force output to come back reviewable. The 400-line figure is not arbitrary, it comes out of measurement
  • Start with two branches — going past three requires a state board and a merge-order rule to already exist
  • Write corrections into files — if you are making the same correction twice, that is a signal that the rule document does not exist
  • Instrument with measurement, not feeling — watch cycle time, rework rate and change failure rate. Feeling faster is not data

One item remains. Raising the delegated share removes the time spent stuck. Being stuck is unpleasant, but it was also the place where skill was being manufactured. That problem is treated separately in After the Stuck Time Disappeared. It never shows up on a balance sheet, yet it is billed on a horizon of years, which is reason enough to keep it on the table when deciding how much to delegate.
