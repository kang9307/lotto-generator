What Changing a License Actually Changes - The Asymmetry of Copies Already Shipped

Only future distribution moves. Consent, duties, and reversibility all follow from that one sentence

2026-09-10 Dev & Infra

When word gets out that an open source project has switched licenses, the conversation almost always drifts toward which license is better. That is not the question that has to be answered first in practice. The question that comes first is what the decision actually changes, and what it can never change no matter how firmly it is announced.

The answer starts from a single sentence. A permission does not attach once to an abstract work; it travels with the distributed copy and is given to the person who receives that copy. New terms therefore bind only copies that go out from here on, and the permission already attached to copies that went out before stays exactly where it is. Miss this asymmetry and you will overestimate the reach of the decision while underestimating the preparation it demands.

The occasion for writing this was a post in which a developer explained that the default license for their own software had moved from MIT to EUPL. This article is not about whether that choice was right or wrong. It works through what moves on both sides of the table, in the project and among its users, when a decision like that is made, at the level of the actual license text. This article describes a general structure and does not stand in for legal advice on any specific matter, and a real decision belongs with a lawyer or a legal review.

The clauses quoted below come from the English authentic text of EUPL v1.2, from the body of the MIT License, and from the text of the Developer Certificate of Origin 1.1. What follows records only what each document requires and does not require. No judgment about which is preferable belongs here, and no specific company or individual dispute is discussed.

The occasion - one developer moves a default license

The gist of the original post is short. A developer with a long history of building open source software has settled on EUPL 1.2 as the default license for their work. Earlier projects used a license from the LGPL family, and the move into JavaScript work brought MIT along with it; the point now is that a permissive license will no longer be the default. The reason given is a judgment that distributing under permissive terms produced results that diverged from what the author had hoped for, together with the observation that EUPL is a strong copyleft license and carries authentic versions in many languages.

The part that matters in practice is not the reasoning but the scope of application. The post says EUPL was applied to several newly started projects, and that one existing project keeps MIT because it carries a large number of third-party contributions. In other words this is not an event in which an existing codebase was relicensed wholesale. It is a decision that changed the default for what will be built from now on.

When that distinction blurs in the retelling, what survives is the sentence: an MIT project became an EUPL project. That sentence is broader than what the original text actually says. And the fact that the contributor situation was given as the reason for leaving the existing project alone means the author had already answered, in their own way, the second question this article sets out to examine.

Meanwhile the original post says nothing about what becomes of previously shipped releases, whether any contribution agreement is in use, or whether the terms might be moved again later. Those gaps are filled in below from the license texts rather than from the post.

First distinction - a license attaches to copies

An open source license is a document in which a rights holder permits another party to use a work on stated conditions. Pin down when and to what that permission attaches and everything else falls out of it.

  • The object is the copy. Whoever receives a copy carrying those conditions holds the permission for that copy. If the rights holder later adopts different terms, the content of the permission already given does not change.
  • A rights holder may run several sets of terms in parallel. The same code can be permitted to different parties on different conditions. That is what makes dual licensing possible, and it is also why adopting new terms does not amount to withdrawing the old ones.
  • Without the rights you cannot set the terms. For parts in which someone else work is mixed, you cannot travel beyond the range that party permitted. This is the root of the consent problem that follows.

Reading exactly what the MIT text requires makes the property sharper. MIT permits use, copying, modification, merging, publication, distribution, sublicensing, and sale, and attaches a single condition: the copyright notice and the permission notice must be included in all copies or substantial portions. No sentence in the body demands that source be made available or that redistribution happen on the same terms.

MIT License — the condition sentence (verbatim)
  The above copyright notice and this permission notice shall be
  included in all copies or substantial portions of the Software.

EUPL v1.2 Article 5 — Copyleft clause (verbatim)
  If the Licensee distributes or communicates copies of the Original
  Works or Derivative Works, this Distribution or Communication will
  be done under the terms of this Licence or of a later version of
  this Licence unless the Original Work is expressly distributed only
  under this version of the Licence

Set the two sentences side by side and the difference is countable. MIT asks only that the notices be kept; EUPL adds to that by specifying the terms under which the thing may go out again. The accurate statement is not that MIT is deficient, but that MIT does not require this and EUPL does.

Copies already shipped are not recalled

The reach of the decision is cut by time. Take the day the license change takes effect as a boundary: everything built and shipped after it carries the new terms, and everything that already went out before it stays marked with the old ones.

The measure that most often misfires here is channel cleanup. Pull old versions from a package registry, delete the tags, and people will have a harder time fetching them. But making something hard to fetch and removing the permission attached to a copy someone already holds are two different acts. Distribution can be stopped; the range of what a prior recipient may do with their copy is set by the conditions that were attached to that copy.

Derivative works push this one step further. If somebody already built their own work on a copy received under the old terms, that work stands on the range those terms permitted. The new terms on the upstream do not reach back and cover that lineage. As a result, a fork can go on living from a point before the decision.

  • What changes — the terms on releases built and shipped after the decision, the terms on newly started repositories, and the duties that will fall on anyone who takes the code from here on.
  • What does not change — the terms attached to releases that already went out, the permission held by whoever received them, and the standing of derivative works built on that permission.
  • What becomes ambiguous — which commit begins the new terms, and which side a maintenance release cut from an existing branch falls on. Nail neither of these down in writing and someone will be inferring the answer later.
  • What is newly created — a state in which terms differ by version, and the practical burden of telling users which is which, close in spirit to a notice obligation.

So the first artifact of a license change should not be a new LICENSE file but a document that records the boundary. Which versions ended under the old terms and which begin under the new ones has to be readable from a single file.

Minimal shape for recording the boundary (example)

LICENSE            ← current terms. only the new license text lives here
LICENSES/          ← old terms preserved. filenames carry the range
  MIT.txt
  EUPL-1.2.txt
LICENSE-HISTORY.md ← v0.1.0 ~ v2.3.0 : MIT

when identifiers are written at the head of source files
  SPDX-License-Identifier: EUPL-1.2      ← new files

The value of this bookkeeping arrives in the future. Terms differing by version is not itself a problem; the problem is that fact going unrecorded. With no record, somebody years from now has to dig through commit logs and infer, and a judgment reached by inference is not evidence in an audit.

Whose consent do you need

The person who can set the terms going forward is the person holding the rights in that part. If the codebase belongs to one person the question does not arise; the moment it belongs to several, it starts to split.

A single author

If one person wrote the code end to end, that person sets the terms going forward, and no one else consent is needed to ship under new ones. That said, this state is rarer than it looks. A single typo fix, one sentence of documentation, one line of build configuration can all shift the picture depending on whether they clear the threshold of protectable authorship.

  • What to check is the author list across the whole commit history. The measure is not the number of accounts but the authors whose code is still present.
  • If fragments were copied in from elsewhere, the original terms on those fragments are still alive. A finding of sole authorship holds only after those fragments have been examined.
  • Code written on commission or in the course of employment has its ownership determined by contract and by law. In that case it is not a matter an individual can settle alone.

Third-party contributions are present

Where a contributor sent their own work to the project and no separate agreement exists, the customary treatment is that the contribution came in under the terms the project was displaying at that moment. If those terms were permissive, the project may go on using that code within the range they permitted.

The difficulty is range. Shipping code received under permissive terms as part of a distribution carrying stronger terms, and swapping the terms on that code itself, are two different acts. The former is a structure that holds as long as the notice obligation from the original terms is honored; the latter requires a basis obtained from the rights holder in that part.

  • Where consent from everyone becomes necessary — when the terms marked on a contributor code are themselves to be changed, or when that code is to go out carrying only the new terms with the original notices dropped.
  • Where it is not necessary — when only newly written code carries the new terms, and when the contributor code keeps its original terms and notices while the combined distribution goes out under the new ones, which works where the two sets of terms do not conflict.
  • The route actually taken in practice — as in the original case, leaving existing projects alone and applying the new terms only to new ones. Gathering consent is the most expensive line item, so this choice is common.

A contribution agreement exists

One more axis splits here. Contribution agreements come in two kinds with entirely different characters, and the similarity of their names causes them to be lumped together.

One kind is the contributor license agreement. The contributor grants the entity running the project a broad set of rights in their contribution, and where that grant includes the power to sublicense, it becomes the basis on which the entity can ship under changed terms. That is why many foundation-run projects use this form.

The other kind is the developer certificate of origin. Here a single signed line on a commit certifies that the contributor has the right to submit that contribution. Its text has four items, each stating that the contribution was created by the contributor, or is based on prior work carrying an appropriate open source license, or was provided directly by another person who made the same certification, plus an acknowledgment that the record of the contribution is kept indefinitely. Nowhere in that text is there a sentence assigning copyright or permitting relicensing.

  • A project that uses only the certification form and wants to change terms must obtain a separate basis from each contributor individually, because that document does not supply one.
  • Even in a project using the agreement form, the measure is what rights that particular document actually grants. Documents with the same name carry different clauses.
  • Which kind is in use is visible in the project documentation. Whether it asks for one signed line or for a separate signed instrument is the first fork.

Three axes where EUPL differs from MIT

EUPL is an open source license produced by the European Commission and it sits on the OSI approved list. The approval date is 13 July 2017 and the identifier is EUPL-1.2. Judged only on being copyleft it resembles other reciprocal licenses, but there are three axes for which the MIT text contains no counterpart at all.

Many linguistic versions carry identical value

Article 13 provides that all linguistic versions of the license approved by the European Commission have identical value. That does not mean translations are reference material; it means each language version is authentic in its own right. The article itself does not state a number of languages, and the official distribution page offers versions in 23 languages.

  • The practical implication is that a user outside the English-speaking world can read the terms in an authentic version in their own language and argue from that reading. Licenses that keep translations usually mark them as informative, so they lack this property.
  • It also brings along the question of how differences of expression among several authentic versions are to be handled. The article says identical value and does not set a precedence order.
  • Korean is not an official European Union language, so it is not in that set. A review conducted in Korea will therefore work from the English authentic text or from another official-language version.

The compatibility clause - an exit to the terms listed in the appendix

Article 5 places a compatibility clause immediately after the copyleft clause. Where a derivative work is based upon both this work and another work licensed under a compatible license, the distribution or communication may be done under the terms of that compatible license. The appendix enumerates which licenses those are.

EUPL v1.2 Appendix — Compatible Licences (verbatim list)
  GNU General Public License (GPL) v. 2, v. 3
  GNU Affero General Public License (AGPL) v. 3
  Open Software License (OSL) v. 2.1, v. 3.0
  Eclipse Public License (EPL) v. 1.0
  CeCILL v. 2.0, v. 2.1
  Mozilla Public Licence (MPL) v. 2
  GNU Lesser General Public Licence (LGPL) v. 2.1, v. 3
  Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike v. 3.0 Unported (CC BY-SA 3.0)
  European Union Public Licence (EUPL) v. 1.1, v. 1.2
  Québec Free and Open-Source Licence — Reciprocity (LiLiQ-R)
    or Strong Reciprocity (LiLiQ-R+)

The clause exists because of the deadlock that arises when two copyleft licenses meet. If each demands distribution on its own terms, the two bodies of code cannot be combined. The compatibility clause is a mechanism written into the text in advance so that one side can step aside. Note that the sentence presupposes the combined work being under terms that appear on that list, so it is not a general permission to switch to arbitrary other terms at will.

Governing law and jurisdiction sit inside the text

Article 14 sets jurisdiction and Article 15 sets governing law. The governing law is the law of the European Union Member State where the licensor has a seat, resides, or has a registered office, and disputes in which a European Union institution is the licensor are assigned to the Court of Justice of the European Union.

  • The MIT text contains neither governing law nor jurisdiction. So the governing law for a dispute over an MIT work is determined by rules outside the license, whereas an EUPL work has a place the text points to first.
  • In corporate review this difference does real work. A document carrying a governing law clause frequently lands in a different bucket of the legal review process.

What happens on the receiving side

From the deciding side this is a change to one file. From the side that uses the code it is the arrival of duties that were not there before. Four items actually move on the receiving side when a component travels from permissive to reciprocal terms.

  • The approval bucket changes. Many organizations manage licenses in tiers. Where permissive terms pass automatically and reciprocal terms trigger review, the same component changes the process it belongs to the moment its version rolls forward.
  • What ships changes. If a source copy has to accompany executable code, the build pipeline gains an artifact. This item survives only if it lives in the pipeline; assembled by hand at release time, it will be missed.
  • A derivative-work determination becomes necessary. How far the derivative of that work extends is what sets the scope of the duty. Static linking, dynamic linking, invocation as a separate process, and structures that only exchange data can be determined differently, and the determination rests on the facts.
  • A version-pinning strategy appears. Staying on the version immediately before the terms changed is a genuine option. But that choice accepts a state in which security updates stop arriving, so it is closer to a trade that converts a licensing problem into a security problem.

Miss these four and the first thing that goes wrong is not compliance but not knowing what went in. The other side of that story, actually meeting duties that are already fixed, was covered separately in where copyleft compliance breaks down. This article looks at the stage before it, the decision in which the duty is newly created.

What counts as distribution - where service delivery splits

Duties under a reciprocal license are mostly triggered by the event of distribution. So until what counts as distribution is settled, whether a duty exists at all is not settled either.

Under the classic picture, an interpretation was widely used according to which using something purely internally, without sending files outside, does not amount to distribution. On top of that reading, a structure held in which delivering only as a service sends no files and therefore triggers no duty, and licenses that name network use explicitly emerged in response.

EUPL 1.2 took the route of drawing the definition itself broadly. In the definitions article, distribution or communication covers selling, giving, lending, renting, distributing, communicating, and transmitting, and also otherwise making copies available online or offline, and providing access to the essential functionalities of the work at the disposal of any other natural or legal person.

EUPL v1.2 Definitions — Distribution or Communication (verbatim)
  any act of selling, giving, lending, renting, distributing,
  communicating, transmitting, or otherwise making available,
  online or offline, copies of the Work or providing access to
  its essential functionalities at the disposal of any other
  natural or legal person.

EUPL v1.2 Article 3 — Communication of the Source Code (substance)
  If the Work is provided as Executable Code, the Licensor
  provides in addition a machine-readable copy of the Source
  Code of the Work.

That final phrase is the heart of the definition. Because the structure is that providing access to essential functionalities falls inside the definition even where no copy leaves, delivery that exists only as a service has to be examined as well. Which particular architectures fall inside that definition is a question of facts and interpretation, and one sentence does not settle the outcome for every service topology. Concrete determinations belong with a lawyer or a legal review.

  • First item to check — by what route that component reaches the outside world in your product. An installer, a container image, a web response, or purely internal.
  • Second item — whether the definition in the license text covers that route. Definitions differ across licenses, so this calls for reading that document rather than reasoning from generalities.
  • Third item — whether the determination was written down. Determinations wobble again when people change, and with no record the work restarts from zero each time.

Can it be reversed

For the parts whose rights you hold outright, the terms going forward can be set again. So reversal itself is possible. The difficulty is that reversing does not return you to where you started.

Copies that went out during the interim carry the terms of that interim, and by exactly the logic of the earlier section they are not recalled. Whoever received a copy in that window keeps those terms, and derivative works built on it follow that lineage. What reversal produces is therefore not a restoration but a third state.

  • Version segments persist. One repository now holds three ranges under different terms, and every maintenance release cut from any of them requires a fresh decision about which terms apply.
  • The contributor mix has changed. Contributions that arrived after the switch came in under the new terms, so reversing raises the consent question again for those parts. The direction of travel is harder, not easier.
  • Forks may already exist. A fork taken from the point just before the change stands on the old terms and moves independently of any reversal.
  • Dual licensing becomes the alternative. Offering two sets of terms in parallel, for the parts whose rights you hold, opens choices on the user side without reversing anything. How far parallel offering can extend loops back to the consent question.

That is what orders the decisions. Applying new terms to a new project has the lowest cost of reversal; changing the terms of an existing project has the highest. The original case, which left the existing project alone, points the same direction as that cost structure.

Read the table vertically and one rule surfaces. Every cell that requires consent is a decision that touches the past, and every cell that requires none is a decision that only sets the future. When people say a license change is hard, the hard part is not picking new terms; it is the portion that reaches backward.

What does not change

Finally, the other side of the ledger: what a license change does not solve, contrary to expectation. Count these in advance or you will overrate the effect of the decision.

  • The standing of derivative works already made — what was built on the old terms follows that lineage. New terms do not reach back over it.
  • Trademarks and names — a license is a copyright permission document, and names and marks are handled by a different regime. A trademark problem is not solved by changing terms.
  • Contributor count and participation — changing terms sets how code goes out, not who comes in. The participation structure is a separate matter.
  • Visibility of compliance — tightening the terms does not by itself reveal who is using what and how. Knowing what went where is separate work solved with inventories and reconciliation.

The same kind of distinction shows up elsewhere. Why an attempt to write the manner of creation into a license document sits at a different layer from the permission itself was covered in putting AI authorship in the license. A license is a document that hands out rights, not one that announces facts — that premise runs under both articles.

What could not be confirmed

The items below were not settled from primary sources within the research scope of this article. Nothing is asserted about them until the basis is in hand.

  • The specific clauses of agreement-form contribution documents — the rights-granting clause of the widely used foundation-form agreement is published only as a PDF, which was outside the research scope here. The relicensing basis is therefore described only as a general structure, and the wording of any individual agreement has to be judged against that project own document.
  • Whether the EUPL service-related language has the same reach as other network clauses — what the text settles is that access to essential functionalities appears in the definitions article. Whether that reach matches licenses that name network use explicitly is a matter for interpretation and precedent, and it is not asserted here.
  • Conclusions under Korean law — this article deals only with the wording of the license texts. How each clause is evaluated under Korean law, and in particular the effective reach of a governing law clause, is a separate area of review.
  • The boundary of the derivative-work determination — determinations by linking method vary with the facts and the forum. No general rule dividing static from dynamic linking has been put into this article.
  • Items absent from the original post — the post does not address the handling of past releases, whether a contribution agreement is in use, or any plan to reverse or dual license. In this article those items come from the license texts and general structure, not from the post.
