When Deception Pays - Why the Deceiving Side Keeps Winning

Producing it costs almost nothing; checking it costs someone something every time

Aug 30, 2026 Trends & Culture

Why telling people to be careful does not work

One message, one short video, one link. Most of them pass without incident. Then one day one of them lands. Look back afterwards and the odd details are obvious. Why were they invisible at the time?

The usual explanations run like this: careless, rushed, uninformed. What they share is that they place the cause inside the person, so the prescription points at the person too. Be more careful, look into it more. It has been the same advice for years and the situation has not improved much. It is worth considering that the prescription has misidentified the cause.

What brought this subject back around is a post the developer Stephen Diehl published on his blog. To attribute his argument to him: the early internet was closer to a public square built by people making things as a hobby, and it is now closer to a machine that locates a person's vulnerable spot and puts a price on it. The cycle of picking out vulnerable targets, watching what they respond to, refining the offer and attaching a payment has been standardised, he writes. Unlike a real transaction, such an offer never resolves the need it addresses, which is how misery becomes recurring revenue in his conclusion.

Start by separating out what holds up. Looking at the structure of the cycle rather than at the morality of individual actors is persuasive, as is the observation that target selection has been automated. This piece starts from the same place.

Three things do not hold up. First, saying the internet as a whole is like this is too broad a generalisation. The core of the problem is that ordinary transactions and deception sit on the same screen, not that everything is deception. Second, the scale is asserted without evidence. Third, the conclusion narrows to personal restraint. Clearing out apps and building in friction is useful advice, but if that is all that remains, the cost has simply been placed back on the individual.

So let us change the question. Why does the deceiving side keep winning? The answer lies in the direction of costs, not in morality.

What follows is a general observation and is not an assessment of any particular company or service.

The costs are cheap on only one side

A deceptive offer is built once and then it is done. Expanding it from ten targets to ten thousand adds almost nothing. Verifying whether it is real, however, does not copy. Every recipient, individually, has to spend fresh time on each encounter.

StageCost to the producing sideCost to the checking side
Building the first oneOnce, then reusedNot applicable
Adding targetsNear zero, solved by copyingRises linearly with volume
Judging authenticityNot applicablePer person, every time, in minutes
On failureMove to the next targetThe reversal procedure is itself a cost
ReputationReset by abandoning a nameSlow to recover once lost
LearningResponse data accumulatesPersonal experience is not pooled

The last row matters most. The producing side learns what works with every attempt. The checking side experiences it separately and forgets it separately. Learning accumulates on one side only.

There is a sentence that summarises this shape. Coined in 2013 by the Italian developer Alberto Brandolini, it holds that the energy needed to refute nonsense is an order of magnitude greater than the energy needed to produce it. It is widely quoted, but it is a compressed observation rather than a measured value.

Economics has an older root. George Akerlof's 1970 paper in the Quarterly Journal of Economics on the used car market showed that when only the seller knows the quality, the good goods leave the market first. Online conditions are worse. There is no face-to-face contact, rarely a second encounter with the same counterparty, and almost no cost to abandoning a name. Reputation requires repetition, and repetition is absent.

One clue to the scale comes from consumer reports published by the US Federal Trade Commission in March 2025. Consumers in the United States reported losing more than 12.5 billion dollars to fraud in 2024, a 25 percent rise on the previous year. The striking number is not the total but the one beside it. The number of reports itself stayed stable, while the share of people who said they actually lost money rose from 27 percent in 2023 to 38 percent in 2024. That can be read as movement in the success rate rather than in the volume of attempts. It is a tally of voluntary reports from US consumers, so it is not the true scale of harm and it does not transfer to other countries.

Being fooled is not a failure of judgment

People are not judging badly. The conditions for judging have been narrowed. Three things narrow them.

First, time. When the window for a decision shrinks, verification is the first step to be cut. A closing-soon marker is not information but time design, and the purpose of that design is to reduce the material available for judgment. Skipping verification when rushed is, in ordinary life, a rational economy. Nobody making hundreds of decisions a day can check everything. When a design targets that economy, the economy becomes the weak point.

Second, form. Since the content cannot all be examined, people gauge trust by form: familiar layouts, familiar phrasing, familiar sequences of steps. This too was a shortcut that used to work. But form is the cheapest thing to copy. Once copying costs nothing, trusting form simply stops carrying information.

Third, the loss of context. The things that used to assist judgment are mostly stripped away in feed-shaped screens.

  • Who passed it along — the introducer disappears and an algorithm takes that seat
  • Where it came from — the origin is pushed off-screen or hidden a link deeper
  • What sat beside it — the other information that shared the page does not travel with it
  • What it looked like before — history is unavailable or has been reset
  • How many others see the same thing — personalised screens remove the control group

Overlay the three and what separates people is not the gullible from the shrewd but those in narrow conditions and those who are not. Those who land in narrow conditions most often tend to be busy people, people carrying responsibility, and people going through an unfamiliar procedure. Blaming the person who was fooled relocates the cause and leaves the structure untouched.

Why it does not get filtered out - the filtering side is wrong too

The natural question is why platforms do not simply filter it. There are three layers of difficulty.

Scale. The volume arriving and the volume that can be adjudicated do not match. Automated judgment becomes necessary, and automated judgment gets things wrong.

The difficulty of the call. The line between exaggeration and deception lies not in the wording but in context and intent. The same sentence is ordinary promotion in one situation and deception in another. A machine reading only sentences cannot separate what humans also disagree about.

And the filtering side is wrong too. There are two kinds of error in adjudication and they move in opposite directions.

AspectMissed casesWrongly flagged cases
Who paysThe userInnocent authors and sellers
When it surfacesAfter the harmImmediately, but quietly
Route to recoveryReporting and payment reversalAppeal, usually not public
Visible in metricsOnly what is reportedAlmost never
Tightening the thresholdFallsRises
Loosening the thresholdRisesFalls

One case shows what false positives look like in practice. A 2023 study by Liang and colleagues in the journal Patterns ran seven widely used detectors over 91 English exam essays written by non-native speakers and 88 essays by US eighth graders. The average false positive rate on the non-native essays was 61.22 percent, while accuracy on native-speaker essays was near perfect. Polishing the vocabulary with a language model brought the false positive rate down to 11.77 percent. What the detectors were actually measuring was closer to how simple the prose was than to who wrote it.

The point is not that the technology is inadequate but that wherever the threshold sits, somebody pays. Tighten it and the innocent get caught; loosen it and what should be caught passes through. The rarer deception is within the whole, the worse this gets. Even a fairly accurate classifier will flag a large share of innocents when the target is rare to begin with. An old trap in statistics shows up here unchanged.

Filtering is therefore less a question of doing it well than of choosing which error to accept. And that choice is rarely disclosed.

What rises when engagement becomes the metric

Optimization, not malice

Once a measure becomes a target it stops measuring what it was meant to measure. It is a long-standing observation in the social sciences and it applies to ranking algorithms unchanged.

Ranking tuned to maximise engagement does not select for what is true. It selects for what produces a reaction. The two overlap in places and diverge in others. Threats and large promises tend to produce large reactions; accurate, heavily qualified information tends to produce small ones. Nobody has to decide to promote falsehood for the result to tilt that way.

The distinction matters because the remedy changes with it. If the cause is malice, replacing people fixes it. If the cause is the incentive structure, replacing people produces the same output. What has to change is what gets counted as success.

Where accuracy sits in a model that sells attention

When the paying party and the using party differ, the optimization target is the paying party. In that structure accuracy is not a goal but a constraint. It is maintained only as far as being wrong is costly. Not out of bad intent, but because that is where it sits in the objective function.

That does not make other models free. Each leaves a different place exposed.

This is not an argument that advertising is bad and subscriptions are good. It is that whichever model is chosen, what that model pushes upward is chosen along with it. For a reader the practical question is one: who is paying for the screen in front of me?

What generative tools changed and what they did not

This is an easy place to overstate, so the parts are worth separating carefully.

What did not change is the structure. Picking vulnerable targets, watching the response, refining the offer and attaching a payment existed in the era of print and telephones. Prize notices arriving by post and sales calls used the same structure. This is not a new problem.

What changed is volume, speed and the language threshold. One person can produce far more per day, and the cycle of watching a response and refining has shortened. There was a period when awkward phrasing served as material for judgment, and that material has weakened. Smooth prose now guarantees nothing.

One common misreading is worth clearing away: since the same tools are available to the filtering side, do they not cancel out? Largely, yes. But the asymmetry from the earlier section remains. The producing side does not pay for false positives and the filtering side does. The producing side can fail nine times in ten at almost no loss, while the filtering side only has to be wrong once for an innocent person to pay. That is why an identical gain in capability does not deliver an identical benefit to both sides.

So the conclusion should be held carefully in both directions. The claim that tools have ended the world and the claim that tools will sort it out are both weakly supported. What changed is the slope of the cost curve.

Why every substitute for trust becomes a forgery target

Because we cannot verify directly, we use proxy signals: verification badges, ratings and reviews, follower counts, domains and business names, the date an account was created.

These signals carry value for a simple reason. They cost something to produce. A signal only carries information when it carries a cost that a trustworthy party can bear and an untrustworthy one struggles to. When that cost falls, the signal loses its value. That is the common principle behind proxy signals collapsing one by one.

  • Verification badges — meaning shifts when issuing criteria change or when they become purchasable, yet readers keep reading the old meaning. The hardest case is a signal whose meaning changes quietly
  • Ratings and reviews — already under regulation. The US Federal Trade Commission rule on consumer reviews has been in force since 21 October 2024 and prohibits reviews from people who do not exist, reviews from people with no actual experience, and reviews obtained for payment. That a rule was needed is itself a signal that the signal had been contaminated
  • Follower and subscriber counts — values that are easy to count are easy to manufacture. A large number says nothing beyond being a large number
  • Domains and business names — cheap to make look similar. Checking by eye is the method the producing side tested first
  • Account age — once a market for aged accounts exists, the cost called time is replaced by money
  • Faces and voices — once the most expensive signal to reproduce, and among those whose cost has fallen fastest

The practical response is therefore not to stare harder at one signal but to overlay two signals of different kinds. Overlaying only means something when forging one costs something different from forging the other. Pairing one on-screen signal with one off-screen signal usually satisfies that condition.

What rules can and cannot do

What rules do is not eliminate deception but shift the direction of costs a little. Moving part of the verification cost onto the producing side eases the asymmetry by that much. Several forms exist.

  • Disclosure duties — Article 26 of the EU Digital Services Act (Regulation 2022/2065) requires platforms to make clear that an advert is an advert, who paid for it, and the main parameters used to show it to this particular user
  • Design regulation — Article 25 of the same regulation prohibits interfaces designed in ways that deceive or manipulate users or otherwise distort their ability to make free and informed decisions
  • National rules — in South Korea, Article 21-2 of the E-Commerce Act took effect on 14 February 2025, prohibiting five practices including drip pricing, hidden renewal, misleading hierarchy, obstruction of cancellation, and repeated interference (Korea Fair Trade Commission)
  • Bans on signal contamination — as with the reviews rule above, raising the cost of producing a proxy signal by fiat

The limits are equally clear.

  • Jurisdiction — the producing side can choose a jurisdiction and the receiving side cannot. Enforcement paths lengthen across borders
  • Speed — rules move after a practice has been defined, and practices change while the definition is being written
  • Enforcement capacity — a rule existing and a rule being enforced are different things. Concluding from existence alone overstates the effect
  • Boundary calls — the line between an inconvenient design and a deceptive one varies case by case and takes time to draw

The provisions above have force only in their own jurisdictions. Scope, commencement dates and penalty levels differ by country, so there is no basis for assuming that a rule in one place works the same way elsewhere.

What an individual can actually do

Checking everything is impossible and there is no need to try. What works instead is concentrating the verification cost on decisions that cannot be undone. What follows is not a list of techniques. Techniques keep changing and habits do not, so habits are the part worth changing.

  • Ask first whether it can be undone. This question comes before the amount. If it can be undone, decide quickly; if not, decide slowly
  • Delay any decision that arrives with time pressure. The inability to delay is itself material for judgment. Genuinely urgent things are usually still there tomorrow
  • Re-enter through your usual route rather than the one presented. Open the app directly or use a saved address
  • Verify from outside the same screen. A contact number printed on a screen cannot validate that screen
  • Look at the exit before signing up. Do not walk into a room whose door you have not located
  • Keep recurring payments in one list. Reviewing it twice a year clears out a surprising amount
  • Set a monetary threshold in advance. One rule about sleeping on anything above a certain figure is enough. Deciding in the moment is already too late
  • Explain it out loud to one person. Gaps appear while explaining. It is the act of explaining, not the other person's judgment, that does the work
  • Make a reversible payment method the default. Whether a dispute route exists is decisive for recovery afterwards
  • Reduce notifications. Much of the time pressure arrives from outside, and most of that is manufactured by notifications
  • Do not lead with self-blame if caught. Shame delays reporting and freeze requests, and delay shrinks the chance of recovery

The list looks long but it is really two things: ask first whether it is reversible, and keep control of your own time. The rest is application.

But being left with only suspicion is also a loss

If all that remains after reading this is suspicion, that too is a cost this structure produced.

Suspicion carries a price. Checking spends time, and repeated, it spends relationships. A stance of distrusting everything does not push away only deception. It also pushes away ordinary transactions, cooperation with people you have not met, and things that are useless if they arrive late, such as public notices and disaster information. The detector story above applies to individuals unchanged: lowering the threshold reduces what slips through, but the innocent side pays.

What trust used to produce is worth noting as well. The small transactions and high-volume transactions that were possible because transaction costs were low, collaborations that could start with someone unknown, information that moved without verification. As trust thins these disappear quietly, and afterwards it is hard even to remember they existed.

Cynicism is comfortable because it requires no judgment. Decide that everything is false and there is nothing left to weigh. But that is the abandonment of judgment rather than judgment itself, a trade in which the verification cost is saved by setting down the capacity to judge.

The goal is therefore not to believe less but to decide where verification gets spent. Concentrating it where things cannot be undone and letting the rest pass works better than distrusting everything a little. Suspicion is a resource, and a resource spread evenly reaches nowhere.

What could not be confirmed

  • The total scale of online deception losses in South Korea. Domestic tallies differ by agency in scope and definition, so they could not be placed on the same axis as the US report data cited above
  • Actual enforcement counts and deterrent effect since Article 21-2 of the E-Commerce Act took effect
  • Public measurements of how much the total volume of deception attempts changed before and after generative tools became widespread
  • Review staffing levels and adjudication accuracy by platform. Disclosure standards differ enough that comparison does not hold
  • The post that prompted this argues that this economy is large, but presents no figures with a named source and year

Summing up

The deceiving side keeps winning not because it is cleverer. Producing costs once and checking costs every person, every time. The costs of failure and of reputation lean the same way, and even learning accumulates on one side only.

So the places to intervene split into three layers. Rules shift the direction of costs through disclosure duties and design regulation. Platforms change what rises by changing what counts as success. Individuals save their own costs by concentrating verification on decisions that cannot be undone. All three share the property of not demanding more attention from people.

There is no reason to end in pessimism. The disclosure duties, design regulation and review rules described above all came into being within the last few years. How large the effect is remains to be seen, but the fact that the structure is something that can be worked on has itself been demonstrated.

One thing is worth stating plainly. Being fooled is not a personal defect. Design narrowed the conditions for judging, and that design was chosen by someone. What was chosen can be chosen again.
