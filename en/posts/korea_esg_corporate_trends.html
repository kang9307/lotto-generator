Introduction: The Shift in Global Business Paradigms and ESG Management

One of the most prominent changes in the global business environment over the past decade has been the rise of ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) management. Gone are the days when a company's value was measured solely by its financial performance and profitability. Today, investors consider a company's environmental impact, social responsibility, and the transparency and soundness of its governance as critical factors in their investment decisions.

This global trend is no exception for Korean companies. South Korea's export-driven economic structure makes it particularly sensitive to international standards and regulations. New environmental rules like the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) and the US Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) have made ESG management not a choice but a necessity for Korean corporations.

This article takes an in-depth look at how South Korea's major companies are responding to ESG management, the current state of the domestic ESG ecosystem, and the outlook and challenges ahead. By comprehensively analyzing corporate case studies, government policy trends, and investment patterns, we present a picture of where Korean ESG management stands today and where it is heading.

1. The Concept of ESG Management and Global Trends

What Is ESG?

ESG refers to three core areas for evaluating a company's non-financial performance:

  • Environmental (E): Evaluates the impact of a company's business activities on the natural environment. This includes carbon emissions, energy efficiency, resource utilization, waste management, and biodiversity conservation.
  • Social (S): Examines how a company interacts with its various stakeholders, including employees, customers, suppliers, and local communities. Working conditions, diversity and inclusion, human rights, product safety, data protection, and community contributions fall under this category.
  • Governance (G): Concerns how a company is run and managed. Board diversity and independence, management transparency, ethical business practices, anti-corruption policies, and shareholder rights protection are all evaluated.

Global ESG Trends and Tightening Regulations

In recent years, ESG has moved from buzzword to the core of real business strategy and regulation. Key global trends include:

  • A Paradigm Shift in Investment: Global asset managers are treating ESG performance as a key factor in investment decisions. Larry Fink, CEO of BlackRock — the world's largest asset manager — has consistently emphasized ESG's importance in his annual letters to CEOs, declaring in 2021 that "climate risk is investment risk."
  • Mandatory ESG Disclosure: The EU's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) began making ESG information disclosure mandatory for large companies in 2024, but the Omnibus amendment in force since March 2026 narrowed its scope (see the section on developments since publication). The climate disclosure rules the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) adopted in 2024 are no longer defended by the SEC since March 2025, and a rescission was proposed in 2026, while the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) is developing global ESG reporting standards.
  • Carbon Neutrality Targets: Net-zero by 2050 has become a global agenda. Both governments and major corporations are declaring Net-Zero targets and establishing specific reduction plans through international initiatives like the Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi).
  • Mandatory Supply Chain Due Diligence: The EU's Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD) requires companies to identify and manage human rights and environmental risks within their supply chains. The 2026 Omnibus amendment has since changed its scope and timing (see the section on developments since publication).
  • Crackdown on Greenwashing: Scrutiny of corporate ESG claims is intensifying. There is a clear trend toward demanding tangible results and evidence beyond mere declarations or marketing.

Impact on Korean Companies

These global ESG trends are directly affecting Korean companies:

  • Export Barriers: The EU's CBAM has applied under its definitive regime since 1 January 2026. Exports of cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity and hydrogen to the EU incur carbon costs, creating a direct cost burden for major Korean exporters.
  • Investment Access: As global investors concentrate capital on companies with strong ESG performance, Korean companies face mounting pressure to strengthen ESG management in order to attract investment.
  • Compliance Costs: As ESG-related regulations multiply at home and abroad, companies face rising compliance costs. Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in particular may find it difficult to absorb these additional expenses.
  • Supply Chain Risks: As global corporations demand ESG compliance from their supply chain partners, Korean SME suppliers face additional pressure.

Against this backdrop, Korean companies have begun to view ESG management not as mere philanthropy or a PR tool, but as a core strategy that can determine their survival and competitiveness.

2. ESG Management Practices at Major Korean Companies

Company-by-Company ESG Strategies and Achievements

Note: the company targets and dates below are as described at publication in June 2025 and could not be re-verified against each company's official materials in this update. Do not use them as a basis for evaluating companies or for investment decisions.

Samsung Electronics

Samsung Electronics, South Korea's representative global company, established its "Samsung Electronics Sustainability Management" ESG framework in 2022.

  • Environmental (E): Targeting carbon neutrality across all business sites by 2050. The goal of expanding renewable energy usage in manufacturing to 100% by 2025 is as stated at publication and could not be re-verified against company sources; investing in product energy efficiency and resource circulation systems. Recently focused on reducing water usage and increasing recycling in semiconductor manufacturing.
  • Social (S): Emphasizes supply chain human rights protection and diversity promotion. Since 2022, introduced a supplier ESG evaluation system expanding ESG management from tier-1 to tier-2 and tier-3 suppliers. Runs the "Samsung Solve for Tomorrow" digital education program worldwide.
  • Governance (G): Since 2018, worked to enhance board independence and diversity. Established an ESG committee for board-level ESG performance oversight.

SK Group

SK Group is widely regarded as one of the most aggressive proponents of ESG management among Korean conglomerates.

  • Environmental (E): Set a 2050 net-zero target with "portfolio transformation" as its core strategy. SK Innovation is accelerating the transition to an eco-friendly battery and materials company, aiming for 70% of revenue from battery and green materials by 2030. SK E&S is making large-scale investments in renewable energy and hydrogen.
  • Social (S): Placed "Social Value" creation at the heart of its corporate mission. Since 2019, practices "Double Bottom Line" management, measuring social value alongside financial performance. Supports social enterprises through the "SK Impact" program.
  • Governance (G): Linked ESG performance to executive evaluation and compensation since 2021. SK Inc. appointed a Chief Risk Officer (CRO) for ESG to oversee group-wide ESG management.

Hyundai Motor Group

Facing demands for decarbonization in the automotive industry, Hyundai Motor Group is strengthening ESG management alongside its pivot to electric vehicles.

  • Environmental (E): Targets carbon neutrality by 2045 and plans a 100% EV transition in the European market by 2040. Actively investing in the hydrogen ecosystem, developing eco-friendly materials, and building circular economy systems.
  • Social (S): Strengthening supply chain ESG management, focusing on human rights and environmental risk management in battery raw material sourcing. Working on "universal mobility" for people with limited mobility.
  • Governance (G): Established an ESG committee within the board in 2021. Considering a holding company structure and enhancing shareholder return policies.

LG Group

LG Group is advancing its ESG management framework alongside green business portfolio expansion.

  • Environmental (E): LG Chem targets carbon neutrality by 2050 and plans 70% renewable energy usage by 2030. LG Electronics is pursuing its "Carbon Neutral 2030" plan to reduce emissions across the entire product lifecycle.
  • Social (S): Focuses on solving social problems through IT technology. Runs support programs for people with disabilities and young people through its "Life's Good" campaign.
  • Governance (G): In 2021, established ESG committees and strengthened dedicated ESG organizations. Improving internal control systems and reinforcing ethical management.

ESG Response by Industry

Semiconductors/Electronics

  • Key Issues: Water usage, energy consumption, greenhouse gas emissions (PFCs), and supply chain management of rare minerals.
  • Current Response: Samsung Electronics and SK hynix are accelerating energy efficiency and renewable energy adoption in fabs. SK hynix is branding "eco-friendly memory semiconductors."
  • Distinctive Strategy: RE100 commitments and active carbon footprint measurement at each manufacturing stage.

Automotive/Battery Industry

  • Key Issues: ICE-to-EV transition, human rights and environmental concerns in battery raw material sourcing, battery recycling.
  • Current Response: Hyundai Motor, Kia, LG Energy Solution, and Samsung SDI are strengthening battery supply chain due diligence and preparing for "battery passport" adoption.
  • Distinctive Strategy: Building a "battery value chain" from resource circulation perspective, investing in ESS applications for used batteries.

Financial Industry

  • Key Issues: Responsible investment, climate risk management, ESG investment product development, green finance activation.
  • Current Response: KB Financial, Shinhan Financial, and Hana Financial have joined Equator Principles and PRB, restricting investment in coal power. Expanding ESG bond issuance.
  • Distinctive Strategy: Developing proprietary ESG evaluation models and running SME ESG management support programs.

Heavy Industry/Chemicals

  • Key Issues: High carbon emissions, energy-intensive processes, EU CBAM compliance.
  • Current Response: POSCO investing in hydrogen-based steelmaking. HD Hyundai expanding into eco-friendly ships and offshore wind. LG Chem and Lotte Chemical accelerating bio-plastics development.
  • Distinctive Strategy: "Green transformation" through technology development and portfolio restructuring. Active participation in hydrogen economy ecosystem.

Characteristics and Limitations

Characteristics

  • Rapid Top-Down Implementation: ESG driven by strong executive commitment among large conglomerates.
  • Active Adoption of Global Standards: Actively joining RE100, SBTi, TCFD initiatives.
  • Integration with Portfolio Transformation: ESG used as a catalyst for new business opportunities.
  • Spreading Across the Value Chain: Large corporations strengthening supplier ESG management.

Limitations and Challenges

  • ESG Washing Concerns: Some companies focus more on PR than substantive change.
  • SME Capability Gaps: SMEs lack personnel, funding, and expertise for ESG compliance.
  • Short-Term Focus: Some prioritize visible short-term results over long-term transformation.
  • Measurement Difficulty: Quantifying ESG performance, especially social value, remains challenging.
  • Insufficient Stakeholder Communication: Engagement with employees, suppliers, and communities is still lacking.

3. South Korea's ESG Ecosystem and Government Policies

ESG Policy and Regulatory Trends

The Green New Deal and Carbon Neutrality Policy

The "Korean Green New Deal" (2020) is a comprehensive policy package for transitioning to a low-carbon economy. The government declared "2050 Carbon Neutrality" in December 2020, followed by the "2030 NDC" setting a 40% reduction from 2018 levels by 2030. That figure is as stated at publication; whether the target has since been revised could not be confirmed.

K-Taxonomy and Green Finance

The "K-Taxonomy" (Korean Green Taxonomy, 2021) establishes criteria for environmentally sustainable economic activities. The Financial Services Commission presented a roadmap for mandatory ESG disclosure:

  • From 2028 (FY2027): KOSPI-listed companies with consolidated assets of KRW 10 trillion or more
  • From 2029: expanded to consolidated assets of KRW 5 trillion or more
  • From 2030: expansion to KRW 2 trillion or more under review (Scope 3 deferred by three years; third-party assurance from 2030)

SME ESG Support

The Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy released the "K-ESG Guidelines" jointly with other ministries in December 2021, and, as described at publication, SME support programs include ESG consulting, financial support, certification, and education for SMEs.

  • ESG management consulting: Professional consulting for SME ESG diagnosis and improvement plans
  • ESG financial support: Financial benefits such as low-interest loans and guarantee preferences for ESG-strong SMEs
  • ESG certification support: Development of SME-tailored ESG evaluation models and certification support
  • ESG education and information: ESG training programs for SME CEOs and practitioners

ESG Investment and Evaluation

Korea's ESG investment market has grown quickly since 2020, and institutional investors including the National Pension Service have expanded ESG investment. The market for ESG bonds such as green, social and sustainability bonds has grown alongside it.

Domestic ESG rating agencies including KCGS, Sustinvest, and Daishin Economic Research Institute are actively operating, though improvements in methodological consistency and transparency are needed.

Challenges for the ESG Ecosystem

Korea's ESG ecosystem is growing quickly, but the following challenges remain.

  • Reliability and standardization of ESG data: ESG disclosure is increasing, but data quality and comparability keep being questioned.
  • Shortage of ESG specialists: A lack of specialists limits capability building at companies and financial institutions.
  • Insufficient SME support systems: Support systems for SME ESG responses are not yet fully in place.
  • Consistency and predictability of government policy: Policy changes with changes of government can create uncertainty for long-term corporate ESG strategies.

4. Future Outlook and Strategic Direction

Note: this section is the outlook as of the June 2025 publication. Later changes to the rules are listed separately in the section on developments since publication.

Standardization and Expansion of Mandatory ESG Disclosure

As ISSB standards become the global standard, companies must build data management systems, strengthen internal capabilities, and apply the "Double Materiality" principle.

  • Build data management systems: Set up systems to measure, analyze and manage ESG performance systematically.
  • Strengthen internal capabilities: Raise in-house ESG disclosure expertise and work with outside experts where needed.
  • Apply the double materiality principle: Disclose not only what affects enterprise value but also the company's impact on the environment and society, as stressed in the EU CSRD.

Increasing Decarbonization Pressure

RE100 participation, supply chain carbon management (Scope 3), and investment in CCUS and hydrogen energy are essential responses.

  • Expand renewable energy: Wider use of renewable energy through RE100 participation and power purchase agreements (PPAs) is essential.
  • Manage supply chain carbon: Measuring and managing Scope 3 emissions (indirect emissions across the value chain) will become important.
  • Invest in carbon reduction technology: Expand investment in innovative technologies such as CCUS (carbon capture, utilization and storage) and hydrogen energy.

Integrated ESG Business Models

Purpose-driven management, circular economy models, and sustainable product innovation will become key competitive differentiators.

  • Purpose-driven management: Linking corporate purpose with ESG will spread.
  • Circular economy models: Business models built on resource circulation, product life-cycle management, reuse and recycling will expand.
  • Sustainable product innovation: Developing products and services that reflect ESG values will become a core element of competitiveness.

Strengthening Stakeholder Capitalism

Expanded employee engagement, stronger supply chain partnerships, and co-existence with communities will be prioritized.

  • Expanded employee engagement: Employee participation and initiative will matter more in ESG management.
  • Stronger supply chain partnerships: ESG cooperation with suppliers will matter more, and large companies should support the ESG capabilities of smaller suppliers.
  • Co-existence with local communities: Business models and social value activities that prosper together with communities will be strengthened.

Strategic Directions

Enterprise-Wide ESG Integration

  • Strengthen ESG governance: Strengthen board-level ESG oversight and clarify C-level ESG responsibility and authority.
  • Link to performance evaluation and pay: Tie ESG results to executive and employee evaluation and compensation to strengthen motivation.
  • Enhance cross-departmental collaboration: Treat ESG as a company-wide task rather than one department's job.

Extending ESG to SMEs

  • Industry ecosystem approach: Large companies should lead industry ESG councils and raise ESG capability across the whole supply chain.
  • Tailored ESG frameworks: Practical, phased ESG guidelines that reflect SME realities are needed.
  • Expanded ESG incentives: Expand incentives such as financial support and procurement preferences for ESG-strong SMEs.

Stakeholder Communication

  • Enhanced ESG communication: Move beyond formal report publication and communicate ESG results through varied channels.
  • Expanded stakeholder participation: Gather opinions and provide participation opportunities when setting and carrying out ESG strategy.
  • Improved ESG literacy: Education and information are needed to raise ESG understanding among consumers, investors and civil society.

ESG Innovation and Differentiation

  • ESG-based new business development: Develop new business models that address climate change, resource depletion and social issues.
  • Industry-specific ESG leadership: Build global ESG leadership in each industry to secure a competitive edge.
  • Stronger ESG-related R&D: Expand R&D investment in eco-friendly and social-innovation technologies.

Conclusion: The Role of Korean Companies in Building a Sustainable Future

ESG management is no longer optional. With tightening global regulations, shifting investor and consumer expectations, and growing business uncertainty, ESG has become a core element for long-term corporate survival and prosperity.

Korean companies have made meaningful progress — building ESG frameworks, declaring carbon neutrality targets, and accelerating green portfolio transitions. Yet the road ahead is long. Internalizing ESG management, extending it to SMEs, strengthening stakeholder communication, and leveraging ESG for innovation remain key challenges.

Ultimately, ESG management is a journey toward balancing profit-seeking with social responsibility. When Korean companies achieve economic performance and social value creation simultaneously, truly sustainable growth becomes possible.

Developments Since Publication (Checked 2026-09-29)

The body of this article reflects what was written at publication in June 2025. Only developments confirmed in official documents since then are listed here. Nothing here is an assessment of any company or investment advice.

  • Korean ESG disclosure: The final plan announced by the Financial Services Commission on 8 July 2026 makes disclosure mandatory in 2028 (FY2027) for KOSPI-listed companies with consolidated assets of KRW 10 trillion or more, with expansion to KRW 5 trillion or more in 2029 and KRW 2 trillion or more in 2030 under review. Scope 3 is deferred by three years (2031, 2032 and 2033 respectively) and third-party assurance becomes mandatory from 2030. The reporting standards are those of the Korea Sustainability Standards Board (KSSB). The progress of follow-up steps such as amending the Financial Investment Services and Capital Markets Act could not be confirmed this time.
  • EU CSRD and CSDDD: The Omnibus I Directive (Directive (EU) 2026/470) was published in the Official Journal on 26 February 2026 and entered into force on 18 March 2026. CSRD now covers companies with more than 1,000 employees and net turnover above EUR 450 million; for non-EU groups the tests are EU net turnover above EUR 450 million (parent) and EUR 200 million for the EU subsidiary or branch. CSDDD covers EU companies with more than 5,000 employees and worldwide net turnover above EUR 1.5 billion, and non-EU companies with at least EUR 1.5 billion of net turnover in the EU. Member States must transpose by 26 July 2028 and apply from 26 July 2029.
  • EU CBAM: Since 1 January 2026 CBAM has applied under its definitive regime, covering cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity and hydrogen. Importers face authorisation, reporting, and purchase and surrender of CBAM certificates. Details such as the exemption threshold and the certificate sales schedule could not be confirmed against EU primary texts this time.
  • US SEC climate disclosure: The rules adopted on 6 March 2024 were no longer defended by the SEC from 27 March 2025, and in 2026 the SEC proposed rescinding them in their entirety. Whether the rescission has been finalised could not be confirmed.
