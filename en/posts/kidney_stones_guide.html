Kidney Stones Complete Guide — Symptoms, Causes, Treatment & Prevention

From excruciating flank pain to blood in the urine — everything you need to know about kidney and ureteral stones in 2026

2026-06-29 Health

Introduction: Why Kidney Stones Are Called One of the Worst Pains

Kidney stones (urolithiasis) are among the most common urological conditions worldwide. Roughly 10% of adults will experience a kidney stone at some point in their lives, and rates have been steadily rising alongside the spread of Western dietary habits. When a stone becomes lodged in the ureter, the resulting renal colic is frequently compared in severity to childbirth or a heart attack — a pain so intense that patients often cannot find a comfortable position.

This guide, updated to June 2026, draws on information from the NIH National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK), Mayo Clinic, and peer-reviewed research to walk you through everything you need to know: what causes stones to form, how to recognize them, how they are treated, and — crucially — how to keep them from coming back. This is general health information and does not substitute for evaluation by a urologist or healthcare provider.

1. What Are Kidney Stones?

A kidney stone forms when minerals and salts dissolved in urine — primarily calcium, oxalate, uric acid, or phosphate — become too concentrated and crystallize. The crystals stick together and grow into hard deposits that can range from a grain of sand to a golf ball. Stones that remain in the kidney may cause no symptoms; problems arise when a stone migrates into the ureter (the narrow tube connecting kidney and bladder) and obstructs urine flow.

Stones located in the kidney are called nephrolithiasis or renal calculi. When they move into the ureter, they are called ureterolithiasis. Together these are referred to as urolithiasis, commonly known as kidney stones or urinary stones.

2. Causes and Risk Factors

Stone formation is multifactorial — no single cause accounts for all cases. The main drivers are:

2.1 Dehydration

Insufficient fluid intake is the single most important modifiable risk factor. When urine volume is low, it becomes highly concentrated, allowing mineral supersaturation and crystal formation. People who live in hot climates, exercise heavily, or work outdoors are at particular risk.

2.2 Diet

  • High sodium: Excess salt increases urinary calcium excretion, raising calcium stone risk.
  • Excess animal protein: Red meat, poultry, and seafood increase urinary uric acid and calcium while reducing urinary citrate — all unfavorable for stone prevention.
  • High-oxalate foods: Spinach, rhubarb, almonds, peanuts, chocolate, black tea, and beets are high in oxalate. In people prone to calcium oxalate stones, excess oxalate elevates stone risk.
  • Sugary drinks: High-fructose beverages and regular soda are associated with increased stone risk in multiple studies.

2.3 Medical Conditions

  • Hypercalciuria: Elevated urinary calcium, sometimes from hyperparathyroidism or certain medications.
  • Gout (hyperuricemia): High uric acid levels promote uric acid stone formation.
  • Chronic diarrhea and malabsorption: Crohn's disease and similar conditions reduce urinary calcium and increase oxalate absorption.
  • Recurrent urinary tract infections: Specific bacteria produce urease, which promotes struvite stone growth.
  • Obesity, diabetes, metabolic syndrome: All independently increase kidney stone risk.

2.4 Additional Risk Factors

  • Personal or family history of kidney stones
  • Certain medications (excess calcium supplements, vitamin D toxicity, some diuretics and anticonvulsants)
  • Anatomical abnormalities of the urinary tract
  • Cystinuria (a rare genetic disorder)

4. Symptoms — How to Recognize a Kidney Stone

4.1 Classic Symptoms

  • Severe flank or side pain (renal colic): Sudden, intense cramping in the side or back below the ribs, often radiating toward the lower abdomen, groin, or inner thigh. The pain typically comes in waves.
  • Hematuria (blood in urine): Urine may appear pink, red, or brown. Microscopic hematuria (invisible to the naked eye) is also common.
  • Frequent, urgent urination with burning: More prominent as the stone approaches the bladder.
  • Nausea and vomiting: Often accompany severe pain episodes.
  • Fever and chills: Signal a possible infection — seek urgent care.

4.2 Silent Stones

Stones that remain stationary in the kidney may cause no symptoms at all and are often discovered incidentally on imaging done for other reasons. However, a silent stone can become symptomatic at any time if it shifts into the ureter.

Seek emergency care immediately if you experience:
  • Pain so severe you cannot sit still or find a comfortable position
  • Fever above 38.5 °C (101.3 °F) with chills — possible pyelonephritis or sepsis
  • Complete inability to urinate (suspected bilateral obstruction or single-kidney blockage)
  • Severe nausea/vomiting preventing any oral fluid intake

5. Diagnosis

5.1 Imaging

  • Non-contrast CT (NCCT): The gold-standard study for kidney stones. Identifies stone size, location, and number, including radiolucent uric acid stones not visible on plain X-ray.
  • Ultrasound: No radiation; preferred for pregnant women and children. Detects kidney stones and hydronephrosis but may miss small ureteral stones.
  • Plain abdominal X-ray (KUB): Quick and widely available; shows calcium stones but misses radiolucent stones.

5.2 Laboratory Tests

  • Urinalysis: detects blood, crystals, and signs of infection
  • Blood tests: kidney function (creatinine), calcium, uric acid, electrolytes
  • Stone analysis: if you pass a stone, collect it for laboratory composition analysis — this is invaluable for tailored prevention

6. Treatment Options

6.1 Watchful Waiting with Medical Expulsive Therapy

Approximately 70% of stones 5 mm or smaller pass spontaneously. Alpha-blockers (such as tamsulosin) relax ureteral smooth muscle and are commonly prescribed to facilitate stone passage. Pain is managed with NSAIDs or, for severe episodes, opioid analgesics. Drinking 2–3 liters of fluid per day and staying moderately active support natural passage.

6.2 Extracorporeal Shock Wave Lithotripsy (ESWL)

ESWL focuses externally generated shock waves on the stone through the skin, fragmenting it into small pieces that pass in urine. No incision is needed; the procedure is performed on an outpatient basis under mild sedation or analgesia. ESWL works best for stones up to 2 cm, particularly calcium oxalate stones in the upper ureter or kidney. Average success rates are approximately 80%, though multiple sessions may be required. Fragments must be monitored until fully cleared.

6.3 Ureteroscopy (URS/URSL)

A thin, flexible scope is passed through the urethra and bladder into the ureter. A holmium laser breaks the stone into fragments that are either removed with a basket or allowed to pass. URS is highly effective for distal ureteral stones and cases where ESWL has failed. It typically requires general or spinal anesthesia and a brief hospital stay.

6.4 Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy (PCNL)

A small incision is made in the back and a nephroscope is inserted directly into the kidney. PCNL is the preferred approach for large stones (greater than 2 cm) or staghorn calculi filling the renal pelvis. It requires general anesthesia and hospitalization but clears large stone burdens in a single procedure.

7. Prevention — The Most Important Step

Kidney stones recur: roughly 50% of patients have another episode within 5 years and up to 80% within 10 years. Prevention is therefore as important as treatment.

7.1 Fluid Intake — The Single Most Effective Measure

The NIDDK recommends producing at least 2–2.5 liters of urine per day. Drink enough so that your urine is pale yellow rather than dark. Water is best; lemonade and orange juice provide citrate that inhibits calcium stone formation. Avoid excess soda and sugary drinks.

7.2 Sodium Restriction

Limit sodium to less than 2,000 mg per day (about 5 g of table salt). Reducing sodium lowers urinary calcium excretion and directly cuts calcium stone risk. Watch hidden sodium in processed foods, restaurant meals, and canned goods.

7.3 Moderate Animal Protein

Keep total animal protein within recommended daily amounts. Excess meat, poultry, and seafood raise urinary uric acid and calcium, while lowering urinary citrate — a triple threat for stone formation.

7.4 Calcium — Don't Over-Restrict

Counterintuitively, dietary calcium is protective: it binds oxalate in the gut and prevents its absorption into the bloodstream and urine. Aim for 1,000–1,200 mg of calcium per day from food sources (dairy, fortified foods). Avoid high-dose calcium supplements without medical supervision, as supplemental calcium taken between meals can increase stone risk.

7.5 Oxalate Management (for Calcium Oxalate Stone Formers)

If you have a history of calcium oxalate stones, moderate your intake of high-oxalate foods: spinach, rhubarb, almonds, peanuts, chocolate, black tea, and beets. Eating these foods alongside calcium-rich foods helps bind oxalate in the gut before it is absorbed.

7.6 Maintain a Healthy Weight

Obesity independently increases kidney stone risk. Regular physical activity and a balanced diet that maintains a healthy BMI contribute meaningfully to stone prevention. Avoid crash diets and sudden large increases in protein intake.

7.7 Prevention Checklist

  • Drink enough fluids so your urine is consistently light yellow
  • Limit sodium to under 2,000 mg per day
  • Eat animal protein in moderation
  • Get calcium from food, not high-dose supplements
  • If you have oxalate stones, eat high-oxalate foods with a calcium source
  • Maintain a healthy body weight
  • Save any passed stones for composition analysis

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Q. How long does kidney stone pain last?

Duration depends on stone size and location. A small stone may pass and resolve pain within hours to a few days. Larger obstructing stones can cause intermittent severe pain until removed or passed — sometimes weeks if untreated. Effective pain management and sometimes a ureteral stent are used to bridge to definitive treatment.

Q. Is kidney stone pain different from muscle pain?

Muscle pain typically worsens with movement or palpation of the affected area and tends to be constant. Kidney stone pain (renal colic) comes in waves, does not improve with positional changes, and is often accompanied by nausea and hematuria. Accurate differentiation requires imaging.

Q. Can I prevent stones with medication alone?

Medications can help for specific stone types: thiazide diuretics and potassium citrate for calcium stones, allopurinol for uric acid stones, long-term low-dose antibiotics for struvite stones, and cystine-reducing agents for cystine stones. However, dietary and lifestyle changes remain the foundation of prevention regardless of which medications are prescribed.

Q. What does it feel like when a kidney stone passes?

As the stone moves from the ureter into the bladder, flank pain often suddenly subsides or significantly diminishes. Urinary frequency and burning may intensify briefly. Many people describe passing the stone itself as feeling a tiny, gritty particle in the urinary stream. Straining urine through a fine mesh or coffee filter makes it easier to collect the stone for testing.
