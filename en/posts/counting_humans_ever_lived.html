Where the Figure of 100 Billion Humans Comes From - The Inputs and Assumptions Behind the Estimate

Starting point, population benchmarks and birth rates are the three inputs, and only one of them really moves the total

2026-09-05 Trends & Culture

The sentence that a hundred billion people have lived on Earth is everywhere. It turns up in documentary narration, in the opening minutes of astronomy lectures, and lately in the description of a website that picks one of those hundred billion at random and shows them to you. The sentence is short and the number is round. Round numbers travel easily, and numbers that travel easily end up moving without a source attached.

Yet this value was never counted. Birth registration exists only at the very tail end of human history, and everything before that is an interval filled in by calculation. The hundred billion is not an observation but the output of a model, and models take inputs chosen by people. Follow those inputs, ask where each came from and how far each could move, and it becomes clear what this number is actually saying.

Every figure below appears as printed in a publicly available primary source, together with the organisation that published it and the year it was published. Where a source gives no value, nothing has been supplied by inference; those gaps are collected near the end. Derived figures state which published number the arithmetic started from.

The gap between the round number and the real one

The primary source most often reached for on this question is a short explainer from the Population Reference Bureau, an American population research organisation. It is titled 「How Many People Have Ever Lived on Earth?」, and the most recent revision carries the date of 15 November 2022 with Toshiko Kaneda and Carl Haub as authors.

The value that revision gives is 117,020,448,575. That is roughly 117 billion, not 100 billion. The 17 billion lost to rounding is not a trivial remainder either. In the same table, everyone born from 1750 onward comes to roughly 19.5 billion, so what gets discarded to make the number tidy is nearly the whole of humanity born since industrialisation.

The point here is not to quibble over digits, and a round number is a perfectly reasonable thing to say out loud. It lies elsewhere. The value of 100 billion carries neither a publisher nor a publication date. Without those there is no way to ask which assumptions it rests on, and with no way to ask, there is no way to set it beside another figure and compare. What happens to a number once its conditions fall away is covered separately in what it takes for a sentence to become a proposition. The work here is to put those conditions back one at a time.

This figure is a model, not a count

Systems that register every birth vary by country, but at the earliest they are a product of the modern era. For everything before that there is simply no ledger to add up. Producing a total number of births is therefore not an exercise in summing records but an exercise in replacing missing records with a structure.

The structure PRB uses is plain. Cut human history into a handful of intervals, pin a population benchmark at the start and end of each, and choose one birth rate to apply across it. That yields a birth count per interval, and adding them all together produces the total. Nothing about the procedure is obscure, and that transparency is precisely what makes it worth examining rather than simply quoting.

births in an interval = person-years in that interval x birth rate / 1,000

  person-years  = length of interval in years x average population
  total births  = the sum of every interval

  worked example: 8000 B.C.E. to 1 C.E.
      length of interval   8,000 years
      birth rate           80 per 1,000
      published births     46,025,332,354
      -> the average population this implies
         46,025,332,354 / 0.08 / 8,000 = about 71.9 million

In this structure the only thing corresponding to observation is the benchmark population, and even that is an estimate from historical demography for anything before the modern period. The birth rate is an assumption from beginning to end. PRB does not hide this. The explainer states outright that the calculation is part science and part art, and it calls the result an estimate of about 117 billion births produced by a semi-scientific approach.

The three inputs the model needs

Because the structure is simple, there are only three inputs. Change any one of them and the total moves.

  • The starting point — from when does something count as a person. The 2022 PRB edition places a population of 2 at 190,000 B.C.E. and begins there. Earlier editions from the same organisation took 50,000 B.C. as the origin
  • Population size by era — 2 million at 50,000 B.C.E., 5 million at 8000 B.C.E., 300 million at the year 1 C.E. Figures from 1950 onward come from United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs statistics and from PRB's own World Population Data Sheet
  • Birth rate by era — the explainer states an assumption of 80 births per 1,000 people annually through 1 C.E. and 60 thereafter until 1750. The birth rate column of the table runs 50 at 1750, 40 at both 1850 and 1900, between 31 and 38 at 1950, 22 at 2000 and 17 at 2022

What matters is that the three inputs are of different kinds. The third has no corresponding observation at all. No record exists of a directly measured pre-modern birth rate, and the values of 80 and 60 are fitted backwards from two facts: that population grew very slowly, and that life expectancy was very short. The explainer states that life expectancy at birth probably averaged only about ten years for most of human history.

The boundary of a person is a matter of definition

The first input is of a different species from the other two. It is not a data problem but a definitional one. When anatomically modern humans appeared is a question that both the fossil record and genetic evidence keep revising, and it is not a field with a single settled year.

The clearest evidence of that is the same organisation moving its own origin from 50,000 B.C. to 190,000 B.C.E. between editions. One revision shifted the line by 140,000 years. Read the value accordingly: not as a settled scientific conclusion but as a convenient boundary adopted by that edition. Fortunately, as the next sections show, the choice does not disturb the total much.

There is more than one public estimate answering this question. The fact that the values differ is itself informative about what kind of number this is.

Rather than asking which value is correct, it is more accurate to read this as a number that natively lives within a band of roughly that width.

Even one organisation's own value shifts between revisions

The same thing happens to the share alive. 6.8 percent, 6.5 percent, 6 percent and 4 percent sit side by side. This ratio has both a moving numerator and a moving denominator. The denominator, total births, gets revised, and the numerator, current population, depends on which year is being used. A share quoted without the year of the population in its numerator is incomplete. Ratios that look inconsistent in the first decimal place are frequently just talking about different years.

Laid out this way, the centre of gravity is immediately visible. The three intervals from 8000 B.C.E. to 1650 generate 73 percent of the total. Everything before 8000 B.C.E. amounts to only 7.7 percent, and the whole modern era from 1750 onward comes to 16.6 percent even when added together. The longest and least documented stretch of human history contributes far less than intuition suggests, and so does the best documented recent three centuries. Both ends of the timeline are thinner than they look, and the mass sits in the middle, in the long agrarian span that rarely gets mentioned when the headline number is repeated.

The argument about the starting date barely moves the total

The first conclusion the table forces is counterintuitive. When humanity began is the most contested item in this whole subject, yet it has the smallest effect of the three inputs.

From 190,000 B.C.E. to 50,000 B.C.E. is 140,000 years, three quarters of the whole span. That interval nevertheless produces 7,856,100,000 births, 6.7 percent of the total. Move the origin back to 50,000 B.C.E. as earlier editions had it and the total becomes roughly 109 billion. Move it forward to 8000 B.C.E., around the start of farming, and it is still roughly 108 billion. Shifting the starting line by 182,000 years in either direction moves the value by under 10 percent.

The reason lies in the structure of the multiplication. Births are a product of duration and population, and the population across those intervals ranges from the hundreds to the hundreds of thousands. However much time is multiplied in, a small number times a large one does not beat a large number times a small one. The total is decided not by how long humanity existed but by how long it existed after becoming numerous. This is worth holding onto, because the instinct runs the other way: deep time feels like it ought to dominate anything measured in mere millennia, and here it does not come close.

What moves it is the birth rate of the farming millennia

If the centre of gravity is the stretch from 8000 B.C.E. to 1650, then the assumptions covering that stretch govern the total. And among those assumptions, the one with no observational support is the birth rate.

The summary is this. The most contested input moves the least, and the input nobody argues about moves the most. Shifting the origin by 140,000 years is worth 6.7 percent, while setting the farming-era birth rate at 40 instead of 80, a parameter that draws no debate at all, removes 19.7 percent. Within only the range varied here, the total wanders between about 94 billion and about 133 billion. That inversion is worth carrying away from this case on its own, because it is not peculiar to demography. Arguments cluster around the input that is easiest to have an opinion about, which is rarely the input the answer actually hangs on.

117 billion counts births, not lives

This is the point to return to the definition. The unit this model counts is one birth. How long the person lived afterwards does not enter the unit. The column headings say as much: births, and number ever born.

And the PRB explainer states that for most of human history, life expectancy at birth probably averaged only about ten years. A life expectancy of ten does not mean people generally lived to about ten and then died. It means a large share died shortly after being born while others lived long, and the average settles there. It is the value a population dominated by infant deaths produces.

Those infants are in the total, each as a full unit. Which means the 117 billion has to be read as follows.

  • What is counted — the total number of events of being born human. A child who lived a day and an elder who lived eighty each count as 1
  • What is included — newborns who died before being named, and every birth that left no record anywhere
  • What is not included — stillbirths and miscarriages are conventionally not counted as births, and the model's birth rates follow that convention
  • What follows from it — draw one of the 117 billion at random and the most likely draw is a child who did not reach adulthood

That last item is arithmetic rather than sentiment. If 73 percent of the total arises between 8000 B.C.E. and 1650, and life expectancy over that period sat around ten years, the conclusion follows. There is a distance between the habit of reading this number as a monument to human achievement and what the number is actually counting. A site that promises to introduce you to one of a hundred billion is, on the model's own definition, promising something considerably more sombre than the phrasing suggests. No specific infant mortality assumption is printed in the 2022 revision, so that gap is recorded further down rather than filled.

The table checks itself

The other virtue of the PRB table is that it carries the inputs alongside the results, which lets a reader check it from inside the table. Convert an interval's births back through its birth rate and its length and the average population that interval presumed falls out. That is the final column of the previous table.

In ten of the twelve intervals the value settles obediently between the benchmark populations. The interval from 1200 to 1650 implies an average of about 473.4 million, comfortably between its endpoints of 450 million and 500 million. The interval from 1850 to 1900 implies about 1.450 billion, between 1.265 billion and 1.656 billion. The structure is consistent.

The two intervals that fall outside the bracket are of entirely different kinds. One is 2010 to 2022, whose implied average of about 8.286 billion overshoots its upper endpoint of 7.9635 billion by roughly 4 percent. That is an explicable deviation. The birth rate fell steadily from 20 to 17 across the interval while only the closing value of 17 enters the calculation, so a low divisor inflates the population. A 4 percent discrepancy comes out of the character of the method.

The other is a different order of thing. The interval from 50,000 B.C.E. to 8000 B.C.E. implies an average population of about 340,000. Its benchmark endpoints are 2 million and 5 million. The implied figure does not reach even a sixth of the lower bound. Worse, the interval immediately preceding it, 190,000 to 50,000 B.C.E., implies about 700,000, twice as much, despite having benchmarks lower at both ends. The implied average population fell across an interval in which population rose.

Reset that interval anywhere inside its own benchmark range and the total rises by between 4.8 and 13.4 percent. There is no basis here for ruling on which reading is right, and no explanation appears in the public material. What the episode does show is that publishing the inputs made the table auditable against itself. In a table carrying only a grand total, a 4 percent discrepancy and a sixfold one are equally invisible. How published figures shift under recalculation is treated separately in a reproduction case study.

Why the half-alive claim fails

One misconception travels alongside this subject: that the people alive now are half of everyone who ever lived, or that the living outnumber the dead. Scientific American put the claim through its fact-or-fiction format on 1 March 2007 and returned a verdict of fiction, giving total births at the time as slightly over 106 billion and the living share as about 6 percent. The same piece describes a variant holding that 75 percent of everyone ever born is alive now, and traces the roots of the idea back to the 1970s.

What deserves notice is that the conclusion does not change with the publisher. 6.8 percent, 6.5 percent, 6 percent, 4 percent. Different vintages, different organisations, different assumptions, and all of them in the low single digits. When that many independent routes arrive at the same place, the conclusion is not resting on any one assumption. That is the practical dividend of setting several estimates side by side rather than adopting one. It also draws a useful line: the total is soft and the share alive is firm, and the two deserve different levels of confidence even though they come from the same table.

The arithmetic that produces the illusion

Why does the half-alive feeling seem plausible in the first place. The illusion is built out of two confusions.

The first is confusing population with births. People reason that since past populations were small, few people were born in the past. But population is a stock at an instant and births are a flow accumulating over a span. Births keep piling up even when population does not grow at all. A population of 5 million holding perfectly flat for 42,000 years at a birth rate of 80 per 1,000 produces 16.8 billion births over that span. The population at the end is identical to the population at the start.

The second is distortion of duration. Population growth over the last century is so overwhelming on a chart that the vertical climb at the right edge looks like the whole story. In the cumulative sum, though, births from 1900 onward are about 12.5 billion, 10.7 percent of the total, and births from 1950 onward are about 9.1 billion, or 7.8 percent. Visual area on a chart and share of a cumulative sum are entirely different quantities. The visible cliff shows the rate of change in population; it does not stand in for the broad, low area lying underneath it.

Three ways of getting the denominator wrong

Misconceptions in this family almost always arise on the denominator side. The numerator, current population, is common knowledge. The denominator is what goes wrong.

  • Using population as the denominator — comparing the population at some past moment with the population now, and letting that stand in for cumulative births. The two quantities were never the same kind of thing
  • Shortening the interval in the denominator — putting only remembered or studied history, generally a few thousand years, into the denominator. Since 7.7 percent of the total falls before 8000 B.C.E. the truncation is not a large error in itself, but the trouble is that the truncation goes unnoticed
  • Removing dead children from the denominator — picturing adults when picturing people. This total counts births. Which of the two is being counted changes the size of the denominator substantially

How to read the 6.8 percent

What is interesting is that the PRB explainer does not present 6.8 percent as a small value. Given a denominator spanning close to 200,000 years, the fact that the people alive at this single moment occupy nearly 7 percent of it is, in the explainer's own framing, actually a fairly large percentage.

The two readings are not in conflict. They are the same value seen from different directions.

  • Read as an answer to the half-alive claim, 6.8 percent is small. The claim does not hold
  • Read as a distribution over the whole of human time, 6.8 percent is large. Out of 200,000 years, roughly one in fourteen humans is crowded into the single moment called now
  • What separates the readings is the comparison object. The first compares against the total, the second against the span. Two true sentences attach to the same number
  • So a ratio needs a direction. Without a statement of what it is a ratio of, calling it large or small produces a sentence that cannot be checked

This structure recurs throughout population statistics. The same problem appears in an analysis of local population change. A sentence saying the rate of decline is severe and a sentence saying the absolute scale is small can both be true at once, and which one is being spoken is settled by the denominator.

Four questions to ask of any large number

What this case yields is not demographic knowledge but a way of reading. Whatever large number turns up, check first whether four things are attached to it.

  • Definition — what was counted as one unit. The 117 billion counts births, not lives lived. This single line settles perhaps half the meaning of the number
  • Interval — from when to when. Both ends are required. The PRB value ends at 2022, and its projections for 2035 and 2050 are carried as separate rows
  • Assumptions — what filled the stretches with no observation. Here it is the population benchmarks by era and the birth rates by era
  • Width — how far the value travels when those assumptions are moved within a reasonable range. In this case, from about 94 billion to about 133 billion

If even one of the four lines is missing, the number is not yet a proposition. That does not make it false; it means its truth value cannot be decided. And once an undecidable sentence gets treated as true, decisions start stacking on top of it.

Moving the same reading to other numbers

The four questions work well outside population statistics, because the structure is the same. Filling unobserved intervals with assumptions, summing interval outputs into one large figure, then detaching the figure and carrying it alone is a pattern found everywhere.

  • Market size estimates — sample surveys plus a growth assumption fill the unobserved stretch. One line of growth assumption changes the headline total by multiples
  • Cumulative savings or cumulative damages — the span chosen decides the total, and the span is usually the first thing dropped from the headline
  • Model performance and cost figures — strip away the measurement conditions and the same model's numbers tell different stories. That case is set out in how to read a published score with its conditions attached
  • Population projections — the PRB table itself continues into rows for 8,899,000,000 in 2035 and 9,752,000,000 in 2050. Those two rows are scenarios rather than observations

They share one property. A large number carries no information by itself. The information lives in the inputs that produced it and in how far it moves when those inputs are shaken. Going from 100 billion to 117 billion, and then to a band of 94 to 133 billion, costs brevity and buys the ability to actually handle the number. The band is not a confession of weakness in the estimate; it is the estimate stated honestly, and a figure quoted without one is simply a band whose edges have been hidden.

What could not be confirmed

The items below could not be settled from primary sources and were therefore left out of the body. Nothing has been filled in by inference.

  • The specific infant mortality assumption — the 2022 revision states only that life expectancy at birth averaged about ten years, and prints no figure for infant mortality by era. The share of the 117 billion who died in infancy is therefore not stated anywhere in this article
  • The basis for the 50,000 to 8000 B.C.E. interval — no explanation appears in the public material for why that interval's implied average population falls below its lower benchmark. The discrepancy is recorded as a fact without any ruling on which side is correct
  • The internal assumptions of the 1966 Keyfitz paper — the total of about 69 billion and the living share of about 4 percent are confirmed, but the starting point and birth rates the paper used sit behind a paywall, so the table carries a placeholder there
  • The range of scholarly agreement on when modern humans appeared — the move from 50,000 B.C. to 190,000 B.C.E. across PRB editions is documented, but the date range currently offered by palaeoanthropology falls outside the scope covered here and is given neither as a single year nor as a range
  • Which edition the Our World in Data figure of around 108 billion derives from — that page gives the total and the 6.5 percent share without stating a starting point or birth rate assumptions, so the corresponding cells in the comparison table are left blank
  • Consistency between the birth rate column and the prose — the explainer text states an assumption of 60 per 1,000 from 2 C.E. to 1750, while the 1750 row of the table shows 50. The table column has been followed here as the one used in the arithmetic, and the discrepancy is noted rather than resolved
