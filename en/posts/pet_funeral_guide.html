Pet Funerals in Korea - The Rules, the Steps, and the People Left Behind

Knowing in advance that a defined procedure exists means fewer decisions in the hardest hour

2026-08-01 Life/Practical

Introduction: The search box is open and you do not know what to type

Awake at an odd hour, or sitting in the car on the way back from the clinic, most people simply stay still for a long while. Then, eventually, they pick up the phone. The search box is open, but nothing comes. What am I supposed to do now, what am I even allowed to do. Those are not questions a person in that state is equipped to answer.

This article was written for that moment. It is not a guide to tidying up your grief. Grief is not a problem to be tidied up. There are, however, a handful of decisions that still have to be made, and fortunately most of them already sit inside a defined procedure. Far more items on the list are simply steps to follow than are genuine choices.

The argument of this article is a single one. What matters is not how to hold a funeral, but knowing in advance that a defined procedure exists. Knowing that reduces the number of decisions you have to make in the hardest hour. Fewer decisions means more of that time spent beside your animal.

One thing first. If your grief feels large right now, that is not strange. It is more so the longer you lived together, and the more sudden the ending. The final part of this article is not about procedure at all but about the people left behind. If all you need right now is what to do next, skip straight to the following section. Coming back later is not too late.

Before you read. The rules and statute citations here reflect Korean law and Korean administrative practice, confirmed against official government sources as of 1 August 2026. Statutes and local government ordinances change. Detailed disposition standards, fine amounts, and filing procedures are all subject to amendment. Before acting, reconfirm the current rules with your local district office and the Korean Law Information Center. This article is information, not legal advice.

Those are points that could not be verified directly in an official source, so please check them yourself through the routes indicated. No specific business or product is named in this article. 1. What to do right now

The first thing to know is this. Korean law does not require anything to be done within minutes. There is no death certificate to obtain within hours, no burial notification due within days, none of the compressed timeline that applies to human funerals. The only item with a deadline attached is the animal registration change notification, and even that allows 30 days.

So the first few hours can be spent saying goodbye. Doing so does not delay anything. The steps that follow can be taken in the order below.

1.1 On keeping the body

  • Lay the body somewhere cool and shaded. Avoid sunlit windowsills, heated floors, and the area near a radiator
  • Wrap it in soft cloth or a towel. Many people use the blanket their animal always slept on. That is fine
  • Fluids may be released from the body. This is not something to be alarmed by. It is a natural process, and an absorbent cloth underneath handles it
  • How long a body can be kept varies greatly with season and indoor temperature. This article deliberately does not state a number of hours. Asking the facility you plan to use or the clinic that treated your animal gives an accurate answer
  • If there are other animals in the home, many people let them come and say goodbye. This is a choice, not an obligation
  • Once rigor sets in, the position becomes hard to change. Laying the body in a comfortable position early makes things easier afterwards

1.2 What you must say if the death happened at a veterinary clinic

This is the single most practically important item. When death occurs at a veterinary clinic, the owner may choose whether to take the body or leave it with the clinic. But that choice is not made automatically.

  • According to the guidance published by the Ministry of Government Legislation, an animal that dies at a veterinary clinic is classified as medical waste, and the clinic may treat it in house or entrust it to a licensed waste treatment operator
  • At the same time, an owner who wishes to may take the body. Once taken, it can be discharged as household waste or brought to a licensed animal funeral facility
  • The problem is that if you say nothing while in shock, the clinic may proceed under its own default process
  • So if you want to take your animal home, say so clearly on the spot. If words will not come, someone who came with you can say it for you
  • If your animal is elderly or seriously ill, it is better to settle this with your family in advance. Section 8 returns to this

2. The three legally permitted disposition methods

This is the core of the article. In Korea there are three lawful ways to handle the body of a companion animal, and each rests on a specific statutory provision. The following is drawn from the public legal guidance portal operated by the Ministry of Government Legislation.

2.1 Using a licensed animal funeral facility

  • The Animal Protection Act treats the animal funeral business as a business requiring a permit. The government guidance cites Article 69(1)4 as the basis
  • The processing methods carried out at such facilities are described as falling under items 4(b), 4(c), and 4(d) of Article 38 of the Enforcement Rule. They are commonly called cremation, desiccation, and alkaline hydrolysis
  • Cremation uses heat, desiccation dries and sterilises before grinding, and alkaline hydrolysis breaks down tissue using an alkaline solution and heat. However, the full text of those provisions could not be opened directly while writing this article, so the precise statutory wording needs confirmation
  • What matters is whether the facility holds a permit. Section 5 covers how to check

2.2 Discharging as household waste

This section may be hard to read. It is the phrase volume-rate bag that does it. That reaction is natural, and this article will not try to argue you out of it. But the facts are set down as they are.

  • Article 2 of the Wastes Control Act explicitly lists animal carcasses among the examples of waste, and anything that is not business waste falls into the household waste category
  • A companion animal carcass therefore qualifies as household waste, and under Article 14 of the same Act it may be discharged in the manner prescribed by local government ordinance. In practice that means placing it in a volume-rate household waste bag
  • This is one of the lawful methods the law provides. Choosing it does not mean you loved your animal less. Financial circumstances, the absence of a facility in the region, and physical difficulty in travelling are all common
  • The details of discharge differ by local government ordinance. Bag type, collection point, and whether advance notification is required can all vary, so check with the district office where you live
  • Some local governments operate a separate collection route or guidance. Regional variation is large, so the specific arrangements need confirmation

2.3 Treatment as medical waste

  • Under the Wastes Control Act, medical waste means waste discharged by health and medical institutions, veterinary clinics, testing and inspection institutions and the like that requires special management
  • An animal that dies at a veterinary clinic falls into this category, and the clinic either treats it in house or entrusts it to a licensed waste treatment operator or facility operator
  • The provisions cited are Article 2 and Article 18(1) of the Wastes Control Act, Enforcement Decree Table 1 item 10 and Table 2 item 2(a), and Enforcement Rule Table 3 item 6
  • This route leaves the owner with almost no separate procedure. Telling the clinic that you wish to leave the body with them completes it
  • However, ashes cannot be returned to you. That should be understood before choosing it

3. Why informal burial is not allowed

This is the most searched item and the most misunderstood one. Burying an animal in the garden, or on the hillside you used to walk together, is not permitted under Korean law. Owning the land does not create an exception.

3.1 The statutory basis

  • Article 8(1) of the Wastes Control Act prohibits discarding waste anywhere other than a designated place
  • Article 8(2) of the same Act prohibits burying or incinerating waste outside a waste treatment facility that has been permitted, approved, or reported
  • The government guidance cites those provisions together with Article 3(1)11 of the Minor Offenses Act and Article 2 of its Enforcement Decree, and Article 68(3)1 of the Wastes Control Act
  • This article does not state penalty levels. Fixed penalty and administrative fine provisions exist, but the amounts change with amendments, so if you need them, read the current text directly at the Korean Law Information Center

3.2 Reasons that matter more than the penalty

This article does not deal with how to avoid getting into trouble. That kind of guidance is not offered here. Instead it sets out why the rule exists.

  • Soil and groundwater contamination. Substances released as a body decomposes seep into the soil and can reach groundwater. Near a water source protection area or a stream, the effect is more direct
  • Drug residue. If your animal was under treatment, chemotherapy agents, sedatives, or euthanasia drugs may remain in the tissue. Released into soil, those compounds affect other animals and plants
  • Infectious disease. Where the cause of death was an infectious disease, the body can become a route for the pathogen
  • Remains being uncovered. Shallowly buried bodies really are dug up by wildlife or other dogs. Being the person who buried them and then sees them again is the worst version of this
  • Land ownership is not permanent. Once you move or the land changes hands, you lose all control over what happens to that spot
  • Parks, hiking trails, and streams are plainly prohibited. Where the land is not yours, there is nothing to discuss

If you have already done it. Some readers will have done this before reading any of the above. It was done without knowing. That in itself is not something to punish yourself over. If you want to know what to do from here, the surest route is to explain the situation to the environmental affairs division of your local district office and ask. The reason no do-it-this-way instruction is given here is that the answer genuinely depends on the region and the circumstances.

4. Cancelling the animal registration - procedure and deadline

If your animal was covered by the registration requirement, the death must be reported. This is the only item in this article with a statutory deadline attached.

  • The deadline is 30 days from the death. This is confirmed in the Ministry of Government Legislation guidance portal
  • The cited provisions are Article 15(2)2 of the Animal Protection Act and Article 11(1)8 of its Enforcement Decree
  • Failing to report may result in an administrative fine under Article 101(4)1 of the Animal Protection Act. This article does not state the amount. It is subject to amendment, so confirm the current provision
  • There are two ways to file. Online through the national animal protection information system, or in person at the district office
  • Online filing requires identity verification. As a rule it is done in the name of the registered owner
  • If you do not know the registration number, you can look up the registration record in the system first. Finding the registration certificate or the paperwork issued at the time makes it easier

4.1 Why this deadline slips past faster than it sounds

Thirty days does not look short. In practice, though, several days pass in grief without any sense of them passing. By the time it comes to mind, the month is often already over.

  • Writing it on a calendar or setting a phone reminder on the day you come home from the funeral is usually enough to keep it from slipping
  • If you have family, you can ask someone else to handle it. If identity verification makes the online route difficult, an in-person filing is available
  • If an implanted microchip was used, how the chip is handled may differ by facility. Whether it is recovered during cremation and whether it can be returned are items needing confirmation, so ask the facility you plan to use

5. How to verify a licensed facility

If you have decided to use a funeral facility, the first thing to check is whether it holds a permit. That is not something to judge from search results or advertising. It is something you look up directly on the official system.

The lookup is done on the national animal protection information system operated by the Animal and Plant Quarantine Agency. The screen structure below was opened and confirmed on 1 August 2026.

5.2 What to look at in the list

  • Whether the facility appears at all. That is the first check. If it is absent, its licence status has to be confirmed separately
  • Which services it handles. The listing shows the specific services such as funeral, cremation, and interment. A site that only cremates differs from one that also offers interment. If you want the ashes kept at the facility, this is the column to read
  • The address. Travel distance and time turn out to matter a great deal, especially when you have to transport the body yourself
  • Phone number and website. It is safer to make enquiries through the contact details shown in the listing
  • If it does not appear, contact the animal affairs division of the local district office to confirm the actual licence status. Listing updates can lag

This article recommends no specific business. Business names, prices, and reviews are left out for two reasons. First, no officially verifiable price data exists. Second, an article that steers people toward a particular business in this situation is itself a problem. Checking the listing yourself and calling to ask is the safest route.

What to ask on the phone. Total cost and what is included, any additional charges that may arise, whether the cremation is individual or communal, whether you may be present, whether ashes can be returned, whether a booking is required, and how the body should be transported. Asking for the quote in writing or by text keeps the verbal explanation and the final bill from diverging.

6.1 What to do with the ashes

This decision does not have to be made now. Many people bring the ashes home and only settle the question months later. That is not late.

  • Keeping them at home. Placed in an urn and kept in the house. This is the most common choice
  • Interment at a facility. Placed in the facility's interment space. As noted above, check the listing for whether the facility offers interment. Duration and renewal terms differ by facility
  • Memorial stones and similar processing. Solidifying ashes into a formed object is offered, but the specifications and whether any regulation applies need confirmation
  • Scattering. For human remains the Act on Funeral Services applies, but the rules on scattering companion animal ashes could not be confirmed while writing this article. It is an item needing confirmation. If you intend to do it, ask both your local government and the funeral facility first
  • Tree burial style interment. Likewise, the statutory basis for such arrangements for companion animals needs confirmation

6.2 On paperwork

  • Some facilities issue a cremation or funeral confirmation document. However, whether this is a statutory obligation could not be confirmed. It is an item needing confirmation
  • If you held pet insurance, the insurer may require particular documents. Ask your insurer what is needed. Related material is covered in the guide to choosing pet insurance
  • Whether separate proof is needed when filing the registration cancellation may differ by local government, so it is worth checking before you file

7. The people left behind - grief is not a problem

That is the procedure. But something remains once the procedure ends. Often the period after it ends is harder. While there are things to be done, people hold up. When those things run out, that is when it becomes real.

7.1 Pet loss is ordinary grief

  • The size of the grief tracks the size of the relationship. How long you lived together, how much you leaned on each other, how much space they took up in the day. If you walked at the same hour every day for years, feeling their absence at that hour is simply what happens
  • Physical reactions are common too. Not sleeping, no appetite, crying for no reason, being unable to concentrate. All of these appear routinely in grief
  • Guilt is one of the most common feelings in bereavement. If only I had noticed earlier, if only I had not made that decision. The thought repeats. This article will not feed it. Judging a decision made with the information you had then, using the information you have now, is not a fair comparison
  • Pressure to get over it quickly does not help. Grief has no fixed duration. Some people feel steadier within weeks, others take years. Neither is strange
  • Being hurt by other people's reactions is also common. It was only an animal, you can always get another one. People really do hear this. The reason it stings is that the loss was not acknowledged. The feeling itself is not wrong
  • Talking to someone who has been through the same thing often helps. Being understood does a great deal of the work in grief

7.2 How to explain it to a child

  • Being straightforward works better. Euphemisms such as gone to sleep or gone on a long trip confuse young children. A child may become afraid of sleeping, or may keep waiting for a return
  • Use words that fit the child's age. If explaining death feels impossible, they are not hurting any more, and we cannot see them again is enough
  • Make sure the child does not feel it was their fault. Children tend to connect their own actions to events. Saying plainly that it was not their fault matters
  • Let the child say goodbye in their own way. Drawing a picture, writing a letter, sorting through photographs together all help
  • It is all right for a child to see an adult cry. It teaches them that this is something one is allowed to grieve over
  • Bringing in a new animal quickly can read to a child as a message that the old one was replaceable. Leaving some time is better

7.3 The other animals in the home

  • Animals who lived alongside them show changes too. Reduced appetite, reduced activity, searching particular places, vocalising more than usual are all reported
  • Keeping the rhythm of the day helps. It is better not to shift walk times and meal times dramatically
  • If reduced appetite or activity continues for more than a few days, it may be illness rather than a grief response. Having a veterinarian check is the safe move
  • Your own state transmits. If the remaining animal seems anxious, it may be responding to the change in you

When you need help. Grief itself is not an illness to be treated. But if daily life does not hold together for a sustained period, if you cannot sleep for an extended stretch, or if you find yourself thinking of harming yourself, that is the point at which enduring it alone is the wrong approach. Counselling is available through your local community mental health centre or a psychiatric clinic. Asking for help is not weakness; it is a reasonable judgement.

Operating bodies, contact numbers, and support programmes vary by region and by year. Confirm the current contact details through your local government website or public health centre. No specific number is printed here because helpline numbers really are consolidated and changed from time to time.

8. What is worth preparing in advance

This section is less for the person who needs it today than for the person who still has time. If your animal is elderly or under treatment, simply having read this far makes a large difference later.

The reason is simple. Judgement is impaired in the hardest hour. Starting the research from scratch at that point means every decision lands at once. Knowing in advance means that in that moment you are only executing what was already decided.

8.1 What to check now

  • Look up the licensed funeral facilities near you in advance. Search by region on the national animal protection information system. Noting the travel distance and phone number is enough
  • Find the animal registration number. Check now where you put the registration certificate or the paperwork from the time
  • Ask your regular veterinary clinic in advance. What the procedure is if death occurs there, whether the body can be released, how they handle it outside normal hours
  • Talk it through with your family in advance. Which method, who makes the calls, how costs will be handled. Families really do split on this in the moment
  • Think about transport. If you have no car, how the body would be moved, whether public transport is workable, whether the facility offers collection
  • If you hold pet insurance, check the required documents in advance

8.2 What people regret leaving too late

  • Photographs and video. Images from healthy days and ordinary days become the most valuable later. They do not have to be special occasions
  • A paw print or a lock of fur. Many people want these, and afterwards it is no longer possible
  • A voice recording. Having plenty of photographs and no sound is a common regret
  • A clip of you calling their name. One short video of them responding lasts a long time
  • Photographs of the two of you together. Having taken thousands of them and none with yourself in the frame is surprisingly common

9. Frequently asked questions

Is there anything that must be done within hours of the death?

Korean law sets no deadline measured in minutes or hours. The only statutory deadline is the 30 days for the registration change notification. That said, if the death occurred at a veterinary clinic, saying on the spot whether you want the body is what prevents the clinic default from taking effect.

What if it happens at night or over a weekend?

Keeping the body somewhere cool and making calls the next day is acceptable. However, how long a body can be kept depends heavily on season and indoor temperature, so no figure is stated here. Whether any facility takes enquiries around the clock is best checked using the phone numbers in the listing you looked up.

How much does it cost?

No figure is given in this article. No verified official price data exists. Cost varies by facility, region, body weight, the method chosen (individual or communal), and whether interment is included. Confirm the total, what is included, and any extras by phone, and ask for it in writing or by text.

My animal was never registered. Do I still need to file a cancellation?

If there was no registration, there is nothing to cancel. That said, if you cannot remember whether it was registered, you can look it up on the national animal protection information system. Registrations made through an agency in the past are sometimes not remembered by the owner.

I keep going over the neutering or euthanasia decision. Is that normal?

It is an extremely common reaction in grief. Judging a past decision by what you know now is not a fair comparison. If the thought repeats to the point that daily life is difficult, seeking counselling helps. That is not weakness but a practical choice in favour of recovery.

10. Summary

  • Almost nothing is urgent. The only statutory deadline is the registration cancellation, and that allows 30 days
  • If the death was at a clinic, the one thing to say on the spot is whether you want the body. That is the single time-sensitive item
  • There are three lawful disposition methods: a funeral facility, household waste, and medical waste. Each has a statutory basis
  • Informal burial is not permitted. Owning the land makes no difference. The real reasons are soil, groundwater, and drug residue rather than the penalty
  • Verify a funeral facility's licence on the official system first. Checking the services handled saves a wasted trip
  • What happens to the ashes can be decided later. Postponing is a choice
  • The rules get amended. Before acting, reconfirm with your district office and the Korean Law Information Center
  • And grief is not a problem. It has no set duration and no set shape. If it is hard, asking for help is allowed

If you are reading this because you need it today, you took good care of them. What has to happen today is listed above. Most of it does not have to be today.

## 5. How to verify a licensed facility

1. Open the national animal protection information system
2. Enter the business operator menu
3. In the companion animal business directory, select the funeral business category

7. If the facility is absent, contact the animal affairs division of the district office

Two things to check: whether the facility is listed at all, and which services it handles (cremation only, or interment as well).

## 6. What to ask on the phone

- Total cost and what is included
- Any additional charges that may arise
- Whether the cremation is individual or communal
- Whether you may be present
- Whether ashes can be returned
- Whether a booking is required
- How the body should be transported
- Whether the quote can be confirmed in writing or by text

No verified official price data exists. Cost varies by provider.

## 7. Individual and communal cremation

| Aspect | Individual | Communal |
|---|---|---|
| How it is carried out | One animal at a time | Several animals together |
| Return of ashes | Generally available | Individual return generally not possible |
| Owner presence | Possible at some facilities | Generally not applicable |
| Time required | Relatively longer | Relatively shorter |
| Cost | Varies by provider | Varies by provider |

Whether these two terms are defined in statute needs confirmation.

## 8. What is worth preparing in advance

- [ ] Note the licensed funeral facilities near you (national animal protection information system)
- [ ] Locate the animal registration number (certificate or original paperwork)
- [ ] Decide with family, in advance, about release of the body on clinic death
- [ ] Check local household waste discharge rules (district office ordinance)
- [ ] Decide transport (car, public transport, whether the facility collects)
- [ ] Know how to file the cancellation
- [ ] Confirm the documents your pet insurer requires
- [ ] Photographs, video, a voice recording, a paw print, pictures of the two of you together

## 10. For the people left behind

- Pet loss is ordinary grief. It has no set duration and no set shape
- Guilt is a common feeling. Judging a decision made with the information you had then, using the information you have now, is not a fair comparison
- Pressure to get over it quickly does not help
- With children, be straightforward and use words that fit their age. Avoid euphemisms. Say plainly that it was not their fault
- If a surviving animal shows reduced appetite or activity for more than a few days, have a veterinarian check
- If daily life does not hold together for a sustained period, or if you find yourself thinking of harming yourself, counselling is available at a local community mental health centre or a psychiatric clinic. Confirm current contact details through your local government website or public health centre
