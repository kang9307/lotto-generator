What Official Platform Support Actually Promises - Reading the Size of Support Through Tiers

It runs and it blocks a release are two different promises

Aug 31, 2026 Dev & Infra

Intro: What Gets Flattened Into the Words We Support It

Say a machine on a new architecture just landed on your desk, or you are deciding whether to add a platform to the standard image, or you read the line now officially supported in a release note. The next thing to establish is not whether it works. It is who has agreed to fix it when it breaks, and whether that breakage stops a release.

Both answers are written down, project by project. The trouble starts when nobody opens the document and only the phrase official support gets read. That phrase is used at three quite different sizes. It can be a promise strong enough to hold back a release. It can be a middle promise to fix things within a day. Or it can be a promise that a buildbot is running, so if it breaks you will at least see it. The third one is also called official support.

That difference in size is the subject here. Nothing about which architecture is preferable, and nothing about vendors. The axis is why tier systems exist at all, what each tier's wording actually commits to, and where that commitment stops.

What Was Decided - The Confirmed Scope

Start by cutting the news to its real size. CPython moved 64-bit RISC-V Linux into Tier 3. Here is what is verifiable.

  • The target — riscv64-unknown-linux-gnu (glibc, clang/gcc) now appears in the Tier 3 list in PEP 11
  • The contacts — that same list names Stan Ulbrych and Emma Smith
  • The hardware — per the announcement, the RISE Project provided several RISC-V machines for CPython
  • The announcement — Python Insider, 24 August 2026, written by Stan Ulbrych, whose work is noted as supported by a Sovereign Tech Agency fellowship
  • What is listed as next — integrating RISC-V directly into CPython's CI through RISE's runners, a longer-term push toward Tier 2, and architecture-specific optimizations

That is the extent of what the documents say. The announcement narrows its own scope at the end: CPython is only one part of the Python ecosystem, and packages, compilers, tooling and infrastructure all have to follow. That single line is the subject of the second half of this article.

Why Tiers Exist in the First Place

A tier system looks like bureaucracy but is really an answer to an arithmetic problem. The combinations to support multiply: instruction set times libc implementation times compiler times kernel version times build options. The number of people who can fix any of them does not even add reliably.

PEP 11 places its own rationale in the same spot. Over time, it says, the CPython source has accumulated various pieces of platform-specific code, which, at some point in time, was considered necessary to use CPython on a specific platform. The problem is the state that follows: nobody knows whether that code is still needed, still works, or is being watched by anyone.

Without tiers, the failure runs in two directions at once.

  • Over-promising — write we support everything and bug reports pile up for platforms nobody can fix, and unanswered issues erode trust in the ones you can
  • Under-promising — write nothing and even a platform that works perfectly well has no basis on which anyone can justify adopting it
  • No basis for cleanup — with no criterion for what may be dropped, stale platform code stays forever and taxes every refactor that touches it

So PEP 11 breaks support into tiers, noting that each tier comes with different requirements which lead to different promises being made about support. The direction of that sentence matters. Requirements come first and the promise is the consequence. How much hardware and how many people were secured determines the size of the promise, not the other way around.

What Each Tier Actually Promises

Transcribed from the text, it comes out like this. The last two columns are the ones to watch. Response time and release blocking are what give a tier its substance.

TierRequired verificationWho is responsibleResponse timeBlocks a release
Tier 1 CI. CI failures block releases All core developers are responsible for keeping main, and thus these platforms, working Changes that would break main are not allowed to be merged; breakage is fixed or reverted immediately Yes
Tier 2 Must have a reliable buildbot At least two core developers signed up to support the platform Breaking changes are to be fixed or reverted within 24 hours Yes
Tier 3 Must have a reliable buildbot At least one core developer signed up to support the platform No response SLA to failures No
Unsupported None Not supported by the core team Not applicable Not applicable

One correction up front. There are not four tiers. PEP 11 defines three, and every platform absent from those lists is unsupported by the core team. Unsupported is not the same as unwelcome: code for such platforms is still accepted when it poses a minimal maintenance burden to the core team, and benefits substantially more people than the contributor. But that is an acceptance condition, not a promise.

The verification column deserves a second look too. Tier 1 says CI while Tiers 2 and 3 say buildbot, and those run at different moments. Tier 1 can state that changes breaking main are not allowed to be merged precisely because the check runs before the merge. A buildbot, by contrast, periodically builds a branch that has already been merged and reports the result. So even though both are automated checking, the first is a gate and the second is an alarm. That is exactly why the announcement lists direct CI integration as future work.

Read the table vertically and the spacing between tiers turns out to be uneven. Tiers 1 and 2 share blocks a release and differ only in scope of responsibility and deadline. Between Tiers 2 and 3, however, the kind of promise changes. The deadline does not stretch from 24 hours to 48; it disappears. Release blocking does not weaken; it is gone. That is where the real boundary sits.

What Tier 3 Does Not Mean

This is the heart of it. The sentence added as an officially supported platform tends to be read as the left column below. What the policy set is the right column.

The impression official support givesWhat the Tier 3 clause actually sets
Tests must pass on this platform before a releaseFailures on this platform do not block a release
If it breaks, it gets fixed soonThere is no response SLA to failures
The whole core team watches this platformOne or more signed-up maintainers satisfies the requirement
Checks run on this architecture before mergeWhat is required is a buildbot - which is why the announcement lists CI integration as future work
Binaries ship for this platformThe tier document does not deal with distributions at all

The confusion comes down to one word. Support gets read as guarantee. But what Tier 3 required is a verification mechanism and a point of contact, not an outcome. What the tier genuinely produces is these three changes.

  • Regressions stop accumulating silently — with a buildbot running, the moment and the commit that broke something are on record. You do not end up six months later unable to say when it stopped working
  • There is somewhere to report — a named maintainer means an issue does not drift in unowned territory
  • Platform code leaves the cleanup pile — while unsupported, related code is a standing removal candidate; listed, that argument goes away

So the accurate summary is this. Tier 3 promises that it will be visible when it breaks, not that it will work. That is not a small promise, but it operates at a different layer than a production adoption decision.

Tiers Move Down as Well as Up

Treat a tier as a property of the code and the following fact makes no sense: a tier can drop without a single line of source changing. The requirements are people and machines.

  • The demotion condition — demotion occurs when the requirements of the current tier are no longer met for an extended period of time, based on the judgment of the release manager or the steering council
  • The practical triggers — a maintainer leaves, the buildbot hardware stops, or results simply stay red. None of the three has much to do with code quality
  • Leaving the tiers entirely — the policy requires recording the name of the system, the first release number that no longer supports it, and the first release where the historical support code is actively removed
  • How users are told — the build is changed to emit a warning if someone tries to install on that platform, and configure is likewise made to warn about the unsupported platform

Promotion is symmetric. Going from Tier 3 to Tier 2 means the signed-up core developers go from one to two, a commitment to fix or revert within 24 hours attaches, and the buildbot has to stay reliable. Every one of those costs somebody's time, which makes promotion less a question of technical maturity than of sustained staffing.

Understanding that also fixes the correct tense for reading this news. Not this platform is stable now, but right now there are people signed up and machines running. The first sentence speaks about the future; the second speaks about the present. Only the second is what a tier certifies.

What Adopters Should Check - A Running Language Is Not a Running Ecosystem

This is where expectations most often slip. An interpreter's tier document covers the portability of the interpreter. PEP 11's jurisdiction is CPython itself; distribution packages and third-party tooling fall outside it. That is exactly why the announcement bothered to add that CPython is one part of the ecosystem.

What actually blocks people is not the language but native extensions. Pure Python code barely notices the architecture, but extension modules written in C, C++ or Rust have to clear both compilation and distribution. Reviewing in this order tends not to leave gaps.

  • Expand the whole dependency tree — not just direct dependencies but transitive ones. The problem is usually two levels down
  • Separate out the native extensions — artifact filenames give you a first pass, since pure-Python and platform-specific builds are named differently
  • Check for a prebuilt artifact on that platform — where none exists, installation falls back to a source build
  • Confirm the source build is feasible — compiler version, headers, and system dependencies such as numeric libraries that need architecture-specific implementations
  • Look at the runtime paths — cryptography, compression and serialization libraries frequently keep architecture-specific accelerated paths. Without one, the code still runs but the performance profile changes
  • Check whether CI runners are obtainable — without them you fall back to emulation and build times multiply. This is a development-velocity cost, so it accumulates quietly
  • Check base images and operations tooling — the deployment pipeline only closes once base images, agents and profilers also ship for that architecture

Put simply, the order of judgment is this. The language tier is a necessary condition, not a starting point. It comes first only in the sense that if the language does not run there is nothing else to check. The reverse does not hold: a running language does not drag the four layers beneath it along.

Prebuilt Versus Built From Source

This is where a new architecture hurts most. On the specification side, the bottleneck is smaller than it used to be.

  • The older Linux artifact tags enumerated architectures in the document. The list the official specification gives for manylinux2014 is x86_64, i686, aarch64, armv7l, ppc64, ppc64le and s390x — riscv64 is not among them
  • PEP 600 changed that structure. The tag takes the form manylinux_${GLIBCMAJOR}_${GLIBCMINOR}_${ARCH}, and ${ARCH} simply follows the platform value Python reports for itself
  • As a result, a new architecture no longer needs a new PEP. PEP 600 states this as a design goal: under the old scheme, adding an architecture meant writing a new PEP, updating the PyPI and pip codebases to recognize the new tag, waiting for the new pip to percolate to users — a fairly elaborate process each time

So the specification is already open. The remaining bottleneck is not the document but who builds and uploads the artifacts. That requires each package's maintainers to attach a runner for that architecture to their own CI, which returns the question to machines and people. The same wall the tier system runs into.

When no artifact exists and installation falls back to a source build, here is what actually changes.

  • The unit of install time changes — what took seconds takes minutes or hours. In a pipeline that rebuilds containers often, that cost recurs every time
  • Build tooling enters the image — compilers and headers left in the final image grow both its size and the surface you have to keep reviewing
  • Failure modes multiply — missing headers, compiler version mismatches and out-of-memory kills during compilation are failures that simply do not exist when you consume a prebuilt artifact
  • Reproducibility weakens — a prebuilt artifact is pinned by hash, but a source build result depends on the build environment. The same lock file can produce different binaries on two machines
  • Air-gapped environments suffer most — if the internal mirror carries only sources and no artifacts, every build dependency has to be mirrored as well

Other Projects Cut It Differently

Other language projects face the same problem and also use tiers, but they chose different axes to divide on. It reads more accurately as a difference in what each project gets asked most often than as a ranking. Three, limited to what public documents state.

Go Broken builds block releases. If a port is broken in release 1.N, the core team can choose to remove it in 1.N+1 Release blocking and builder ownership

Lay the three documents on top of one another and three things converge despite the different wording.

  • Hardware is a precondition. All three require an automated verification mechanism, and Go writes ownership of the builder machine straight into the requirement
  • Named people are required. Go asks that at least two developers be named and agree to maintain a new port, plus one named to maintain the builder; CPython sets the number of core developers per tier
  • The lowest tier promises nothing about working. The phrasing differs, but may or may not work is the shared text

How to Read This News

Both inflation and dismissal move away from the source. The inflated misreading translates it into it is production-ready now. The Tier 3 clause carries no such meaning. The two statements that there is no response SLA and that failures do not block a release define its size on their own.

The dismissive misreading is a low tier means nothing. That is wrong too. There is a real difference between absent from the list and present on it, and the difference is the three items above: buildbot, maintainer, exemption from the cleanup pile. While unsupported, you cannot even establish when a regression appeared.

Splitting what changes from what does not gives this.

  • What changes — regressions surface earlier, issues acquire a recipient, platform code leaves the removal shortlist, and one precedent now exists for other projects to cite
  • What does not — the release gate, the response deadline, artifact availability, containers and toolchains, and performance. Architecture-specific optimization is listed by the announcement itself as future work
  • Keep the tense honest — a tier describes the people and machines currently secured, not a promise about the future

One more thing. News like this is usually a midpoint in a process rather than a decision. A name reaches a tier list after somebody sourced machines, attached builds and fixed failures one by one, not before. So swapping the question from when can I use it to what has been secured and what is left makes the judgment easier. Here, what is secured is machines, maintainers and the listing; what the announcement names as remaining is CI integration, a higher tier and optimization. That the remainder is written down is itself a signal worth trusting — more than an announcement claiming everything is done.

If you keep one working rule, keep this. Next time you read the words official support, open that project's tier document and find just two lines: does a failure block a release, and is there a response deadline. Those two lines almost always contain the true size of the support, and the rest of the page is footnotes to them.

What I Could Not Confirm

Collected here rather than scattered through the text. Filling blanks with guesses is the worst option.

  • The steering council approval date and the listing date - several summaries circulate August 20 and 22, but I could not open a primary document stating those dates, so they are not in the body. The date used above is the announcement's publication date
  • Buildbot count, specifications and uptime - I found nothing that pins down how many machines are running, in what configuration, or how reliably
  • Actual availability of prebuilt packages for the architecture - this varies by package and I found no public tally covering the whole index, so no figure is given
  • Timing of any Tier 2 promotion - the announcement states it as a goal, with no plan or schedule for meeting the requirements
  • Performance - no cross-architecture comparison figures appear here. I could not confirm public measurements taken under equivalent conditions, and an unverified performance comparison is no different from an invented one

Conclusion: Reduced to Three Sentences

First, a tier records a commitment made by people, not the state of the code. Because the requirements are a buildbot and named maintainers, the tier drops when the people leave even if the source is untouched. Second, what Tier 3 sets is it will be visible when it breaks, not it works. The two clauses about no response SLA and no release blocking draw that boundary precisely. Third, the adopter's judgment starts at dependencies, not at the language. The interpreter running and your stack running are different questions, and the tier document governs only the first.

If you do one thing, do this. Open the dependency list of something you currently operate and count only the packages that ship native extensions. That number is how many items you would actually have to review to move to a new architecture, and the language's support tier is one line at the top of that list.
