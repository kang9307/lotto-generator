How to Choose an Ice Maker - Which Homes Need a Separate Machine

The daily output figure comes with conditions attached, and storage capacity is a different number entirely

Aug 26, 2026 Life & Practical

If your trays are empty every day in high summer and you keep buying bagged ice whenever guests come over, an ice maker starts to look obvious. The trouble is that the disappointments arrive in a predictable order. The rated daily output does not show up, the ice you made has melted, it is louder than expected, and cleaning turns out to be a chore. None of those is a defect. All four fall out of how the machine is built.

So it pays to work the decision from the front. First decide whether your home actually needs a separate machine, then narrow the type by what kind of ice you want, and only then read the printed numbers together with the conditions attached to them. Run in that order and a fair share of readers reach a do not buy conclusion at step one. That is a valid outcome too.

What follows describes general operating principles and how specifications are stated. It does not warrant the performance of any particular product. The sanitation section explains conditions created by the structure of the machine and is not medical advice. Cleaning agents, concentrations and intervals differ by model, so follow the manufacturer manual.

1. The first question - do you actually need a separate machine

Most homes already have two or three ways to get ice: freezer trays, the automatic ice maker built into the fridge, and water dispensers that produce ice. Buying a dedicated machine is a decision you make once it is clear what those three cannot do, not simply because you want ice.

A separate machine earns its place under three conditions, and only when all three hold at once.

  • Turnover - demand arrives in bursts. A freezer ice maker halts when its bin is full, and refilling takes time after you empty it. What runs short in a heavy-use moment is not total volume but recovery speed.
  • Shape - the ice you want is something the fridge cannot form, such as soft chewable nuggets or large clear cubes. That is a question of type, not quantity.
  • Frequency - you use ice on a daily rhythm rather than a weekly one. In a machine that sits idle, water sits with it, and that state is where the maintenance burden begins.

Conversely, if the following describe you, trays or the function you already own will finish the job.

  • You use ice on a handful of days per month, and those days need no more than two or three trays.
  • You have no particular requirement about ice shape, and no permanent counter space to give a machine.
  • What actually bothers you is the drinking water itself. In that case the thing to read is not an ice spec but how to choose a filtration method and cartridges.

2. The ice shape decides the use case

The real first fork is not the spec table but what kind of ice comes out. Shape looks like a matter of taste, yet it is the physical variable that sets how fast the ice melts and how fast it chills a drink. At equal weight, ice broken into small pieces has more surface area, so it chills quickly and melts quickly. A large piece with little surface area does the opposite.

The making method splits too. US federal regulation defines two families: a batch type is a machine having alternate freezing and harvesting periods, while a continuous type is one that continually freezes and harvests ice at the same time. That distinction shows up directly in the shape. Batch machines release solid pieces shaped by a mold, while continuous machines scrape a frozen layer away without stopping, which yields granules or chips.

The judgment really turns on one question. Is the ice there to be drunk with, or to make something cold? If it stays in the glass to the last sip, large slow-melting ice wins. If you want a drink cold in seconds, finely broken ice wins. Wanting both from one machine is difficult.

3. Why some ice is cloudy and some is clear

The difference comes less from water purity than from the direction and speed of freezing. Liquid water holds a certain amount of dissolved air, and ice can hold far less. So as freezing begins, the air the ice cannot take is pushed out. Where that displaced air goes determines how the finished cube looks.

A freezer tray chills from the sides and the base at the same time, so it freezes from the outside inward. The displaced air keeps crowding into the still-liquid center until it has nowhere left to go and is sealed there. That cluster of fine bubbles scatters light and reads as a white core. Cloudy ice is not dirty water; it is air with no escape route.

Clear ice is made by opening that route. Freeze slowly and directionally, from one end toward the other, and the air keeps being pushed ahead into the liquid that remains. Commercial machines that run water continuously down a chilled plate and build thin layers work the same way, and the flowing water washes bubbles and impurities off as it goes.

  • Clarity has a price. Directional slow freezing takes longer and sends some water down the drain. A clear-ice machine of the same size having a lower daily output is expected, not a defect. In exchange, ice with fewer bubbles has fewer fracture planes, so it breaks less and melts more slowly.
  • Boiling the water first is only half right. It does reduce dissolved air, which helps, but if the freezing direction is unchanged the remaining bubbles gather in the middle again. Direction is the larger variable.
  • A white core is not a hygiene problem in itself. What is trapped is mostly air. If taste or smell bothers you, that is a question about the supply water, not about clarity.

4. Water supply - reservoir fill versus plumbed

Water supply looks like a convenience question, but it settles placement freedom, maintenance and sanitation in one stroke. A reservoir model can move around, at the cost of somebody pouring and emptying water. A plumbed model asks less of you day to day, but its position is fixed and drainage has to be solved alongside it.

With reservoir models the detail worth watching is what happens to meltwater. Many of them route water that melts in the bin back to the reservoir below and freeze it again. That is a sensible way to save water, and it also means the same water stays inside the machine for a long time. In a household that uses the machine once every few days, that design is the maintenance burden. Leaving no water in it during idle stretches is the safer habit.

For plumbed models, how the drain is handled decides whether installation is possible at all. If the machine sits below the drain, gravity alone will not move the water and a pump is required, which adds one more noise source. The plumbing and drain connection itself is something an installer judges on site, so before buying it is enough to know the distance and height to the nearest drain, which makes that conversation much faster.

5. Kilograms per day - reading a number that carries conditions

This is the most misread figure on an ice maker spec sheet. A daily output value is measured under fixed test conditions, not a promise about your kitchen. US federal regulation defines harvest rate as the amount of ice (at 32 degrees F) in pounds produced per 24 hours, and the Department of Energy test procedure requires ambient room temperature, relative humidity and inlet water temperature to be held within a specified range while measuring. For portable ice makers the procedure goes as far as requiring an initial water temperature of 70 degrees Fahrenheit plus or minus one degree.

Why the conditions have to be pinned down is clear from the principle. What an ice maker does is pull heat out of water and dump it somewhere else, and that rate depends on a short list of things.

  • Inlet water temperature - warmer incoming water means more heat to remove before freezing even starts. Summer tap water is far warmer than winter tap water.
  • Ambient temperature - the condenser dumps the extracted heat into room air. The warmer the room, the harder that is and the longer each cycle runs.
  • Continuous operation - the first cycle starts with a cold machine, but after a few hours the unit and the surrounding air have warmed and the same cycle takes longer. Multiplying the first hour by 24 does not give a daily figure. Opening the bin often lets warm air in, melting ice you already made so it has to be remade.

One conclusion follows. The season when an ice maker performs worst is the season you need ice most. If summer is the point of buying, leave headroom rather than budgeting against the rated number.

The other thing to separate on a spec sheet is production versus storage. They are different numbers, and industry practice treats them as different machines to begin with. US regulation defines ice-making units that do not contain integral storage bins as their own category, and industry performance standards cover ice makers and ice storage bins under separate standard numbers. In other words, a machine that makes 20 kg in 24 hours is not holding 20 kg at any one moment.

6. Storage is not freezing

This is what surprises first-time owners most. A bin filled in the morning is down by evening, and what is left has fused into a block. Nothing is broken. The bin is simply not a freezer.

In most ice makers there is exactly one refrigerated place, and it is the section that forms the ice. The bin is insulated, which slows how quickly heat gets in, but it does not lower the temperature on its own. On top of that, a full bin trips a sensor that stops production, so from that point nothing is being made inside and only melting continues. The machine itself is also giving off heat nearby.

  • Meltwater goes somewhere. It either leaves through the drain or, on a reservoir model, returns to the supply side to be frozen again. The second saves water and keeps the same water in circulation longer.
  • Move ice you intend to keep. If shape matters, transferring it to a freezer container after production holds up better. An ice maker is a machine for making ice, not for keeping it.
  • Bigger storage is not automatically better. If the bin outsizes your consumption, the ice at the bottom melts and refreezes on repeat. Storage capacity is a number you match to how much you use at once.

7. Sanitation is the real maintenance burden

This is where owning one actually costs you time, and the burden is not about diligence. It comes from conditions the structure creates. Inside an ice maker water stands, surfaces stay permanently wet, and the temperature hovers near freezing point. There are many wet surfaces that hands do not reach, such as supply tubing, the forming tray and the inner walls of the bin. Microbes settle into places like that and form films.

One more condition makes it decisive. Ice is a food that reaches the mouth with no cooking step in between. There is no boiling or heating stage, so the path it is made along becomes its final hygienic state. That is also why a standard exists specifically for the materials, design, construction and performance of automatic ice making equipment (NSF/ANSI 12).

Commercial data shows where this breaks down in practice. Korea's Ministry of Food and Drug Safety collected 451 edible ice samples from cafes, fast food outlets and convenience stores nationwide between 2 and 17 June 2025 and tested five items including salmonella, E. coli and bacterial count. Six samples exceeded the bacterial count limit, and five of those six were ice produced by an ice machine. The ministry distributed guidance to operators covering cleaning and disinfection intervals by component and the agents approved for use, and urged regular cleaning.

That survey covered commercial premises, so it does not transfer directly to a home. Still, which parts fail first when maintenance slips follows from the same structure. The places that ask for attention are broadly these.

  • Reservoir and supply tubing - where water sits longest. On reservoir models the return path for meltwater belongs here too.
  • Forming tray and evaporator - the surface where water freezes and releases over and over. Scale buildup makes ice release poorly, which drags output down with it.
  • Bin interior and drain - where meltwater pools. A blocked drain leaves water behind. The air intake and condenser grille, where dust collects, are worth checking on the same schedule.
  • Extended periods of non-use - drain it completely and dry the interior before storing. Leaving it standing with water inside is the worst state.
  • Cleaning agents and intervals - materials and internal layout differ by model, so follow the method and schedule in the manufacturer manual. Improvised chemicals can damage parts or leave residue.

8. Noise, heat and the space it needs

An ice maker moves heat with a compressor, so it shares the nature of a refrigerator. Two things differ. It usually sits close to people, on a kitchen counter, and it makes a periodic sound of ice dropping. Even when steady running is quiet, that impact noise is a separate event.

  • There are three noise sources. The low hum of the compressor, the fan moving air across the condenser, and ice falling into the bin. A catalogue dB figure generally covers the first two. Using a pump where gravity drainage is unavailable adds a fourth.
  • The heat it removes goes into the room. Both the heat pulled from the water and the electricity the compressor used end up in room air. In a small room the temperature rises, and that in turn cuts output.
  • It needs clearance. Pushing the back and sides against a wall or cabinetry sends hot air straight back into the intake and performance falls. The required clearance differs by model, so add the manual figure to the machine footprint before measuring your space.
  • Studios and bedrooms deserve caution. In a quiet room, ice dropping is conspicuous at night. Switching it off overnight also means no ice in the morning.

9. How to think about the electricity bill

Estimating an ice maker from rated power alone is usually wrong. That number applies while it is running, and the bill is that figure multiplied by how many hours a day it actually ran. It stops when the bin is full and restarts when you take ice out. So the same machine costs more in a household that uses more ice.

The arithmetic itself is simple.

  • Daily consumption = power draw (W) × actual daily run hours (h) ÷ 1,000 → kWh
  • Monthly consumption = daily consumption × 30
  • When run hours are unknown - divide the ice you use per day by the rated output for a rough duty ratio. Rated output is a favourable-condition number and summer cycles take longer, so assume real run hours are longer than that estimate.

In Korea one more variable applies. Residential electricity is billed on a tiered structure where the unit rate rises with consumption, so the amount your bill actually increases depends on which tier the added kWh lands in for that month. A household already using a lot pays more for the same kWh. Rather than fixing a figure in advance, work out the added kWh and enter your expected monthly total into the KEPCO electricity bill calculator. How to read appliance power labels and total cost of ownership more generally is covered in the piece on efficiency ratings and running costs.

10. Which homes it fits and which it does not

Pull the axes together and the judgment gets fairly simple. An ice maker is a machine for households that use ice often, and in bursts. Outside that condition, what you take on outweighs what you gain.

  • Good fit - you make several iced drinks a day. Ice shape is part of the result, as with home coffee or a home bar. Guests are frequent and demand arrives all at once. Freezer space spent on trays feels wasteful.
  • Borderline - you use it for a few summer months only. That means running it exactly when performance is at its worst, and emptying and drying it for storage the rest of the year. Treat it as seasonal equipment and plan accordingly.
  • Poor fit - usage is once or twice a week or less. Water stays behind whether you use it or not, and the less you use it the worse its condition gets. What remains is not convenience but maintenance.
  • Poor fit - there is no permanent counter space. Any appliance you have to fetch each time ends up unused. And if what you want is cold water rather than ice, that is a chilled-water problem instead.

One more thing. If a fridge replacement is coming up, settle the order first. Moving to a fridge with automatic ice production can leave the separate machine occupying counter space for nothing.

What could not be confirmed

  • The standard rating conditions in commercial ice maker performance standards - that industry performance standards cover ice makers (AHRI 810) and ice storage bins (AHRI 820) as separate documents is established, but the standards themselves are paid publications, so the rating condition values were not cross-checked against the source text. The commonly cited pairing of 90 degrees Fahrenheit ambient and 70 degrees Fahrenheit inlet water is widely quoted in secondary material; this article uses only the definitions and test conditions verified in US federal regulation.
  • Whether ice makers fall under Korea's energy efficiency labelling scheme - if they did, an efficiency grade label would allow direct comparison between machines. The schedule of covered product categories was not verified, so the label is not used here as a decision axis.
  • Output labelling rules for household ice makers sold in Korea - whether any domestic rule dictates the conditions under which a stated output must be measured could not be established. For that reason comparability between printed figures is not assumed.
  • Public Korean surveys of household ice maker hygiene - the collection and testing results cited above covered commercial premises. No public domestic study sampling household machines was located.

11. Checklist before you buy

  • Ice used per day, and ice used at one time - the first number sets production, the second sets storage.
  • The ice form you want - should it melt slowly, chill fast, or be chewable?
  • Whether production and storage are both stated - at what ambient and water temperature the output was measured, and whether storage capacity appears as its own figure.
  • Water supply and drainage - reservoir or plumbed, and for plumbed, the distance and height to the drain.
  • Measure the actual spot - not just the machine footprint but the clearance the manual requires, added on.
  • Cleaning access and prescribed intervals - whether reservoir, tray and bin detach, and whether the manual lists intervals by component.
  • Stated noise and where it will live - in a studio or bedroom, can you live with ice dropping at night?
  • Power draw and expected run hours - how much it adds to your monthly consumption tier.
