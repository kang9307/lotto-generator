Korea Capital Gains Tax - A Complete Guide to Scope, Exemptions and Rates

From taxable gain to the one-home exemption, long-term holding deductions, rates, filing and payment (2026 basis)

Jun 16, 2026 Finance

Introduction: the wall you hit when you sell a home

When you buy and sell property in Korea, or cash out certain shares, one term always follows the deal: capital gains tax (yangdo sodeukse). Two people can sell at the same price, yet one pays nothing while the other pays tens of millions of won. The difference is not luck. It comes down to requirements, deductions, holding period and filing. Capital gains tax is widely regarded as one of the most complex parts of the Korean tax system, tangled with exemptions, reliefs, surcharges and special cases, so small differences turn into large amounts.

This article is an informational guide to help newcomers grasp the big picture at once. It walks through what Korean capital gains tax is, what it applies to, the conditions for the one-household one-home exemption, how the long-term holding deduction and basic deduction work, how rates are set, and when you must file and pay and how to reduce the tax lawfully. Note that tax law changes often, and real estate taxation in particular moves quickly with policy. All figures here are general, as of 2026, and should be read with a strong assumption that the current rules may differ.

1. What is capital gains tax?

Capital gains tax is a tax on the profit (the taxable gain) made when you transfer an asset. The key is that it is charged on the gain, not on the full sale price. If you bought for 500 million and sold for 700 million, the starting point is the 200 million gain (then reduced further by necessary expenses), not the full 700 million. If you sell at a loss, capital gains tax generally does not arise.

1.1 What is taxable

Taxable assets are mainly real estate such as land and buildings, rights related to real estate such as sale rights and redevelopment membership rights, and certain shares and equity interests, along with assets like memberships. Everyday secondhand sales or ordinary bank interest are not subject to capital gains tax. Rates, deductions and exemption conditions all differ by asset type, which is the first reason the tax is so complex.

1.2 The meaning of transfer

Here, transfer means a change of ownership for consideration, through sale, exchange, or contribution in kind to a corporation. The essence is handing over the asset for value. A gift or inheritance, which passes property without consideration, falls under gift tax or inheritance tax instead, not capital gains tax. Even when property changes hands, the applicable tax differs entirely depending on whether value is exchanged.

In one line: capital gains tax is charged on the gain you earned, not the price you sold for. The devices that reduce that gain are deductions and exemptions.

2. How the tax is calculated

Capital gains tax is not set in one step. It runs through several stages, and understanding the flow shows where the tax falls.

2.1 From gain to taxable base

First, sale price minus acquisition cost and necessary expenses gives the taxable gain. Subtract the long-term holding special deduction to get the capital gains income, then subtract the basic deduction (2.5 million won per year) to reach the taxable base to which rates apply. Multiplying the base by the rate gives the computed tax.

2.2 Stage by stage

  • 1. Taxable gain = sale price − acquisition cost − necessary expenses (brokerage, acquisition tax, capital improvements, etc.)
  • 2. Capital gains income = taxable gain − long-term holding special deduction
  • 3. Taxable base = capital gains income − basic deduction (2.5 million won/year)
  • 4. Computed tax = taxable base × rate − progressive deduction

So the core of saving is to fully recognize necessary expenses in stage 1, maximize the holding deduction in stage 2, use the basic deduction in stage 3, and manage the rate brackets that shift with holding period and number of homes in stage 4.

3. The one-household one-home exemption - the strongest benefit

By far the biggest benefit in capital gains tax is the one-household one-home exemption. Meeting the conditions can mean little or no tax on the gain, which makes it the single most important item for the many people who own one home to live in.

3.1 Core conditions - holding and residence

As commonly cited, to qualify you must meet the following. First, on the date of transfer, one household owns only one home in Korea. Second, the home has been held for at least two years. Third, if it was in an adjustment-target area at the time of purchase, a requirement of at least two years of actual residence is added (homes acquired before the area was designated are generally fine with two years of holding alone).

3.2 The 1.2 billion won high-price exception

Even a single home is not exempt without limit. As commonly cited, a sale price up to 1.2 billion won is exempt, but a high-priced home above that is taxed only on the portion of the gain attributable to the amount over 1.2 billion won. Even then, the long-term holding deduction described below can apply heavily, easing the actual burden.

The one-home exemption has many special cases such as temporary two-home situations, inheritance, marriage, and combining households to care for elderly parents, and they change often. This article covers only the basic skeleton, so never assume you are eligible; always confirm with the NTS or a tax professional.

4. Long-term holding special deduction - a reward for holding

The long-term holding special deduction removes a percentage of the taxable gain when you have held an asset for a long time. It is understood to ease the burden of taxing, in a single year, a gain that accumulated over many years.

4.1 Ordinary real estate - up to 30%

As commonly cited, ordinary land and buildings qualify from three years of holding, growing by about 2 percent per year, up to a maximum of 30 percent at 15 years or more. The longer you hold, the larger the deduction, so the system is designed to favor long holding over short-term flipping.

4.2 One-household one-home - up to 80%

A qualifying one-household one-home enjoys a much larger rate. As commonly cited, combining a holding-based deduction and a residence-based deduction can reach up to 80 percent (for example, up to 40 percent for holding plus up to 40 percent for residence). Even a high-priced single home above 1.2 billion won can see its taxable amount fall substantially thanks to this large deduction. Note that failing to meet the residence requirement can lower the rate.

5. Basic deduction and tax rates

After the holding deduction, you subtract the basic deduction and apply the rate. These two set the final tax.

5.1 Basic deduction - 2.5 million won per year

Capital gains income gets a basic deduction of 2.5 million won per person per year (per tax period). If you sell several assets in one year, they are combined and the deduction applies once, so spreading sales across different years to use the deduction multiple times is a frequently cited saving point.

5.2 Base rates - 6% to 45% progressive

For assets held two years or more, the base rate is progressive from 6 percent to 45 percent of the taxable base (the same structure as the comprehensive income tax base rate). The larger the base, the higher the bracket, with a progressive deduction subtracted in each bracket.

5.3 Short holding and multi-home surcharges

Short holding sharply raises the rate. As commonly cited, under one year is 50 percent and one to two years is 40 percent (homes and redevelopment membership rights may follow base rates or separate standards depending on the asset). In addition, multiple-home owners in adjustment-target areas may face a surcharge added to the base rate. Multi-home surcharges have been suspended and reinstated repeatedly, so always confirm whether and how much they apply right now.

Short-holding rates and multi-home surcharges are the parts that change most frequently with real estate policy. The 50%, 40% and surcharge figures here are general standards only, so always confirm the current rules with the NTS or Hometax before a transaction.

6. Filing and payment - miss the deadline and penalties follow

Capital gains tax is self-assessed: you calculate, file and pay it yourself. Missing the deadline brings penalties, so managing the schedule matters.

6.1 Preliminary return - two months from month-end of transfer

As commonly cited, when you transfer real estate you must file and pay a preliminary return within two months from the end of the month of transfer. For example, if you received the balance on July 15, the deadline is two months after that month-end (July 31), namely September 30. Failing to file can trigger non-filing and late-payment penalties.

6.2 Final return - May of the next year

If you transferred several assets in one year or need to combine them, you make a final return in May of the following year. If you transferred only one asset and the preliminary return already fixed the tax, the final return may be omitted. You can file electronically on Hometax or through your district tax office.

6.3 Installment payment

When the tax due exceeds a certain amount (a commonly cited threshold is 10 million won), installment payment of part of the tax may be possible. This helps when paying a large sum at once is difficult. Exact limits and periods vary over time, so confirm them.

7. Lawful ways to reduce capital gains tax

The point is not to avoid tax you owe, but to reduce it within what the rules allow. Here are commonly cited lawful principles.

7.1 Meet the exemption and deduction conditions

The surest saving is meeting the one-home exemption conditions (two years held, two years resided) or the long-term holding deduction. People often pay heavy tax by selling just days before meeting a requirement, so simply timing the sale after the conditions are met can change the result dramatically.

7.2 Document necessary expenses fully

Acquisition tax, legal and brokerage fees, and capital improvements such as balcony extensions may count as necessary expenses deducted from the gain. Documentation (tax invoices, receipts, contracts) is required, so keep property-related receipts as a habit. Some items, such as routine repairs, are not recognized, so the distinction matters.

7.3 Use sale timing and the basic deduction

If the holding period is just crossing the one-year, two-year, or long-term thresholds, a few days can change the rate and deduction. Because the 2.5 million won basic deduction applies each tax period, spreading sales across years lets you use it multiple times. Realizing gaining and losing assets in the same year so they offset is also discussed.

The principles above are general directions, not guaranteed results. Eligibility, special cases and surcharges differ for each person, and aggressive attempts (under-reporting contracts, false residence registration) are illegal and carry heavy penalty and criminal risk. Always discuss actual application with a tax professional or the NTS.

8. Foreigners and overseas Koreans selling Korean property

Foreigners not resident in Korea, and overseas Koreans, are also taxed when they transfer Korean real estate. However, exemptions and deductions can differ depending on whether you are classified as a resident or a non-resident.

As commonly cited, benefits such as the one-home exemption and the long-term holding deduction largely assume a resident, so a non-resident may have limited access to them. Procedures where the buyer withholds tax from the price, and tax treaties between countries, may also be involved, so the outcome varies greatly with nationality, place of residence and length of stay. The resident/non-resident test has also been revised, so foreigners and overseas Koreans selling Korean property should always get individual advice from a Korean tax professional.

Conclusion - conditions and timing decide the tax

Capital gains tax is the most complex part of the Korean tax system, yet also a tax you can reduce as much as you understand it. Three points stand out. First, it is charged on the gain, not the price, and that gain is cut step by step by exemptions, the long-term holding deduction, the basic deduction and necessary expenses. Second, the one-home exemption and the long-term holding deduction are the strongest devices, sharply lowering the burden once you meet the requirements of holding, residence and number of homes. Third, missing the preliminary return deadline (two months from the transfer month-end) brings penalties, so schedule management matters.

Finally, once more: tax law changes often, and real estate taxation in particular moves quickly with policy. The rates, deduction rates, conditions and deadlines here are general, as of 2026, and may be amended. Whether you qualify for exemption, face a surcharge, or can use a special case depends heavily on your circumstances. This article is general information and does not replace professional tax advice.
