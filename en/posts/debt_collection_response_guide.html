Introduction: The Line Between Legal and Illegal Debt Collection

Receiving debt collection calls is stressful, but even debtors have rights protected by law. Korea's Act on Fair Collection of Claims (the Fair Debt Collection Act) sets clear limits on what collectors can and cannot do. Collectors who cross those limits expose themselves to criminal penalties and administrative sanctions.

This guide explains what constitutes illegal debt collection under Korean law, what rights debtors hold, and how to respond and report abuse. The Individual Debtor Protection Act, which came into effect in October 2024, is also reflected here.

1. Korea's Fair Debt Collection Act — an Overview

The Act on Fair Collection of Claims has been in force since 2009 and has been amended several times since. It applies to financial institutions, licensed lenders, debt-collection companies, and anyone acting on a creditor's behalf to collect a debt. The law lists specific prohibited acts and attaches criminal penalties (imprisonment or fines) and administrative sanctions (fines) for violations.

Debtors can invoke this law to challenge abusive collection methods, demand that collectors stop certain conduct, and file complaints or criminal reports when violations occur.

2. Key Prohibited Acts for Debt Collectors

2.1 Violence, Threats, and Coercion (Article 9)

Collectors must not assault, threaten, detain, confine, deceive, or coerce a debtor or related person. The penalty for violation is up to five years in prison or a fine of up to 50 million KRW. Veiled threats such as "you know what will happen if you don't pay" can also qualify as threats under this provision.

2.2 Night-Time Contact Ban

Without justifiable reason, contacting a debtor or related person between 9 p.m. and 8 a.m. by visit, phone, text, audio, image, or any other means is prohibited if it causes fear or anxiety and severely disturbs the person's private life or work. Repeated late-night contact compounds the violation.

2.3 Restriction on Repeated Contact

Making multiple contacts in a single day without justification is also restricted. The Financial Supervisory Service (FSS) guidelines set a standard of no more than two contacts per day. Even a pattern of frequent daytime calls can constitute a violation if it causes distress — keeping a log of every call is therefore important.

2.4 Third-Party Disclosure Prohibited

Collectors may contact third parties (family, employer, neighbours) only to locate the debtor. Disclosing the existence, amount, or content of a debt to a third party is prohibited. Statements such as "your son owes us money and won't answer his phone" made to a family member can constitute a violation.

2.5 False Statements and Misleading Information

Collectors must not give false information to debtors or related persons, nor imply that legal action is more imminent or serious than it actually is. Telling a debtor "you will be arrested tomorrow" or "your wages are already being garnished" when neither is true is a violation.

2.6 Direct Contact Prohibited After Attorney Notification (Article 8-2)

If a debtor appoints an attorney or law firm as a representative and notifies the collector in writing, the collector must deal only with the representative and may not contact the debtor directly. Exceptions exist only where both the debtor and representative consent, or where the collector genuinely cannot reach the representative.

4. Debtor Rights — What You Can Demand

4.1 Right to Verify the Debt

You may ask the collector to provide written documentation of the debt: the original creditor, the principal amount, interest breakdown, and the history of any debt assignment. Do not make any payment or acknowledgement before verifying that the debt is genuine and correctly calculated.

4.2 Right to Request Restricted Contact

You may request that collectors stop contacting you by a particular channel (e.g., phone) or during certain hours. Submit the request in writing and keep a copy. While this request has less legal force than appointing an attorney, financial institutions generally have internal policies requiring them to respect reasonable contact restrictions.

4.3 Right to Appoint a Representative

Appointing an attorney or law firm and notifying the collector in writing creates a legal barrier to direct contact. If you cannot afford private counsel, Korea Legal Aid Corporation (☎ 132) and the FSS (☎ 1332) provide free legal representation for illegal debt collection victims.

4.4 Right to Challenge Credit Default Registration

If you are actively disputing the debt through litigation, the collector may not register you as a credit defaulter. If you have already been registered, the collector must remove the registration within 30 days of learning that proceedings are under way.

5. Practical Steps When Facing Illegal Collection

Step 1 — Gather Evidence

  • Record phone calls (use your phone's built-in recording feature)
  • Screenshot text messages, chat messages, and emails
  • Note the date, time, and location of any in-person visits
  • Write down verbatim any threats or abusive language as soon as possible
  • Log every contact attempt with date and time

Step 2 — Verify the Debt

Ask for written confirmation of the debt. Check who the original creditor was, the current owner of the claim, and whether the statute of limitations has expired. If the debt is unfamiliar, do not acknowledge it — demand documentary proof first.

Step 3 — Request Contact Restriction or Appoint a Representative

If illegal contact continues, send a written request for it to stop, or appoint an attorney and notify the collector in writing. Once the collector receives that notice, direct debtor contact is legally prohibited.

Step 4 — Report and File a Complaint

  • FSS Illegal Finance Report Centre ☎ 1332 (press 3) — phone or online report
  • Police ☎ 112 — criminal complaint for assault, threats, or extortion
  • Financial Services Commission — violations by licensed financial institutions
  • Korea Consumer Agency ☎ 1372 — consumer damage consultation

6. Statute of Limitations on Debts

6.1 Key Periods

Debt TypeLimitation Period
General civil debt (Civil Code Art. 162)10 years
Commercial debt (bank loans, credit cards — Commercial Act Art. 64)5 years
Interest, rent, wages, short-term periodic claims3 years
Any debt confirmed by final court judgment10 years (regardless of original type)

6.2 Caution: Actions That Reset the Clock

If the limitation period has expired, the debt is no longer legally collectible. However, making any voluntary partial payment or explicitly acknowledging the debt in writing after expiry resets the limitation period from zero. Before paying anything on an old debt, verify whether it is time-barred.

6.3 Small-Amount Debts

Never ignore collection notices, even for small amounts. If a creditor files for a payment order (jibgeum myeongnyeong) and you fail to object within two weeks, it carries the force of a final judgment and can be used for compulsory enforcement. Always read and respond to any official legal notice.

7. Debt Adjustment Options

7.1 Individual Debtor Protection Act (effective October 2024)

Since October 17, 2024, debtors who have fallen behind on payments to a financial institution may request payment deferral, extended repayment terms, or interest rate reduction directly from that institution. This bypasses the previous requirement of going through a public debt-adjustment body as a first step.

7.2 Credit Counselling and Recovery Committee

The Korea Credit Counseling and Recovery Service (www.ccrs.or.kr) offers debt adjustment programmes for those with multiple debts or long-term arrears, including interest waivers and instalment plans.

7.3 Court-Based Individual Rehabilitation and Bankruptcy

For debts that cannot realistically be repaid, courts offer individual rehabilitation (for those with regular income) and personal bankruptcy with discharge. Korea Legal Aid Corporation (☎ 132) provides free assistance with these procedures.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Does ignoring collection calls protect me legally?

No. Ignoring calls does not extinguish the debt. Creditors may pursue legal remedies such as lawsuits or payment orders. You could also miss important legal notices. It is better to verify the debt and respond appropriately than to ignore it entirely.

Q. Can I go to jail for not repaying a debt?

Not paying a debt out of genuine inability is not a criminal offence in Korea — it is a civil matter handled through lawsuits and enforcement. Criminal fraud arises only if money was borrowed with no intention of repaying it from the outset.

Q. A different company is now collecting an old debt. What should I do?

Debts are frequently sold to third-party collectors. Ask the new collector for written documentation of the assignment and verify the debt's history. If the limitation period has run, the debt is unenforceable. Contact the original creditor or the FSS if you have doubts about the claim's legitimacy.

9. Conclusion — Knowing Your Rights Changes Everything

Even as a debtor, you have real legal protections. The night-time contact ban, the prohibition on third-party disclosure, restrictions on repeated contact, and the mandatory cessation of direct contact after attorney notification — these are enforceable rights, not mere guidelines. When illegal collection occurs, gather evidence, challenge it in writing, and report it to the FSS or police. If the underlying debt is unmanageable, explore the 2024 individual debtor protections, the CCRS, or court-based relief.
