What Readers Turned Against Is a Promise, Not a Tool - A Survey of 668 Developers Read With Its Conditions

The question was not 「did a machine write this」 but 「does it look that way」. That difference changes the meaning of the result

2026-09-08 Trends & Culture

On 5 September 2026, Bryan Cantrill published a post on his own blog titled The revolt of the reader. The argument compresses into one sentence. Readers are recognising writing produced by a language model, and once they recognise it they do not merely close that one page; they strike the author off the list entirely.

The evidence he cites is a survey of developers run by Cynthia Dunlop. Six hundred and sixty-eight responses. The numbers are strong. Seventy-eight percent said they stop reading immediately when a post looks as though a model wrote it, seventy-one percent said they will avoid that author in future, and ninety-eight percent said they would rather have the author's own words, awkward patches and all, than a machine-polished version of them.

Numbers like these usually split a room in two. One half concludes that AI writing is simply bad. The other half concludes that readers are over-reacting. The two positions look opposed, yet they stop at the same place. Each decides for or against a tool and then treats the matter as settled.

Read the survey questions as written, though, and something else appears. What was asked was not did a machine write this but does this look as though a machine wrote it, and what respondents recorded was a statement of what they would do once it looked that way. If that is so, the object of the reaction is not the tool at all. It is a judgement that the tool has replaced something. Give that something a name and the name is a promise.

Every percentage below comes from a single survey, and the sample size and recruitment method are given alongside it. For detector accuracy, a vendor's self-reported figures and third-party research are kept in separate columns. Neither set of numbers is offered as grounds for ruling on how any particular person produced a piece of writing. Items whose wording could not be pinned down are not carried into the body; they are gathered in a later section with the reason attached.

The trigger - what 「the revolt of the reader」 actually rests on

The structure of the original post is plain. It quotes the survey results, records that those results match the author's own experience, and then reports that he changed a rule inside his company as a consequence. That last part carries the most weight here, because it is not an expression of personal taste but a case of an organisational rule actually changing. Tastes can differ without consequence. Rules leave consequences for anyone who ignores them.

He writes that he extended RFD 576, one document in Oxide Computer's internal request-for-discussion system, so that it explicitly covers public writing. That document is published on the open web and its state is marked as published. The requirement arrives in two steps. First, public company writing is not to be written with a language model. Second, and this is the part that goes further, the text must also not read as though a model wrote it. A sentence then follows saying that before publication a detector should report the writing as entirely human-written.

It matters not to miss that the second condition is a different kind of demand from the first. The first concerns what the writer did. The second concerns how the result appears to whoever is reading. A writer controls the first and does not control the second. Writing composed honestly and entirely by a person can violate the second condition, and in practice that happens every day.

The document also explains why the rule goes as far as it does. When a writer represents the company, what comes under suspicion is not the credibility of one post but the authenticity of the company's voice itself. That sentence lands on exactly the same spot as the survey's seventy-one percent. If what a reader discards is not the article but the author, then inside an organisation that author is the organisation.

What broke is a promise, not authorship

Is it the bare fact that a machine wrote something that angers readers? There is good reason to doubt it. The same readers show no comparable reaction to text that has passed through a spell checker, a translation engine, a grammar tool or an automatic index. The bare fact of machine involvement in a sentence does not account for the size of the response.

A different sentence does account for it. When a reader reads, several promises are assumed at once. Those promises go unnamed most of the time and become visible only when one of them feels broken. What is happening now is that, as the cost of producing text has collapsed, four promises have been shaken at the same moment.

Wrote it and answers for it are different sentences

There is no row for authorship. That omission is deliberate. Write down one by one what the reader actually loses, and it turns out to be the things that used to travel with authorship rather than authorship itself. The fact that a person wrote it has long served as a proxy indicator for the other four. A person wrote it, so time was spent; time was spent, so things were checked; a name is attached, so someone will answer for it. The chain of inference followed automatically.

Once that proxy became cheap to imitate, the links in the chain came apart. The place where readers direct their anger is precisely that break. It is not the thought that a machine wrote this but the sense that the indicator survived while the thing behind it vanished. Which is why the same reader has no complaint about a chart obviously produced by software, yet reacts fiercely to a smooth paragraph carrying a human byline.

Set that distinction up, and the next question changes shape. It is no longer how to make writing look as though no machine touched it. It becomes what will re-tie the four broken links. That is what the closing sections are for.

What the survey actually asked

The person who ran the survey writes a newsletter about technical blogging. There were 668 responses, more than three times the 181 gathered by the same author's earlier writing survey. Distribution was by posting the link on X, on Bluesky and on LinkedIn. The survey was anonymous, and the author herself notes that there is therefore no guarantee about the demographics of the sample.

Those three sentences come before the percentages. Respondents were not developers drawn at random but people who saw a post by that author on one of those three platforms and chose to take part. The probability that someone with no feelings about machine-written text clicks a survey link and answers to the end is not the same as the probability for someone who has already been irritated several times. That property does not void the values, but it does fix the direction in which they lean.

Look again at the row marked multiple selection. Seventy-eight plus seventy-one plus fifty-seven exceeds one hundred. The same people ticked several boxes, which means the 78 percent and the 71 percent are not separate groups. Miss this and the sentence 71 percent of respondents block the author gets carried over as 71 percent of the 78 percent who left immediately. Denominators go wrong at exactly this kind of junction.

The scale item deserves reading as written too. Eighty-five percent scored it five out of five and eleven percent gave it four. Together, ninety-six percent sit in the top two boxes. That distribution does not describe a question people are divided on; it describes a sample of people who had already made up their minds. It has to be read together with the recruitment method described a moment ago.

Not 「when they detect it」 but 「when it looks that way」

The sentence that travels furthest is that 78 percent stop reading immediately when they detect AI. The actual question is worded differently. It asks what respondents do if they think a post is AI-assisted or AI-authored, and the scale item likewise asks about a blog that seems that way. Neither item tests whether the respondent's judgement was correct, because there is no labelled set of texts mixed in for them to identify.

The difference looks small and changes the reading completely. Of the three things below, this survey measured the third.

  • How well readers can identify machine-written text. Not measured. There is no labelled sample anywhere in the instrument.
  • Whether machine-written text is in fact worse. Not measured. No item evaluates the quality of any writing.
  • What readers do once they come to believe a machine wrote it. This was measured, and the result is very strong.

The third value alone carries enough for practice. What differs is the direction of the implication. The risk is not use a model and you will be caught but look as though you used one and you will pay for it. And looking that way happens to people who never opened such a tool. Writers whose English is a second language and whose prose has become uniform because of it, writers whose register hardened after years of corporate templates, publications whose editors smooth every sentence to an even finish: all of them produce the same impression.

The survey contains one more item that meets this problem head on. It asks whether the response would change if the author were a non-native English speaker using assistance. Twenty-three percent said it would. Turned around, roughly three in four said their reaction would be the same even knowing the circumstances. Which groups pay first when style becomes the basis for a verdict is set out with published research figures in the table that puts detector false positives next to their conditions. Those figures and this 23 percent are different kinds of evidence pointing at the same place.

Which surface signals readers use to adjust trust, and the fact that the same signals appear in human writing, is covered in the piece separating the signals from the cost of misjudging them. What follows here is the stage before that. Not what happens when the signal is read, but what happens once the reading turns into belief.

Where self-report cannot reach

I would do that and I do that are different sentences. A survey collects the first; actual traffic records the second. The two do not always diverge, but when they do there is a regularity to the direction. The more a question has a socially preferable answer, the more the stated intention overshoots the behaviour. This survey asks questions of exactly that kind.

Three gaps

  • Selection - this arises from the sample rather than the wording. Respondents saw a post on the subject on one of three social platforms and walked in of their own accord. A reader indifferent to the topic never enters the sample at all. The 96 percent piled into the top two scale boxes is partly a product of that structure.
  • Distance between statement and act - avoiding an author in future requires remembering the name and recognising it next time. A value that records a moment of irritation and a pattern of avoidance still holding months later have no reason to be the same size.
  • Retrospective attribution - when someone closes a dull article and then reaches for a reason, it felt like AI is the nearest explanation available today. Departures that a few years ago would have been filed under there was nothing in it or it was an advertisement can be absorbed by the same phrase. The measurement then captures the newest name for boredom rather than a response to machine authorship.

Setting out those three is not an attempt to diminish the survey. The direction is the opposite. That 668 people produced responses this heavily massed on one side is itself a strong signal, and the fact that the researcher stated the limits of her sample before anyone else did raises the credibility of the data rather than lowering it. What lowers it is the habit of stripping that sentence out when the numbers are quoted onward, which makes the values look firmer than they are.

The right way to use this data in practice is to quote rank and direction rather than percentages. A statement such as immediate departure is the most common response, with author-level avoidance close behind survives sampling bias well. A statement that 78 percent leave immediately moves whenever the sample does.

Norms in formation, part one - an internal company rule

RFD 576 is not solely about public writing. It divides writing into kinds and attaches a different standard to each kind. That structure by itself reveals the shape of the norm now taking form.

Public writing is treated most strictly. Company blog posts, press releases, social posts and public RFDs fall here. Personal communication is treated strictly as well: the document says models should never be used to write direct communication such as chat, review comments or email. Operational writing is far looser. Where accuracy matters more than authorship, the document states that models may be used in the mechanics of that writing, including generating the text. Code sits in yet another column. Models are acceptable for experimental or auxiliary code, with the caveat that self-review becomes essential before the work goes to peer review.

Look at what separates the four columns and the logic of the rule appears. The more a piece of writing carries a relationship between people, the stricter the standard; the more it carries only the accuracy of an artifact, the looser. The proof is that email and review comments sit in the same column as the public blog. Those two differ completely in audience size and search exposure, yet they are alike in that a relationship with a recipient is at stake.

The most striking passage concerns disclosure. The document says that volunteering the fact of model use may instead read as implicitly distancing oneself from responsibility for the content, and may erode the trust a team depends on. That runs directly counter to the assumption that labelling makes a writer honest. And the same document states plainly that responsibility for the artifact lies with the person using the tool.

Place the two sentences side by side and what the rule is really protecting comes into view. Not the purity of authorship. The line of responsibility staying unblurred. The moment a tool wrote this part can be read as so this part is not mine to answer for, disclosure stops being a device for honesty and becomes a device for indemnity. The document is guarding against that possibility.

Norms in formation, part two - what academic publishing already settled

One field codified this problem long before any company rule. The recommendations on authorship issued by the ICMJE, the international committee of medical journal editors, contain a separate clause on artificial intelligence assisted technology.

The core sentence is that chatbots should not be listed as authors. The stated reason overlaps precisely with the axis of this piece. Not because a machine did the writing, but because a chatbot cannot be responsible for the accuracy, integrity and originality of the work. What bars authorship is the capacity to answer for something, not the act of composition.

Disclosure is required instead. The moment of disclosure is submission, and the location is specified. Writing assistance goes in the acknowledgements; data collection, analysis or figure generation goes in the methods section. That split matters, because it encodes a judgement that the weight a reader needs to know about depends on where the tool was used. Smoothing a sentence and producing a number cannot go in the same box.

Finally, authors are told to review and edit the result carefully. The reason given is that such output can sound authoritative while being incorrect, incomplete or biased. That sentence binds the two earlier requirements together: disclosing is not sufficient, and after disclosure the duty to check remains exactly where it was.

Two sets of norms pointing at the same place

One is an internal document a startup revised in 2026. The other is an international recommendation medical journal editors have refined over many years. The worlds they came from differ and so does the nature of their force. Put three rows in one table, though, and the overlap surfaces.

The three values in the focus column differ: how it reads, where responsibility sits, the relationship with the writer. Yet none of the three points at authorship itself. Even the strictest rule does not treat a human wrote it as the final goal; it treats that as a means of protecting something behind it. The ICMJE spells the reason out in a sentence, and the company rule reveals it through its responsibility clause.

Read the three rows together and the name for what is happening becomes clear as well. This is not a dispute about a tool. It is a rewriting of public norms. And whenever norms are being written, the same problem appears: demands are easy to put on paper, and checking them is not.

What can be enforced and what cannot

Where the gate sits decides the outcome

The yardstick for a rule is not whether it is right but whether it can be checked. A demand with no method of checking burdens only the people who comply and does nothing at all to the people who do not. Run five demands past that yardstick and it becomes clear which of them can stand as rules.

DemandHow it is checkedEnforceabilityMain failure mode
Facts must match their sourcesFollow the link and compare against the originalHigh. Human-written and machine-written text go through the identical procedureChecking takes time, so it gets skipped when things are busy
Unconfirmed items must be statedSee whether the section exists and whether its contents are specificHigh. Presence and absence are visibleThe form survives while the contents fill with boilerplate
Figures must carry sample and conditionsSee whether a denominator and question wording sit beside the percentageHigh. A missing slot shows plainlyIf the primary source lacks conditions, none can be attached
Tool use must be disclosedIt depends on self-reportLow. There is no method of checking beyond the reportOnly honest people label, and the rest do not
A human must have written itA detector, or the impression of whoever is readingLow. The verdict carries error and evasion is not difficultThe wrongly accused are caught first and evaders pass

The dividing line between the top three rows and the bottom two is sharp. In the top three, the object of the check is inside the text. Press the link, look for the section, look for the denominator. In the bottom two, the object sits outside the text. It lives in the writer's head and in the process of the work, and the text preserves only fragments of that.

That the disclosure clause falls into the low column does not mean the clause is bad. It means self-report rules generally behave this way. A self-report rule earns its keep when it works as a declaration of a norm rather than as a punishment. The ICMJE disclosure clause carries weight because in academic publishing the cost of later exposure is heavy and there is a body able to impose it. A personal blog has no such body.

The last demand from the company rule, that a piece must not read as model-authored, is not in the table. It is not a check but an outcome. Turn an outcome into a rule and the work of smoothing style becomes an end in itself. An organisation can decide to bear that cost internally; generalising it into a rule is much harder.

What happens when a detector becomes the gate

Vendor figures and third-party figures belong in different columns

The tool that the company rule designates as its pre-publication gate has its own technical report on arXiv. It is dated 29 July 2026 and all seven listed authors belong to the company that makes the product. The reported values are an AUROC of 0.9916, a false positive rate of 0.0041 percent and a false negative rate of 0.3396 percent. The abstract does not name the evaluation sets on which those values were measured.

What has to travel with a figure is who measured it. A vendor's self-reported number is not false by virtue of being self-reported, but it cannot go in the same column as a value a third party obtained on a different sample. Without a published evaluation set, neither replication nor rebuttal is possible. What a 2023 study found when it ran seven detectors against non-native writing samples, and what its samples and conditions were, sits in the table that keeps those figures next to their conditions. Those are numbers from an earlier generation of tools and cannot be transposed directly, a caveat recorded there as well.

Even granting the vendor figures, a separate reason keeps them from serving as grounds for judging an individual. A very low false positive rate still yields an absolute number of false positives that cannot be waved away once the volume checked is large enough. And for the individual on the receiving end of one, the probability is not 0.0041 percent but one hundred percent. There is also no practical way for that person to demonstrate innocence, because no evidence has been offered beyond the uniformity of their prose.

So the same tool becomes an entirely different act depending on where it is placed. Running your own draft through it before publishing is self-inspection. If the result displeases you, rewrite; if the verdict was wrong, nothing was lost. Running someone else's writing through it and reaching a conclusion is adjudication. There is no way back once it is wrong, and the cost falls on the person judged rather than the person judging. The company rule put the tool in the first position. Kept there, most of the concern in this section does not apply.

What remains is what happens when the rule leaks outside the organisation. Once a gate designed for pre-publication self-inspection migrates into a reader's instrument of judgement, the demand sitting lowest in the enforceability table turns into the sharpest weapon available.

What a writer can actually hold on to

By this point one question remains. Whatever tools are or are not used, what re-ties the four promises the reader felt were lost? The six habits below are the answer, and what they share is stated in the closing paragraphs.

  • Attach conditions to every figure - when a percentage goes down, put the sample size, the recruitment method, the question wording and the date in the same sentence. A sentence carrying those four is hard to mass-produce, and more importantly it opens a route for the reader to check independently.
  • Go down to the primary document - cite the source, not a summary of it. Paraphrase enters at every stage of summarising, and paraphrase generally shifts values toward the stronger reading. That the 78 percent and the 71 percent share a denominator only becomes visible in the original survey.
  • Gather the unknowns in one place - rather than scattering items that could not be pinned down throughout the body, put them in a dedicated section with reasons. Scattered, hedging becomes a style; gathered, it becomes a list. A list is something the next person can pick up.
  • Leave a route for being wrong - a correction history, a way to make contact, an updated date. Writing down the method of answering for something beats declaring an intention to answer for it. This is the slot corresponding to the first promise in the earlier table.
  • Separate what was lived from what was read - writing that keeps this distinction is hard to imitate. Without it, two kinds of sentence carry equal weight and the reader ends up trusting neither.
  • Put contrary evidence in the same section - place material that cuts against your own argument in the body, beside the claim, rather than in a footnote at the end. If the reader finds it somewhere else first, the loss is far larger. The researcher stating the limits of her own sample before anyone else is exactly this device in action.

The six have something in common. None of them asks what was used to write this. All of them ask what this was verified against. Change tools and the six remain; never touch a tool and their absence still breaks the promise. The concrete steps for moving fact-checking out of individual talent and into procedure are set out in the process running from claim extraction to tracing a primary source.

Read the survey's ninety-eight percent again from here and the meaning shifts slightly. What respondents chose was not awkward prose in itself. It was the trace of a person that survived even at the cost of awkwardness. That trace does not live only in style. Figures carrying their conditions, sources that connect, slots deliberately left empty, a history of corrections: all of it is the same trace.

Which is why the conclusion resists becoming guidance about whether to use a tool. What the revolt aimed at was not smooth prose but the sense of emptiness behind it. Roughening sentences on purpose does not touch that sense. Only filling in what stands behind them does.

What could not be pinned down

  • The occupations and regions of the 668 respondents - the survey ran anonymously and the researcher states that demographics are not guaranteed. All that is published is her note that she targeted developers and readers of tech blogs.
  • The raw response counts per item - only percentages are published. That the 78 percent and the 71 percent share the same 668 as their denominator follows from the multiple-selection format, but the absolute count in each box does not appear in the text.
  • The version date of the ICMJE recommendations - no version marking is visible on the page defining authorship. When the artificial intelligence clause was added and when it was last revised cannot be established from that page alone.
  • The revision history of RFD 576 - the document state reads as published, but there is no marking within the document pinpointing when the public writing clause was added. The original blog author's note that he extended it recently is the only clue to timing.
  • The composition of the detector report's evaluation sets - the abstract carries the false positive and false negative rates but does not name or size the datasets on which they were measured. That gap has to be read together with the fact that the listed authors work for the vendor.
