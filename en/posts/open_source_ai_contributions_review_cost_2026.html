The Trigger — An Essay That Calls Its Own Title Hyperbolic

James Ross published a post titled "Open Source as We Know It Is Dead" on his personal blog on October 5, 2026, and it was introduced on GeekNews on October 7. According to the author box at the bottom of the post, he is the chief technology officer and co-founder of Nodecraft and a self-taught software engineer. In the body of the post he writes that game servers are now literally his job.

Taken alone, the title is a flat declaration, but the author qualifies it himself in the fourth paragraph. The original reads: "This isn't easy to write and the title is a bit hyperbolic. But it's honestly how I've started to feel: open source, as I've known it, is dying. And I sadly think AI is what's killing it." Those few sentences carry four layers of qualification.

  • The title. The author writes that the title is "a bit hyperbolic".
  • The scope. What is said to be dying is not open source as a whole but open source "as I've known it".
  • The tense. The wording is "is dying", not "is dead".
  • The strength. It is not a report of fact but a statement of "how I've started to feel", and on the cause, too, he writes "I sadly think".

The nature of the piece matches these qualifications. It is not a survey that gathers statistics but an essay that joins a personal recollection to links to announcements by a number of projects. The argument it puts forward reduces to one line: the cost of creating a contribution has dropped while the cost of reviewing it has not, and that gap is closing the practice of accepting pull requests (PRs) from strangers.

The Author's Recollection — An Account From 2011 and Two Pull Requests From 2014

The first part of the essay is the author's own experience. Everything below is a recollection that James Ross wrote in the first person.

  • June 17, 2011. He made his GitHub account and, about two minutes later, opened his first issue. It asked why teleporting was broken in TShock, a Terraria server mod.
  • Before GitHub. He mentions SVN checkouts that broke easily, DarkRP on Garry's Mod servers and SourcePawn plugins for Counter-Strike: Source.
  • Self-taught. He writes that he learned most of what he knows by reading someone else's code or by opening an issue and having a maintainer explain, and that he would not have a career without open source.
  • The first PR in June 2014. It was three years to the day after he made the account. The Node.js client of PointDNS crashed with SyntaxError: Unexpected end of input when listing records on a big zone; the cause was that it did not handle chunked responses, and he fixed that.
  • The second PR two hours later. It was a "global tidy up" that touched almost every line in the file. The author describes it as 300 lines of unsolicited changes.
  • The merge. A maintainer merged both the next morning. In the author's words there was "no back and forth, no checklist, no bots", and he says the experience made him feel he belonged.

The author writes that, looking back, he is a little embarrassed by that second PR: an unsolicited rewrite from someone nobody had heard of, with a slightly cocky description. If the same PR landed in a repository today, he writes, he would assume an agent wrote it too, and he would not blame anyone for closing it. That first PR, quiet for over a decade, picked up a string of comments last November from an account he had never seen, he says: a #, a ###, a broken screenshot upload. He writes that he does not know whether it was a bot or a person, and that this is "kind of the point".

The Signal Said to Have Collapsed — What a Stranger's Pull Request Used to Mean

The open source the author describes is closer to people than to code. He writes that a PR is not just a diff but someone saying "I used your thing, I cared enough to make it better, and I'm trusting you with my work", and that reviewing it is the reply "I see you, let's do this together". Two quotations support that argument, and the wording of both can be found in the originals.

The first is Miguel Grinberg. The author quotes Grinberg, who maintains Flask-SocketIO, as having written this year: "Today, an unsolicited PR is a red flag." That sentence appears verbatim in Grinberg's post "I Am Not a Reverse Centaur", dated June 12, 2026. In the paragraph immediately before it, Grinberg writes that in pre-LLM days receiving an unexpected pull request from a fellow coder was a source of excitement and pride.

The second is Andreas Kling. The Ladybird announcement of June 5, 2026 contains the sentences: "A substantial patch used to imply substantial effort, and that effort was a reasonable proxy for good faith. That assumption no longer holds." The author attaches his own sentences to it: "The effort was the signal. That's what's gone."

The author sketches two scenes of the present. In the good case a human wrote the PR, yet when it is opened on a popular repository a bot returns an AI summary of the change within seconds, another bot posts a preview deployment, another adds a sequence diagram nobody asked for, and by the time a person looks, the real conversation is buried in the middle of a wall of generated text. As the bad case he lists PRs that "fix" issues that do not exist, PRs that rewrite half a file with plausible but wrong code, and security reports whose vulnerability turns out to have been hallucinated. On the claim that there are accounts opening dozens of these a day across hundreds of repositories, the author himself attaches the word "apparently". That marks it as something he has heard, and no source for the numbers is given.

The sentence about cost is a conditional. The author writes that "when generating a PR takes ten seconds and reviewing it properly takes thirty minutes", the work has not vanished; it has only shifted from the person who wants something onto the person who has to say no. The ten seconds and the thirty minutes are illustrative figures, not measurements. His point is that the person saying no is almost always a volunteer who was already stretched thin.

The author's sentences tying the cases together run like this. The details vary, but the shape is the same: maintainers are no longer just deciding whether a contribution is good, they are deciding whether they can afford to find out. Every maintainer closing the door is probably making the rational choice for their project and their time, but when enough people make that same rational choice, open source starts to become something else.

Where the Summary and the Original Diverge

Set the author's one-line summaries beside the wording of the original material, and in several places the scope of the action and the reasons given are narrower than, or different from, the summary. These are differences that are lost when everything is filed under "blocked AI contributions".

  • Ladybird: not a ban on AI. The announcement says "Whether code was typed by hand is beside the point", and states that the team uses AI tools every day. The reasons it gives are the security model of a browser working toward its first alpha release and the question of who is responsible for code. The project remains open source, and bug reports, website testing, standards discussion and security reports are still welcome. It states firmly that there will not be a separate process for submitting patches by other means.
  • curl: a story about security reports, not PRs. The post by Daniel Stenberg says the confirmed rate of bug bounty submissions fell from somewhere north of 15 percent in previous years to below 5 percent starting in 2025. "Not even one in twenty was real" is the sentence. The same post states explicitly that on the GitHub side, in issues and pull requests, curl has "not (yet)" seen that problem.
  • tldraw: a temporary policy. The issue describes it as a temporary policy until GitHub provides better tools for managing contributions. The approach is to close first and selectively re-open what is actually under consideration, and it says issues, bug reports and discussions continue to be welcome.
  • Ghostty: not a blanket ban. The PR description by Mitchell Hashimoto says AI generated contributions will only be allowed for accepted issues and maintainers, and that drive-by pull requests with AI generated content will be immediately closed. "10x if not more" is a maintainer's statement about the count of poor quality issues and PRs, and no tally is attached.
  • Jazzband: two reasons. The sunset notice says the "slopocalypse", the flood of AI-generated spam PRs and issues, made untenable a model in which everyone who joins receives push access. It then sets out separately an older problem, that the organization was always run by a single person, and says the sustainability question was raised as early as 2017.
  • Godot: an announcement of amendments. The Godot Foundation post of June 30 says the project "will amend" its contributing policy. New contributors, defined as those with three or fewer merged pull requests, would not be able to submit new features or significant refactoring without explicit permission from maintainers; AI assistance would be limited to menial things such as code completion, regex or find and replace, and its use would have to be disclosed in the PR. The post says autonomous agent use already leads to an automatic ban from the repository. Machine translation remains acceptable as long as the original content was written by a human.
  • Miguel Grinberg: not a block on PRs. His guideline is to introduce a change in an issue first. When an unexpected PR arrives, he writes, he determines within a few seconds whether a person is behind it, and closes it immediately if he sees no proof of human involvement.

The situation of those on the receiving end of AI-generated security reports is laid out in the article reading a GNOME maintainer's post in numbers, the reasoning of a project that does not accept AI-generated code in the article on the position of SQLite, and the attitude of the kernel on the other side in the article on the remarks by Torvalds.

The Explanation and the Features GitHub Put Forward

Saying that "even the platforms are saying it out loud", the author quotes the GitHub blog: "Welcome to the Eternal September of open source. Here's what we plan to do for maintainers.", posted on February 12, 2026 by Ashley Wolf, Director of Open Source Programs at GitHub. The quoted sentence, "The cost to create has dropped but the cost to review has not.", is in the original as written. The setting the author says shipped "the next day" is confirmed in the changelog entry dated February 13.

  • Disabling pull requests entirely. According to the changelog, pull requests can be turned off from the repository's Settings just like wikis, issues and discussions. When disabled, the pull requests tab is not visible, and no one can see existing pull requests or open new ones.
  • Restricting to collaborators. Pull request creation is restricted to collaborators with write access. In this case the pull requests tab remains visible and all pull requests can be seen and commented on.
  • Availability. Both settings are available for all public and private repositories, under Settings > General > Features.
  • What the blog announced. It says GitHub is working on deleting spam or abusive pull requests from the UI, and exploring criteria-based gating such as requiring a linked issue before a pull request can be opened, as well as triage tools that evaluate contributions against a project's own guidelines.
  • Relayed by the author. The author writes that since then GitHub has added caps on open PRs per user (AI agents count toward them), a way to restrict who can open issues, and a way to archive spammy PRs so nobody else can see them.

The two texts take a different tone toward the same phenomenon. The GitHub blog writes that "walls don't build communities", says there is no single correct solution, and describes the present situation as a sign of something worth celebrating, namely that more people want to participate than ever before. It also holds that low-quality contributions are not a uniquely recent phenomenon. Looking at the same list of features, James Ross writes that the platform that made the pull request famous has spent the year building ways to turn it off.

Moves away from the platform also appear in the essay. The author relays that Zig moved to Codeberg last year and that more and more people are spinning up their own Forgejo or Gitea instances. He says he does not blame them, but writes that every project that leaves takes its issues, its history and its people somewhere new, and makes the next stranger even less likely to wander in.

Where the Author Stands — An Essay by Someone Who Uses AI Every Day

This essay was not written by someone who does not use AI. In the section titled "I'm part of this too", the author states that he uses AI every single day. He writes code with it, reviews code with it, and says he even had help organizing the rambling notes that became the post. He adds that this made him feel slightly uncomfortable while writing.

  • His assessment of the tool. He calls it an amazing tool that has made him faster and, on good days, better at his job.
  • What he does not ask. He writes that he is not there to tell anyone to stop using it.
  • What he takes issue with. That many people, himself included, have been treating its cost as zero. "It isn't - the cost just moved."
  • The concluding sentences. "AI made contributing cheap. It didn't make maintaining any cheaper - if anything, it made it a lot more expensive."

The essay does not single out any particular contributor or company. On maintainers it carries a section heading that says he does not blame them one bit, and it explains why AI slop PRs are the worst kind of work: they look like contributions, so one feels obligated to read them. The same framing is present in the Ladybird announcement and the Godot post. Ladybird writes that the team uses AI tools every day, and Godot writes that the amount of effort required to make a PR has gone down while the amount of work to review PRs and the number of people available to review has stayed the same.

The Author's Position on a Fix — The Scope of Not Knowing

The last section is headed "I don't know what the fix is", and the author writes that he wishes he had a neat answer but does not. Instead of proposing an alternative, he assesses the measure of banning AI PRs as follows.

  • Defense. He writes that it feels like the right call for a lot of projects and that he will defend any maintainer who makes it.
  • A blunt tool. It catches the person who used AI to help write one good test alongside the person who pointed an agent at a thousand repositories.
  • What it relies on. It relies on honesty, or, in the author's words, on "detection that doesn't really work".
  • Where it nudges. Toward a world where repositories are closed by default, where one needs to be vouched for before being allowed to help, and where the default answer to a stranger is no.

The author neither asserts that direction nor opposes it. "Perhaps", he writes, that is just where things are heading: smaller, trusted circles, with source available but contributions by invitation; "maybe that's even fine", and he may be nostalgic for a version of the Internet that was never going to last. Even so, he writes, the early 2010s version of himself, nervously sending a stranger 300 lines of "tidy up", would not get through that door, and something real is lost when people like that cannot get in anymore. The essay ends: "I don't know what the fix is. I just know I miss how it used to feel."

The GitHub blog cites experiments in the same direction as examples. It writes that some projects have moved to invitation-only workflows, and it introduces the Vouch project by Mitchell Hashimoto, in which contributors must be vouched for by trusted maintainers before they can participate, with the caveat that it is experimental and that some aspects will be debated.

What Could Not Be Confirmed

  • Two posts on X. The original posts by Sindre Sorhus and Yusuke Wada could not be included among the sources. The quotations in the body are the strings as James Ross reproduced them.
  • OpenJDK, COSMIC, Codeberg and Zig. These were not checked against the original material the essay links to. The scope and timing of the actions follow the author's summary.
  • The remaining GitHub features. The detailed behavior of the cap on open PRs per user, the restriction on issue creation and the archiving of PRs was not checked against the respective announcements.
  • Aggregate figures. The essay contains no statistics such as the share of external PRs that are AI-generated or the number of repositories that have closed PRs. The 5 percent is the confirmed rate of the curl bug bounty, the 10x is one maintainer's statement, and the ten seconds and thirty minutes are illustrative figures.
  • Mass PR accounts. The essay gives no source for the accounts said to open dozens of PRs a day across hundreds of repositories.
  • Links in the recollection. The original pages of the TShock issue and the two PointDNS PRs were not checked. The dates and the course of events are the author's account.
  • The date for Hono. The author writes only "this week".
