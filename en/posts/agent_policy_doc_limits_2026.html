A Long Policy Document Will Not Govern Your Agent - Add Enforcement, Not More Rules

Why more rules do not stop the same incident, and how to choose what to push down into code

2026-07-31 Tech & IT

Opening: the policy is now fifteen pages and the same incident happened again

Suppose a team has an incident. An automated job touched data it was not supposed to touch. There is a review, and a line goes into the policy document: handle production data with care. Two months later something similar happens. Another review, another line. The document is eight pages now. Six months on it is fifteen pages, and the shape of the incidents has not really changed.

The obvious diagnosis is that nobody reads the document. So the response stays on the document side. Someone writes a summary, bolds the important parts, moves them to the front, adds the word must. But if the same thing happens to an agent that has the whole policy pasted into its context, the diagnosis was wrong. The agent definitely read it. It was in context on every single call.

The claim of this article is simple. A document carries intent; enforcement has to live on the call path. What makes a rule hold is not how firmly the sentence is worded. It is the thing that actually blocks the action when it goes wrong. So neither write better documents nor documents are useless is the conclusion here. They are two layers doing different jobs, and most organizations currently have only one of them.

What I measured, and what I did not. I did not measure any model's instruction-following rate. That is not an experiment I can reproduce in my environment. What I measured instead is a property of documents themselves: how fast contradiction grows with rule count, how much of it a single precedence sentence erases, and what share of a policy can be pushed down into a pre-execution check. Every figure about model compliance is quoted from a public paper, with its source and grading rule named each time. No vendor marketing claim is used as evidence anywhere in this article.

Environment. The simulations ran on Node.js v22.16.0 with fixed random seeds, and each row is the mean of 400 repetitions. Every company, policy, and incident used as an example is fictional.

1. What the claim is - why an attached document still gets violated

A paper posted to arXiv on 28 July 2026, HANDBOOK.md: A Benchmark for Long-Context Agentic Instruction Following, takes this problem head on. Existing benchmarks mostly ask did the agent finish the task. This one asks something different: did the long policy document sitting in context actually constrain every action that followed.

The design shows the question was taken seriously. As described in the paper:

  • 65 agentic tasks. Each runs in a self-contained company environment: a file workspace plus mock services for email, chat, calendar, issue tracking and commerce
  • The governing document for each task is an expert-written standard operating procedure running 20 to 124 pages
  • Five domains and ten fictional companies: finance, medical billing, insurance, logistics and HR
  • To resist memorization, every task modifies one of the base handbooks, altering the specific rules and thresholds that grading turns on, so no two tasks share a policy
  • Grading is fully programmatic. There are 824 criteria in total, checking both that required actions occurred and that prohibited actions did not

That last item is the heart of the benchmark. Most evaluations only look at what was done, but policy compliance is half about what was not done. If you do not check mechanically that the forbidden thing did not happen, you have not measured compliance at all.

The result is stated in the abstract. Under strict grading, where a trial passes only if every criterion is satisfied, the best of thirty evaluated configurations passed 36.2 percent of trials, and most frontier configurations remained below 25 percent.

How to read that number. The 36.2 percent is a value inside this benchmark's strict grading rule. Breaking one of 824 criteria fails the whole trial. So it must not be reread as some product's instruction-following rate, and I will not attach a number to any named model. What this article takes from the paper is not a leaderboard but the shape of the failures.

The paper describes that shape in two parts. First, the standing document does not act as a persistent authority that screens candidate actions; it acts more like one more retrieved source whose influence decays with distance. Distance has three forms here: turns accumulating, tool calls accumulating, and the environment sending competing signals.

The second part is the more operational one. The paper reports that nearly every failed trajectory ends with a confident statement that the handbook was followed, and calls the agent's self-report the least reliable artifact in the trajectory. That single sentence should change how you run things. Do not accept an agent saying it complied as evidence of compliance. Evidence has to come from the execution record.

2. Why a document is weak as a control - advice versus blocking

Why does this happen? There is a structural reason that precedes any benchmark result. A document does nothing at execution time.

Say the policy file contains: no writes to the production database. Where is that sentence at the moment a write occurs? Nowhere. It exists as text somewhere in context, and the actual write is performed by a database client that has never heard of it. There is no connection at all between the rule and the execution.

AspectDocument (policy)Enforcement (call path)
What the rule doesRecords intentJudges the call
On violationNothing happensThe call fails
When it is noticedAfter the fact, if someone looksJust before execution, automatically
What you can showDistribution historyBlock records
As rules growContradiction grows, later text gets buriedEvaluation cost grows (measurable)
Gray-zone handlingPossibleNot possible, becomes false positives
What it isWhat you hope was readWhat cannot be done

That yields a usable test. If you cannot state in one sentence what fails when a rule is broken, that rule is a record, not enforcement. Apply the test across an entire policy document and the result is usually grim: for nearly every clause you end up writing nothing happens.

The reason this still works tolerably in human organizations is that there are other layers besides the document. Hiring screens, training, a supervisor's eye, employment consequences, the judgment of colleagues, all of it sits behind the rule as indirect enforcement. An agent has none of that layer. Drop a single policy file in front of it and apply the human playbook, and what you transported is the rules while what you left behind is all of the enforcement.

3. What happens as rules pile up - measured directly

The idea that many rules collide is intuitively obvious, but how fast they collide is not. So I designed a small experiment aimed at the document itself.

The setup: a request space of 12 resources by 8 actions by 8 contexts, which is 768 requests. Each rule applies to some scope (a specific resource or all of them, a specific action or all of them, with or without a context condition) and says either allow or deny. Growing the rule count, I count the share of requests where the matching rules disagree.

NOTE: this measures a DOCUMENT, not a model. No model compliance rate was measured here.

Here is how to read it.

  • At 16 rules, 40.9 percent of requests are already contested. You reach that point with about one page of policy
  • 76.5 percent at 32 rules, 97.3 percent at 64. By around a hundred rules, effectively every request falls in the contested region
  • The more important column is order-decides: the share of requests where reading order alone flips the verdict. It is 45.3 percent at 32 rules and then flattens near 50 percent
  • That fifty is the frightening part. Once a rule set is large enough, the answer the document gives on half the requests is indistinguishable from a coin flip
  • rules/req is how many rules apply to one request at once. At 128 rules, 16.8 apply on average. Human or model, someone has to hold those sixteen in mind and pick one
  • conflicting pairs counts mutually contradictory rule pairs. At 128 rules that is 1,500 pairs. No review process catches that by reading

The limits of this experiment, stated plainly. Rule scopes and verdicts were drawn at random. A real policy is organized by topic, so it overlaps less and contradicts less than this. Do not read it as 76 percent of our policy is contested. What to read is the slope, not the absolute value. Contradiction grows far faster than linearly in rule count, and it has to, because the number of rule pairs grows with the square of the rule count. Six pairs at four rules becomes 8,128 pairs at 128.

And here is the crux: what to pick when rules conflict is usually not written down anywhere. People who write policy add clauses one at a time; they do not first build a precedence system across clauses. So the verdict falls to accidental factors: reading order, position in the document, how forcefully something is phrased. Half of the later text gets buried phenomenon is an attention problem, but the other half is that there was never a defined answer.

4. What one precedence sentence buys, and how much text goes dead

So how much does writing precedence help? I reran the same rule sets after adding one sentence: the narrower rule wins. The rules were untouched; only an interpretation rule was added.

E3. what a written precedence rule buys, and how much text goes dead
precedence tested: most-specific rule wins; ties fall through to the next tie-break
400 seeded trials per row, same request space as E1 (768 requests)

rules   ambiguous, no precedence   ambiguous, most-specific-wins   rules that never decide
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    8                     13.7%                            5.7%                     0.1%
   16                     40.9%                           14.9%                     0.5%
   32                     76.5%                           28.3%                     1.4%
   64                     97.3%                           38.6%                     4.2%
  128                    100.0%                           44.6%                     9.6%
  256                    100.0%                           56.5%                    19.3%

reading: one sentence of precedence removes most of the ambiguity, and removes
none of the rest. the last column is text that is present and inert.
NOTE: simulation of a rule set. no model was queried, no compliance rate measured.

Read the result in two directions.

  • One sentence does a lot. At 32 rules, 76.5 percent became 28.3; at 64 rules, 97.3 became 38.6. That came from changing not a single rule, only writing down an interpretation
  • But it does not disappear. 44.6 percent remains at 128 rules, because rules of equal scope are not separated by specificity. So write it in two clauses: first, narrower scope wins; second, on a tie, deny beats allow
  • The last column is the unexpected finding. The share of rules that never decide any request, always shadowed by something more specific, is 9.6 percent at 128 rules and 19.3 percent at 256
  • Growing a rule set also grows the amount of inert text. It consumes a reader's attention, enters every review, gets touched in every revision, and decides nothing

Translated into practice, two checks follow. Periodically count rules that have never fired, and check whether any request is judged by no rule at all. The first is dead text; the second is a hole. Neither is discovered by adding rules; both require treating the rule set as data.

5. Where the local reality bites - why we distributed a guideline is not evidence of control

This is where regulated environments matter. The habitual move when introducing automation is to add a clause to the internal policy and distribute it. Data handling principles, approval workflow, access scope, all written into a document, and that document dropped into the agent's context. Then this gets called control.

The problem is that supervisors are not looking for the existence of a policy. The pillars of the safeguards regimes that apply to personal data handling are management of access rights and retention and review of access records. That is, they ask whether permissions were actually narrowed and whether there is a record of who did what and when. A document answers neither question.

  • Saying a guideline was distributed only proves a policy exists
  • The next question is always whether that access was actually blocked, and that is where the answer stops
  • For humans, training completion is a partial answer. An agent has no concept of training
  • So when the policy file is the only layer, effective control is close to zero. The document cannot even record the violation
  • And as section 3 showed, adding clauses grows contradiction until the verdict falls to chance. Thickening the policy points the wrong way

Approval workflows have the same structure. Leave payments above a threshold require approval as prose only, and the agent may read it and seek approval, or it may decide for itself and proceed. As the paper noted, it may also report afterwards that it followed the approval process. Make the payment tool refuse to run without a valid approval identifier argument, and the path closes. What remains is a record in a form you can hand to an auditor.

Three things worth preparing for a compliance conversation. First, a configuration that narrows permissions to the scope of the work. You need to be able to show not what you granted but what you did not grant. Second, records of violating calls that were blocked. A record of attempts that hit a wall is stronger than a claim that nothing went wrong. Third, evidence that a test which deliberately violates the gate runs on a schedule. Without it, nobody knows when the gate stopped working. All three are artifacts of the system, not of the document.

6. Pushing enforcement points down into code

So what goes where? Enforcement is not one thing but several layers, and each layer differs in what it stops, what it costs, and where it fails. This section stays at the application and tool-permission layer. Isolation below that, at the operating system level, is a different subject and belongs to the kernel-level safety discussion.

LayerWhat it stopsWhen it actsCostLimit
Narrow the tool catalogThe capability is never grantedSession startCheapestBlocks legitimate work too
Argument schemaArguments outside format or rangeJust before the callLowRight format, wrong intent
Pre-execution gateCalls that violate a ruleJust before the callMediumNeeds the facts at call time
Confirmation stepIrreversible actionsJust before the callHuman timeOveruse breeds numbness
Post-exec verify and roll backResults unlike the estimateRight after the callHighIt already happened
Audit logStops nothingAlwaysLowRetrospective only
DocumentStops nothing—LowOwns the gray zone

Three cautions when using this table.

  • Higher rows are cheaper and more certain. The strongest control is not a clever checker but never handing over the tool. A session without a write tool beats any sentence of policy
  • Counting the audit log as control breaks the arithmetic. A log stops nothing. It is an after-the-fact instrument, and the reason you need one is that the layers above are incomplete
  • Placing one rule in several layers is design, not waste. Remove the tool, block it in the schema, block it at the gate. When one layer quietly turns off, the others remain

A common objection arrives here: with that many gates, nothing gets done. That is a fair worry, which is exactly why you need criteria for choosing what to push down first. Section 9 covers it. How to actually implement a hook or a gate is already covered in the piece on compiling rules into hooks, so it is not repeated here.

7. Where a document is still required

Read this far and you might conclude: drop the document, push everything into code. That is also wrong. Rules that genuinely cannot be pushed down exist, and there are more of them than you would guess. I measured how many.

The method: take 24 clauses from a fictional logistics company's policy. List the facts each clause needs to be judged. Then ask whether those facts exist at the instant before the tool call runs. What exists at call time is roughly the tool name, argument values, target path, actor role, timestamp, prior call history, environment name, affected row count, and an approval token.

In this corpus, 58.3 percent pushes down to a pre-execution gate. More than half. But 25 percent admits no mechanical verdict at all. Those six are the document's territory.

  • Gray zones. Use judgement on borderline disputes. The moment you define what borderline means, the definition generates a new gray zone
  • Priority principles. Prefer the least disruptive fix requires comparing the fallout of two options, and that comparison is not in the call-time facts
  • Exception principles. Exceptions are allowed when clearly in the customer interest. Force this into a gate and you produce false positives, and false positives produce workarounds
  • Conflict resolution. When rules conflict, follow the stricter one is undecidable as written, but as section 4 showed, rewrite it as narrower scope wins, deny beats allow on a tie, and it pushes down
  • Post-hoc items. Four sit here. They need the result to be judged, so they cannot be gates; rollback or an alarm is the answer

That last point is the real lesson of the table. Many clauses are not undecidable so much as not yet written in a decidable form. Rewrite and they move up a tier. That rewriting is the actual work.

Turning a rule into something decidable has a technique to it. Below are sentences you see everywhere and their conversions. Every organization and rule here is fictional.

The principle reduces to three lines.

  • Replace adjectives with numbers or lists. Careful becomes a threshold, sensitive becomes a pattern list, sufficient becomes the name of a check that must pass
  • Verify the facts the judgment needs exist at call time. If they do not, it is post-execution verification, not a gate. The classification in section 7 is exactly this work
  • You must be able to write in one sentence what fails on violation. If you cannot, it is still on the document layer

The last row was left in deliberately. Not converting what cannot be converted is part of the job. Force do not contact customers unnecessarily into no more than three per day and it becomes decidable but it is now a different rule. And when false positives get frequent, people build detours, and once detours exist the credibility of every gate collapses.

9. Rollout - how to choose what to push down first

Moving an entire policy into gates at once fails. You need an order. Treating priority as a product keeps it manageable.

Priority = (irreversible) × (facts available at call time) × (low human confirmation cost)

  • Irreversibility is the first axis. Anything you can undo is adequately covered by post-execution verification. Start with things that cannot be taken back: deletion, transmission, payment, publication
  • Decidability is the second. Use the classification from section 7 directly. Call-time facts alone means a gate; needing the result means rollback; needing a person means the document
  • Confirmation cost is the third. Attach a human confirmation to something that fires two hundred times a day and clicking becomes reflex and the confirmation becomes meaningless. Confirmation steps are worth their price only where they fire rarely
  • Put rules with an actual violation history first. Blocking imagined violations buys certain cost and uncertain benefit
  • Measure the false-positive rate first. Before attaching a gate, replay the logs and count how many calls this rule would have blocked. If it catches a lot of legitimate work, rewrite the rule

And one habit has to change: after an incident, add enforcement instead of adding a rule. The moment a review document says clause 3.4 added, that incident has been recorded but not closed. The questions worth asking instead are these.

  • What exactly was the tool call that produced this incident
  • Could that call have been filtered from its arguments alone? If so, it is a gate
  • If not, could the tool simply not have been granted in the first place
  • If neither, could it have been detected after execution and rolled back
  • If none of those, then and only then write it in the document — and state in the review that it landed in the document only

That last item carries the procedure. Not hiding the fact that something landed only in prose is the point. It is what lets you say precisely, the next time the same incident occurs, that it happened not because there was no rule but because there was no enforcement.

What every gate needs attached to it. A deliberate violation test. Build a call that breaks the rule, throw it at the gate, and treat a pass as a failure. Without this, nobody knows which deployment turned the gate off. And the test exposes the fact that a gate that only warns and lets the call through is not a gate but a log. Plenty of so-called blocking mechanisms spend months in exactly that state.

10. Closing: distrust the reflex to write one more rule

To summarize: documents and enforcement do not substitute for each other. A document carries what is inherently beyond mechanical judgment — intent, priority, exception principles — and enforcement is the layer on the call path that actually makes a violating call fail. Most organizations currently hold only the first, and when an incident happens they make the first one thicker.

The measurements again. As rule count grows, the contested region grows along with the number of rule pairs, and once large enough, reading order decides the verdict on roughly half of requests. One precedence sentence erases a large share of that but not all of it. And as rules grow, so does the share of sentences that decide nothing. These are properties of the rule set itself, not of any model; a human reader hits the same wall.

The citation again. The benchmark paper that measured long-context instruction following head on reported 36.2 percent for the best of thirty configurations under strict grading, and noted that nearly every failed trajectory ended with a confident claim of compliance. I did not measure any model's compliance rate myself. Those numbers mean something only inside that paper's grading rule.

One sentence to carry into practice. What makes a rule hold is not the force of the sentence but the mechanism that blocks the action when it goes wrong. So the next time add a line to the policy is about to be the only action item in a review, ask once more: what fails if this rule is broken? If the answer is nothing happens, that is a record, not a control.

There is something you can do right now. Open the rule document currently attached to your agent and pick out only the clauses that govern irreversible actions. Next to each, write what fails when it is violated. The number of blanks is your current level of control.

# Governing Agents — Turning Policy Prose Into Enforcement

> Source: braindetox.kr — A long policy document will not govern your agent
> Basis: a self-run simulation of document properties (Node 22) plus one public benchmark paper

> ⚠ Nothing below means「this makes you safe」. It means you are stacking layers that lower the odds.

## 0. Premise — documents and enforcement are different layers

| Layer | What it does | When violated | Is it control? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Document (policy)** | Records intent, priority, exception principles | Nothing happens | ❌ A record |
| **Enforcement (call path)** | Actually stops the call | The call fails | ✅ Control |

- A document is **what you hope was read**. Enforcement is **what cannot be done**.
- Adding one more line after an incident is not control. It is an **updated record**.
- Test: if you cannot name **what fails** when the rule is broken, it is not enforcement yet.

## 1. Vague rule → decidable rule conversion table

|---|---|---|---|

**Three lines of conversion**
1. Replace adjectives with a **number** or a **list** (careful → threshold, sensitive → pattern list).

3. You must be able to state **what fails** on violation in one sentence.

## 2. Enforcement placement table

| Layer | What it stops | When it acts | Cost | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Narrow the tool catalog** | The capability is never granted | Session start | Cheapest | Also blocks legitimate work |
| **Argument schema** | Arguments outside format or range | Just before the call | Low | Misses correct format with wrong intent |
| **Pre-exec gate** | Calls that violate a rule | Just before the call | Medium | Needs the facts at call time |
| **Confirmation step** | Irreversible actions | Just before the call | Human time | Overuse breeds numbness |
| **Post-exec verify and roll back** | Results that differ from the estimate | Right after the call | High | It already happened |
| **Audit log** | Stops nothing | Always | Low | Retrospective only |
| **Document** | Stops nothing | — | Low | Owns the judgment-shaped gray zone |

- **Higher rows are cheaper and more certain.** Lower rows are later and more expensive.
- Placing one rule in **several layers** is not waste. It is design.
- The moment you count the audit log as「control」the arithmetic breaks. It is an **after-the-fact tool**.

## 3. Choosing what to push down first

Priority = (irreversible) × (facts available at call time) × (low human confirmation cost)

- [ ] Is it **irreversible**? If it can be undone, lower the rank.
- [ ] Are the facts the judgment needs **all present at call time**? If not, it is post-exec verification, not a gate.
- [ ] Has this rule **actually been broken**? Do not start with imagined violations.
- [ ] **What share of legitimate work** would the block catch? Frequent false positives create workarounds.
- [ ] Would **nobody notice if the gate were off**? Then it is not a gate.

## 4. Rollout checklist

- [ ] Extracted the **irreversible action list** from the rule document
- [ ] Moved the ones decidable from call-time information alone **into gates**
- [ ] Confirmed the call **actually fails** when a gate fires (not merely logged and let through)
- [ ] Added a test that **deliberately violates** the gate, and treats a pass as a failure
- [ ] Wrote the **conflict precedence** into the document in one sentence
- [ ] Checked whether any request is **judged by no rule at all** (the hole)
- [ ] Periodically count rules that **have never fired** (dead text)
- [ ] Tool permissions are narrowed to the **minimum the work needs**
- [ ] Confirmation steps exist **only where they are truly needed** (overuse → numbness)
- [ ] Audit records carry **actor, time, argument digest, and whether it was blocked**
- [ ] The agent's **self-report is not accepted as evidence** (judge from the execution record)
- [ ] Post-incident review considers **adding enforcement before adding a rule**
- [ ] Judgment-shaped items were **left in the document**, not forced into gates
- [ ] You can present **block records**, not「we distributed the guideline」, to an outside reviewer
- [ ] A **conflict check** runs whenever the rule count grows

## 5. Do not

- ❌ Add **one more line** after every incident and stop there — that is a record, not control
- ❌ Use rule-document **length as an indicator of control**
- ❌ Force judgment-shaped items **into gates** — false positives create workarounds
- ❌ Treat a **generalized marketing claim** about instruction following as a design premise
- ❌ Accept the agent saying「I followed the policy」as **evidence of compliance**
- ❌ Leave a gate that only **warns and lets the call through** — that is a log
- ❌ Declare that「this combination is safe」— stacked layers only lower the odds
