The Long-Standing Barrier Imperfect-Information Games Posed to AI

In artificial intelligence research, strategic board games have long served as primary benchmarks for measuring computational power and search algorithms. In environments where piece locations and ranks are completely transparent to both players, standard methodologies for projecting future moves across structured trees became deeply established. However, when private information is concealed from view, standard search trees cannot be directly evaluated.

In imperfect-information settings, an agent cannot uniquely identify the true world state. Because optimal responses diverge depending on hidden opponent variables, the system must maintain probability distributions across multiple hypothetical states. Computing moves at decision time becomes computationally intractable as unknown enemy identities cause the branching factor of the search tree to explode.

The classic board wargame Stratego stood as an enduring obstacle across both the scale of hidden information and the length of decision horizons. Dozens of concealed pieces maneuver across the grid while combat unfolds under fog of war, demanding continuous bluffing and deception. Even well-funded industrial research laboratories previously encountered severe difficulty in constructing systems capable of reliably defeating human champions.

The primary bottlenecks that imperfect-information environments impose on AI algorithms include the following structural hurdles:

  • Agents cannot identify the exact global state and must instead maintain probability distributions over vast hypothetical setups
  • Test-time search becomes computationally intractable because unknown piece identities cause search tree branching factors to explode
  • Successful play demands deception and bluffing, but excessive bluffing renders threats hollow while completely honest moves become trivial to read
  • Self-play reinforcement learning algorithms frequently fall into cyclical loops where policies endlessly counter previous versions without converging

Stratego Hidden Piece Rules and the Challenge of 2,000-Move Horizons

Stratego is a two-player zero-sum board game contested on a 10x10 grid where each competitor commands 40 pieces with the ultimate goal of capturing the opponent flag. The army comprises ranked military officers ranging from Marshal and General down to Scouts and Spies, supplemented by stationary Bombs and the Flag itself.

The defining rule is that a player sees where opposing pieces are positioned, but not what ranks they hold. Identities remain hidden until two pieces collide on the same square. At that moment, both pieces are revealed; the lower-ranked piece is permanently removed, while the victor retains its position with its identity now visible to both sides. Players must reconstruct the opponent order of battle by evaluating combat outcomes and historical movement trajectories.

Furthermore, Stratego features remarkably long game horizons. As Gabriele Farina noted, while standard games in other traditional board pastimes often conclude in roughly 40 moves, a contest of Stratego routinely extends across hundreds and easily up to 2,000 moves as players maneuver cautiously and probe defenses. Maintaining consistent probabilistic beliefs across 40 hidden units over thousands of plies represents an immense cognitive burden.

Replacing Exhaustive Search with Belief Network Sampling

In Stratego, the number of possible initial configurations is astronomical. Because 40 pieces can be arranged in any order across the back ranks, exhaustive enumeration of all possible enemy setups is completely impossible. If an AI attempted to evaluate every potential placement before choosing an action, the computation would instantly freeze.

To overcome this computational barrier, the collaborative research team from CMU, MIT, NYU, and Stanford engineered a second neural network called a belief model. The belief model is trained specifically to guess the identities of the opponent hidden pieces based on how they have moved across the board throughout the game.

Equipped with this belief network, Ataraxos avoids the need to iterate through all possible arrangements. Instead, it probabilistically samples a manageable set of plausible setups that align with observed movement histories. The AI then simulates candidate moves within these sampled boards, ultimately selecting the action that delivers the strongest evaluated outcome.

The core functions of the belief model in replacing exhaustive search with informed sampling include:

  • Ingesting historical movement trajectories to estimate the true rank and identity of hidden opponent units
  • Sampling high-probability plausible setups rather than computing intractable combinations across the entire state space
  • Simulating candidate actions across sampled boards to select moves with the highest expected win probability
  • Enabling responsive test-time search under severe imperfect information without introducing computational latency

163 Million Self-Play Games — Staged Adjustments to Prevent Policy Cycling

Alongside test-time search enabled by the belief model, Ataraxos derived its baseline strategic policy from an extensive self-play reinforcement learning campaign encompassing 163 million games. Rather than imitating human tournament logs, the AI competed against iterations of itself, discovering winning heuristics directly from foundational rules.

However, self-play in imperfect-information environments carries a well-documented vulnerability. Because players possess asymmetric information, self-play algorithms tend to travel in circles rather than converging monotonically. A policy developed to exploit one setup becomes vulnerable to a counter-strategy, which in turn leads back to the original vulnerability, causing cyclical oscillations.

The research team resolved this instability by instituting a staged update schedule. Early in training, the algorithm applied big, bold policy adjustments after each game, encouraging broad exploration across diverse formations and tactics. Later in the training lifecycle, update steps were constricted to small, careful adjustments, preventing disruptive oscillations and allowing the policy to settle into a stable equilibrium.

Key attributes of this self-play curriculum include:

  • Executed 163 million self-play matches to explore robust piece formations and deceptive maneuvering strategies
  • Enacted bold policy modifications early in training to avoid becoming trapped in premature local equilibria
  • Shifted to small, precise updates during later phases to suppress cyclical policy looping under hidden information
  • Achieved superhuman performance using approximately 34 times fewer self-play games than prior frontier architectures

Empirical Measurements from 20 Games Against a World Champion and 40 Championship Matches

The practical playing strength of Ataraxos was evaluated through direct head-to-head matches against elite human champions. The primary opponent was Pim Niemeijer of the Netherlands, a four-time world champion ranked world number one for more than 600 weeks and widely recognized as the premier Stratego player in history. Niemeijer contested 20 online matches against Ataraxos across three weeks, incentivized by a $100 payout for each individual win.

Niemeijer knew in advance that the AI operated as a static, non-adaptive model that would not tailor counter-tactics specifically to his habits, giving him ample opportunity to probe for algorithmic weaknesses. Despite this preparation, Ataraxos achieved 15 wins, 1 loss, and 4 draws. Niemeijer was held to just a single victory. Subsequently, at the 2025 Stratego World Championship, Ataraxos challenged attendees and seasoned competitors across 40 games, securing 38 victories (a 95% win rate).

The research team emphasized that the solitary loss conceded to Niemeijer did not reflect a fatal flaw in the algorithm. Playing Stratego effectively mandates randomizing piece setups, ensuring that luck remains an inescapable variable. Even a theoretically sound strategy will occasionally lose to unfavorable starting alignments, and researchers acknowledged that good fortune also aided the AI in several victories.

During competitive play, Ataraxos exhibited distinct behavioral patterns:

  • True to its ancient Greek namesake representing undisturbed calm, it responded to deficits methodically without desperate gambles
  • When assessing that the opponent lacked factual basis to suspect a weakness, it left the position untouched despite visual exposure
  • Free from human psychological projections, it did not assume the opponent knew its secrets and executed logical bluffs
  • Successfully orchestrated deceptive comebacks from estimated victory probabilities as low as approximately 2%
  • Regularly deployed unconventional setups, such as placing the flag in a corner protected by only two bombs

From 1,024 Custom Chips to 16 GPUs — Cost Reductions and Simulator Architecture

Beyond competitive match results, Ataraxos attracted major attention for radically reducing the computational capital required to solve complex games. DeepMind's pioneering DeepNash model, published in Science in 2022, trained for two to three months across 1,024 specialized Google TPU chips. The Ataraxos team calculated that running equivalent compute at 2025 cloud pricing would require approximately $3 million to $4.5 million.

In contrast, Ataraxos completed its core training using 16 standard commercial GPUs for one week, accompanied by 4 GPUs running for four days to train the belief network. Total computational expenditure amounted to only a few thousand dollars. Operating within academic laboratory constraints, the researchers attained superior performance while lowering compute costs by several orders of magnitude.

This breakthrough was unlocked by an ultra-fast simulator engineered by lead author Samuel Sokota and Professor Gabriele Farina. By compiling Stratego rules directly onto parallel graphics processor pipelines, their simulator executed millions of moves per second, eliminating communication overhead between host CPUs and accelerators.

Key highlights of the computational architecture include:

  • Replaced months of training on 1,024 custom chips with a single-week run on 16 commercial GPUs
  • Engineered a native GPU simulator executing millions of moves per second to eliminate data pipeline bottlenecks
  • Reduced total self-play match volume by approximately 34 times while achieving higher playing strength through belief sampling
  • Demonstrated that refined game-theoretic modeling and simulator optimization outperform brute-force hardware scaling

Extension to Other Games and the Limits of Explainability

The researchers demonstrated that belief-guided sampling extends effectively across diverse imperfect-information environments. In Barrage Stratego, an accelerated 8-piece tactical variant, the system defeated three world champions. It also attained state-of-the-art cooperative scores in Hanabi, where players see partner hands but not their own, and outperformed specialized bots in the popular Chinese card game Dou Dizhu.

Nevertheless, the team highlighted unresolved technical limitations. Chief among these is explainability. As Farina stated, Ataraxos cannot explain why it chooses specific moves. While its neural networks and search routines identify highly effective tactical maneuvers, the underlying causal reasoning remains locked within an uninterpretable black box.

Furthermore, board games operate within rigid rule sets and unambiguous termination criteria. Transferring these techniques to open-ended human domains such as commercial negotiations, economic forecasting, or real-world defense operations presents substantial theoretical hurdles. The researchers noted that engineering systems that are both strategically dominant and human-interpretable remains a critical open challenge.

The scope and boundaries of the research can be summarized as follows:

  • Established a versatile decision-making paradigm spanning competitive, cooperative, and team-based imperfect-information games
  • Demonstrated strong potential for strategic wargaming and adversarial counter-move modeling in structured domains
  • Remains constrained by black-box execution, unable to provide causal explanations for tactical decisions to human operators
  • Requires substantial theoretical expansion before translating from fixed-rule board games to dynamic real-world environments
