Anti-Social: Your Feed Is Now Ruled by Trends, Not Friends

How Social Media Moved From the Friend Graph to the Recommendation Graph

Jun 10, 2026 Tech

Introduction: Where Did the Friends Go?

Open Instagram or Facebook after a while and start scrolling. Something feels off. The updates from friends, the travel photos of acquaintances, the everyday moments of colleagues are nowhere to be found. Instead you get an endless stream of short videos from people you have never followed, challenges that are apparently trending somewhere, and content an opaque algorithm decided "you might like." Despite carrying the name "social," the traces of social relationships in the feed are fading away.

This is not just our imagination. According to internal documents Meta disclosed during the 2025 U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) antitrust trial, the share of time spent viewing friend content on Facebook had fallen to around 17 percent, and on Instagram the share of friend content was only single digits. Even Mark Zuckerberg admitted that "sharing with friends is declining." This article takes the discussion of social media's "anti-socialization" featured on GeekNews (news.hada.io) as a starting point, then adds my own analysis and the perspective of a user. We will look in a balanced way at why the friend feed became the trend feed, what that change leaves us with, and how to use these apps without being pushed around by them.

1. The Original Promise of "Social" Media

The core value of early social media was clear: connecting with people you already know. Facebook began as a service for finding schoolmates, Twitter was a space for following acquaintances' thoughts in chronological order, and early Instagram was closer to an album of photos posted by friends, shown by time.

Underneath this structure lay the "social graph," which we can call the friend graph. Whom am I connected to, and content flows along that web of relationships. When you friended or followed someone, their posts entered your feed. Chronological ordering was the default, and what you saw was the direct result of the relationships you had formed. In other words, the entity deciding what you saw was, in the end, your own relationships.

This model had a clear limitation. If you had few friends or they were inactive, the feed went empty, and the content supply was tied to the diligence of your network. That very limitation became the seed of the next change.

2. The Rise of the Recommendation Graph - The Game TikTok Changed

The one that flipped the board was TikTok. Its For You page barely looked at friend relationships from the start. Instead it learned what you watch to the end, what you linger on, what you replay, and pushes you an endless stream of videos you are statistically likely to enjoy, regardless of relationships. This is the "interest graph," the recommendation graph.

The difference between the friend graph and the recommendation graph can be compressed into one sentence:

The friend graph fills your feed by "whom you know," while the recommendation graph fills it by "what you react to."

This difference creates an unlimited content supply. The posts your friends make are finite, but selecting from the content of hundreds of millions of people worldwide makes supply effectively infinite. And infinite supply means the possibility of infinite watch-time. As TikTok grew explosively, rival platforms had no choice. So began what is commonly called "TikTokification."

3. Why It Happened - Watch-Time and Ad Revenue

The reason platforms abandoned the friend feed for the recommendation feed is not a question of morality but of economics. Most social media revenue comes from advertising, and ad revenue, simplified, is proportional to "time a user stays multiplied by ad density." The longer they hold you, the more ads they can show.

Here the fates of the two models diverge.

  • The ceiling of the friend graph: The posts your friends make in a day top out at a few dozen. Once you have seen them, there is nothing left, so you close the app.
  • The infinity of the recommendation graph: It keeps pulling the optimal next video from a global content pool. It is designed so that the moment of "I've seen it all" never arrives.

From the platform's standpoint the answer is obvious: redesign the feed toward whatever holds the same user longer. When Meta argued at the FTC trial that "we now compete with TikTok and YouTube as a discovery and entertainment space, not as a social network in the old sense," it was the most candid revelation of this change, a transition from a space for friends to a space for time.

4. Friend Graph vs Recommendation Graph - A Head-to-Head

Placing the two models side by side makes the nature of the change clear.

Aspect Friend Graph (Social) Recommendation Graph (Anti-Social)
Feed driver Relationships you formed Taste an algorithm predicts
Content supply Finite (your network's activity) Effectively infinite (global pool)
Ordering Mostly chronological By predicted engagement score
Creator exposure Depends on follower count Competes on content itself
User experience Updates from people you know Trend content from strangers
Key metric Relationship retention Watch-time maximization

What matters is that the recommendation graph is not unconditionally "evil." The fact that a brand-new creator with zero followers can earn millions of views on content merit alone is a clear upside of the recommendation graph. It broke a structure where your network was your power and opened opportunity to the unknown. The problem begins with the fact that this engine is optimized not for "discovering good content" but for "holding you longer."

5. What It Leaves Users With - Comparison, Dopamine, Weakened Connection

Let me sum up what the recommendation feed leaves in a user's mind in three points.

5.1 Endless Comparison

The recommendation feed shows you only "the best-edited moments" selected from the entire world: the most stunning trip, the most successful person, the most perfect body, the most viral clip. You end up comparing your ordinary everyday life not with a friend's ordinary life, but with the top one percent of highlights curated from across the planet. Analyses suggest that the accumulation of this upward comparison tends to harm self-esteem and satisfaction.

5.2 The Dopamine Reward Loop

Infinite scroll and autoplay place occasional rewards (the rare "jackpot" video) unpredictably. This is a variable reward structure like a slot machine, creating the urge to swipe just one more time. The more polished the stimulation, the harder it is to stop, and the result is unintended overuse.

5.3 The Weakening of Real Connection

This is the most paradoxical result. The longer you use "social" media, the less you actually interact with friends. As trend content from strangers takes over the feed, news from people close to you gets buried. As Zuckerberg himself acknowledged, sharing with friends has moved from the public feed to private messengers. The public square has become a venue for entertainment consumption, while real relationships are retreating ever deeper into private chat rooms.

This trend oddly resembles the phenomenon of people spending more time talking with AI, in that "something well optimized" replaces the place of relationships. I explore that theme more deeply in a separate piece, People Tired of Talking to AI.

6. Platform Patterns - Same Direction, Different Speeds

Every platform is heading toward the recommendation graph, but the pattern and pace differ.

  • TikTok: The original, born on the recommendation graph. Friend relationships are merely an add-on; the For You page is the service itself. It became the reference point everyone else imitates.
  • Instagram: The case that changed most abruptly. By putting Reels front and center, it rapidly grew the share of recommended video. Its identity shifted from "an album of friends' photos" to "a short-video recommender," and the pushback from users was significant.
  • Facebook: It officially redefined its main feed as a "discovery and entertainment space." The share of friend content fell into the low double digits, and Zuckerberg went so far as to say "Facebook is no longer the culture."
  • X (formerly Twitter): It separates the chronological "Following" tab from the algorithmic "For You" tab, but pushes For You as the default. It is the case that most blatantly baked the move to the recommendation graph into the UI.

The interesting point is that the place where the original spirit of the friend graph survives best is not the public feed but private messengers and group chats. High-density spaces such as KakaoTalk open chats, Discord servers, and group conversations have become the last bastion of the "social."

7. A User's Perspective - The Particulars of Our Context

Mapping this change onto a real-world context reveals a few distinctive textures.

  • A messenger-centric culture: In some markets, like Korea, a strong messenger such as KakaoTalk anchored the center of relationships early on. So even as public feeds turned into entertainment, "real relationships" were already living in chat and group rooms. The shock of the recommendation-feed era was relatively cushioned.
  • Sensitivity to trends: In cultures where trends spread fast, the "trend amplification" effect of recommendation algorithms works especially strongly. A particular challenge or meme can become a nationwide phenomenon in a very short time.
  • Combination with a comparison culture: When a social atmosphere sensitive to comparing oneself with others meets a recommendation feed that shows "top one percent highlights," there is a risk of amplified relative deprivation. This is an area worth consciously managing at the level of personal usage habits.

8. How to Use It Healthily - Becoming the Owner of Your Feed

Turning the algorithm off entirely is unrealistic. Instead, I propose practical ways to shift your center of gravity from "passive consumption" to "intentional use."

  1. Change your default tab. Where platforms allow it, like X's "Following" tab or Instagram's "Following" feed, make the chronological, friend-centric tab your default. Enter the algorithmic recommendation tab only by conscious choice.
  2. Turn off autoplay and notifications. The two fuels of infinite scroll are autoplay and notifications. Just turning these off greatly reduces the "suddenly 30 minutes gone" effect.
  3. Set a time ceiling. Use the app's own usage limits or your OS screen-time tools to build a physical ceiling. The key is to block with tools rather than willpower.
  4. Ask "why did I open this?" Before opening the app, ask yourself once: do I have a specific purpose, or is it just habit? If you opened it with no purpose, that itself is a signal to close it.
  5. Keep relationships in relationship spaces. If you are curious about a friend's news, do not dig through the public feed; send them a direct message. Separating the recommendation feed as entertainment from real connection as conversation is the healthiest way to use these apps.

9. The Alternative Trend - Small Communities and a Return to Messengers

The user backlash has already begun. People who feel the fatigue of the giant public square are clearly moving back toward smaller, denser spaces.

  • Small communities: Closed spaces of a few hundred people, such as Discord servers and interest-based small groups, are drawing attention again. Content is made by shared interest and conversation, not by an algorithm.
  • A return to messengers: More people are moving the everyday moments they used to post on public feeds into close-friend group chats or one-on-one conversations. The "growth of private sharing" Zuckerberg acknowledged is exactly this trend.
  • "Slow" services: Chronological, subscription-based services that intentionally exclude infinite scroll and algorithms, along with old approaches like newsletters and RSS, are being re-evaluated in the context of digital detox.

What all these trends share is the will to return from "infinite content an algorithm picks for you" to "finite relationships and information you chose." The larger question of whether technology should replace or merely assist human relationships also touches the debate around AI. 10. A Critical Balance - Not Demonizing the Recommendation Feed

Here we must keep balance. Seeing the recommendation graph as purely "bad" is also a lazy conclusion. Recommendation algorithms have created clear value.

  • Democratized discovery: Creators with no network can now succeed on content alone. The lower barrier to entry is clear progress.
  • Refined interests: You can encounter deep content on topics you truly love without the limits of your network. It does not matter whether your friends share that interest.
  • Connecting minorities: People with niche tastes or identities who are geographically isolated can connect with the same-interest community worldwide.

In the end the problem is not the tool itself but whose interest the tool is optimized for. The current recommendation engine is tuned to platform watch-time, not to user satisfaction or growth. If the same recommendation technology were optimized for "letting you leave well" rather than "holding you longer," it could produce entirely different results. The target of criticism should be the objective function, not the recommendation technology.

11. Looking Ahead - A Diverging Future for Social

Going forward, social media is likely to move from a form where one giant platform holds everything toward one that diverges by purpose. Entertainment consumption goes to recommendation feeds (the TikTok type), real relationships to messengers and small communities, and information subscription to newsletters and RSS. The era of expecting "friend news and trend content at once" in a single app is effectively ending.

The regulatory environment is another variable. Debates over protecting minors, algorithmic transparency, and limits on infinite scroll are underway in many countries and can directly affect recommendation-engine design. Yet unless regulation changes the foundation of the business model, the maximization of ad-driven watch-time, the change is likely to stay on the surface.

12. Conclusion + Three Things You Can Do Today

The name "social media" is now only half true. It is media, but it is becoming less and less social. The move from the friend graph to the recommendation graph is not a conspiracy but the natural consequence of an ad-based business model, and not something to be judged good or evil in itself. What matters is to understand the change precisely and to wield the feed as a tool instead of being dragged along by it.

Let me close with three things you can apply today.

  1. Change the tab. Make chronological "Following," not the algorithmic "For You," your default. Take back from the algorithm the initiative over what you see.
  2. Separate relationships from consumption. Real friend news goes to direct messages; the recommendation feed stays light entertainment. Do not try to get both from one app.
  3. Ask "why did I open this?" If you started infinite scroll with no purpose, that is your signal to close it. The owner of the feed should be you, not the algorithm.

That trends rather than friends now rule the feed is a current that is hard to reverse. But who owns your time and attention is still something we can choose. Real connection is not recommended by an algorithm. It always begins when we reach out ourselves.
