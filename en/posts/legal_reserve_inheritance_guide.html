1. What Is Forced Heirship (Yurubun)?

In Korea, a person generally has the freedom to dispose of their assets during their lifetime through gifts, or by will after death. However, if this freedom were unlimited, close family members could be left with nothing at all. To prevent this, Korea's Civil Act establishes a system called yurubun (유류분) — commonly translated as "forced heirship" or "forced share."

Forced heirship is the minimum portion of a decedent's estate that the law guarantees to certain heirs, regardless of the decedent's will or lifetime gifts. It represents a balance between the decedent's freedom to distribute property and the protection of close family members. The rules are set out in Civil Act Articles 1112 through 1118.

The system changed substantially after a landmark Constitutional Court ruling in 2024 and two later amendments to the Civil Act (Act No. 20432 of September 20, 2024, and Act No. 21454 of March 17, 2026). This guide describes the statutory text in force as of September 29, 2026.

2. Who Is Entitled to Forced Heirship?

Not every heir can claim a forced share. Civil Act Article 1112 defines "the forced share of heirs" and limits it to specific classes of relatives.

2.1 Current Entitled Heirs

Under the current text of Civil Act Article 1112, the forced heirs are:

  • Lineal descendants: children, grandchildren, and other downward-line relatives
  • Spouse: the legally married spouse (common-law/de facto spouses are not included)
  • Lineal ascendants: parents, grandparents, and other upward-line relatives

Lineal ascendants only become heirs — and therefore forced heirs — when there are no lineal descendants or surviving spouse. In practice, forced heirship claims are most often brought by children and spouses.

Because the forced share belongs to heirs, a person who loses heir status through a family court's declaration of forfeiture of inheritance rights (Civil Act Article 1004-2) cannot claim a forced share either. The March 17, 2026 amendment extended that declaration from lineal ascendants to all heirs, including lineal descendants and spouses (see 7.2).

2.2 Siblings: Removed After the 2024 Constitutional Court Ruling

Former Civil Act Article 1112(4) granted siblings a forced share of one-third of their statutory share. On April 25, 2024, the Constitutional Court declared this provision unconstitutional outright (2020HunGa4 et al.).

The Court reasoned that granting siblings a forced share, even though their contribution to forming the estate and their expectation of inheriting it can hardly be recognized, lacks a justifiable basis. A provision declared unconstitutional loses effect from the date of the decision, so siblings have had no forced share since April 25, 2024. The Civil Act amendment of September 20, 2024 (Act No. 20432) then deleted the item from the text, which now reads "Deleted <2024.9.20>".

3. Forced Share Ratios — How Much Is Guaranteed?

The forced share is calculated as a fraction of each heir's statutory share under the Civil Act. The ratio depends on the type of heir (Civil Act Article 1112).

Entitled HeirForced Share Ratio
Lineal descendants1/2 of statutory share
Spouse1/2 of statutory share
Lineal ascendants1/3 of statutory share

Example: Suppose the decedent leaves a spouse and two children. Under Civil Act Article 1009, the statutory shares are: spouse 3/7, each child 2/7. The forced share of the spouse is 3/14 (half of 3/7); the forced share of each child is 1/7 (half of 2/7). Even if the decedent willed the entire estate to a third party, these family members may claim back their forced share.

Statutory shares depend on who inherits together, so the exact forced share always starts from the statutory share.

4. Calculating the Forced Share

Before computing the forced share, it is necessary to determine the base estate for the calculation. Civil Act Article 1113(1) provides the formula (translation):

The forced share shall be calculated by adding the value of donated property to the value of the property held by the decedent at the time of commencement of succession and deducting the full amount of debts.

4.1 The Formula

Base Estate = (Property held at death) + (Gifts made during lifetime) – (Debts)

Under Civil Act Article 1114, gifts are included only if made within the one year before succession commenced, unless both parties made the gift knowing it would harm forced heirs, in which case earlier gifts are also included. However, case law holds that gifts to co-heirs (other heirs) are included regardless of when they were made.

4.2 Computing the Shortfall

The amount actually recoverable is the forced share shortfall: the forced share minus what the heir has already received.

Shortfall = Forced Share Amount – Special Benefits Already Received (prior gifts/bequests to the heir) – Net Inheritance Received

This calculation can be complex in practice. Professional legal assistance is strongly recommended.

4.3 Compensation for Care and Contribution

A contribution share (Civil Act Article 1008-2) may be credited to an heir who made special contributions to the estate. However, Article 1118, which lists the provisions applied mutatis mutandis to forced shares, did not include Article 1008-2, so gifts a caregiving heir received in return for years of support were still counted in the forced share calculation. The Constitutional Court found this part incompatible with the Constitution in 2024.

The March 17, 2026 amendment added a proviso to Article 1008: where a gift or bequest was made as compensation for specially supporting the decedent through cohabitation, nursing, or other means over a considerable period, or for specially contributing to maintaining or increasing the decedent's property, it is not treated as a special benefit to the extent corresponding to that contribution. Because Article 1118 applies Article 1008 to forced shares, the annotation on the current text states that this amendment reflects the intent of the ruling on Article 1118. Under Article 2 of the Addenda, the proviso also applies to successions that commenced on or after April 25, 2024.

5. How to File a Forced Heirship Claim

When a forced heir's share has been infringed, they may assert their right to claim return under Civil Act Article 1115.

5.1 Who to Claim Against

The respondent is whoever received property that infringes the forced heir's share:

  • Legatee: a person who received property under the decedent's will
  • Donee: a person who received a lifetime gift from the decedent

Civil Act Article 1116 provides that a claim against a gift may not be made until the bequests have been returned. Claims must therefore be directed first at legatees; claims against donees are possible only if the shortfall cannot be fully satisfied from bequests. Where several persons received gifts or bequests, each returns in proportion to the value received (Article 1115(2)).

5.2 How to File

A forced heirship claim does not have to be made through litigation; it can also be exercised out of court.

  • Out of court: A written demand (e.g., certified mail / registered letter) or oral notice is sufficient to exercise the right. Certified mail is recommended for evidence and to toll the statute of limitations.
  • Civil lawsuit: If the respondent refuses to cooperate, the heir may file a civil action in the competent court.

The method of return changed fundamentally with the Civil Act amendment that took effect on March 17, 2026. Amended Article 1115(1) provides that the forced heir may claim payment of the monetary value of the property up to the amount of the shortfall, and that interest accrues from the date payment is demanded (Act No. 21454, promulgated and effective March 17, 2026). The obligor therefore pays money rather than transferring the asset itself. The official statement of reasons for the amendment likewise lists, as a main change, that the method of returning the forced share shortfall is set as return of value.

The previous text said only that the heir could claim return of the property, and case law treated return in kind as the rule, allowing monetary compensation only where return in kind was impossible or significantly difficult. That approach produced fragmented co-ownership of real estate and scattered shareholdings that prolonged disputes, which is why the legislature switched to monetary payment.

The change is not retroactive. Article 3 of the Addenda (application of the forced-share preservation rule) provides that amended Article 1115(1) applies to successions commencing on or after the effective date of the Act. The controlling fact is therefore the date succession commences, i.e. the decedent's date of death. If that date falls near the boundary, confirm the current statutory text with a lawyer.

6. Statute of Limitations

The right to claim a forced share does not last forever. Civil Act Article 1117 provides that the right to claim return is extinguished by prescription unless exercised within one year from the time the forced heir learned of the commencement of succession and of the gift or bequest to be returned, and likewise when ten years have passed since succession commenced.

6.1 Short Period: 1 Year from Knowledge

The claim expires one year from the date the heir becomes aware of both the commencement of succession and the existence of a gift or bequest that infringes the forced share. Courts have interpreted "awareness" as knowledge of both facts — merely knowing a gift was made is not enough without also knowing it infringes the forced share.

6.2 Long-Stop: 10 Years from Succession

Regardless of whether the heir is aware, the claim expires ten years from the date succession commences (i.e., the date of the decedent's death). This is an absolute deadline; once ten years pass, the claim cannot be filed even if the infringement was only recently discovered.

6.3 Practical Notes

Whichever period expires first extinguishes the claim. For example, if the heir learns of the infringement nine years after the decedent's death, the ten-year period may expire before the one-year period runs. Prompt action is essential. The statute of limitations can be interrupted by filing a lawsuit, obtaining a provisional attachment, or obtaining an acknowledgment of the debt.

7. The 2024 Constitutional Court Ruling and the Civil Act Amendments

2024 was a watershed for Korea's forced heirship system. Below are the Constitutional Court's decision and the legislation that followed.

7.1 Constitutional Court Decision of April 25, 2024 (2020HunGa4 et al.)

The Court ruled provision by provision. It upheld Articles 1113 through 1116 (calculation, gifts included, preservation, order of return); the other holdings were:

  • Siblings' forced share (Article 1112(4)) — unconstitutional: unanimous decision; the provision lost effect from the date of the decision.
  • No grounds for losing the forced share (Article 1112(1)–(3)) — incompatible with the Constitution: the Court found it unreasonable that heirs who, for example, abandoned or abused the decedent for a long period still kept a forced share because no separate grounds for losing it were provided. The provisions remained applicable until amended, with a deadline of December 31, 2025.
  • Contribution share not applied (Article 1118) — incompatible with the Constitution: because Article 1008-2 on contribution shares was not applied to forced shares, gifts received by a person who supported the decedent were still included in the base estate. Applicable until amended, with the same deadline.

7.2 Key Points of the 2024 and 2026 Amendments

The follow-up legislation came in two stages. The amended Civil Act (Act No. 21454) was promulgated on March 17, 2026 and took effect the same day.

  • Deletion of siblings' forced share — 2024 amendment: Article 1112(4) was deleted by the amendment of September 20, 2024 (Act No. 20432, effective January 31, 2025). The same amendment created the declaration of forfeiture of inheritance rights (Article 1004-2) aimed at lineal ascendants, effective January 1, 2026.
  • Forfeiture extended to all heirs — 2026 amendment: where an heir seriously breached the duty to support the decedent, committed a serious crime against the decedent or certain relatives, or otherwise treated them in a grossly unjust way, any heir — lineal ascendants, lineal descendants, and spouses alike — can now be subject to a declaration of forfeiture (Article 1004-2). No separate "forfeiture of the forced share" article was created; losing inheritance rights means the forced share cannot be claimed either.
  • Compensatory gifts and bequests excluded from special benefits — 2026 amendment: gifts and bequests made as compensation for specially supporting the decedent or contributing to their property are not treated as special benefits to the extent of that contribution (new proviso to Article 1008; see 4.3).
  • Switch to return of value — 2026 amendment: amended Article 1115(1) replaces return in kind with a claim for payment of monetary value as the rule, with interest from the date of demand. Under Article 3 of the Addenda it applies only to successions commencing on or after March 17, 2026 and is not retroactive (see 5.2).

The reach of the amended provisions differs by article under the Addenda. Article 2 applies the Article 1008 proviso to successions commencing on or after April 25, 2024, and applies amended Article 1004-2 to such successions where the relevant conduct occurred before the Act took effect. Article 4 gives co-heirs a separate six-month window from the effective date to seek forfeiture where succession commenced between April 25, 2024 and the effective date and they already knew, before the effective date, that a person with grounds for forfeiture had become an heir. Article 3, by contrast, applies amended Article 1115(1) only to successions commencing after the Act took effect, so the switch to monetary payment is not retroactive. It is therefore wrong to say that "the 2026 amendment is retroactive" across the board; the answer depends on which article is at issue.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Can a will override forced heirship?

Not entirely. Even a clearly expressed will cannot eliminate the forced share of entitled heirs. However, if a prospective heir falls under the grounds in Article 1004-2 — such as a serious breach of the duty of support — the decedent may express an intention to forfeit that person's inheritance rights by a notarized will, and the executor must then petition the family court. Once forfeiture is declared, that person is no longer an heir and cannot claim a forced share. Whether to grant the petition is decided by the family court, weighing the circumstances and severity of the grounds, among other factors.

Does renouncing inheritance also waive the forced share?

Yes. An heir who formally renounces their inheritance loses their status as an heir and therefore cannot claim a forced share. In the case of a qualified acceptance (limited liability), heir status remains, but the situation is more nuanced and requires professional advice.

Does a gift made 20 years ago count toward the forced share calculation?

Gifts to third parties are generally included only if made within one year before death (or made with knowledge of harm to forced heirs). However, gifts to co-heirs are included regardless of how long ago they were made. If such a gift was compensation for care or contribution, the Article 1008 proviso added in 2026 may exclude it from special benefits to the extent of that contribution.

Do I need to go to court to make a forced heirship claim?

No. You may make the claim out of court — for example, by sending a formal written demand. However, if the other party refuses to cooperate, litigation will be necessary. Bear in mind the statute of limitations: 1 year from knowledge, 10 years from death.

How does the forced share work when a spouse and children both survive?

When a spouse and lineal descendants inherit together, the spouse's statutory share equals a child's share plus 50% (for example, with a spouse and two children, each child takes 2/7 and the spouse 3/7). Because the forced share is half of each statutory share, each child's forced share is 1/7 and the spouse's is 3/14. The actual calculation can become complex depending on the assets involved.

## 3. Before you claim

- [ ] Confirmed the decedent's date of death (commencement of succession)
- [ ] Wrote down the day you learned of the succession and of the gift or bequest to be returned (1 year from that day, Art. 1117)
- [ ] Confirmed that 10 years have not passed since succession commenced (Art. 1117)
- [ ] Calculated the statutory shares first (the spouse gets a child's share plus 50%)
- [ ] Set the base estate as property at commencement + gifts - debts (Art. 1113(1))
- [ ] Included gifts to third parties only from the final year (earlier ones too if both parties knew of the harm, Art. 1114)
- [ ] Included gifts to co-heirs regardless of timing (case law)
- [ ] Marked separately any gift made as compensation for care or contribution (Art. 1008 proviso)
- [ ] Ordered the respondents: legatees (bequests) first, donees (gifts) only for any remaining shortfall (Art. 1116)
