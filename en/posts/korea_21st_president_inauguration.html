Inauguration of South Korea's 21st President: The Dawn of a New Era

An In-Depth Look at the New Administration Born from Political Upheaval

June 5, 2025 Finance

In June 2025, the Republic of Korea entered a new era with the inauguration of its 21st presidential administration. Following the unprecedented impeachment of the previous president, this election carried profound significance in both its process and outcome. With the election of President Lee Jae-myung, the new government took its first steps amid a mountain of domestic and international challenges, carrying the weight of both national expectations and concerns. This article provides an in-depth analysis of the inauguration, the new president's profile, and the direction South Korea is heading.

I. A New Beginning for South Korea: The 21st Presidential Inauguration

A. Inauguration Overview: Date, Venue, and Key Moments

President Lee Jae-myung was officially inaugurated as South Korea's 21st president on June 4, 2025. This followed the snap presidential election triggered by the impeachment of his predecessor, President Yoon Suk-yeol. In an unusual turn of events, there was no formal presidential transition committee; the new president began his duties the day after the election. This rapid transfer of power was intended to minimize any vacuum in governance, though it also raised questions about whether the new administration had sufficient time to prepare policies and assemble its initial cabinet. Having taken office in the wake of the political upheaval caused by Yoon's impeachment, the new government faced the immediate challenge of demonstrating stable governance from day one.

The inauguration ceremony was held at the Rotunda Hall of the Gukhoeuisadang (National Assembly) on Yeouido, Seoul. Holding the ceremony at the National Assembly symbolized a commitment to cooperation with the legislature and marked a new beginning before the nation's representative body. President Lee's first official engagement on inauguration day was a visit to the Seoul National Cemetery (Gungnip Seoul Hyeonchungwon). Accompanied by First Lady Kim Hye-kyung, he laid a wreath at the Memorial Tower and paid tribute to the nation's fallen heroes. In the guest book, he wrote: "I will work together with the people to build a nation where the people are the true owners -- a happy country where everyone prospers together." This traditional first act by an incoming president was interpreted as an expression of dedication to national unity and development, particularly poignant given the deep political divisions the country had just endured.

After the swearing-in ceremony at the National Assembly, President Lee had a luncheon with National Assembly Speaker Woo Won-shik and leaders of both ruling and opposition parties at Sarangchae on the Assembly grounds, signaling his commitment to bipartisan cooperation. Given the extreme political polarization, this gesture was read as a symbolic indication that the new president would prioritize dialogue and compromise in governing. However, whether such symbolism would translate into substantive political cooperation remained to be seen, especially considering Lee's reputation for strong reformist tendencies and at times confrontational political style.

B. Key Messages from the Inaugural Address

In his inaugural address, President Lee pledged to be "a president for all the people," committing to serve every citizen regardless of whether they voted for him. This was seen as establishing national unity as the new government's top priority, seeking to overcome the extreme political polarization revealed during the election.

Economic recovery was one of the most emphasized themes. The president promised to "immediately launch an Emergency Economic Task Force to fight the threat of recession head-on" and announced plans to boost economic vitality through expanded fiscal spending, reflecting the public's deep concerns about the economic downturn exacerbated by the previous administration's political turmoil.

On foreign policy and national security, he stated that South Korea would maintain a strong U.S.-ROK alliance (Hanmi dongmaeng) as the foundation for powerful deterrence against North Korea's nuclear threats, while simultaneously reopening dialogue channels with Pyongyang (North Korea) to pursue peace on the Korean Peninsula. This marked a departure from the Yoon administration's hardline North Korea policy, partly inheriting the engagement approach of previous progressive governments. However, given North Korea's consistent rejection of dialogue since 2019 and its recent advances in nuclear capabilities and deepening military cooperation with Russia, questions persist about the effectiveness of renewed engagement, alongside diverse domestic and international perspectives on a conciliatory approach.

The president also outlined a vision of pragmatic, national-interest-centered diplomacy to navigate relations with the United States, Japan, China, and Russia, with particular emphasis on strengthening trilateral U.S.-Japan-ROK security cooperation. He declared his intention to lead a "government of just integration and flexible pragmatism," adopting useful policies from administrations as ideologically different as those of Park Chung-hee and Kim Dae-jung if they served the national interest. This signaled a flexible, results-oriented approach to governance that transcends ideological boundaries.

C. Key Initial Appointments

Immediately after taking office, President Lee made several key personnel appointments. He nominated four-term National Assembly member Kim Min-seok as Prime Minister (Gungmuchongni) candidate, a veteran legislator with deep political experience and acumen. For Chief of the Presidential Secretariat (Daetongnyeong biseosilchang), he appointed three-term lawmaker Kang Hoon-sik, a close ally, signaling the importance of shared governing philosophy and smooth communication.

For Director of the National Intelligence Service (Gukgajeonwonchang), he nominated Lee Jong-seok, a former Minister of Unification (Tongilbu jangkwan) under the Roh Moo-hyun administration. Known as an advocate of engagement with North Korea, this nomination was widely interpreted as a strong signal of the president's intent to resume dialogue with Pyongyang.

These initial appointments drew assessments that most were longtime political allies or close associates of the president. While this could be seen as a practical choice to ensure efficiency and executive control given the absence of a formal transition period, some critics raised concerns about "revolving-door appointments" and an over-reliance on inner-circle figures, potentially limiting the diversity of perspectives in governance.

II. The 21st Presidential Election: The People's Choice and a Shifting Political Landscape

A. Election Background and Process

The 21st presidential election was held on June 3, 2025. It was a snap election triggered by the impeachment of former President Yoon Suk-yeol, who was removed from office by the Constitutional Court (Heonbeop jaepanso) after the National Assembly passed an impeachment motion following his declaration of martial law (gyeomnyeong) in December 2024. This unprecedented presidential impeachment sent shockwaves through South Korean society, and the election took place as a direct consequence of this political upheaval. The election date was confirmed at a Cabinet meeting on April 8, 2025, with the official campaign period running a relatively short 22 days, from May 12 to June 2. Early voting on May 29-30 helped facilitate voter access.

The election was characterized by extreme social tension and political turmoil. The impeachment had deepened the rift between conservative and progressive camps, and these conflicts were sharply exposed during the campaign. Then-candidate Lee framed the situation as "a crisis caused by former President Yoon's declaration of martial law" and characterized his victory as "the beginning of normalizing the nation." The election indeed took on the character of a public reckoning on the Yoon administration's governance -- particularly the extraordinary act of declaring martial law. This political backdrop is widely seen as having created favorable conditions for the progressive camp.

Despite the turmoil, voter turnout reached a provisional 79.4%, reflecting intense public interest. This was the highest turnout since the 15th presidential election in 1997, demonstrating the people's determination to exercise their sovereignty in a time of national crisis. It also represented a powerful expression of the public's desire for the new president to restore order and stabilize the country.

B. Major Candidates and Final Results

In the 21st presidential election, Lee Jae-myung of the Deobureo Minjudang (Democratic Party of Korea) won with 49.42% of the vote (17,287,513 ballots). His main rival, Kim Moon-soo of the Gungminuihim (People Power Party), received 41.15% (14,395,639 votes). Lee Jun-seok of the Gaehyeoksindang (Reform Party), running as a third-party candidate, earned a significant 8.34% (2,917,523 votes). Lee Jae-myung defeated Kim Moon-soo by an 8.27 percentage-point margin (2,891,874 votes).

This was President Lee's third presidential bid. He had narrowly lost the 20th presidential election in 2022 to then-PPP candidate Yoon Suk-yeol by just 0.73 percentage points -- the smallest margin in South Korean electoral history -- making this victory all the more personally significant.

A closer look at the results reveals that while Lee won with a near-majority, the combined vote share of conservative candidates was formidable. Kim Moon-soo (41.15%) plus Lee Jun-seok (8.34%) totaled 49.49%, narrowly exceeding Lee Jae-myung's 49.42%. This shows that even with a split conservative vote, the overall electorate remains closely divided between progressive and conservative camps. Some analysts have thus characterized Lee's victory more as a "measured endorsement" than an overwhelming mandate. This outcome suggests that the new president faces the challenge of also heeding the voices of nearly half the population that did not support him.

The election has also been described as a "shadow election," dominated more by political recriminations than substantive policy debate, conducted under the long shadow of the previous president's impeachment. Unlike the 2017 election, where centrist candidate Ahn Cheol-su captured over 20% of the vote, this election saw the center virtually disappear, replaced by a sharp binary contest between progressive and conservative camps. This extreme polarization makes meaningful policy discussion difficult and risks deepening political conflict even after the election.

C. What the Election Results Mean

The 21st presidential election results carry several important implications. First, the outcome is widely seen as a public reckoning on the Yoon administration's governance, particularly the martial law declaration that shook the constitutional order. It can be read as a firm warning from the people against extraordinary breaches of democratic norms.

Second, Lee's victory represents a change of government after just three years, giving the progressive camp another chance at the helm. This reflects both a backlash against the conservative government's missteps and a degree of public hope for the policy vision Lee presented.

Third, the 79.4% turnout is a positive signal demonstrating the resilience of South Korean democratic institutions and the public's high level of political engagement, even amid political turmoil. Voters actively expressed their will through the ballot box, suggesting that democracy's self-correcting mechanisms remain functional.

Yet the results also starkly reveal the deeply rooted ideological conflict and political polarization that continue to plague South Korean society. As noted, the combined conservative vote virtually matched Lee's total, confirming that the nation remains deeply divided along partisan lines. This sharp polarization portends significant difficulties for the new government in achieving national unity. The election both showcased democracy's resilience and exposed its deep internal fractures, leaving the new administration with the weighty task of reconciliation and healing.

III. Who Is President Lee Jae-myung: Leadership Forged Through Adversity

A. Early Life and Upbringing

President Lee Jae-myung was born on December 8, 1963 (registered as December 22, 1964) as the fifth of seven siblings in a poor farming family in Andong, Gyeongsangbuk-do Province. His childhood was marked by extreme poverty and hardship. Unable to attend middle school due to his family's financial difficulties, he moved with his family to Seongnam in Gyeonggi-do Province and went to work as a child laborer in a factory. During this period, he suffered a serious industrial accident when his arm was caught in a press machine, leaving him with a permanent disability in his left arm -- which later exempted him from military service.

These hardships profoundly shaped Lee's life and political philosophy. He has described his core political belief as "eokgangbuyak" -- restraining the powerful and supporting the vulnerable -- a conviction clearly rooted in his childhood experience of poverty and factory labor. This personal history played a crucial role in shaping his image as a politician who deeply empathizes with the struggles of the economically disadvantaged. Despite these adversities, he never abandoned his dream of education, completing middle school and high school equivalency exams (geomjeonggoshi) through sheer determination.

B. Education and Career as a Human Rights Lawyer

Overcoming his years as a child laborer, Lee earned his academic credentials through equivalency exams and entered Chung-Ang University's Department of Law on a scholarship in 1982, graduating with honors in 1986 while simultaneously passing the 28th Korean Bar Examination. After completing judicial training, he chose the path of a human rights lawyer rather than the more secure career of a judge or prosecutor. This decision was reportedly influenced by a lecture by the late President Roh Moo-hyun. He opened a law office in Seongnam and became a member of Minbyeon (Lawyers for a Democratic Society), actively working on labor and human rights issues. He also led the campaign to establish a municipal hospital in Seongnam to address the region's poor healthcare infrastructure. However, encountering the limits of civic activism led him to enter politics directly.

C. Entry into Politics and Key Career Milestones

Lee entered party politics in 2005 by joining the Uri Party (a predecessor of the Democratic Party). After several unsuccessful campaigns, he was elected mayor of Seongnam in 2010 and won re-election in 2014. As mayor, he attracted nationwide attention with bold and innovative policies, including resolving the city's debt, providing free school uniforms, and introducing a cheongnyeonbaedang (youth dividend) -- a pilot program with characteristics of Universal Basic Income (UBI) that generated significant public debate.

In 2018, he was elected governor of Gyeonggi-do Province, gaining experience as the head of South Korea's most populous province. During the COVID-19 pandemic, his rapid and proactive response earned positive reviews, though he also drew controversy for policy clashes with the central government and independently distributed emergency relief payments. He participated in the Democratic Party's presidential primaries in both 2017 and 2022, and ran as the party's candidate in the 20th presidential election in 2022, losing narrowly to Yoon Suk-yeol. After that defeat, he won a by-election for a National Assembly seat in Incheon Gyeyang-gu, entered parliament, and subsequently served as leader of the Democratic Party, solidifying his position on the national political stage. On June 3, 2025, on his third presidential bid, he was finally elected as the 21st president. His political journey has been one of relentless challenge and crisis-overcoming, profoundly shaping his leadership style.

IV. Policy Direction of the Lee Jae-myung Administration

A. Economy: Livelihood Recovery and Growth Engine Development

The Lee administration has placed economic recovery at the top of its governance agenda. The president pledged to "immediately launch an Emergency Economic Response Task Force with a wartime footing against recession," signaling strong commitment to livelihood stabilization and economic recovery. New growth engines include aggressive investment in artificial intelligence (AI) with the goal of becoming a "top-3 AI nation," expansion of K-content and cultural industry support targeting "Global Big 5 cultural power" status with KRW 50 trillion in cultural exports, and major investment in K-defense industry and semiconductor/advanced strategic industries.

On the business environment, the administration plans to shift regulations to a "negative list" system and activate corporate investment. It has also set goals of becoming a "Global Top 4 venture power" through expanded R&D budgets and venture investment market development. In agriculture, the administration plans to expand smart data farming, food tech, green bio industries, and K-food exports. For trade, the government intends to negotiate with a national-interest-first approach regarding U.S. protectionism and tariffs, while actively re-engaging in multilateral trade platforms.

B. Foreign Policy and National Security: Korean Peninsula Peace and Pragmatic Diplomacy

The administration's foreign policy rests on two pillars: "peace on the Korean Peninsula" and "national-interest-centered pragmatic diplomacy." On North Korea, it will pursue a dual-track strategy: maintaining powerful deterrence through the U.S.-ROK alliance while simultaneously working to restore dialogue channels and build peace through cooperation. The nomination of engagement advocate Lee Jong-seok as NIS director symbolically underscores this intent.

The U.S.-ROK alliance remains the cornerstone. The administration also plans to strengthen U.S.-Japan-ROK trilateral security cooperation while pursuing early transfer of wartime operational control (jeonsi jakjeontongjekkwon; OPCON). With neighboring powers, the administration emphasizes pragmatism, seeking to de-escalate tensions with China and Russia while maintaining the alliance framework -- a "recalibration" from the Yoon administration's strongly U.S.-centric, Japan-friendly posture. Relations with Japan will be managed by separating historical issues from security and economic matters.

C. Society and Politics: National Unity and Reform

The administration recognizes severe social conflict and political polarization as key governance challenges. The president declared he would be "a president for all" and called for sending "outdated ideology to the museum of history." Political reforms include a proposed constitutional amendment (gaeheon) to change the presidential term from a single five-year term to a renewable four-year term. Judicial reform plans include expanding the number of Constitutional Court justices and recalibrating prosecutorial authority. The administration has also pledged a thorough investigation into the Yoon administration's martial law declaration, along with measures to prevent recurrence. In technology governance, it plans domestic legislation on AI ethics and security, along with strengthened global partnerships.

V. Challenges Facing the New Administration

A. Pressing Domestic and International Issues

The Lee administration faces a daunting array of challenges from day one. On the economic front, a slowing economy is compounded by high household debt and persistent inflation. The Bank of Korea has already cut its 2025 GDP growth forecast to 0.8%, reflecting the severity of the downturn. Trade pressures from U.S. protectionism add further burden.

On national security, North Korea's nuclear and missile capabilities continue to advance, and its deepening military cooperation with Russia has made the security environment more precarious. Domestically, the aftermath of the impeachment has left deep partisan rifts and social divisions that could undermine the new government's policy momentum.

Perhaps the most significant risk factor is the president himself: Lee faces multiple ongoing criminal trials. With a retrial on election law violations scheduled for June 18 following a Supreme Court remand, the outcome could significantly impact governance. These interconnected challenges mean that problems in one area can quickly cascade into others.

B. Public Expectations and Concerns

Public sentiment toward the new administration is a mix of hope and anxiety. Many citizens expect the Lee government to deliver economic recovery, political reform, and social integration. The president's personal story of overcoming adversity and his reputation for decisive execution fuel these hopes. Civil society groups are also pressing for action on long-standing reform agendas such as an anti-discrimination law.

On the other hand, concerns are substantial. Some fear that aggressive investigations into the previous administration could devolve into political retaliation. The president's personal legal risks loom as a potential disruption to governance. The ruling party's National Assembly majority raises concerns about unchecked executive power. Regarding North Korea policy, some citizens feel uneasy about a more conciliatory approach given the security environment. Media outlets have urged the new government to stay true to its promises of unity, cooperation, and pragmatism.

VI. Conclusion: Outlook for the Lee Jae-myung Era

Launched amid both high expectations and formidable challenges, the Lee administration will have a profound impact on South Korea's future over the next five years. Born from the unprecedented impeachment of its predecessor, this government bears not only the responsibility for policy success but also the weighty historical mission of "restoring and advancing democracy."

How the new administration addresses economic recovery and livelihood stabilization, heals extreme political polarization through national unity, and pursues peace on the Korean Peninsula amid a grave security environment -- and what kind of leadership President Lee demonstrates in the process -- will be closely watched. Whether the values of "pragmatism" and "integration" emphasized in the inaugural address remain mere slogans or become substantive governing principles will be the critical measure of the next five years' success or failure.

Citizens seek real improvements in their lives built on political stability, while the international community expects South Korea to fulfill its role as a responsible middle power. Whether the Lee administration can meet these internal and external expectations and take the Republic of Korea to the next level -- that journey has only just begun.
