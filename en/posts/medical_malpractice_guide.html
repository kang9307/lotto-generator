Responding to a Medical Accident - A Practical Beginner's Guide

From the difference between accident and negligence to securing records, preserving evidence, and choosing a path

Jun 12, 2026 Law

Introduction: When you are most rattled, order matters most

Surprisingly many people have walked out of a hospital gripped by the dread that "something went wrong." An unexpected complication, a lingering aftereffect, a course of recovery that differs from what was explained - any of these can make the mind go blank. Yet at that very moment, the gap between someone who knows what to do first and someone who does not only widens with time. Medical disputes are fought not with emotion but with records and evidence, and that evidence fades as the days pass.

This article is an informational guide that gathers, in one place, the basics a layperson should know when a medical accident is suspected. It starts with the biggest misconception - why "bad outcome = negligence" simply does not hold. It then covers what to do first: securing copies of medical records and writing a chronological account, methods of preserving evidence, the three resolution paths of hospital talks, the Korea Medical Dispute Mediation and Arbitration Agency, and litigation, and finally why proving negligence and causation is so hard and where you can seek advice. It centers on Korea's system, but note up front that every procedure, agency name, and deadline may differ by time and case.

⚠️ Please read first - Legal and medical disclaimer

This article is general information for educational purposes and is neither legal advice nor a medical diagnosis or opinion. The laws and systems governing medical accidents and disputes (the Medical Service Act, the Act on Remedies for Injuries from Medical Malpractice and Mediation of Medical Disputes, the Civil Act, and so on) may be amended, and all the procedures, agency names, deadlines, fees, and requirements in this article may vary by time, jurisdiction, and your individual circumstances. Whether a given outcome amounts to medical negligence is a matter requiring advanced medical and legal judgment, and a general explanation cannot decide your own case. When you actually need to respond, please consult a licensed professional or public body about your specific situation - a medical-litigation lawyer, the Korea Medical Dispute Mediation and Arbitration Agency, or a legal aid corporation. 1. A medical accident is not the same as negligence - bad outcome ≠ fault

The first step to understanding a medical dispute is to separate two words that are often used interchangeably. Miss this distinction, and the patient's expectation that "the result was bad, so of course the hospital must answer for it" runs in parallel forever with the hospital's stance that "we did our best, so there is no liability."

1.1 Medical accident - a factual concept pointing to the outcome

A medical accident is a broad factual concept for an unexpected bad outcome that befell a patient during the course of care - diagnosis, testing, medication, surgery, and so on. It does not yet contain any judgment about whose fault it was. In other words, saying a medical accident occurred is merely a description of the fact that "something bad happened."

1.2 Medical negligence - a legal judgment pointing to fault

By contrast, medical negligence is a legal judgment that the bad outcome stemmed from a provider's failure to observe the duty of care required of an ordinary practitioner at the clinical-medicine standard of the time. Negligence, in short, includes a finding that the provider "did what should not have been done, or failed to do what should have been done."

1.3 Why a bad outcome is not automatically fault

Medicine inherently carries uncertainty. The same treatment can yield different results depending on the patient's constitution, underlying conditions, and the natural course of the disease. Even with full explanation and the best care, complications or side effects can arise, and these may not be negligence. To impose legal liability on a provider, the mere fact of a bad result is not enough; it must be established that there was negligence and a causal link between that negligence and the harm.

In one line: a "medical accident" is the fact of a bad outcome; "medical negligence" is the judgment that the outcome stemmed from fault. The heart of a dispute is not how bad the result was, but whether fault and causation are recognized.

2. The very first step when you suspect harm - secure copies of the records

When a medical accident is suspected, the first thing to do - before any emotional protest - is to secure the medical records. The records are the most basic and decisive material showing what happened, and they can become harder to obtain once time passes or the dispute surfaces.

2.1 The right to copies of records - guaranteed by the Medical Service Act

Korea's Medical Service Act, Article 21 provides that a patient may inspect or request copies of all or part of their own medical records, and that the institution may not refuse without a justifiable reason. An institution that refuses without justification may be subject to penalty. In other words, requesting copies is not a favor but a right guaranteed by law.

2.2 What and how to request

It is wise to request broadly: the medical chart, nursing records, surgical and anesthesia records, test result sheets, and the copies and image data of imaging studies (X-ray, CT, MRI, and so on). Bring your ID; a family member acting on your behalf may need documents proving the family relationship plus the patient's consent or power of attorney. Because the required documents, procedure, and turnaround can vary by institution and time, confirm with the hospital.

2.3 Why obtain them quickly

Securing the records early reduces the chance of omissions, edits, or alterations and lays an objective foundation for sorting out the facts afterward. It is safest to obtain copies before the dispute escalates - ideally right after suspicion arises.

Requesting copies of your records is a patient's right guaranteed by the Medical Service Act. If an institution refuses without a justifiable reason, that refusal itself can be a problem. That said, the scope of what you may request and the required documents can vary by time and institution, so confirm with the hospital.

3. A chronological account - memory fades, records remain

If the chart is the hospital's record, the chronological account is the record the patient side keeps. Disputes drag on and memory fades fast, so organizing events in time order from right after the incident is a powerful asset.

  • A timeline journal: note when symptoms appeared, the dates and times of consultations, tests, and procedures, and what the medical team explained, day by day.
  • Notes of conversations: record as concretely as possible what the staff explained, when, and how. It helps to note who said it (role and name).
  • Changes in your condition: keep a journal of changes such as pain, loss of function, and aftereffects. This later helps gauge the extent of harm.
  • Cost records: gather expenditures tied to your loss - extra treatment costs, transport, caregiving, and time off work.
Tip: a contemporaneous account is more credible than one written "all at once later." The timestamps on messenger and note apps can serve as objective circumstantial evidence.

4. Preserving evidence - the perishable things first

In a dispute, the outcome ultimately turns on evidence. Among it, prioritize what can vanish or change over time.

4.1 Records, tests, and images

The medical records and imaging material covered in Section 2 are key. In addition, gather prescriptions, medication packets, diagnoses and opinion letters, and admission and discharge confirmations. Receive imaging material on separate media (such as a CD) and store it.

4.2 Photographs and physical items

Capture changes that show on the body - wounds, scars, swelling - in dated photographs. If there is an item or medication you suspect was used wrongly, do not discard it; preserve it as is.

4.3 An expert's opinion

Seeking an objective opinion from another practitioner in the same field is also an option. That said, a full-scale appraisal premised on dispute is often done through public procedures at the mediation or litigation stage described below.

5. Resolution path ① - talks with the hospital

Once you have secured a degree of evidence, the next step is choosing a resolution path. The first that comes to mind is direct talks with the hospital.

Talks are a process of confirming the facts and hearing the team's explanation, with the advantage that a settlement can wrap things up relatively quickly. But the patient side is easily disadvantaged in information and expertise, so be cautious about a hasty settlement or signing a settlement agreement. If the agreement contains a clause to the effect that you waive all future claims (a covenant not to sue, and the like), it may become hard to contest even larger harm that emerges later. Even at the talks stage, it is safer to have a professional review.

Caution: talks and settlement are not bad in themselves, but an agreement signed before the full extent of harm is known is hard to undo. Always check the wording of a settlement carefully.

6. Resolution path ② - the Korea Medical Dispute Mediation and Arbitration Agency

If talks with the hospital are difficult or you need an objective judgment, you can use the mediation and arbitration system of the Korea Medical Dispute Mediation and Arbitration Agency (K-medi). This body handles mediation and arbitration of medical disputes along with medical-accident appraisal.

6.1 The advantages of mediation

Compared with litigation, mediation is faster and lower in cost and goes through the appraisal and judgment of medical and legal experts. An application is made in writing or in person in principle, but online, postal, and fax applications are also possible; the fee is calculated by the claim amount, and basic-livelihood recipients, persons of national merit, and others may be exempt.

6.2 Application deadline and how proceedings begin

By the known standard, an application must be made within 10 years from the date the act that caused the accident ended, or within 3 years from the date you learned of the harm and the wrongdoer. Ordinarily, mediation begins only after the other party (the respondent) indicates willingness to take part, but for certain grave outcomes - death, unconsciousness lasting a month or more, or severe disability above a set level - mediation may begin automatically even without the other party's consent. Because the specific requirements, reference dates, deadlines, and fees of automatic commencement may be revised, always confirm the current rules with K-medi before applying.

Note: if both sides agree in mediation, the result carries a certain effect. If mediation does not succeed, the matter can move to litigation, and an appraisal made during mediation may serve as reference material in later proceedings.

7. Resolution path ③ - a damages lawsuit

When talks and mediation fail to resolve the matter, or the dispute is wide and an enforceable judgment is needed, you file a damages lawsuit in court. Litigation is the most powerful means of resolution, but you must accept that its time, cost, and burden of proof are the greatest.

In a medical lawsuit, the patient (plaintiff) must generally assert and prove the provider's negligence, the harm the patient suffered, and the causal link between negligence and harm. As shown below, this proof is very hard, but courts have eased the patient's burden to a degree in light of the special nature of medicine. Lawsuits often do not end quickly, so it is best to weigh cost, time, and the state of the evidence coolly with a professional before deciding.

8. The difficulty of proving fault and causation - and the doctrine of easing

The biggest reason medical disputes are called hard is the wall of proof. The side that best knows what went wrong is the hospital, yet the burden of proving it falls on the patient, who lacks information - a structural imbalance.

8.1 Why proof is hard

Because a medical act requires advanced expertise, a layperson finds it very hard to show directly whether there was a breach of the duty of care and whether causation links that breach to the harm. Moreover, the institution holds most of the key evidence - the records and the grounds for the medical judgment.

8.2 Easing the burden of proof - the Supreme Court's stance

Because of this imbalance, the Korean Supreme Court has developed doctrine that eases the patient's burden. Settled precedent holds that if a patient proves, in light of ordinary common sense, that there was a negligent act in the course of the medical care and that no cause other than the medical act could intervene between that act and the result, causation between fault and result may be presumed. Going further, a 2023 Supreme Court ruling held that if the patient side proves the existence of an act assessed as a treatment-related fault and the probability that the fault caused the harm, it is proper to presume causation and ease the burden of proof.

8.3 Easing is not exemption

That said, "easing" is not "exemption." A presumption can be overturned by counter-evidence, and the patient side must still prove at least the existence of an act assessed as a fault and the probability that harm arose. In the end, securing the records and obtaining expert medical and legal advice remain the decisive factors.

Individual precedents and doctrines apply differently from case to case and can change over time. The explanation of precedents here only introduces general tendencies; it does not guarantee the outcome of your case. Always consult a professional.

9. Where you can seek advice

Rather than carrying it alone, getting help from professionals and public bodies early reduces trial and error.

  • Korea Medical Dispute Mediation and Arbitration Agency (K-medi): the public body for medical-dispute counseling, mediation, arbitration, and appraisal. You can be guided through the procedure, how to apply, and the current rules.
  • Korea Legal Aid Corporation: provides free legal counseling and litigation support to those who struggle to receive the protection of the law due to financial hardship. Phone counseling is at 132 (no area code), weekdays 09:00-18:00.
  • A medical-litigation lawyer: if the case is complex or you are considering litigation, you need the advice of a lawyer experienced in medical disputes, who can also help weigh cost and prospects.
  • Health and Welfare Counseling Center (129, no area code): a window for general guidance on health and medical matters.
In one line: secure the records → write the account and preserve evidence → seek advice (K-medi, legal aid, a specialist lawyer) → choose a path (talks, mediation, litigation). This order reduces trial and error.

10. Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

Q. If I sign a settlement, can I no longer contest it later?

If a settlement contains a clause to the effect that you will raise no objection or claim in the future, it may become hard to contest even larger harm that emerges later. So be cautious about a hasty settlement before the full extent of harm is known, and have a professional review the wording before signing.

Q. How do a criminal complaint and a civil damages claim differ?

A criminal proceeding seeks to hold the provider criminally liable (for professional negligence causing death or injury, and the like), while a civil proceeding seeks monetary compensation for the harm. The two differ in purpose and standard of proof, so their outcomes may not match. Which path fits depends on the case and your goal, so consult a professional.

Q. What if the hospital ultimately will not give me the records?

Under the Medical Service Act, refusal without a justifiable reason is not permitted. If refusal continues, record that fact itself and consult legal aid, a specialist lawyer, or the competent health authority to find a course of action. The specific steps depend on the case.

Conclusion - with records and order, not emotion

The moment you suspect a medical accident feels overwhelming to anyone. But the outcome of a dispute ultimately turns on records, evidence, and the right order. Remember three things first. First, a bad outcome is not automatically medical negligence. Liability arises only when fault and causation are recognized. Second, when you suspect harm, first secure copies of the records, write a chronological account, and preserve the perishable evidence first. Third, there are three resolution paths - hospital talks, the dispute mediation agency, and litigation - each differing in speed, cost, and enforceability.

And the most important final note. This article is only general information for education, not legal or medical advice. Systems such as procedures, agency names, and deadlines may be revised, and whether a given outcome is negligence requires advanced judgment case by case. If you actually need to respond, please consult a licensed professional or public body about your situation - a medical-litigation lawyer, the Korea Medical Dispute Mediation and Arbitration Agency, or a legal aid corporation.
