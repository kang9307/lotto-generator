Cabbage Juice and the Evidence - What a 1949 Report on Thirteen Patients Actually Said

Where the stomach claim came from, what the name vitamin U conceals, what survives the press and the heat, and why waiting it out with a pouch of juice is the one harm that has actually been demonstrated

Read this before the article - the waiting is the risk

The harm actually documented around cabbage juice is not an ingredient. It is a delayed consultation. Persistent heartburn and upper abdominal pain can sit on top of a peptic ulcer, a Helicobacter pylori infection or a gastric cancer, and symptoms alone do not tell those apart. If any of the following applies, this is not a situation for drinking something and watching.

  • Unintentional weight loss
  • Food sticking, or difficulty swallowing
  • Black stools, bloody stools, or vomiting blood
  • Anemia noted on a blood test
  • Persistent vomiting
  • New symptoms that began after your forties
  • A family history of gastric or oesophageal cancer

In Korea, where gastric cancer incidence is high, national guidance recommends endoscopy every two years from age 40 to 74 even without symptoms. With symptoms the order is clearer still. Examination comes first; the conversation about what to drink comes after.

Introduction: one sentence stuck to the refrigerator door

You will find the same object in holiday gift boxes, in the bottom shelf of a parent's refrigerator, and in a full row at the supermarket. A dark green liquid in a pouch. And beside it, almost without exception, the same sentence: cabbage is good for the stomach.

The belief is unusually durable. Other health foods ride a trend and vanish; cabbage juice has held its shelf for over twenty years and picked up a nickname along the way, something close to the natural stomach medicine. Beliefs that set that hard usually have a source. They start somewhere, get copied, and lose a condition at each copy until only the sentence is left.

This article opens the source. The claim really did begin with one researcher, and what that research observed, when, and how is still on the record. So is what came afterwards, or more precisely what did not come afterwards. The point is not to demolish a belief but to measure the height it is standing at.

There is no conclusion here telling you to stop drinking it. Cabbage is a vegetable and there is no case against eating vegetables. But eating a vegetable and managing a gastrointestinal condition are acts on entirely different levels, and the real damage begins when the two start to look like one. For the same method applied to a different ingredient, see the arginine piece; for what actually sits behind stomach symptoms, see the gastritis and Helicobacter piece.

1. Where the belief came from - thirteen patients in 1949, a prison in 1956

The starting point is identifiable. In January 1949, California Medicine published a report by Garnett Cheney of Stanford University School of Medicine titled Rapid healing of peptic ulcers in patients receiving fresh cabbage juice. Essentially every version of the modern claim descends from this paper.

1.1 What the paper records

  • Subjects - 13 patients with peptic ulcer: 7 duodenal, 6 gastric.
  • Measurement - time for the ulcer crater to heal, observed radiologically and gastroscopically.
  • Results - mean healing of 10.4 days for the 7 duodenal cases and 7.3 days for the 6 gastric cases.
  • Comparison - not a concurrent control group but figures already reported in the literature: 37 days across 62 duodenal patients, and 42 days across 6 gastric patients on standard therapy.

On the numbers alone it is striking. Forty-two days down to 7.3. Put the design beside the numbers, though, and the line between what this can and cannot support becomes visible.

1.2 What this study cannot answer

The largest hole is that there is no control group. That does not mean no comparison was made; it means no comparison was made under matched conditions. The comparators were patients diagnosed and treated at other centres, at other times, by other criteria. Whether the diagnostic threshold was the same, whether the ulcers were comparable in size and site, cannot be reconstructed. And on the gastric side the count is 6 against 6. A mean difference drawn from six and six is well within what chance produces on its own.

There is also no blinding. The patient knows they are drinking cabbage juice and so does the person reading the films. Judging whether a crater has closed on an image involves interpretation, and nothing in this design shields that interpretation from expectation.

One more thing belongs here. In 1949 the cause of peptic ulcer disease was itself unknown. That Helicobacter pylori is a principal cause emerged in the 1980s, and the Nobel Prize for that discovery came in 2005. Cheney had no way of knowing whether his patients were infected, and the natural history of ulcers healing and relapsing on their own was not mapped as it is now. Taken together, the 1949 report was a signal that the direction deserved a proper trial. The question is what came next.

1.3 1956 - one trial with a control arm

A follow-up did come. In January 1956 the same journal published Cheney, Waxler and Miller, Vitamin U therapy of peptic ulcer: experience at San Quentin Prison. The design steps up.

  • Design - a double-blind controlled experiment; neither participants nor assessors knew who received what.
  • Intervention - concentrated cabbage juice or a placebo facsimile made to look identical.
  • Assessment - repeated x-ray examination of the ulcer crater.
  • Window - 22 days allowed for crater healing.
  • Reported outcome - concentrated cabbage juice found effective; a success ratio of 92% is the figure usually quoted.

1.4 What has to sit alongside that trial

The setting is a prison. Prisoner research was common in mid-century America for an obvious reason: diet and daily routine are controlled and nobody is lost to follow-up. From a designer's point of view the conditions are ideal. They are also conditions that today's research ethics will not reproduce. Whether consent given under incarceration is voluntary became a question that the following decades answered in regulation. That is not a charge against a researcher of that era; it is one of the reasons this trial was never repeated in the same form.

The assessment method has also moved. Healing was judged by barium upper GI series, whereas the modern standard for judging ulcer healing is direct endoscopic visualisation, and contrast studies are known to miss small and superficial lesions. The 22-day window is short as well. Observing that a crater is no longer visible at day 22 and knowing that the patient's ulcer did not return are two different statements. This trial saw only the first.

1.5 And then seventy years

Here is the core of it. Since 1956, no randomized controlled trial meeting modern standards has been found that confirms or overturns the result. A registered protocol, a pre-specified primary endpoint, endoscopic adjudication, an adequate sample, an independent funder. Seventy years produced none of that.

A critical narrative review published in Pharmaceuticals in May 2026 addresses the situation directly. Grading the S-methylmethionine literature by level of evidence, it concluded that despite a substantial preclinical base and historical clinical observations, the level of evidence for many indications remains limited, and that well-designed Phase II randomized controlled trials are urgently needed. When the 2026 summary of a substance studied for over seventy years says Phase II trials are urgently needed, that sentence is itself the position statement.

What not replicated actually means
Refuted and not replicated are different things. Refuted means somebody redid it properly and it did not hold. Not replicated means nobody redid it properly. Cabbage juice is the second case. So it is equally inaccurate to say the effect has been disproven. The precise statement is: a seventy-year-old observation exists, and the modern trial that would test it does not.

2. The work the name vitamin U is doing

The second pillar of the belief is a name. The evidence offered in advertising and blog posts is almost always the phrase vitamin U, and the phrase persuades all by itself, because it contains the word vitamin.

  • What it is - S-methylmethionine (SMM), formed when an extra methyl group attaches to the sulfur of the amino acid methionine. It occurs in many plants, not only cabbage.
  • Where the name came from - a label applied during ulcer research in the late 1940s, with U generally read as ulcer. It is not an official classification assigned in sequence with the other vitamins.
  • What a vitamin requires - the body cannot make enough of it, and lacking it produces a specific deficiency disease.
  • Where SMM actually stands - it is not classified as an essential nutrient, there is no corresponding deficiency disease, and there is no established daily requirement.

The word vitamin therefore certifies nothing about this compound. There is something almost wry in it: the man who coined the name could not have known what the name would go on to do. What began in the 1950s as convenient shorthand for a working hypothesis is now quoted, seventy years later, as though it were the evidence.

None of which means the molecule is inert. S-methylmethionine can participate in methyl-transfer reactions, and signals related to gastric mucosal protection have been reported in cell and animal work. But what is seen in cells and animals sitting on a different level from symptom improvement in people is the whole problem, and the trials that would cross that gap are the ones that are missing.

There is one way to read this table. A true row does not underwrite the rows below it. Product copy almost invariably starts on the established first line and slides down to the empty ones. The join is the moment between cabbage contains vitamin U and so your stomach settles.

3.1 The Helicobacter row - why close to refuted

The relevant trial did not use cabbage, but it points the direction. Broccoli sprouts, another brassica, are high in sulforaphane and have been examined against Helicobacter several times. A trial published in Cancer Prevention Research in 2009 randomized 48 infected people to 70g of broccoli sprouts daily, containing 420µmol of sulforaphane precursor, or to alfalfa sprouts, for 8 weeks.

The result runs two ways. In the broccoli arm, markers of bacterial colonisation and of gastric inflammation fell. Then, eight weeks after stopping, the markers returned to pre-treatment levels. The authors' reading is explicit: colonisation is reduced, but this is not eradication.

That distinction decides the practical question. The purpose of eradication is to remove the organism, not to depress a marker temporarily, and an intervention whose effect reverses on withdrawal does not serve that purpose. Cabbage also carries less sulforaphane precursor than broccoli sprouts. If the stronger material fell short of eradication, there is no basis for expecting it from the weaker one.

4. What changes the moment it becomes juice

Something else splits here. Nearly all the evidence discussed so far concerns cabbage, or fresh cabbage juice, while what people actually consume is usually a shelf-stable pouch. A processing step sits between the two, and it happens to act on the very compound in question.

4.1 Heat - the smell is the evidence

S-methylmethionine is heat-labile. Its breakdown to dimethyl sulfide (DMS) during heating and storage is well characterised in food chemistry, and the product of that breakdown is extremely familiar: the particular smell of cabbage boiling is dimethyl sulfide. There is published work quantifying SMM in raw vegetables and green malt and comparing it against DMS formation after heat treatment, which is to say this compound is a textbook case of something that leaves during thermal processing.

A second, indirect indication is more telling. Food-engineering work has been published on raising the S-methylmethionine content of cabbage juice using non-thermal treatment and lactic acid bacteria fermentation. The existence of that research reveals its premise: that conventional heat processing lowers this compound is treated as a problem to be solved within the industry itself.

A pouch that keeps for months at room temperature is unlikely to have skipped sterilisation. Yet there is no way for a buyer to learn how much survives in any given product, because no rule requires S-methylmethionine content to be declared. A package can carry the phrase rich in vitamin U while the label alone tells you nothing about how much is in it.

4.2 What the press throws away - fibre

The second change is simpler and more certain. When you juice, most of the dietary fibre leaves with the pulp. A substantial part of what makes eating cabbage worthwhile lives there: satiety, bowel regularity, food for gut bacteria. Those are things fibre does, and juice does not carry them.

Put differently: converting cabbage into juice is close to trading away a well-evidenced component to obtain an unevidenced one. The relationship between fibre intake and health outcomes rests on far thicker evidence than the relationship between S-methylmethionine and stomach health.

4.3 What concentrates - sugar and potassium

A cup of juice compresses several heads of cabbage, and compression is not selective. Sugars and potassium concentrate along with everything else. For most people this is unremarkable, but it is worth checking if you are managing blood glucose or are on a potassium restriction for reduced kidney function. Products blended with apple or pear juice for palatability push the sugar higher again, which is what the ingredient proportions on the back panel are for.

5. Start with what is behind the symptom - where the evidence is solid

In gastrointestinal symptoms, the well-evidenced ground lies elsewhere. One thing needs to be explicit first: everything below requires diagnosis and prescription, and it is set out here so you know what gets handled in a consulting room, not so you can choose among these yourself.

  • Identifying the cause comes first. The same burning sensation is handled entirely differently depending on whether it is acid reflux, an ulcer, an infection or functional dyspepsia. That separation is made by history and investigation, not by something you drink.
  • Helicobacter status is determined by testing. Breath tests, serology and biopsy at endoscopy are used, and whether to eradicate a confirmed infection and with which combination is a physician's decision. The detail sits in the gastritis and Helicobacter piece.
  • Acid-directed medicines differ by type and purpose. Agents for immediate relief and agents for suppression are used differently, and duration depends on the diagnosis. That call belongs to the prescriber.
  • The medication itself can be the cause. Long-term anti-inflammatory painkiller use is an irritant to the gastric lining in its own right. What that situation needs is a review of your medication list, not a beverage.
  • Lifestyle measures are adjuncts. Not lying down straight after eating, avoiding late heavy meals, stopping smoking, moderating alcohol - the usual reflux measures, covered in the reflux management piece. Adjuncts are adjuncts; they do not substitute for a diagnosis.

Ask where cabbage juice belongs on that list and the honest answer is off it. Drinking something because you like vegetables and managing a gastrointestinal condition are separate acts.

6. When to see a doctor - the most important section here

The warning at the top is worth unpacking once more, for a single reason. The risk that actually materialises around cabbage juice is not an ingredient effect but the time that passes before someone is examined.

Signs that call for prompt medical assessment
  • Unintentional weight loss - weight falling without eating less
  • Difficulty swallowing - food sticking, or trouble getting it down
  • Black or bloody stools, or vomiting blood - dark tarry stool can signal gastrointestinal bleeding
  • Anemia - flagged on a check-up, or with dizziness and pallor
  • Persistent vomiting
  • New symptoms starting after your forties - indigestion that was not there before
  • Family history - gastric or oesophageal cancer in a parent or sibling

If any of these applies, do not set aside a period to watch and wait. Drinking something while observing is close to the one thing not to do in this situation.

6.1 There is a schedule even without symptoms

Where gastric cancer is common, screening is organised rather than symptom-driven. Korea's National Cancer Center revised its national gastric cancer screening recommendation in 2026, ten years after the previous version, and it reads as follows.

  • Population - asymptomatic adults aged 40 to 74
  • Interval - every two years
  • Modality - endoscopy alone. Upper GI series had previously been an option, but domestic studies found endoscopy superior both in reducing gastric cancer mortality and in accuracy, so endoscopy became the sole first-line method.
  • Aged 75 and over - decided in consultation with a clinician, weighing health status and life expectancy.
  • Basis - a systematic review of roughly 6,800 domestic and international papers using GRADE methodology.

The age threshold is where this gets interesting. The 2017 dyspepsia guideline from the American College of Gastroenterology and the Canadian Association of Gastroenterology recommends endoscopy from age 60, while adding that people who spent childhood in a country with high gastric cancer incidence, or who have a family history, may be offered endoscopy younger. Korea is exactly the country that caveat describes, which is why its own threshold starts at 40 rather than 60. If you grew up in such a country and now live elsewhere, that caveat is about you, and it is worth saying so to your doctor.

So: there is a fixed schedule from 40 even with no symptoms, and with symptoms the consultation comes regardless of the schedule. No judgement about cabbage juice alters that order.

7. Cautions - some people genuinely need to check

Cabbage has been eaten for a very long time and is safe for most people. Juice, though, is consumed daily, in volume, over long periods, and that form raises a handful of specific checks.

The moment a vegetable becomes something drunk daily, it becomes something whose quantity is managed.

8. What is on the box, and what is in the rules

Knowing the regulatory position changes how you read a label. Korea's food labelling system runs in three tiers, and most countries have an analogous structure worth locating your own product in.

  • Health functional food - products using ingredients whose function and safety the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety has assessed and recognised. They carry a certification mark and may use defined functional wording. Ingredients are either notified or individually recognised.
  • Functional-claim ordinary food - a scheme in force since December 2020. An ordinary food may carry limited functional wording if it meets specific conditions: it must use one of 29 notified ingredients or an individually recognised ingredient at 30% or more of the daily intake, be manufactured at a HACCP-certified facility, display this product is not a health functional food on the consumer-facing surface, and not be shaped like a tablet or capsule.
  • Ordinary food - everything else. No functional wording permitted.

Most retail cabbage juice sits in the third tier, labelled under categories such as liquid tea or other processed food, with no health functional food mark. Which means the packaging cannot state a direct functional claim about stomach health, so the front of the pack carries factual statements instead - the ingredient, the origin, 100% domestic cabbage. The functional story moves off the package altogether: into advertising, blog posts, reviews, and the sentence people simply repeat to each other.

Checking is quick. Find the food category field on the back, look for a health functional food certification mark, and see whether the line this product is not a health functional food appears. Those three tell you which tier you are holding.

9. What could not be verified

Collected in one place, the items that did not resolve.

  • The daily volume used in the 1949 study - around one litre a day is widely quoted, but this could not be confirmed at the level of the original abstract.
  • Participant numbers and group allocation in the 1956 San Quentin trial - the abstract carries the design and conclusion but not the arm sizes or the statistical handling. The denominator behind the quoted 92% is in the same position.
  • Whether cabbage or cabbage extract is listed as a recognised stomach-health functional ingredient by Korea's Ministry of Food and Drug Safety - the ingredient database was searched without a definitive answer. Concluding that it is not listed is equally outside what this article can say. What is verifiable from the labelling system is that most retail products are sold as ordinary food. For a specific product, searching it by name on the Food Safety Korea portal is the accurate route.
  • Actual residual S-methylmethionine in retail pouches - no disclosure requirement exists, and no published data were found.
  • Any human trial comparing juice directly against whole cabbage - none found.
  • Any modern randomized controlled trial with cabbage juice itself as the intervention - none found. That this row is empty is part of the article's conclusion.

Not verified does not mean does not exist; it means the answer is not in hand. If material filling these gaps appears, rungs on the table can move up at any time.

Frequently asked questions

Are you saying not to drink cabbage juice?

No. If you enjoy it and it agrees with you, that is reason enough. The one thing this article argues against is juice standing in for a consultation. If your stomach has been uncomfortable for a while and you are managing it with a drink instead of getting examined, the problem is not what is in the pouch. It is the time going by.

So is whole cabbage better?

On fibre, yes, whole cabbage gives you more. Fibre sits on thick evidence and juicing removes it. But comparing the two as treatments for a stomach condition does not work in the first place, because neither form has evidence at that level. It is more accurately a question about which way of eating a vegetable you prefer.

My stomach did feel better afterwards. Was that in my head?

There is no basis for calling it imagination and none for crediting the compound either. Digestive symptoms fluctuate on their own, and the moment somebody starts something new frequently coincides with a stretch of improvement. Add placebo effects, and the other habits that tend to change alongside. Nobody needs to dismiss a personal experience, but a personal experience does not demonstrate an ingredient effect. Control groups exist to make that separation, and cabbage juice has been without one for seventy years.

An endoscopy showed gastritis. Would cabbage juice help?

If you have already been scoped, the most important step is behind you. The next question is what kind of gastritis, what caused it and what to do, and that is settled in the consulting room with the report in hand. Whether to drink cabbage juice alongside is a question to ask in the same conversation, particularly if you are taking anything.

Is a vitamin U supplement better than juice?

Isolating the compound lets you control the dose, but it leaves untouched the fact that the evidence for what it does in people is missing. A known quantity and a known effect are different things. If anything, a tablet makes it easier for the daily amount to climb, so if you take any medication the case for checking gets stronger rather than weaker.

Conclusion

The origin of the claim can be named. It is a 1949 report in California Medicine on 13 patients, with no control group, compared against historical figures from the literature. Seven years later a placebo-controlled double-blind trial was run in a prison and reported a positive result, but adjudication was by x-ray, the window was 22 days, and in the seventy years since, no trial meeting modern standards has confirmed or overturned it.

Vitamin U is a nickname attached to a working hypothesis of that era. The substance behind it, S-methylmethionine, is not an essential nutrient and has neither a deficiency disease nor a recommended intake. Yet the name still opens product copy as though it were evidence. A name has been standing in for evidence for seventy years.

The form compounds it. Pressing removes the fibre, and heating for shelf stability pushes the compound in question down. It is a trade of something well-evidenced for something unevidenced, and the label will not tell you how much of the latter is even left.

So the conclusion is not do not drink it. It is do not change the order. Persistent heartburn means finding the cause first, and past 40 there is a scheduled examination whether or not you have symptoms. Black stools, weight loss, difficulty swallowing, anemia, persistent vomiting: do not set aside a period to watch. Keep that order, and cabbage juice is simply one way of drinking a vegetable. Judge it from there.
