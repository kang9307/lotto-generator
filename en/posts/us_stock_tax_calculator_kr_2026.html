Introduction: a calculator gives you an answer but not its assumptions

You sold some overseas shares and you want to know what you owe. Search and you will find several calculators. Enter a purchase amount and a sale amount, and out comes a tax figure. So far nothing looks wrong.

The problem is that the same trade log can produce different numbers in different tools. And most calculators do not explain why their number came out the way it did. There are two or three input boxes, but the decisions the calculation actually requires are far more numerous. The rest are assumptions the tool made on your behalf.

The argument here is not that you should avoid calculators. It is that if you do not know what a calculator assumed, you have no basis for believing its output. So instead of computing your tax for you, this article makes you walk the structure once by hand. Once you know the structure, you can tell when a result is odd, and you know where to go and ask.

Here is the conclusion up front. Taking one hypothetical trade log built below and changing only the assumptions, three ways, the tax came out as follows.

  • Earliest shares treated as sold first, each lot converted at its own trade-date rate gives KRW 637,995
  • A won-denominated weighted average unit cost used as the basis gives KRW 678,095
  • Gain computed in foreign currency first, then converted once at the sale-date rate gives KRW 441,505

Not one character of the trade log changed. Only the assumptions did, and the largest figure is 1.536 times the smallest. The spread is KRW 236,590.

2. What is actually taxed - starting from the structure

The computation structure for capital gains tax on shares published by Korea's National Tax Service runs in this order. This flow is confirmed.

  1. Transfer price - what you sold for
  2. less necessary expenses - acquisition cost + transfer costs
  3. = transfer gain (income amount)
  4. less basic deduction - per National Tax Service guidance, 2.5 million won per year across domestic and overseas shares
  5. = tax base
  6. times the rate
  7. = computed tax
  8. less credits and reductions = determined tax

Several properties fall straight out of this.

  • What is taxed is the gain, not the sale proceeds. Sell for 100 million won something you bought for 99 million and the gain is 1 million
  • The basic deduction is annual. It is not per security; it comes off once from the income aggregated across the whole tax period
  • If the gain is smaller than the deduction the tax base is zero. The calculation below actually lands in that region at one point
  • The rate multiplies the tax base, not the transfer gain. It applies after the deduction has been taken off. Reverse the order and the tax comes out overstated
  • Necessary expenses come in two parts. What you paid for the shares and what it cost you to sell them enter separately

The structure is that simple. The difficulty is producing the values you put into it, because overseas shares trade in foreign currency. To enter them on a return denominated in won you have to convert somewhere, and the moment you do, choices appear.

3. The exchange rate shows up at least three times

This is where overseas shares part company decisively with domestic ones. The same dollar amount becomes a different won amount depending on which day's rate you apply. And the rate does not appear only once.

Where it appearsWhat is convertedThe fork it createsEffect
1. At purchaseForeign currency spent into a won acquisition costExecution date or settlement date. Is each purchase converted separatelyA larger acquisition cost means a smaller gain and less tax
2. At saleForeign currency received into a won transfer priceSame question. And is it the same basis used at purchaseA larger transfer price means a larger gain and more tax
3. At filingFixing the won figures written on the returnUse the separately converted values from 1 and 2, or compute the gain in foreign currency and convert onceThis single choice changes the whole tax base

The third is the most important and the most often misunderstood. Compare the two paths.

  • Path 1 - convert each side, then subtract. Build a won acquisition cost at the purchase-date rate and a won transfer price at the sale-date rate, then subtract won from won. Here currency movement over the holding period feeds straight into the gain
  • Path 2 - subtract in foreign currency, then convert. Compute the profit in dollars and convert only that profit once. Here the currency move multiplies only the profit, and the currency effect on the principal drops out of the calculation
  • The two paths agree only if the exchange rate never moved. The moment it moves they must diverge
  • Even if the share price in dollars is exactly flat, under path 1 a rising exchange rate produces a won-denominated gain. This is the single most common surprise in overseas share taxation
  • Conversely, a dollar profit paired with a sharply falling exchange rate can shrink or erase the gain under path 1

Marked for confirmation. This article does not state which path actually applies, nor whether the reference day is the execution date or the settlement date. The statutory text could not be opened. There are two routes to confirm it: read the relevant Income Tax Act provision directly on the Korean government law information portal, or put your specific trading pattern to the National Tax Service enquiry channel. The calculation below is not a claim that either path is correct; it exists to show how large the difference between them is.

4. Setting the cost basis when shares were bought in several lots

Buy once and sell all of it and there is nothing to decide. In practice, though, buying in several lots and selling only part is common. Then you must decide which purchase the sold shares came from before you can state an acquisition cost.

Two methods dominate.

  • First-in-first-out. The earliest shares are treated as sold first. Since each purchase has its own date, you end up using each purchase's own exchange rate
  • Moving or weighted average. Compute an average unit cost across all purchases and multiply by the quantity sold. Whether the average is struck in dollars or in won is yet another fork

The two methods also leave different cost bases on the shares you still hold. So it is not only this year's tax that shifts; the tax when you sell the remainder later shifts too. Compare a single year in isolation and that consequence stays invisible.

This article could not settle from a primary source which method applies. So rather than assert one, it shows by calculation that the method genuinely changes the result. That alone carries the practical implication: a tool that does not disclose its method produces a figure you cannot verify.

5. How far does loss offsetting reach

Here there is confirmed material. This is what was read directly on the National Tax Service guidance page.

  • Offsetting gains and losses between domestic and overseas shares is permitted. It applies to transfers made on or after 1 January 2020
  • The basic deduction is 2.5 million won per year across domestic and overseas shares combined. It is not 2.5 million on each side separately
  • The important caveat. The same guidance attaches the condition that domestic shares not themselves subject to capital gains tax cannot be offset against overseas shares

That third point is what calculators tend to miss. Not every domestic share qualifies for offsetting. You have to determine first whether that domestic share falls within capital gains taxation at all, and that turns on conditions such as ownership percentage and the nature of the security. It is not an area where an individual can be confident unaided.

  • If you simply typed a domestic loss figure into a calculator, the tool cannot have adjudicated eligibility. It just believed what you entered
  • Offsetting a loss that does not qualify makes the tax base smaller than it should be, and that becomes a problem at the filing stage
  • Conversely, omitting a loss that does qualify means paying more than you needed to
  • When and how to realise a loss is a matter of strategy and is out of scope here. That subject is handled separately in the structure of loss realisation and tax offsetting
  • If eligibility is unclear, confirming with the National Tax Service is the safe course. This is not a judgement to make on instinct

5.1 Overseas shares held through a fund are outside this article

One easily confused boundary is worth stating. Buying and selling overseas shares directly and holding them indirectly through an overseas fund are taxed under different regimes.

  • What this article covers is capital gains from direct investment - buying and selling securities in your own account
  • Indirect investment through a fund is handled as dividend income, and the calculation structure itself differs. That side is set out in overseas fund investing and its tax treatment and is not repeated here
  • Two people can both say they invested in overseas shares and be under different rules depending on which wrapper they used. Get that right before you even pick a calculator

Line by line, each row maps onto a step of the structure above.

  • Step 1, transfer price. 150 shares times 130 dollars is 19,500 dollars, times the assumed rate of 1,380 gives 26,910,000 won
  • Step 2, transfer cost. The 48.75 dollar sale fee at the same rate is 67,275 won
  • Step 3, acquisition cost. The 21,152,750 won built above
  • Step 4, transfer gain. 26,910,000 − 67,275 − 21,152,750 = 5,689,975 won
  • Step 5, basic deduction. 2.5 million won per National Tax Service guidance. Here it is applied in full, assuming no other share disposals that year
  • Step 6, tax base. 5,689,975 − 2,500,000 = 3,189,975 won. A negative result is treated as zero
  • Step 7, computed tax. 20% of 3,189,975 is 637,995 won

The recheck block recomputes the same values by a different arithmetic path. The figures the program built with a loop and the figures written out as a plain expression agree on all three items. Without that check there is no way to catch an error in the calculation itself.

The assumptions inside this calculation. 1. a 20% rate applies 2. the full 2.5 million deduction is available 3. no other share disposals that year 4. all trading fees qualify as necessary expenses 5. the exchange rates are arbitrary 6. local income tax excluded 7. rounding rules such as truncation to whole won are not applied 8. residency conditions are met. Of those eight, exactly one, number 2, was confirmed from a primary source. That is how many cells a calculator quietly fills for you.

7.1 What the three assumptions did

Look at the comparison again. Same trades, different assumptions.

  • A, first-in-first-out with per-lot rates. Acquisition cost 21,152,750 won, tax 637,995 won
  • B, won-denominated weighted average. Acquisition cost 20,952,250 won, tax 678,095 won. Blending in the cheap 90 dollar purchase pulled the acquisition cost down, which widened the gain
  • C, gain in foreign currency then one conversion. Acquisition cost 22,135,200 won, tax 441,505 won. Dropping the holding-period currency appreciation out of the calculation produced the smallest gain of the three
  • A to B differs by 40,100 won; A to C by 196,490 won. The FX treatment mattered far more than the cost-basis method
  • More purchases, a longer holding period, and larger currency moves over that period all widen this spread further

To isolate the effect of an assumption, everything was held fixed and only the sale-date exchange rate was varied. Share price, quantity and the purchase history are unchanged.

The result is fairly dramatic. A tax of 132,263 won at a rate of 1,250 becomes 988,118 won at 1,470. That is 7.47 times. And in dollar terms the shares were sold at exactly the same price.

  • The rate moved from 1,250 to 1,470, a 17.6% rise. The tax rose 647%
  • The amplification happens because the 2.5 million deduction is a fixed amount. When the gain sits near the deduction, a small increase doubles the tax base
  • Once the gain is large the amplification fades. Between 1,420 and 1,470 a 15% rise in gain produced a 24% rise in tax, a much gentler ratio
  • The break-even rate was 1,216. Below that the gain falls short of the deduction and the tax base is zero. The verification line confirms the gain landing exactly on 2,500,000 won
  • The practical implication is single. One FX assumption changes the order of magnitude of the tax. If a calculator does not show which rate it used, its output cannot be checked

9. Fees and costs - the boundary of necessary expenses

What the structure confirms is only that necessary expenses consist of acquisition cost plus transfer costs. Which individual items land on which side, and what qualifies at all, could not be settled from a primary source.

Some things are nonetheless clear from the structure.

  • Larger necessary expenses mean a smaller transfer gain. Omit a cost and you pay more tax than you needed to
  • In the hypothetical calculation above, purchase fees were added to the acquisition cost and the sale fee was deducted as a transfer cost. That is a treatment adopted for the worked example, not an assertion about what qualifies
  • Purchase fees require apportionment on a partial sale. That is why only 15 of the 30 dollar April fee appeared above
  • A fee paid in foreign currency is itself subject to conversion. So the exchange-rate question from section 3 applies to fees as well
  • Items commonly raised in practice include trading commissions, local transaction or stamp-type taxes, and costs incurred in currency exchange, but whether each qualifies needs National Tax Service confirmation
  • Claiming a cost requires documentation to survive. Check in advance whether your broker's transaction statement itemises these separately

A caution. This section is not a guide to inflating expenses to reduce tax. What qualifies as a necessary expense is fixed by the rules, not selectable. The purpose here is to make you check whether your calculator has a field for fees at all, and if so whether it routes them to the acquisition cost or to transfer costs.

The confirmation route is clear. Work through it in this order.

  1. Find the guidance for the relevant tax period in the National Tax Service capital gains tax filing guidance. The agency refreshes this material each filing season
  2. Check the actual form and its input fields in the capital gains section of the Hometax portal. The fields on the form are the list of information required
  3. If you need the statutory basis, open the Income Tax Act provision directly on the government law information portal. The provision view shows the effective date and amendment history, so always verify you are looking at the version currently in force

  5. Where the amounts are large or the structure complex, have a tax professional confirm it. This article recommends no particular practice

11. Dividend income runs on an entirely separate track

Holding overseas shares can generate two distinct taxable events. Mix them and the calculation goes wrong from the outset.

  • Capital gains. Arise when you sell. That is everything this article has covered. Tax attaches to the gain, and the basic deduction and loss offsetting apply
  • Dividend income. Arises while you hold, when a dividend is paid. The calculation structure is different entirely. The base is the amount received rather than a gain, and the 2.5 million capital gains deduction does not apply here
  • The two do not offset each other. Large dividends do not shrink a capital gain, and a capital loss does not reduce tax on dividends
  • Needs confirmation. The withholding rate, the threshold for aggregating financial income, and the treatment of tax already paid abroad could not be confirmed from a primary source for this article. No figures are stated
  • One structural point is still worth knowing. A dividend paid abroad may arrive already reduced by foreign withholding. How that is treated domestically becomes a separate question, so check whether your dividend records distinguish gross from net
  • Do not put dividend amounts into a capital gains calculator, or the reverse. When choosing a tool, first confirm which kind of income it is built for

13. Closing - why compute it yourself

The goal here was never to compute your tax for you. It was to make you walk the structure once by hand.

  • The structure itself is simple. Subtract necessary expenses from the transfer price, subtract the deduction, multiply by the rate. Eight lines and it is done
  • What is hard is producing the values you feed it. Foreign currency introduces an FX choice; split purchases introduce an allocation method
  • In the hypothetical example, changing only the assumptions spread the tax by a factor of 1.536. Moving the exchange rate alone spread it by 7.47 times. The trades were identical
  • Less could be stated as settled than one might expect. The calculation structure, the 2.5 million deduction, offsetting and its caveat, and the current effective date of the provision - roughly that much
  • What could not be confirmed was written down as not confirmed. The FX basis, the cost-basis method, the filing deadline, penalties, the local income tax rate, and the dividend figures
  • That list is itself the conclusion. A calculator fills those cells silently. Not knowing what it filled them with, you cannot verify the result
  • Use a tool after you understand the structure and you can tell when a result is odd. That is the state this article aimed at
  • Final confirmation belongs to the National Tax Service and to tax professionals. This article does not replace that confirmation and could not

Frequently asked questions

In what order is Korean capital gains tax on overseas stocks computed?

The National Tax Service structure is transfer price − necessary expenses (acquisition cost + transfer costs) = transfer gain − basic deduction = tax base × rate = computed tax − credits and reductions = determined tax. The basic deduction per National Tax Service guidance is 2.5 million won per year across domestic and overseas shares combined. The structure is simple, but producing the values you feed it involves several hidden assumptions. Individual cases must be confirmed with the National Tax Service or a tax professional.

Do you use the exchange rate from the purchase date or the sale date?

This article does not state a definitive answer, because the statutory text could not be opened. What was confirmed is that the rate appears at purchase, sale and filing, and that converting each trade separately versus computing the gain first and converting once produced a difference of nearly 200,000 won in the hypothetical calculation. Confirm the real basis with the National Tax Service or the government law portal.

If shares were bought in several lots and only some are sold, how is the cost basis set?

This too could not be settled from a primary source. In the hypothetical calculation, first-in-first-out produced an acquisition cost of 21,152,750 won and a won-denominated weighted average produced 20,952,250 won, a 40,100 won difference in tax. The more purchases, the longer the period and the larger the currency swings, the wider the gap. Confirm the applicable method with the National Tax Service.

Can losses on Korean domestic shares be offset against overseas gains?

What the National Tax Service guidance confirmed is that domestic and overseas share results may be offset, applying to transfers on or after 1 January 2020. But it carries the caveat that domestic shares not themselves subject to capital gains tax cannot be offset against overseas shares. Since not every domestic share qualifies, confirming with the National Tax Service whether your holdings are in scope is the safe course.

Is the argument that people should not use tax calculators?

No. It is that you should be in a position to verify the result. A tool that does not disclose its FX reference point, its cost-basis allocation, and its handling of offsetting and deductions is producing an indication only. Changing only the assumptions on one hypothetical trade log spread the tax by a factor of 1.536. Walk the structure once and you can see which cells are facts and which are choices.

## 0. Premise

To trust a calculator's number you must know what the calculator assumed.
Same trade log, assumptions changed only: tax ranged from KRW 441,505 to KRW 678,095.
The trades never changed. Only the assumptions did.

## 1. Confirmed (primary sources opened directly, 2026-08-01)

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Calculation structure | Transfer price - necessary expenses (acquisition + transfer costs) = gain - basic deduction = tax base x rate = computed tax - credits = determined tax |
| Basic deduction | Domestic and overseas shares **combined, KRW 2.5 million per year** |
| Loss offsetting | Domestic and overseas shares may be offset (transfers on or after 2020-01-01) |
| Offset caveat | **Domestic shares not subject to capital gains tax cannot be offset against overseas shares** |
| Law as-of | For the relevant section: Act No. 21221, partially amended 2025-12-23, effective 2026-01-01 |

## 3. Calculation steps (hypothetical example, FIFO + per-trade FX)

| Step | Item | Computation | Amount (KRW) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Transfer price | 150 sh x USD 130.00 x 1,380.00 | 26,910,000 |
| 2 | Transfer cost | USD 48.75 x 1,380.00 | 67,275 |
| 3 | Acquisition cost | Feb lot 100 sh + Apr lot 50 sh | 21,152,750 |
| 4 | Transfer gain | 1 - 2 - 3 | 5,689,975 |
| 5 | Basic deduction | assumed fully available | 2,500,000 |
| 6 | Tax base | 4 - 5 (floored at zero) | 3,189,975 |
| 7 | Computed tax | 6 x 20% | 637,995 |

Local income tax is a separate item and is not included in the figures above.

## 4. Change the assumptions - same trades, different tax

| Method | Acquisition | Gain | Tax base | Tax |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. FIFO + per-trade FX | 21,152,750 | 5,689,975 | 3,189,975 | **637,995** |
| B. Won weighted average | 20,952,250 | 5,890,475 | 3,390,475 | **678,095** |
| C. Net in USD, convert once | 22,135,200 | 4,707,525 | 2,207,525 | **441,505** |

Spread between highest and lowest: KRW 236,590. Highest is 1.536x the lowest.

| Sale-date FX | Gain | Tax base | Tax |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,250 | 3,161,313 | 661,313 | 132,263 |
| 1,300 | 4,133,875 | 1,633,875 | 326,775 |
| 1,350 | 5,106,438 | 2,606,438 | 521,288 |
| 1,380 | 5,689,975 | 3,189,975 | 637,995 |
| 1,420 | 6,468,025 | 3,968,025 | 793,605 |

The fixed KRW 2.5 million deduction amplifies the effect near the threshold.

## 6. Assumptions baked into this calculation (8)

- [ ] 20% rate applied
- [ ] Full KRW 2.5 million basic deduction available
- [ ] No other share disposals that year
- [ ] All trading fees qualify as necessary expenses
- [ ] Exchange rates are arbitrary (not published reference rates)
- [ ] Local income tax excluded
- [ ] Rounding rules such as truncation to whole won not applied
- [ ] Residency conditions met

Of these eight, only the basic deduction was confirmed from a primary source.

## 7. Documents to obtain from your broker

- [ ] Complete transaction statement for the tax period (all buys and sells)
- [ ] Records showing both execution and settlement dates per trade
- [ ] Statements itemising fees and transaction taxes separately
- [ ] Any broker-produced capital gains figures, plus the basis used
- [ ] Dividend records, gross and net distinguished

## 8. What to confirm with the National Tax Service

- [ ] Capital gains filing guidance for the tax period (deadline, forms, procedure)
- [ ] FX reference date and conversion method
- [ ] Cost-basis method for shares bought in multiple lots
- [ ] Whether your domestic shares are offset-eligible
- [ ] Which cost items qualify as necessary expenses
- [ ] The actual input fields on the Hometax filing form

## 9. Eight questions before using any calculator

- [ ] Which date supplies the exchange rate, and is it shown
- [ ] Is the rate applied per trade, or once to the net gain
- [ ] How is the cost basis allocated across multiple purchase lots
- [ ] Are purchase fees apportioned on a partial sale
- [ ] Is the basic deduction subtracted automatically, and can partial prior use be reflected
- [ ] When domestic results are entered, is eligibility adjudicated
- [ ] Does the result include local income tax
- [ ] Are the as-of date and governing provisions displayed

If any question has no answer, treat the output as indicative only.

## 10. Do not confuse these

- Capital gains and dividend income are **separate tracks**. They do not offset each other
- The KRW 2.5 million basic deduction is a **capital gains** device. It does not apply to dividends
- Direct investment (capital gains) and fund-held exposure (dividend income) follow **different rules**
- The deduction is not per security; it comes off once from **income aggregated across the tax period**
- The rate multiplies the **tax base after the deduction**, not the transfer gain
- What is taxed is the **gain**, not the sale proceeds

## 11. Do not do this

- Using rate or deduction figures that carry no source and no as-of date
- Copying calculator output onto a return without verification
- Using the hypothetical amounts in this document as your own tax figure
- Entering dividend amounts into a capital gains tool
- Deciding offset eligibility on your own judgement
- Using numbers from stale material without checking when it was verified

**Final confirmation belongs to the National Tax Service and to tax professionals. This document does not replace it.**
