Accessible Is Not the Same as Permitted

Taking apart the legal layers of AI training data, and the structure of the disputes around it

2026-07-29 Tech & IT

Introduction: what this covers, and the case it could not pin down

A scope note first. This piece started from talk about a large streaming company and AI, but no lawsuit against that company about AI training data could be confirmed against a primary source. Nothing in the publicly available court filings, regulator announcements or party statements confirmed such a case. Writing an unconfirmed case next to a company name is itself a problem, so this article is about the issue structure of AI training-data disputes rather than about any one company. It relies instead on matters that can be confirmed from primary or near-primary sources, and on published statutes and standards documents.

The reader assumed here is a developer, a product manager or a content lead. That changes the question. Not who wins, but where my project is standing right now. The whole argument compresses into one line.

Being able to reach something sits on a different layer from being permitted to use it.

If that sounds obvious, recall how often the two travel together in real meetings. Everyone has sat in a review where a data-collection decision closed on the single fact that the server returned 200. What follows separates that into seven layers, splits the input-side risk from the output-side risk, shows where jurisdictions diverge, and then goes down to provenance records, opt-out handling and contract clauses.

1. The legal status of data is not a single layer

The point where practical discussions most often collapse is flattening judgements that belong to different layers. The sentence it is public data so we are fine bundles at least seven separate determinations. Pulled apart, it looks like this.

LayerThe actual questionHow to checkWhat happens if you trust only this layer
1. Technical reachabilityDoes a request get a responseHTTP status code, whether authentication is requiredThey did not block us so it is allowed is not a valid inference
2. Crawl signalsWhat has the site expressed in machine-readable formrobots.txt, well-known policy files, HTTP headersIgnoring an expressed reservation can read as intent later
3. Terms of serviceDo account or API terms restrict training useSignup terms, API conditions, partnership agreementsA contract claim can stand independently of copyright
4. LicenceWhat conditions does the attached licence imposeDataset cards, repository licence filesBreaching attribution, share-alike or non-commercial terms is unlicensed use
5. Copyright and neighbouring rightsIs it protected and who holds the rightsThe statute and exceptions of the relevant jurisdictionIf this layer closes, the four above being open changes nothing
6. Personal data and likenessAre there people inside the dataData protection law, image and publicity rightsIt survives even after every copyright question is settled
7. Unfair competition and othersIs this free-riding on another party's investmentJurisdiction-specific unfair competition doctrinesIt routes around the argument over protectability entirely

The table makes clear that layer one tells you nothing about the other six. Yet the evidence recorded in a typical data-collection ticket is layer one alone, a single line saying crawl confirmed. Without this separation another fact also stays invisible: trusting licence metadata is itself a risk.

There is a measurement. A large-scale dataset audit released in October 2023 traced the lineage of more than 1,800 text datasets and reported licence omission above 70 percent and error rates above 50 percent on widely used dataset hosting sites. What that number means is simple. The licence written on a dataset card is somebody's input field, not a verified fact.

  • Never put layer one and layer five in the same sentence — it is public so there is no copyright issue mixes two layers, and should be the first thing cut in review.
  • Layer three survives independently — winning the argument over protectability leaves the terms-of-service breach standing, because it is a different cause of action.
  • Layer four is automatable — check licence fields in the ingestion pipeline and record exceptions, rather than asking a human to read them.
  • Layer six is the one copyright owners forget — data with faces, voices or addresses needs a separate process even after rights are cleared.
  • Each layer has a different owner — engineering holds one and two, procurement holds three and four, legal holds five and seven, privacy holds six. Assign them all to one person and something will be missed.

2. Where the layers come apart in practice

Left abstract, none of this gets used. So here are the typical scenes where layers diverge. Each row is a case that clears one layer and fails another.

What the six rows share is that none of them is obviously reckless. They all look like reasonable calls, which is exactly why they pass review. Without the layer separation, this table never gets written.

3. The input side - what training actually triggers

Training-stage disputes usually turn on three things. Was there copying, how was it acquired, and does an exception apply. The order matters. Many meetings discuss only the third, when the first two are frequently decisive.

First, the existence of copying is rarely contested. Large-scale training downloads, stores and preprocesses source material, so physical copies exist. The argument is whether that copying is an intermediate transient step or a use to be judged on its own.

Second, the acquisition path is the biggest practical variable. The same work obtained by scanning a lawfully purchased copy and the same work pulled from a pirate library may be treated differently. That distinction was the central axis in the case covered separately in the Anthropic copyright settlement piece. The practical lesson is blunt. If you did not record where it came from, you cannot prove it later.

Third, whether an exception applies varies completely by jurisdiction, which the next section tabulates. One point belongs here. United States fair use asks a court to consider four factors on the facts of the case: the purpose and character of the use, the nature of the work, the amount and substantiality used, and the effect on the potential market for or value of the work. It is a list of factors, not a checklist. There is no threshold you clear by satisfying all four; a court weighs the whole matter. Deciding in advance that a use is fair therefore does not work as a plan.

That this area has not set is visible in the state of the regulators' own documents. The United States Copyright Office released Part 3 of its report on copyright and artificial intelligence, the part covering generative AI training, as a pre-publication version on 9 May 2025, and as of the check date the final version has not appeared. The Office says it expects no substantive change to the analysis or conclusions, but the pre-publication status is itself a signal. More importantly, an agency report is not law and does not bind a court. When citing it in an internal document, state what kind of document it is.

  • Provenance records come before exception arguments — without an acquisition path you never reach the stage where exceptions are discussed.
  • We only used it for training does not erase the storage — whether originals were retained after training can be a separate issue.
  • Preprocessing is copying too — deduplication, cleaning and tokenisation artefacts belong in the record if you keep them.
  • Internal data is not automatically safe — every internal wiki contains documents pasted in from somewhere else.
  • Synthetic data has a lineage — data generated by another model can carry that model's terms along with it.

4. The output side - the risk does not end when training does

This is the part worth emphasising most. Many organisations review training data and skip output review entirely. Yet for a company that merely uses a model, the residual risk is larger on the output side. The reason is simple. You cannot see the training data, but you publish the outputs yourself.

The output risk is technically real. The November 2023 paper Scalable Extraction of Training Data from (Production) Language Models showed training data can be extracted from open weight models and from production chatbots, and its abstract concludes that current alignment techniques do not eliminate memorisation. The implication is not that models leak; it is that a design that assumes zero probability of output overlapping a source has no basis.

The law separates the two stages as well. A law-firm analysis of the June 2025 fair use decision from the Northern District of California states plainly that the ruling does not address whether the outputs of the AI product are infringement or fair use. In other words, a favourable finding on the training side is not absolution for the output side. Practitioners should hold on to that sentence, because vendor material does sometimes quote only the training-side conclusion and present the whole question as settled.

The axes argued on the output side differ from the input side.

AxisInput, training stageOutput, generation stageCan the adopting company control it
Conduct at issueCollection, storage, copyingGenerating, publishing, distributingOutput side yes, input side effectively no
Test appliedWhether an exception covers itSubstantial similarity to an existing workSimilarity review can be made a process
EvidenceDataset inventory and acquisition logsPrompt, output, review recordWithout records neither side is defensible
Who answersCloser to the model developerWhoever published the outputOnly so much of this can be moved by contract
Remedies soughtInjunction, damages, destruction of dataTakedown, damagesDestruction claims can reach the model itself
MitigationLicensing, honouring reservationsSimilarity filters, attribution, human reviewOutput side gives more per unit of cost

The last row is the operational conclusion. An internal rule that says verify the training sources cannot be executed by anyone, because users of commercial models are not given that information. A rule that says review outputs for similarity is executable. The piece on rights in AI-generated work reached the same conclusion from a different direction.

5. Jurisdictions diverge - same data, different answer

This is the most dangerous spot for any team shipping internationally. Identical data and identical training can produce different conclusions depending on the jurisdiction. As checked in July 2026 the picture is as follows, with primary links in the references below.

JurisdictionBasis for training-purpose useCan rightsholders reservePractical caution
United States17 U.S.C. 107 fair use, four factors weighed case by caseNo institutional opt-out mechanismThe answer cannot be settled in advance; outcomes differ by case
European UnionText and data mining exceptions in Articles 3 and 4 of Directive 2019/790Yes - Article 4 recognises reservation in an appropriate machine-readable mannerWithout a process for reading reservations the exception is hard to rely on
KoreaNo dedicated information-analysis exception; the general fair use article appliesNo institutionalised reservation mechanismProposals under debate are not law; confirm the statute in force
JapanCopyright Act Article 30-4, information analysis not aimed at enjoymentA proviso limits it, so it is not unlimitedTraining concentrated on one creator's works can still be a problem
United KingdomNon-commercial research exception; government position is that commercial training needs a licenceGovernment did not adopt a reservation-based exceptionThe March 2026 government report chose the status quo

Two takeaways. First, the EU structure requires you to read reservations before you can invoke the exception, so when and how you checked has to exist as a process artefact. Second, Korea routes through a general clause, which makes advance predictability low. Reasoning that Korea must resemble Japan because Japan has an information-analysis provision does not hold.

The EU adds another layer. Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the AI Act, requires providers of general-purpose AI models to publish a sufficiently detailed summary of the content used for training and to put a copyright policy in place that respects reservations. What matters is that this operates as a documentation obligation separate from infringement. Even where nothing infringes, failing to produce the summary is itself exposed. National implementation in Korea is covered separately in the AI Framework Act compliance piece.

The timetable is worth checking too. On the text of the Regulation, obligations for general-purpose AI models apply from 2 August 2025, and the Regulation applies generally from 2 August 2026. The training-content summary is not a free-form document: the article requires it to be drawn up according to a template provided by the AI Office, and that template was published on 24 July 2025. A misreading arises here often. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, which entered into force on 27 July 2026, pushed back the application dates for high-risk system obligations, but the general-purpose AI provisions and the transparency provisions were not part of that. Hearing only the summary that the AI Act was delayed, and deferring training-data documentation on that basis, is a misread.

6. What ongoing disputes actually argue about

No case summaries here. Instead, the structure of the issues. Because the same axes recur across jurisdictions, knowing the structure lets you map any new case onto your own project.

To be explicit: what follows is the type of argument each side typically advances, not a finding of fact. Where no judgment has issued, this article does not take a view on who is right.

IssueWhat rightsholders typically assertWhat developers typically answerWhat it implies for your project
Identifying the worksLarge volumes of protected works were usedWhich works, used how, has not been specifiedWithout an inventory both sides struggle. Records are defence and offence
Acquisition pathThe material was obtained unlawfullyIt was lawfully obtained or openly accessibleWith no acquisition log you cannot even mount the rebuttal
Exception coverageThe character of the use falls outside the exceptionTraining is analytical, not consumption of expressionStart from the fact that the governing article differs by jurisdiction
Market effectIt substitutes for or dilutes the market for the workIt is not a substitute and serves a different marketAsk honestly whether your service replaces consumption of the source
OutputsOutputs reproduce the protected workReproduction is exceptional and mitigations existBuild the response procedure for reproduction cases in advance
Terms and contractUse exceeded the scope of the agreed termsThe use falls within the termsContract wording operates independently of the copyright debate
Scope of remedyCessation plus destruction of the training dataDestruction is a disproportionate remedyWithout segregated datasets you cannot comply with partial destruction

The same structure appears in Korea. A copyright infringement action brought by the three terrestrial broadcasters against a domestic portal operator was filed in January 2025 and is being heard at the Seoul Central District Court, and according to reporting the bench indicated that individual works have to be identified to sustain an infringement claim. The same broadcasters filed against an overseas AI developer at the Seoul Central District Court on 23 February 2026, seeking, according to reporting, damages together with an injunction against reproduction and transmission and deletion of the datasets used for training. Neither matter has been decided, and each side's position remains an assertion at this stage.

Here is the part this article has to state most honestly. As of the check date, no United States appellate court has decided whether training-purpose use is fair use. Everything currently cited is a first-instance ruling, and judges within the same district court have written in different directions about market effect. Cases that end in settlement never get appellate review at all. So when you meet a sentence presented as settled doctrine, the first thing to check is which court, which level, and which case it came from.

Disputes also do not necessarily start with a complaint. According to reporting from early 2026, several large content companies sent letters to a video generation model developer demanding removal of their intellectual property from training datasets. No suit was filed, and the assertions in those letters are untested. This stage matters for a practical reason of its own. When a letter arrives the response window is short, and what you need first is not a lawyer but a data inventory. If you cannot say within days which sources entered and when, there is no negotiation to have.

  • Identify the individual works is an operational signal — inventory management decides outcomes for rightsholders and developers alike.
  • Destruction claims aim at the model itself — if datasets are lumped together, partial destruction becomes physically impossible.
  • Unfair-competition style claims bypass protectability — winning the argument that something is not protected may not end the matter.
  • A settlement is not precedent — a case that ends in settlement fixes no legal rule. Do not assume it carries to the next case.
  • Different jurisdictions can reach different results on the same facts — an international service is safer designed to the most conservative jurisdiction it serves.

7. What honouring an opt-out technically means

We respect rightsholder reservations goes into a company page easily. Taken down to engineering it becomes concrete work. Three families of signal are currently referenced in practice.

First, the robots exclusion protocol, the oldest, an access control at the acquisition stage. Second, the TDM Reservation Protocol, a W3C community group specification that places a JSON file at a well-known location on the server expressing whether rights are reserved and the URL of the licensing policy. Third, the IETF AI Preferences work. That working group treats content acquisition and downstream use as conceptually separate, and the separation matters: the standards discussion mirrors exactly the point that being allowed to fetch and being allowed to train are different layers.

# Minimum signals an ingestion pipeline should check per host
# 1) Robots exclusion protocol - acquisition stage
GET https://example.com/robots.txt

# 2) TDM Reservation Protocol - policy file at a well-known location
GET https://example.com/.well-known/tdmrep.json
#    tdm-reservation of 1 means rights reserved; tdm-policy is the policy URL

# 3) Reservation expressed in response headers
GET https://example.com/article/123
#    inspect X-Robots-Tag and tdm-reservation style headers

# 4) In-document markers - HTML meta level
#    collect during parsing and store alongside the source

One mistake is common here: reading a signal and throwing it away. If you check at crawl time, record only a pass or fail, and never persist the signal itself, then two years later in a dispute you have no way to prove what that site was expressing at the time. Sites change their policies, and the changed policy will look as though it always applied.

  • Snapshot the raw signal — store the original bytes and the timestamp, not just your parsed verdict.
  • Record exclusion reasons as codes — why you excluded something becomes evidence of diligence later.
  • Set a re-check interval — reservations get added after the fact. An old verdict is not permanently valid.
  • Do not design around access restrictions — this article does not describe circumventing them, and such attempts can read as intent.
  • Track infrastructure defaults — a major CDN has already moved to blocking AI crawlers by default, so the same URL answers differently over time.
  • Watch the open licensing frameworks — the open licence community is preparing its own system for expressing AI-use preferences.

8. The provenance ledger - a minimum schema

Everything above compresses into one artefact: a data provenance ledger. It has a single purpose. To be able to answer where did this data come from two years from now. No new system is needed; add fields to whatever dataset registration you already have.

{
  "dataset_id": "ds_support_tickets_2026q2",
  "acquired_at": "2026-06-04T02:11:00Z",
  "source": {
    "kind": "internal_logs",
    "origin": "customer_support_platform",
    "collection_purpose_notice": "notice_v3_2025-11-02"
  },
  "rights": {
    "license": "internal_only",
    "license_evidence": "s3://provenance/ds_support_tickets_2026q2/terms.pdf",
    "third_party_content": "possible",
    "personal_data": true,
    "personal_data_basis_ref": "privacy_review_2026_0417"
  },
  "signals_snapshot": {
    "robots_txt": null,
    "tdm_policy": null,
    "checked_at": null
  },
  "processing": {
    "dedup": true,
    "pii_redaction": "v2.3",
    "retention_days": 1095
  },
  "usage": {
    "allowed_for": ["finetune_internal_assistant"],
    "blocked_for": ["public_model_release", "vendor_sharing"]
  },
  "owner": "team_ml_platform",
  "approved_by": "approver_id_0217",
  "review_due": "2027-06-04"
}

The design point is making not known expressible. In the example above the signal fields are null because the data was not externally crawled. If you cannot distinguish an empty value from a check that never happened, the ledger stops being trustworthy.

  • A licence is not a string, it is a string plus evidence — do not copy the dataset card value; attach the source document.
  • Record both allowed and blocked uses — listing only what is allowed invites expansive reading later.
  • Make the review date mandatory — licences and terms change, and an approval with no expiry is a liability.
  • Store datasets segregated by source — removing one source later has to be physically possible.
  • Stamp the dataset list into the model artefact — you must be able to trace which checkpoint used which ledger entries.
  • Keep personal data as its own field — merged into the copyright field, a different team's problem gets missed.

9. The output review gate

Output risk drops substantially with a single filter immediately before publication. It is not a complete barrier, but the existence of the procedure itself changes how a dispute plays out. A minimal form you can attach to an internal release pipeline looks like this.

output_review:
  scope: "externally published text, image and code artefacts"
  gates:
    - id: prompt_hygiene
      rule: "block prompts naming a real person, title or living artist"
      action: block
    - id: similarity_scan
      rule: "compare generated results against internal corpora and public search"
      threshold: "long matching spans escalate to human review"
      action: escalate
    - id: attribution_check
      rule: "confirm reproduced spans can carry a source citation"
      action: escalate
    - id: license_check
      rule: "code artefacts must pass a licence scanner"
      action: block
  record:
    keep:
      - prompt
      - model_name_version
      - output_hash
      - reviewer
      - decision
      - decided_at
    retention_days: 1825
  escalation:
    owner: "team_legal_liaison"
    sla_hours: 48

The record block is the core. What matters is less that a gate was passed than on what basis it was passed. Set retention against your dispute horizon, not your log policy. Inherit the log policy and the evidence is gone exactly when you need it.

10. Clauses worth putting in the contract

What technology cannot prevent, contracts allocate. Buyer and supplier care about different things, so the table splits them. These are items to open the discussion with; actual wording and adequacy need a lawyer's review.

ClauseWhat the adopting side asks forWhat the supplying side defendsCommon trap
Training data warrantiesRepresentations on acquisition path and clearanceLimited to the extent of actual knowledgeDiluted into best efforts and it means nothing
No re-trainingInputs and outputs are not used for trainingCarve-outs such as abuse detectionApplies only to paid tiers while free usage is excluded
Rights in outputsScope of the right to use the outputsNo exclusivity grantedMissing that identical outputs may go to competitors
Defence and indemnityCover if a third party asserts rightsConditioned on keeping safety features onVoid where filters were disabled or bypassed
Notice obligationsAdvance notice of training-data policy changesLimits on channel and notice periodUpdating a terms page treated as notice
Audit and evidenceAbility to review processing records when neededTrade secrets carved outA right with no procedure attached cannot be exercised
Post-terminationDeletion of data and derivatives, with confirmationBackup retention periods reservedDerivatives omitted, so embeddings survive

The row most often skipped is post-termination. Writing only that originals are deleted leaves embeddings, indexes and fine-tuned weights outside scope. Derivatives have to be defined explicitly.

This is already operating as a contractual gate somewhere. A large streaming company's published generative AI guidance for production partners conditions use on tools that do not store, reuse or train on production inputs or outputs, and requires that outputs not replicate identifiable characteristics of material the production does not own. Written approval is required where generated material reaches final deliverables, where a real person's likeness is involved, or where third-party intellectual property is implicated. The interesting part is that this is not litigation but a buyer imposing conditions on its supply chain. Contracts move before regulation settles, so a team selling to large buyers is being practical if it prepares its training-data position as sales collateral in advance.

11. What each role can do this week

You do not need all of it. The items you cannot answer are the backlog.

  1. Build an inventory of the data sources currently used for training, fine-tuning and retrieval augmentation. Not knowing how many there are is already finding one.
  2. For each source, mark which of layers one through seven you have actually checked. The point is to leave the unchecked cells visibly empty.
  3. If you crawl externally, confirm whether raw signals from the crawl time are retained. If not, fix the pipeline to store them from the next run.
  4. For purchased or partnered data, find the warranty clause in the agreement. If it takes more than thirty seconds, contract management is the problem.
  5. If internal wikis and document stores are indexed, check whether a rule filters out externally sourced documents.
  6. If customer data feeds training, pull the purpose notice given at collection and compare it against current use.
  7. If you use commercial models, capture the actual data-use terms of that service. They can differ by plan.
  8. Confirm that a pre-publication review procedure exists as a document for externally released outputs.
  9. Check whether the retention period for review records simply inherits the log policy.
  10. Confirm that datasets are stored segregated by source. Lumped together, partial removal is impossible.
  11. Test whether you can trace a model checkpoint back to the dataset list it was trained on.
  12. If you serve EU users, check that a documented process for reading reservations exists.
  13. If you use synthetic data, confirm the terms of the model that generated it.
  14. Ask vendors in writing about their training-data policy and keep the answer. Not getting one is also information.
  15. Collect the unanswered items and turn them into a backlog with owners and dates.

12. Common misconceptions

  • It is public data so there is no copyright — publication and the existence of rights are unrelated. Publication is a fact about the access layer.
  • Crediting the source makes it fine — attribution is a condition of certain licences, not a basis for use in itself.
  • It is non-commercial so it is fine — non-commercial character is one factor among several and does not settle the question.
  • There is no original inside the weights so it is safe — the extraction literature makes that premise hard to accept as given.
  • A settlement means the law is now settled — a settlement resolves parties, not doctrine.
  • The vendor indemnifies us so we are safe — indemnities usually carry conditions and caps, and do not cover downtime or reputational damage.
  • It is a foreign service so local law does not apply — a different jurisdiction can attach based on where users are.
  • Japan has an information-analysis provision so ours must be similar — statutory structures differ. Check the article, do not reason by analogy.
  • Honouring robots.txt is enough — it is an acquisition-stage signal and was not designed to govern later use.
  • This is legal's problem, not engineering's — engineers create the records. Without records legal has nothing to work with.

Conclusion: separate the layers and the work becomes visible

Compressed to a sentence: it is public is a fact about the access layer, while we may use it is a conclusion that has to clear at least six more layers. The habit of writing those two as one sentence creates most of the trouble.

And it is more honest to admit this is contested territory. The governing article differs by jurisdiction, many matters are pending, and cases that settle fix no doctrine. So this article does not say do this and you are safe. What it can say is this. Whichever way the law lands, the party with records is better off. Acquisition paths, signal snapshots, licence evidence, output review records. None of the four is ever a loss in any jurisdiction.

Three things are possible this week. Build the inventory of sources feeding training and retrieval. Mark which layers you actually checked for each. Pick one empty cell and attach an owner and a date. That part holds regardless of how the case law goes, and it is the whole of what this article set out to cover.
