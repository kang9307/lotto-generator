Linus Torvalds Says Linux Is Not an Anti-AI Project

What it means for a trust-critical codebase like the kernel to treat AI as just another tool - not blind faith, not rejection

2026-07-18 Trends & Culture

Introduction: Why One Line From Torvalds Made Headlines

On July 15, 2026, a short reply Linus Torvalds posted to the Linux kernel mailing list rippled through the developer community again. The trigger was a debate over how far AI should be allowed into kernel code review. When some developers voiced skepticism or open distaste toward AI use, Torvalds drew a line using his authority as top-level maintainer. Linux is not an anti-AI project, he said flatly, adding that if anyone has a problem with that direction, they can do the open-source thing and fork it, or simply walk away.

What makes this worth analyzing is not that it was provocative. The Linux kernel underpins server, cloud, and mobile infrastructure worldwide - a codebase where a single bug can ripple across billions of devices, and where correctness is about as non-negotiable as software gets. That the founder of a project like this would declare AI is 'just a tool' and embrace it pragmatically plants an important marker in the broader AI-adoption debate now playing out across open source. This piece analyzes, as of July 2026, the context and implications of that statement, and contrasts it with Godot, a project that reached the opposite conclusion around the same time.

1. What Happened - The Context Behind the Statement

1.1 The Trigger - Sashiko and the Debate Over AI Review Tools

Behind this dispute sits a specific tool: Sashiko. Built by Google engineers and released in March 2026, it is an agentic code review system that ingests patches posted to the kernel mailing list and sends them to an LLM - primarily Gemini, though it also supports Claude and other models - to flag potential bugs. Tested against roughly 1,000 recent kernel issues tagged 'Fixes:', it reportedly caught around half of them ahead of time, and it has since become an ongoing open-source project operated under the Linux Foundation. The friction is that a tool like this can add to maintainer review load and occasionally generate noise through false positives. That was the basis for some developers' skepticism toward the spread of AI-assisted review and contribution, and Torvalds pushed back directly.

1.2 What Torvalds Actually Said

His point was straightforward: AI is just like the other tools the community already uses, and it is clearly a useful one. He argued that anyone who doubts that usefulness clearly has not actually used it, while also stating that although he is not forcing anyone to use AI, he will flatly ignore arguments trying to stop others from using it. He reiterated a founding principle of the project - that kernel development is decided by technical merit, not ideology or sentiment. This is consistent with his long-standing insistence that Linux is not, and never has been, a 'social warrior' project.

2. The Real Weight Behind 'It's Just a Tool'

2.1 Neither Blind Faith Nor Rejection - Adoption Premised on Verification

What matters here is that Torvalds was not uncritically praising AI. In the same reply, he acknowledged AI is not perfect and can be a genuinely painful tool - one that keeps surfacing embarrassing bugs. But his prescribed response was not to put your head in the sand and pretend not to hear it; it was to deal with the problems the tool creates, alongside its benefits. That stance lines up precisely with a long-standing norm in kernel culture. Every time a new compiler, static analyzer, or fuzzer was introduced, the community followed the same procedure: don't trust the output blindly, have a human verify it, and merge only what survives verification. My reading is that treating AI as a new input into that same old verification pipeline is the real substance of what Torvalds said.

2.2 Why This Stance Carries More Weight in a Domain Like the Kernel

In a typical web application, an AI-introduced bug is usually contained. The kernel is different. A single flaw in memory management, concurrency control, or driver code can corrupt data, crash a system, or open a security hole. That the top authority in a domain like this chose not to reject AI outright, but to declare he'd use it as a tool premised on verification, reads as a signal that AI has become a constant even the most conservative corner of software engineering culture can no longer set aside. The key point is that this acceptance does not mean merging whatever AI writes - it sits on top of the existing principle that a human still reviews, and is still accountable for, whatever AI produces.

3. The Open Source Spectrum on AI - Contrasted With Godot

3.1 Godot's Opposite Choice

By coincidence, just two weeks before this statement, the open-source game engine Godot announced a policy pointed in the exact opposite direction (see our related analysis: Godot Bans AI-Authored Code Contributions). It barred AI-authored code, PRs submitted by autonomous agents, and pasting AI-generated text directly into communication with maintainers. Godot's maintainers explained that they cannot trust contributors who lean heavily on AI to actually understand and be able to fix the code they submit. The decisive reasons were copyright uncertainty and a surge of low-quality 'AI slop' PRs that were burning through volunteer reviewers' time.

3.2 curl and QEMU Each Took Their Own Path

The spectrum doesn't stop at two projects. curl shut down its entire bug bounty program in early 2026 after plausible-but-wrong AI-generated vulnerability reports flooded in. QEMU, meanwhile, kept a blanket ban in place while considering carve-outs for AI use on small, mechanical changes. In other words, open source has not converged on a single standard. Depending on each project's review resources, codebase risk, and community culture, positions are scattered across a wide spectrum from outright prohibition to cautious acceptance.

3.3 The Pragmatic Middle Ground Linux Occupies

Within that spectrum, Linux's position is distinct. It is neither an outright ban like Godot's, nor unconditional blanket acceptance. It is a middle ground in which humans retain final review and merge authority while AI is folded in as a review aid (Sashiko) and a development aid. My view is that this middle ground is possible precisely because the Linux kernel already runs one of the strictest human review systems in software - multi-stage subsystem maintainer review, static analysis, and extensive test infrastructure are already in place, so AI is an added filter on top of that, not the only safeguard. Conversely, the more chronically short-staffed a project's review pipeline is, the stronger the incentive to close the door defensively, as Godot did.

4. Where LLMs Actually Help in Kernel and Systems Programming - and Where They Don't

4.1 Where They Help - Boilerplate and First-Pass Review

The areas where LLMs deliver real value in kernel work are fairly well defined. Repetitive driver boilerplate, drafting commit messages and documentation, mechanical refactors that follow existing patterns, and above all, first-pass screening review. As Sashiko demonstrates, filtering a high-volume patch stream for patterns a human reviewer might miss - a missing error-handling branch, code resembling a known bug signature - is exactly the kind of tireless, consistent work an LLM can do well. This is not a replacement for human reviewers; it's an absorption layer for low-difficulty work that frees humans to spend their time on harder judgment calls.

4.2 Where They Fall Short - Subtle Concurrency and Security Bugs

The flip side is equally clear. Deadlocks tangled up in lock ordering, missing memory barriers, race conditions - defects that don't show up on a static read of the code and only manifest under specific execution orders or timing - are not something today's LLMs reliably catch. These are exactly the kinds of bugs even seasoned human experts miss without years of domain-specific experience, which makes it especially risky when an LLM confidently declares 'looks fine.' That's precisely why, in a domain where the cost of failure is as high as the kernel's, a human still has to make the final call rather than trusting LLM output outright. Torvalds' insistence on the word 'tool' can be read as deliberately keeping the LLM in the role of assistant, not final arbiter.

5. Where the 'Distaste' Comes From - and What a Mature Stance Looks Like

Reducing AI distaste to simple technophobia misses the point. In my reading, at least three distinct concerns are tangled together here. The first is a quality concern - the real fatigue of low-quality AI output eating into already-scarce review resources, as curl's bug bounty collapse illustrates. The second is a copyright and accountability concern: as Godot pointed out, code substantially written by AI carries unclear copyright attribution, and when a contributor submits code they don't understand themselves, that becomes a long-term maintenance burden for the project. The third is hype fatigue - years of marketing claiming AI solves everything have built up a backlash where even legitimate criticism of AI's limits gets dismissed as coming from someone who 'doesn't get it.'

All three concerns are grounded in something real. What makes Torvalds' statement read as mature is not that he dismisses these concerns, but that he separates 'rejecting a tool because it has problems' from 'using a tool pragmatically while knowing its problems.' He acknowledged AI is not perfect, while drawing a line against using that imperfection as grounds to reject the tool outright. My conclusion is that a genuinely mature stance is neither enthusiasm nor rejection - it's understanding a tool's actual limits precisely, and extracting value within them.

Conclusion: Where Developers Should Land

Torvalds' statement doesn't establish a new principle so much as apply the one Linux kernel development has always followed - that technical merit beats ideology - to a new variable called AI. And the fact that Godot arrived at the opposite conclusion around the same time shows this isn't a question with a single right answer; it's a context-dependent judgment that varies with each project's review capacity, risk tolerance, and community trust structure.

Three practical takeaways follow for individual developers. First, use AI as a tool, but never remove the habit of verifying its output from the seat of final judgment. Second, assess your own project or team's review capacity and codebase risk before deciding how much to adopt - the right answer for a codebase with kernel-grade verification infrastructure will differ from one with only one or two reviewers. Third, step outside emotional camps, whether distaste or blind faith, and verify for yourself what's actually useful and what's still risky. As of July 2026, open source has not settled on a single answer to this question, and that diversity itself looks like a reasonable state for where things stand today.
