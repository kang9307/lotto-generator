How to Choose a Smartwatch - Health Sensors, Battery, and Compatibility

A Criteria-First Buying Guide: Compatibility, Battery Math, Sensor Limits, and Water Resistance

Jul 26, 2026 Tech

Introduction: Why Smartwatches Are Unusually Hard to Choose

You can stare at smartwatch spec sheets for an hour and still not decide. The reason is structural. Unlike most electronics, a smartwatch has specs that eliminate entire swathes of the market outright. A laptop short on RAM can be upgraded later; a camera takes a different lens. But with a smartwatch, the operating system on your phone makes more than half the candidates literally unbuyable before you compare anything else. On top of that, advertised battery figures diverge noticeably from real use, and the long list of health features never comes with a clear statement of how far those numbers can be trusted.

So this article does not list products. Instead it walks through five decision criteria in the order that eliminates candidates fastest. Compatibility cuts the field by more than half, battery math cuts it again, and sensors plus water resistance clean up what remains. At the end there is a use-case table and a checklist you can carry into a store. No specific brand or model is recommended, and the criteria are written so that they still work whichever new products launch in a given year.

One thing up front. As covered in detail below, smartwatch health readings are not medical diagnoses. The reason this guide spends so long on sensors is not to sell you on them, but to draw a line around how far each number should be taken.

1. Criterion One: OS Compatibility - The Constraint You Cannot Undo

The first thing to check is neither the screen nor the battery, but the operating system on your phone. It is the one item you cannot fix later with money.

Here is where things stand. The Apple Watch requires an iPhone from initial setup onward and does not work with Android phones; Apple has been reported as saying Android support was not workable because of technical limitations. The reverse holds too. Samsung and Google Wear OS watches are bound to the Android ecosystem from the setup app onward, and are described as effectively unusable on iOS. Some third-party Wear OS models can technically connect to an iPhone, but this is generally described as a deliberately limited experience, with restrictions on things like replying to messages or invoking voice assistants.

The genuinely cross-platform option is brands that maintain their own app ecosystem: Garmin, Fitbit, Withings, Amazfit, Polar, Suunto, Coros and Huawei are commonly cited here. Even so, expect that notification replies, voice assistants and tap-to-pay are often missing or curtailed on iOS.

Verdict: if there is any chance you will switch phone platforms in the next year or two, a cross-platform brand costs you less than a platform-locked watch. If your phone platform is settled, a same-ecosystem watch wins on integration polish.

2. Criterion Two: Battery - Convert the Quoted Figure Into Real Use

Battery is the spec most likely to disappoint if you take it at face value. Manufacturer figures are typically measured with the always-on display (AOD) disabled, satellite positioning unused, and few notifications arriving. Real life brings dozens of notifications, GPS during workouts, and auto-brightness pushing the screen harder.

2.1 The Real-World Conversion Formula

A workable rule of thumb looks like this.

  • Real runtime is roughly the quoted figure multiplied by 0.5 to 0.7 (moderate notifications, AOD on, GPS workouts two or three times a week)
  • If you have a target charging interval, work backwards: required quoted runtime = desired interval divided by 0.6
  • Example 1: you want to charge weekly (7 days) → 7 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 12 quoted days as a safe bar
  • Example 2: charging every other day (2 days) is fine → 2 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 3 to 4 quoted days usually suffices

The display is reported to account for roughly 30 to 50 percent of a smartwatch power draw, with a bright screen and GPS named as the two biggest drains. In other words, enabling AOD means running the most power-hungry component all day long. If AOD is non-negotiable for you, step up one tier from what the formula suggests.

2.2 Charging Behaviour Matters Too

Just as important as runtime is how long a charge takes and what the charger looks like. If you plan to use sleep tracking, you cannot take the watch off at night, so the deciding factor becomes whether a 20 to 30 minute top-up while you shower covers a full day. Proprietary docks also mean one more cable to remember when travelling.

3. Criterion Three: Health Sensors - What They Measure, and How Far to Trust It

This is the most heavily marketed area of the category and the one that deserves the coolest head. Do not just check whether a sensor is present - ask what the resulting number is actually good for.

3.1 Heart Rate (Optical PPG)

Nearly universal. The sensor shines light into the wrist and infers pulse from changes in reflected light. Studies are reported to show relatively good accuracy at rest and during low-intensity activity, with error increasing as exercise intensity rises. That degradation is most noticeable in activities with heavy arm movement or wrist flexion, such as strength training or racket sports. If you train by heart rate zones, check whether the watch can pair with a chest strap.

3.2 Blood Oxygen (SpO2)

Same optical principle, far more sensitive to conditions. Reviews repeatedly note that ambient light can move the reading, that cold temperatures constrict peripheral vessels and reduce accuracy, and that skin pigmentation differences affect the measurement. Add differing sensors and algorithms between manufacturers and cross-device comparison becomes unreliable too. The sensible use is watching a trend under consistent conditions, not reading an absolute value.

3.3 Electrocardiogram (ECG)

This records a single-lead ECG using the potential difference between wrist and fingertip. Irregular-rhythm notification features on some products have received regulatory clearance, and studies report relatively high sensitivity and specificity for atrial fibrillation screening. The essential point, however, is that screening and diagnosis are different things. Academic literature continues to flag inconclusive recordings and remaining algorithm limitations. If the device flags something, that is a prompt to see a clinician, not a diagnosis.

3.4 Sleep Tracking

Sleep literature generally assesses consumer wearables as reasonable at estimating total sleep time but mixed at staging - useful for longitudinal self-tracking, not for polysomnography-level decisions. If you have symptoms suggesting sleep apnea, confirm it with a study rather than a device graph. Our Sleep Apnea Guide and Sleep Quality Improvement Guide cover that ground.

3.5 So How Do You Pick Sensors?

  • You only need daily activity, steps and calorie estimates → a basic PPG heart rate sensor is enough. There is little reason to pay up for higher-tier sensors.
  • You want serious training-intensity management → look at heart rate zone alerts, chest strap pairing and GPS accuracy. SpO2 is low priority.
  • You are concerned about heart rhythm → ECG and irregular-rhythm notifications can be informative, but treat them purely as something that brings a clinic visit forward. Our Atrial Fibrillation Guide gives the background.
  • You need outdoor metrics like altitude and barometric pressure → check separately for an altimeter, compass and multi-band satellite support.

4. Criterion Four: Water Resistance - 5ATM and IP68 Certify Different Things

This is the single most misread line on a product page. The two labels mean the device passed different tests; neither is a superset of the other.

RatingWhat it certifiesLimitsSuitable for
IP68 Dust ingress protection plus immersion in still water to a set depth and duration Does not assume moving water where pressure keeps changing Hand washing, rain, dropping it in a sink
5ATM Static pressure test equivalent to 50 metres of water High-speed water flow, diving and water-sport impacts are separate Swimming, snorkelling and other surface activities
IP68 + 5ATM together Both dust protection and water pressure covered Hot water, saunas and soap remain outside the guarantee General daily use including swimming

Three practical rules follow. First, if you intend to swim, treat 5ATM as your floor; an IP68-only label is not described as a swimming rating. Second, no rating certifies hot showers, saunas, soap or shampoo. Heat expands sealing components and surfactants degrade the water-resistant structure, and damage from these situations is frequently excluded from warranty coverage. Third, water resistance is a consumable property - it reflects a test at manufacture, and seals age. Rinsing with fresh water after seawater exposure is the standard advice.

5. Criterion Five: Size, Weight, Display and LTE

5.1 Size and Weight - Can You Wear It 24 Hours?

Sleep tracking means wearing the watch overnight, and at that moment case diameter and weight matter more than the feature list. Rough guidance:

  • Wrist circumference under 15cm → a case of 40 to 42mm or smaller sits properly; larger cases can overhang the wrist.
  • Wrist circumference 16 to 18cm → 44 to 46mm carries comfortably.
  • Over 50g including the strap → complaints about overnight wear become more common. If night wear is the plan, go lighter.
  • Over 12mm thick → noticeably more snagging on shirt cuffs and long sleeves.

5.2 Display and Always-On

AMOLED-family panels look excellent and read well at night but draw more power, while reflective low-power panels are easier to read in direct sunlight and kinder to battery at the cost of indoor image quality. The short version: reflective panels favour heavy outdoor training; AMOLED favours notification checking and visual appeal. AOD is a convenience feature, but as shown above it changes the premise of your battery math.

5.3 When LTE Is Actually Justified

LTE models cost more up front, add a monthly line fee and consume more battery. If none of the following three conditions applies, it is reasonable to skip it.

  1. Do you exercise without your phone (running, swimming, cycling) four or more times a month?
  2. Is standalone calling or emergency contact from the wrist a real requirement (caregiver contact, personal safety)?
  3. Do you plan to stream music or maps directly on the watch?

If all three are no, a Bluetooth and Wi-Fi model is enough. Spending the saved money on more battery or a higher water resistance rating usually delivers more satisfaction.

8. Final Pre-Purchase Checklist

  1. Do all features work on your phone OS? (Connecting is not the same as working fully.)
  2. Does quoted battery ÷ 0.6 produce the charging interval you want?
  3. Will you enable AOD? If so, did you step up one tier?
  4. Do you plan to swim? If so, is 5ATM printed on the spec sheet?
  5. Have you separated the sensors you need from the ones that are merely nice to have?
  6. Have you checked case diameter against wrist circumference, and weight against overnight wear?
  7. Does any of the three LTE conditions apply? If not, did you downgrade to non-LTE?
  8. Is the strap a standard lug width (20/22mm) or proprietary? (This changes replacement cost.)
  9. Is a software update support window published?
  10. Have you checked battery replacement and repair policy and cost?

9. Conclusion - Cut in Order and the Answer Appears

Smartwatches are hard to choose not because there are many criteria but because there is no obvious order. Impose one and it gets simple. First, cut by phone OS - a non-negotiable that removes more than half the field. Second, set your target charging interval and divide the quoted figure by 0.6 to get a real-world baseline; add headroom if AOD is on. Third, if water is in the picture, nail 5ATM as the floor. Fourth, keep only the sensors whose numbers you will genuinely look at and treat the rest as price inflators. Fifth, let overnight wear decide weight and size. By this point the shortlist is usually two or three watches, and what remains is design and budget.

One last emphasis. Health features are a weak basis for a purchase decision. Heart rate, SpO2, ECG and sleep metrics are all reference-grade trend information; they shift with measurement conditions and differ between devices. Where a smartwatch genuinely contributes to health is not precision numbers but the habit of wearing it daily and moving more. That in turn settles the buying criteria: the watch you can wear around the clock without irritation, and that never stresses you about charging, is the one you will still be using in three years. Health Features Notice: The health-related functions discussed here - heart rate, blood oxygen (SpO2), electrocardiogram (ECG) and sleep tracking - do not replace diagnosis by a medical device or clinician. Consumer wearable readings can vary with how the device is worn, ambient light and temperature, skin characteristics, exercise intensity and manufacturer algorithms, and academic literature frequently recommends interpreting them as trends rather than absolute values. If the device flags an abnormality, if unusual readings persist, or if you have symptoms such as chest pain, fainting, shortness of breath or severe palpitations, seek medical care immediately rather than relying on the device. Conversely, a normal device reading does not guarantee safety when symptoms are present. This article is general information about product selection criteria and is not medical advice.
