Opening: when two numbers on a spec sheet drift apart

Line up accelerator spec sheets by generation and one thing jumps out. The compute number multiplies. The memory bandwidth number does not keep up. The gap between the two widens every generation, and that gap is a direct measure of how many arithmetic units are sitting idle.

Here is the conclusion first. The honest way to raise bandwidth is to widen the wiring, and on a flat substrate that method has already run out of room. There is nowhere left to put more pins, pushing the rate per pin degrades the signal, and pushing both at once breaks the power budget before anything else. So one direction remains. Instead of making the wires wider, make them shorter - take what was spread out sideways and stack it upward.

The moment you stack, though, the problem does not disappear. It relocates. It moves from bandwidth to heat, yield and test access. This article follows that relocation in order: why the plane ran out, what three dimensions buy you geometrically, what you pay for it, and where the line currently sits between products you can buy and papers you can read.

Scope is narrow on purpose. This is about the wiring structure between memory and compute. Why memory prices swing is covered in DDR5 Memory Price Surge 2026 and Memory Price History 1960-2026, and pushing the same bottleneck from the software side belongs to LLM Architecture Trends 2026. Process detail stops where public disclosure stops, and everything past that line is collected in what could not be verified.

1. What the phrase bandwidth bottleneck actually means

The sentence gets used so often that its meaning has blurred. Stated precisely, it works like this.

1.1 Arithmetic intensity and the machine balance point

Whether a computation is limited by arithmetic or by memory is decided by arithmetic intensity: how many operations you perform per byte fetched from memory. The machine has a matching quantity. Divide peak compute throughput by peak memory bandwidth and you get the balance point.

machine balance point = peak compute (FLOP/s) / peak bandwidth (byte/s)

kernel intensity > balance point  ->  compute bound (the units are busy)
kernel intensity < balance point  ->  memory bound  (the units are waiting)

Each generation, the numerator grows faster than the denominator. The balance point slides to the right, and kernels that were compute bound yesterday get reclassified as memory bound today. The parts did not get worse. The reference line moved. That is the whole content of the phrase.

The workload that feels this first is token generation in large language models. With a small batch you must read essentially the entire weight set to produce a single token, and the number of operations performed per byte fetched is minuscule. Double the arithmetic throughput of such a machine and almost nothing changes in perceived speed. The concrete arithmetic behind that is worked out in Fitting a Large MoE on One GPU.

1.2 Running out of capacity is a different problem from running out of bandwidth

Conflating the two produces confused remedies. Keep them apart.

  • Capacity shortfalls are solved with money. Buy larger modules, fill the slots, add nodes. Expensive, but there is a path
  • Bandwidth shortfalls are not. Buying more parts does not widen the channel between a chip already soldered down and the processor next to it. That width is frozen the moment the package is built
  • So capacity is a purchasing decision and bandwidth is a design decision. That distinction explains why stacking is a problem that has to be solved at the component design stage
  • There are approaches that redraw the boundary between the two, such as unified memory. What that changes and what it cannot is covered in Nvidia's Unified-Memory Windows PC

1.3 Addresses are tangled for bandwidth reasons too

There is a telling piece of evidence here. When a memory controller translates a physical address into actual DRAM coordinates, it deliberately tangles the mapping. Consecutive physical addresses do not land in adjacent cells. A handful of address bits get XORed and scattered across channels, ranks and banks.

Why? Parallelism. A DRAM bank takes a minimum time to open and close. If sequential access piles onto a single bank, you are trapped inside that timing and only a fraction of the theoretical bandwidth comes out. Spread the access across banks and channels and you can be opening one while another is already busy, and channels run independently altogether. In other words, the spaghetti-like address mapping is not a design accident but a structure built on purpose to extract bandwidth.

A recently published piece of security research showed that this mapping can itself be perturbed at runtime. On a particular processor generation, flipping a bank swizzle configuration bit makes the same physical address land on different cells, so protection boundaries drawn in terms of physical addresses stop meaning anything underneath. The public repository limits its scope to an older AMD family where the registers are documented and unlocked, and states that root privilege and a kernel module are prerequisites. The interest here is not the vulnerability. It is why that tangle exists in the first place, and the fact that the industry has been willing to contort even the address scheme to squeeze out bandwidth. That in itself signals how depleted the planar toolbox has become.

2. Three reasons planar wiring cannot get wider

Wider wiring means more bandwidth. That part is true. The problem is that the ceiling arrives from three directions at once.

2.1 There is no room

Signals leave a chip through the die edge and the package balls. But grow a die and area rises with the square while perimeter rises only linearly. However much compute you pack inside, the width of the door out does not scale with it. Layer on ball pitch, substrate layer count and routing density as manufacturing realities, and you get a practical ceiling on how many signals one chip can bring out.

2.2 Going faster breaks the signal

If width is capped, speed is the next move. But as frequency rises, transmission line loss rises with it. Current crowds toward the conductor surface and effective resistance climbs, dielectric loss increases, and adjacent traces interfere with one another. One symbol smearing into the decision window of the next gets worse too.

There are fixes. Add equalizers, train the link, bolt on error correction. In exchange those circuits consume power, area and latency of their own. Parallel buses carry an extra burden: the arrival times of many wires must stay aligned, and the tolerance narrows as the rate climbs. The split of the market into narrow-and-fast GDDR style interfaces and wide-and-slow HBM style interfaces is the outcome of that trade.

2.3 Power breaks first

The third one is what bites first in practice. Pushing a signal off chip costs orders of magnitude more energy than moving it on chip. Transmission energy scales roughly with wire capacitance times voltage squared times frequency, and traces crossing a package and a board are far longer, therefore far more capacitive, than on-die routing.

In a datacenter, power is not an adjustable variable but a fixed total budget. If doubling bandwidth costs more than double the power, that power has to come out of the compute side, which erases the reason you wanted the bandwidth in the first place.

Read the right-hand column downward and the answers converge on one word: distance. All three limits derive from it. Shorten the wire and capacitance falls so power drops, loss falls so you need less compensation, and you gain access to the whole die face so width grows as well. It is one of those rare places where fixing one thing improves three.

3. What stacking actually shortens

This is where geometry enters, not as a marketing line but as an effect you can compute.

3.1 Distance and cross section improve together

Lay N elements out on a plane and the average distance between them grows roughly with the square root of N. Pack the same elements into a cube and it grows with the cube root. The gap widens as scale increases.

The more important quantity is cross section. Count the wires that can pass through a surface cutting the arrangement in half: on a plane it scales roughly with the square root of N, in a volume with N to the two-thirds power. That difference in exponent is a difference in bandwidth. What three dimensions buy is shorter distance and larger cross section, and those two together relax all three limits from the previous section.

In real implementations, a vertical connection is about as long as a die is thick. That is orders of magnitude away from crossing several centimetres of substrate. The energy to send the same bit drops sharply, and because loss is small, the compensation circuitry shrinks too.

3.2 What irregular wiring really means

Planar processing assumes a regular grid. Same rules per layer, same pitch, same direction. Go vertical and that assumption breaks.

  • Wiring density and pattern differ per layer. Memory dies and logic dies are built on different processes to begin with
  • Alignment precision differs per bonded interface. Bump-based joining and direct copper joining have tolerances an order of magnitude apart
  • Power and ground paths have to climb vertically alongside the signals. Current swings on an upper layer disturb the reference voltage of the layer below
  • Design verification tools must absorb that irregularity. Thermal, mechanical stress and power integrity have to be solved across layers together, which is not how the planar-era tool flow was built
  • In short, the hard part of three dimensions is not routing upward, it is joining things that are unlike each other

4. HBM - three dimensional and already shipping

Stacking should never be written in the future tense. It is a product you can buy today.

4.1 The structure in one paragraph

HBM stacks several DRAM dies, connects them vertically with through-silicon vias, and puts a base die at the bottom to handle the interface with the outside world. That stack sits beside the compute chip rather than on top of it, on a silicon interposer that exists purely to carry wiring. The reason it is beside and not above is the heat discussed in the next section.

The decisive part is that I/O emerges from the entire face of the die rather than its edge. That is exactly where the perimeter constraint of section 2.1 dissolves, which is what makes widths unthinkable on a flat substrate suddenly possible.

4.2 The ceiling the HBM4 standard set

JEDEC published the HBM4 standard as JESD270-4 in April 2025. The ceilings the standard permits are these.

  • Interface width of 2048 bits per stack, double the previous generation
  • Up to 8 Gb/s per pin, for up to 2 TB/s of total bandwidth per stack
  • 32 independent channels per stack, each carrying two pseudo-channels
  • Stack heights from 4-high to 16-high, with die densities of 24 Gb or 32 Gb. A 32 Gb 16-high stack gives 64 GB in one cube
  • Directed refresh management for row-hammer mitigation, and separation of the command bus from the data bus

These are ceilings the standard permits, not measurements of a product. Which vendor ships at which speed, stack height and capacity has to be read from that vendor's own announcements. Quoting a standard's maximum as if it were product performance is the single most common error in coverage of this field.

There is movement in the opposite direction at the standards level too: a specification that narrows the interface to 512 bits so that an organic substrate can replace an expensive silicon interposer. Wider is evidently not always the answer, and the trade between width, cost and packaging difficulty is now being negotiated in the specifications themselves. The detailed parameters of that spec could not be verified for this article.

4.3 What HBM has not done yet

One distinction matters a great deal. In HBM the three dimensional thing is how the dies are stacked, not the DRAM die itself. The cell array inside each die is still planar. What went vertical is the chip, not the cell.

Miss that distinction and two very different classes of article blur together: one about stacking that is already in volume production, another about standing up cells that is still research. The second is the subject of section 7.

5. Where it stops, part one: heat, because volume grows faster than surface

The same geometry that created the gain now works in reverse.

Stack devices and the heat generated grows with volume while the surface available to remove it does not keep pace. Volume goes as the cube; surface goes as the square. Heat that must escape per unit area therefore keeps climbing. The die in the middle of a stack fares worst, sandwiched between neighbours with the longest possible escape path.

5.1 DRAM is unusually sensitive to heat

DRAM holds information as charge on a cell capacitor. That charge leaks away over time, and leakage worsens sharply as temperature rises. Hence periodic refresh to top it back up, and hence standards that require more frequent refresh in the high temperature range.

Here the feedback loop closes. While a bank is refreshing it can be neither read nor written. More frequent refresh means less time available to actually move data. In other words heat claws back part of the bandwidth. You stacked to gain bandwidth, and the heat created by stacking takes some of that gain away again.

5.2 There are responses, but all of them are mitigations

  • Die thinning - grinding dies thinner shortens the thermal path. In exchange they become harder to handle and more prone to warping and cracking
  • Thermally conductive fill - raise the conductivity of the material between dies so heat can move sideways
  • Dummy structures - build pure thermal paths that carry no signal. They consume area for the privilege
  • Placement avoidance - do not sit on the hot compute die, sit beside it. This is what HBM actually does, and it is simultaneously the reason true vertical integration remains out of reach
  • Better bonding - remove the bumps and join copper directly, lowering thermal resistance. In exchange the requirements for surface flatness and cleanliness become extreme

All five help. None of them reverses the geometry. The direction - things get worse as you add layers - stays exactly as it was. Which is why the question of how many layers high you can go is not a bragging right but a thermal budget question.

6. Where it stops, part two: yield, test, and no repair

If heat is a physics problem, this one is an economics problem. And in practice this is usually what sets the product schedule.

6.1 The curse of multiplication

For a stack to work, every die inside it must be good and every bond must be good. In other words stack yield is close to the product of the individual yields. Add layers and you add factors to that product. One bad element and the whole stack is lost.

Hence the practice of screening for known good dies before bonding. The problem is that some defects cannot be screened out: those that only appear after assembly stress, at a bonded interface, or after thermal cycling are invisible to testing on a loose die.

6.2 Once bonded, you cannot see inside

A loose die can be probed directly. Bond sixteen of them together and there is no physical way to reach the one in the middle. All you have is indirect visibility through a standardized test port.

Reduced observability is not merely inconvenient. It means root-causing failures takes longer, and that is what sets the pace of a production ramp. When a new generation of parts slips, the reason is usually not that the cells could not be made but that it took too long to learn why they did not work.

6.3 You cannot repair it

DRAM has always shipped with defect tolerance built in. Spare rows and columns substitute for bad cells, and some repair remains possible even after packaging. But if a through-die electrode is severed or a bonded interface is defective, there is no spare resource to substitute. The entire stack is scrapped.

The difference in scale is the point. A defect at the wafer stage costs you one die. A defect at the final assembly stage throws away a dozen or more dies, the base die, and every process step invested up to that moment. That is why back-end yield dominates cost in this field.

7.1 Why 3D DRAM cannot simply copy the NAND playbook

NAND flash already went vertical, standing cells up and adding capacity by layer count. Doing the same in DRAM is what people mean by 3D DRAM, and it does not copy across cleanly.

A NAND cell exists to trap charge, and the longer it holds it the better. A DRAM cell is the opposite: it must fill and read quickly, and the charge on its capacitor leaks away regardless, which is why it is continually topped up. Stand that structure up and capacitor geometry, access-device leakage and layer-to-layer uniformity all arrive as fresh problems. Work is under way, but the public record stops at patents and conference papers, and no manufacturer has announced a production schedule. An intermediate step involving a denser cell structure is discussed first, and even that sits at trade-press level rather than official disclosure.

8. The other road - moving less data

The same bottleneck can be attacked from the far side: instead of widening the channel, reduce what has to be sent. There are three branches and they differ wildly in maturity.

  • Compute near the memory - put arithmetic units inside the memory die or in the base die so data is processed without travelling. There have been announcements and demonstrations, but the absence of a general-purpose software stack has held back adoption, and gains appear only for specific access patterns
  • Raise arithmetic intensity in the algorithm - enlarge the batch, fuse kernels so intermediates never touch memory, restructure caches, read at lower precision. This is the work of pushing the kernel rightward in the picture from section 1
  • Separate the capacity tier - extend memory over a dedicated interconnect. Note that this addresses capacity and sharing, not the bandwidth bottleneck. Conflating them produces disappointed expectations
  • Only the second one is available today - the others wait on a component generation or on ecosystem maturity. So when you meet a bandwidth-bound workload, the first move is always on the software side

Here is a usable criterion. If arithmetic utilization is low while memory bandwidth utilization is high, look at the kernel before you look at the parts list. If both are low, the bottleneck lives somewhere else entirely. Blaming hardware without measuring both numbers together is the most common waste in this area.

9. Where standards collide with customization - the view from Korea

Finally, what this technical shift means structurally for the industry. This is a description of technical structure, not investment advice.

9.1 DRAM was a commodity for a very long time

The defining feature of DRAM was that the specification defined the product. If the spec matched, any vendor's part dropped into the slot. Competition was therefore narrow and legible: how fine can you print, how many good dies per wafer, what does it cost. Cycles governed price and price governed profit.

9.2 Three things stacking disturbs

  1. The bottom of the stack is logic. A base die handles interfacing and control, which is logic circuitry. As generations advance there is discussion of customer requirements shaping it, which turns a memory maker from a spec taker into a co-designer alongside customer and foundry
  2. Value shifts backward in the flow. How well you bond, test and extract heat now sets cost as much as how finely you print cells. The table in section 6 is, read another way, a cost table
  3. Volume becomes tied to specific customers. When a part stops being a commodity, redirecting inventory gets harder, which removes a buffer when demand wobbles

9.3 So what changes

Read from Korea, it looks like this. Within a three-supplier memory landscape, a single competitive axis widens into several. Where rank once followed from lithography alone, how well you bond and how well you test now enter with comparable weight. Materials, equipment and test partners gain share of the value, and workforce composition broadens from cell processing toward packaging, thermal and signal design.

At the same time this direction changes the character of the risk. Back-end yields are not disclosed, qualification schedules sit with the customer, and a customized part carries higher switching cost than a standard one. This is a domain where being technically ahead and being commercially advantaged are not the same statement, and most of the information that bridges the gap is confidential. That is why this article names no winners.

For general users and domestic datacenters there is one more practical consequence. When wafer and back-end capacity concentrates on stacked memory, ordinary DRAM supply feels it. That is not a forecast but a road already travelled, and it is mapped in DDR5 Memory Price Surge 2026. Conclusion: the bottleneck does not vanish, it relocates

First, bandwidth bottleneck is ultimately a statement about wire width. On a flat substrate, widening is blocked simultaneously by room, signal quality and power. And since all three descend from distance, shortening the distance is the one intervention that fixes three problems at once.

Second, three dimensions buy shorter distance and larger cross section, and the price is heat and yield. Heat in particular claws back bandwidth through refresh, so part of what stacking gains, stacking takes back. The bottleneck did not disappear; it moved. Understanding that is what makes clear why layer count is a thermal budget question rather than a bragging right.

Third, when you read the word 3D, separate dies from cells. Stacking dies is production. Standing up cells is research. That one distinction removes most timeline confusion, and filtering out the second common error - quoting a standard's maximum as product performance - removes nearly all the rest.

One closing note. What makes this field interesting is not that one approach decisively won. Three physical constraints pull in opposing directions, and the compromise between them is recalculated every generation. Widen and power bites; stack and heat bites; move aside to dodge the heat and distance bites. When the next generation of parts is announced, check only which of those three axes was conceded and which was gained, and you will read past the marketing into what actually changed.
