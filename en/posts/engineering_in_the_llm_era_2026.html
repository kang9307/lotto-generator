Engineering in the LLM Era 2026 - How Software Development Has Fundamentally Changed

What software engineering looks like when LLMs write half the code, and where the job moved

Jun 2, 2026 Tech & IT

Software Engineering Changed Quietly, but Fundamentally

By mid-2026, software engineering has changed at the root, and almost nobody declared it loudly. There was no triumphant marketing slogan; the shift happened quietly, inside everyday workflows. The developer who was typing functions by hand last year now reads, edits, and merges code produced by tools like Claude Code, Cursor, and GitHub Copilot. Code production is undeniably faster. And yet a strange unease has spread among senior engineers, captured in a single sentence: "Code is faster, but systems seem to break faster too."

This article is an opinionated analysis of what actually changed in software engineering now that LLMs write more than half the code, written from the vantage point of someone who has rolled AI tooling across Korean SI contracts, startups, and large-enterprise modernization. This is not a "look how amazing AI is" piece. It is a concrete map of what disappears, what becomes more important, and which skills engineers must now cultivate. The central message is simple: the faster AI generates code, the more valuable the parts that are not code become.

In early June 2026, "Engineering in the LLM Era" rose to the top of GeekNews (news.hada.io), Korea's most-read developer community. That is not a coincidence. Two to three years after AI coding tools went mainstream, the industry has moved past the first wave of excitement ("the tools are fast!") into the harder second question: "so what has our job actually become?" I hope this piece is a practical answer.

Before vs After LLMs: The 5 Core Shifts

If you reduce the change to "AI writes the code now," you miss the substance. What really happened is that the center of gravity of engineering work moved in five places at once.

  • From production to verification. The old bottleneck was how fast you could write code. The new bottleneck is how fast you can be confident the generated code is correct.
  • From implementation to definition. Define clearly what to build and the implementation is largely automated. Throw vague requirements at an AI and you get plausibly wrong output.
  • From memorizing knowledge to supplying context. The value of memorizing API signatures dropped; the value of feeding the model exactly the right context rose.
  • From solo work to orchestration. One person now coordinates several AI agents and people at the same time.
  • From local optimization to system thinking. AI writes good functions but cannot own the consistency of the whole system. That responsibility concentrates on humans.

These five shifts interlock, and they share one theme: the value of what machines do well (repetitive code production) fell, while the value of what only humans can do (judgment, verification, design, orchestration) rose.

It is worth pausing on why these shifts are structural rather than temporary. Each one follows from a single property of large language models: they are extraordinarily good at producing plausible output and structurally incapable of guaranteeing correct output. Every downstream change in the engineering job is a human response to that one fact. When a machine can produce ten plausible candidates in the time it used to take a human to write one, the scarce resource is no longer production capacity — it is the judgment required to tell a correct candidate from a merely plausible one. Markets reprice scarce resources upward, and that is exactly what is happening to engineering judgment in 2026.

From Writing Code to Reviewing and Curating It

The most direct change is the engineer's daily motion. A developer's day used to begin with a blank screen, filled in one line at a time. The 2026 developer starts with a screen already filled in by AI. The work is not generation; it is review and selection.

I call this the move from author to curator. Just as a museum curator does not paint the pictures but decides what to hang and what to take down, the engineer now judges which of the AI's many options to accept and which to discard, rather than writing every line personally. This is not easier work. It demands a higher level of taste.

The heart of curation is the ability to reject. AI-suggested code usually runs. That creates a strong temptation to just accept it. But running is not the same as correct. A security assumption may be wrong, an edge case missed, the whole thing out of step with the system's patterns. The ability to say "it works, but I am not accepting it" is the defining trait of the curator-engineer.

There is a subtle psychological trap embedded here that I have watched catch even strong engineers. When you write code yourself, you carry an internal model of every decision you made, so reviewing your own logic is cheap. When you accept AI code, that internal model does not exist, and reconstructing it is expensive — often more expensive than writing the code would have been. The temptation is therefore to skip the reconstruction and trust the output, precisely because it looks finished. This is why curation is harder than authoring, not easier: authoring builds understanding as a side effect, while curation demands that you build the same understanding deliberately, against the grain of code that already appears complete. Teams that miss this distinction report feeling faster while quietly shipping code that nobody actually understands.

The 5 New Core Skills

So what should you cultivate now? Based on rolling AI tooling across five teams, these five stand out.

1. Context management

An LLM's output quality is directly proportional to the quality of its input context. Deciding what to show and what to hide, which constraints and examples to provide, determines output quality. This goes beyond "writing good prompts" into information design: selecting, from a huge codebase, exactly the context the model needs right now.

2. Verification

AI code arrives fast, so the ability to verify it quickly and rigorously sets the bottleneck. Combining test design, static analysis, and rapid manual reasoning, you must be able to decide "can I trust this code?" in a short window.

3. System thinking

AI is locally smart and globally blind. Holding the whole architecture, the module dependencies, and the data flow in your head is something AI cannot replace, and its importance has grown rather than shrunk.

4. Domain knowledge

To judge what the correct answer is, you must know the field. Without the domain rules of finance, healthcare, or commerce, you cannot catch plausibly wrong code. Domain knowledge is the baseline of verification.

5. Collaboration

The set of collaborators now includes AI agents. You need to orchestrate multiple AIs and multiple people at once, make ownership explicit, and integrate AI output into the team's shared understanding.

One caution: the left column has not become useless. The need to perform those tasks dropped, but the ability to judge whether they were done correctly is still required. You may not write the boilerplate yourself, but you must still know whether the AI's boilerplate is right.

The LLM-Era Engineering Workflow: 7 Practical Steps

Here is the workflow I actually use and recommend to other teams, in seven steps.

  1. Define the problem. Write, in one paragraph, what you are building and why. If this step is fuzzy, everything downstream is fuzzy.
  2. Prepare context. Gather the relevant code, constraints, examples, and non-functional requirements into a form the model can consume.
  3. Generate. Have the AI produce a draft. Do not expect a finished product in one shot; break it into small units.
  4. Verify. Check assumptions, edge cases, security, and system fit. Having it write tests first is a good technique.
  5. Curate. Decide what to accept and what to discard, and fix it yourself where needed.
  6. Record the context. Leave the "why" in the PR description and ADRs. AI does not leave intent behind; a human must.
  7. Integrate. Merge through team review, and feed the learned patterns back as context for the next task.
The crux is steps 1, 4, and 6. Definition (1), verification (4), and recording (6) are the human's job; generation (3) is the AI's job. Reverse those weights and you get speed but a breaking system.

Redefining Code Review: How to Review AI Code

Reviewing human-written code and AI-written code rest on different premises. With human code, the default assumption is "there is intent, but there may be a mistake." With AI code, the default is "the syntax is almost right, but the intent may be absent or wrong."

So AI code review need not spend time on style or typos. Instead, focus on:

  • Validity of assumptions. Are the assumptions baked into this code actually true in the real system?
  • Edge cases. Did the AI handle only the happy path and skip the exceptions?
  • Security. Are input validation, authorization, and secret handling safe? AI can faithfully reproduce bad patterns from its training data.
  • System fit. Does it clash with the existing patterns and conventions of our codebase?

The most important difference is that you cannot ask the author "why did you write it this way?" The AI has no intent to defend. So the reviewer must reconstruct and justify that intent themselves. This work has become the center of gravity of review.

The Shift in Testing and Quality Assurance

Paradoxically, the value of testing rose in the AI era. The faster code pours out, the more urgently you need an automatic safety net that confirms it is correct.

The approach I recommend is to treat tests as the first line of verification. Having the AI write tests before implementation turns those tests into a specification of intent and a yardstick for verifying the generated implementation. But a human must check that the AI's tests are not "fake tests written to pass easily." AI sometimes loosens tests to fit the implementation.

The center of gravity of QA also moves from after-the-fact discovery to up-front design. Mechanisms that prevent wrong code from ever merging — static analysis, type systems, contract-based tests — have grown in value, because the volume of AI-produced code is hard to absorb with human review alone.

A practical corollary: invest in making your codebase maximally legible to verification tools, because that investment now pays a double dividend. Strong types, narrow interfaces, and explicit invariants do not only catch human mistakes; they also constrain what the AI can generate in the first place. When a function signature and its contract are precise, the model has far less room to produce plausible-but-wrong code, and your static checks can reject what slips through. In other words, the more you encode your intent in machine-checkable form, the more of the verification burden you can hand back to machines — which is the only sustainable way to keep pace with machine-speed code production.

Senior vs Junior: Why the Gap Is Widening

This change does not benefit everyone equally. If anything, it works to widen the gap between seniors and juniors.

Seniors already have system thinking, verification taste, and domain judgment, so they use AI as a powerful amplifier. For someone who knows what to build and what is wrong, AI is a pair of wings. Juniors, by contrast, have lost the path of building skill by writing lots of simple code, because AI took that ladder away. AI gives them "working code," but the process of learning "why this is correct" has vanished.

Risk: if a junior develops the habit of accepting AI output without verification, they may stagnate, never developing the core skills of verification and system thinking. AI is both a shortcut to learning and a trap for it.

The remedy is to deliberately design juniors to skip the simple-production stage and jump straight to verification, system understanding, and domain learning. Left unattended by the organization, the gap widens structurally.

Changes at the Team and Organization Level

Beyond individuals, teams and organizations must change too. The first thing to break is the habit of measuring productivity by lines of code. In an era where AI mass-produces code, line counts are meaningless or even harmful. Metrics should move to outcome-centric measures like change lead time, defect rate, and recovery time.

Second, a decision-recording culture becomes mandatory. Since AI leaves no intent, humans must preserve it through ADRs (architecture decision records) and substantive PR descriptions. Organizations that neglect this fall, six months later, into the state where "the code exists but nobody knows why it was written this way."

Third, review capacity gets redistributed. As the verification burden on AI-generated code grows, the time of seniors who verify well becomes the bottleneck. Spreading verification skill across the whole team becomes an organizational task.

There is also a hiring and structure implication that few organizations have priced in yet. If juniors can no longer climb the ladder by writing simple code, and seniors are increasingly the only people who can verify what the machines produce, then a team that is structured as a thin layer of seniors over a thick layer of juniors becomes fragile in a new way: it concentrates the irreplaceable work in a few people while the replaceable work is exactly what AI now does for free. The healthier structure inverts the old assumption. Hire fewer people, invest far more in growing each of them into a verifier and system owner quickly, and treat the deliberate transfer of judgment as a first-class engineering activity rather than something that happens by osmosis. Organizations that keep optimizing for raw headcount and lines shipped will discover, around the time their senior engineers burn out, that they built a code factory with no one left who understands the factory.

Applying It in the Korean IT Landscape (SI, Startups, Enterprise)

The Korean field plays out differently by organization type.

SI (system integration)

SI sees large short-term productivity gains from AI coding, but it is also exposed to the greatest risk. In a structure with frequent handovers and staff turnover, the "intent-less code" AI mass-produces easily becomes a maintenance bomb. The more SI-like the project, the harder you must enforce decision records and verification gates.

Startups

Startups use AI most aggressively, producing big results with small teams. But if they get drunk on early speed and skip system thinking, the accumulated "theory debt" detonates all at once as the product scales. They need the balance of moving fast while still recording the key decisions.

Enterprise

Large enterprises adopt AI tools slowly due to security and regulation, but once adopted, the scale effect is large. The key is governance: which code may use AI, how to unify verification standards, and how to guarantee internal data protection. The organization that settles these clearly wins.

The Risk: Overestimating and Underestimating the Change

Two opposite errors are both dangerous here.

Overestimation: the claim that "we don't need engineers anymore" or "AI writes everything." The reality is that AI only accelerates code production; deciding what to build and why, and verifying that it is correct, is still the human's job. Overestimation leads to unverified code mass-production and system collapse.

Underestimation: dismissing the change with "AI is a toy" or "my way is faster." This leads to falling behind in the productivity gap. The gap between engineers who use AI as an amplifier and those who refuse it is already a reality.

The right stance is in between: use AI aggressively as a powerful tool, but never delegate the responsibility of judgment and verification.

One more nuance is worth naming, because it is where most overestimation hides. The impressive demos — a working app generated from a one-line prompt — are real, but they measure the wrong thing. They measure time-to-first-version, which AI genuinely collapses. They do not measure time-to-correct-version, time-to-maintainable-version, or total cost over the system's life, which are the numbers that actually decide whether a project succeeds. The gap between those two metrics is exactly the space where engineering still lives. A reasonable rule of thumb from my own projects: AI compresses the first 20 percent of the work dramatically and barely touches the last 20 percent, which is where correctness, edge cases, and integration live — and where the value usually is.

Conclusion + 3 Things You Can Apply Today

Engineering in the LLM era has shifted its center of gravity from "writing code" to "defining what and why to build, verifying what the AI made, and recording the intent." As code production gets cheaper, the uniquely human abilities of judgment, verification, design, and orchestration get more expensive. That is the core reality of 2026.

Three things you can apply right now:

  1. Make verification your first habit. When you get AI output, ask "why is this correct?" before "does it work?"
  2. Record intent. Leave one paragraph for every non-obvious decision in the PR description or an ADR. AI leaves no intent.
  3. Treat tests as the spec. Define tests before implementation and use them as the baseline for AI generation.
