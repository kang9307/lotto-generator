Why the Observability Data You Collect Never Gets Read - Logs, Metrics and Traces Answer Different Questions

Instrumenting happens once. Reading happens every day.

Aug 23, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: Forty Dashboards, Three of Them Opened During an Incident

Teams that have run observability tooling for a few years tend to share a landscape. The dashboard folder holds dozens of screens, and for most of them the only person who knows what the title means is whoever made it. When something breaks, nobody opens that folder. People open the three screens they happen to remember and write the rest of the queries on the spot.

That is a strange outcome. The data is all there. Collectors are running, the storage bill arrives every month, and instrumentation work consumed several quarters of engineering time. Yet at the exact moment the data is needed, the collected version goes unused and gets rebuilt by hand.

The usual diagnosis for this gap is either that the tooling is bad or that people are lazy. Both are wrong. This is not a question of individual habits but a predictable drift produced by the fact that collecting and using have different cost structures. Adding instrumentation has an end. Reading does not. When only the side with an end attracts budget and performance metrics, the outcome is fixed in advance.

A post that touches the adjacent problem went up recently: OTel isn't going well, and I made a spreadsheet about it, which uses repository data to ask why OpenTelemetry ships slowly. That piece is about a standard arriving late. This one looks at the floor below it: why the data already collected under a standard that did arrive is never opened.

What this article does not cover. Vendor pricing structures and product comparisons are out of scope. Which storage engine suits logs is covered in Why ClickHouse Is Winning the Observability War, and how far a free stack gets you is covered in Don't Build Your Own Monitoring. What is in scope is the step before tool selection: what you collect, why, and when you actually open it.

1. What the Original Post Actually Counted - The Bottleneck Was Not Semantic Conventions

Start with a short, fair summary of the piece that prompted this. Mat Duggan pulled 24 months of merge history from several OpenTelemetry language SDK repositories and the semantic-conventions repository, with Prometheus and Envoy alongside as a comparison set. He wanted two things: who actually performs merges, and where review stalls.

The result was sharp. Across several language SDKs, one person handled the bulk of the merges.

These figures are the author's own tally, published on his blog. The sample is the merged pull requests of each repository over the trailing 24 months, compiled in August 2026. He states plainly that he pulled them with a hacked-together Python script, so read them as an indication of orders of magnitude rather than as precise statistics.

The most interesting passage is where the author drops his own hypothesis. He began assuming the bottleneck would be semantic convention debate, and found instead that the time went into per-feature public API approval review. The slowest cases he lists are a semantic-conventions PR at 277.5 days with 115 reviews and a Python SDK PR at 361.1 days with 19 reviews. The queue is not long because review is scarce; it is long because the set of people who can approve is small.

He makes three proposals. The first is a beta tier with a guaranteed duration between experimental and stable, with twelve months given as an example. The second is to publish maintenance levels honestly per language, on the grounds that pretending every language is equally supported misleads users. The third is that one of the three among stability promises, scope and headcount has to give. Trying to hold all three is what kills the pace.

That is the original piece. It is a sound diagnosis, and the second proposal has immediate value from a user's seat. But the analysis stands on the supply side: it is a story about when a standard delivers what. The problem users meet daily sits one floor down. The standard arrived, the instrumentation went in, and the data that resulted does not get opened.

2. The Three Signals Are Not Three Shapes of One Thing - They Ask Different Questions

Introductions to observability usually call logs, metrics and traces the three pillars. That phrasing creates one misunderstanding: it sounds as though the same facts are stored in three formats. Read that way, you land naturally on the idea that since it is all there, any of them will do. It is not so.

The three differ not in what they store but in what they ask. Each signal has its data shape fixed in advance to answer a particular form of question, and that shape blocks other questions.

  • Metrics are numbers pre-aggregated along dimensions chosen in advance. The individual events are already gone at write time. They answer whether things are healthy now, when the degradation started, and how bad it is against baseline
  • Logs are descriptions of individual events. They take arbitrary fields and let you reconstruct one occurrence in full. They answer why this request failed and what values came in at the time
  • Traces record the path a request took and the time in each segment, with causality carried across service boundaries. They answer where the time went and which hop is slow

The difference is not taste but the shape of the cost curve. Ten times the traffic barely changes metric storage as long as the number of time series is unchanged. Logs grow in direct proportion. Traces grow in proportion too, then get cut back by sampling. There was never a reason for the three to share a retention period.

2.1 What to Use When

There is an order. The Google SRE book splits the question monitoring must answer into what is broken and why. The first is the symptom and the second is the cause, and the book calls that distinction one of the most important in writing monitoring with maximum signal and minimum noise. Laid over the three signals, the distinction produces a sequence.

Metrics come first. You decide here what is broken and whether users were actually affected. The four golden signals from the same book, latency, traffic, errors and saturation, belong at this stop. Traces come next. Once the blast radius is known, narrow down which segment consumed the time. Skipping this step on a request that crosses several services means ending up in someone else's service logs. Logs come last. After the segment is narrowed, open the individual events inside it. Starting from logs means changing search terms without knowing what you are looking for.

That order is also a cost order. The habit of opening logs only inside a narrowed time window is itself a query bill reduction. The reverse habit means sweeping the most expensive axis over the widest range every single time.

2.2 Pick the Wrong Signal and the Wrong Answer Arrives Quietly

Confusing the signals produces one of two outcomes. Either the bill explodes, or, worse, a wrong answer arrives looking plausible.

The exploding-bill case is easy to spot. Use logs as an aggregation axis and every query becomes a scan; once one dashboard starts sweeping a day of logs every five minutes, that screen is the most expensive object the team owns. Putting request or user identifiers into metric labels is the same story. It runs fine at first, and then one day traffic rises and the collector dies before anything else does.

The genuinely dangerous case is the other one. Counting occurrences from traces leaves no warning anywhere. Traces usually arrive sampled, so counting failures returns a number far smaller than reality, and nothing on the screen says so. The number is quietly wrong, and decisions get made on it.

3. Instrumenting Happens Once, Reading Happens Every Day

Here is the core. Data goes unread not because of anyone's diligence but because the two tasks have different natures.

Adding instrumentation has an end. A ticket opens, a PR goes up, review attaches, and the merge closes it. It counts as output and fits in a quarterly goal. Reading has no end. Yesterday's chart has to be looked at again today, and when nothing happens no artifact remains. A day when careful reading prevented an incident is a day with no record.

Organizations attach resources to work that ends. So over time the shape settles: collected volume rises monotonically while read volume stays pinned to headcount. The distance between the two lines is exactly the data that was collected and never read.

The same asymmetry lives in the code. Adding instrumentation gets reviewed; removing it gets looked at by nobody, so in practice there is no reverse gear. New services copy an existing template, and inside that template sits the instrumentation a previous team needed. Building a case for removal requires evidence that nobody uses it, and gathering that evidence is another job. What settles in is a structure where adding is an individual decision and deleting needs consensus.

3.1 Why More Dashboards Means Fewer Opened

The same asymmetry shows up worse on screens. A dashboard grows from one duplicate button and needs no approval to create. The trouble starts afterwards.

  • The name does not explain itself. A screen title carries the maker's context on that day, so six months later it is a name that only reveals itself when opened
  • One broken panel drains trust from the whole screen. A renamed metric or a dropped label leaves an empty graph, and then the numbers in the surrounding panels are suspect too. A suspect screen does not get opened during an incident
  • Duplicates slow the decision. With three similarly named screens on different time ranges and different filters, the first cost is working out which one is right
  • There is no owner. When the maker changes teams the screen leaves the maintenance set but stays in the list

The public document that states this head-on is the Google SRE book. Its line is that signals which are collected but exposed in no prebaked dashboard and used by no alert are candidates for removal. The same chapter says the rules that catch real incidents most often should be as simple, predictable and reliable as possible, and it warns that monitoring itself can become fragile, complicated to change, and a maintenance burden.

3.2 What Actually Survives

What survives in practice is not dashboards but alerts. The reason is simple: alerts hurt when they are wrong. A false page wakes someone; a missed one lets an incident grow. So they get maintained. A wrong dashboard hurts nobody. It just stops being opened.

So the screens that last are usually the ones linked from an alert. When a path of use exists, breakage is found immediately. Queries embedded in a runbook last for the same reason. Documenting where something is used makes it a maintained object the same day. Screens built because someone might want to look someday, by contrast, are abandoned without exception, because no occasion for looking was ever defined.

The bigger problem is that a new joiner cannot see this distinction at all. Live screens and dead screens sit in one list, so which ones to trust has to be learned by asking a person. That is where onboarding cost quietly rises.

4. What a Standard Solves and What It Does Not

The standard's territory needs drawing precisely, because what OpenTelemetry genuinely fixed and what was never its job get discussed as one thing.

What the standard solved is the coupling between instrumentation and backend. Changing observability vendors used to mean rewriting application code. Now instrumentation is written against a standard API and shipped over a common protocol, so swapping the backend mostly drops down to a configuration problem. When a library ships its own instrumentation, the application writes nothing. Context propagation across language boundaries is the standard's job too.

What the standard does not solve is the entire floor above it.

  • What to instrument is domain knowledge. What unit to count payment failures in, and whether a retry is a separate event, is not something a convention decides
  • How to read it is even more so. A standard fixes the shape of the data, not the question
  • What to delete is decided by nobody. Standardized data that goes unread is still just cost
  • Alert thresholds and noise management differ per service, and that is not territory a standard can enter

On top of that, saying you adopted the standard means different things in different teams. The official status page shows that maturity varies by language and signal even inside the same standard.

5. Cost - Cardinality, Retention, Sampling

If unread data were harmless the article could stop here. The reason it is not is cost, and cost arrives along three routes.

5.1 Cardinality - Adding a Label Multiplies, It Does Not Add

This is where metrics blow up most often. The official Prometheus instrumentation guidance states flatly that each labelset is an additional time series that has RAM, CPU, disk and network costs. The practical bar the same document gives is to keep the number of values per label to ten or fewer, hold labels above that to a small minority across the whole system, and look at other approaches once you pass a hundred.

  • Three labels with ten values each make a thousand combinations. One more makes ten thousand. Human intuition does not track this growth well
  • The worst case is putting effectively unbounded values into labels. Request and user identifiers are the classics, and those values need moving to logs or traces, the axes that deal in individual events
  • Paths are a particular trap. Without normalizing to a template like /orders/{id}, every order creates its own time series
  • If it already blew up, dropping the label does not end it. The old series linger for the retention period and keep queries slow

5.2 Retention and Sampling - Keep Everything and It Becomes Unusable

Giving the three signals the same retention is convenient, not correct. Metrics are small and stay useful for a long time; comparing against the same month last year needs a year of them. Logs are large and mostly consumed within days. Traces are shorter still.

For traces, the sampling strategy is the cost design. The OpenTelemetry documentation splits it in two. Head sampling decides as early as possible, which makes it easy to understand, simple to configure and efficient, but because the decision happens without seeing the whole trace it cannot guarantee that every trace containing an error is kept. Tail sampling decides after considering all or most of the spans in a trace, so it can select on error presence, latency or specific attribute values. The same document is explicit about the price.

  • Tail sampling is a stateful system. Spans must be held until a decision is made, which costs memory and nodes, and at scale it can require dozens to hundreds of them
  • As the system changes, the rules need continuous updating. This is not a configuration you write once and forget
  • The documentation also notes that effective tail sampling may be limited to specific vendor solutions. For an organization that adopted the standard for vendor neutrality, that is an irony
  • The common compromise is keeping every error and slow request while keeping the rest at a low rate. Just do not compute rates from the surviving sample

This is where the paradox in the title lands. Keep everything and it becomes unusable. More retained data means slower queries, and slower queries mean people stop opening them. The storage bill for data nobody opens keeps arriving. The decision to just keep it all looks like the safe option, but in practice it often raises cost and lowers usage at the same time.

6. What You Actually Look At During an Incident

A routine dashboard and an incident query have different purposes. The routine screen exists to confirm the normal range. You only need to see whether a handful of series hold their familiar shape, which is why a fixed screen works.

An incident needs the opposite. It needs the ability to narrow down what changed, and that question does not resolve on a fixed screen, because you have to keep splitting axes.

  • Split error rate by release version. If it clusters on the build that just shipped, the cause narrows immediately
  • Split by route or endpoint. If one path is bad rather than everything, suspect one dependency
  • Split by availability zone or node. Clustering on one side points at infrastructure
  • Split by client type or customer tier. One integration failing alone is more common than people expect
  • And splitting is only possible if that dimension was instrumented beforehand. You cannot add instrumentation mid-incident

6.1 What Can Be Prepared in Advance

Speed during an incident comes from connections laid down beforehand, not from fast hands in the moment. There is a clear set of things to do ahead of time.

  • Put in the identifier that joins the three signals. Carrying the trace identifier on every log line lets you go from an anomaly on a chart down to individual requests in one step. Without that link, the three signals sit isolated in three tools
  • Put deployments and configuration changes on the same time axis. With release times, feature flag flips and infrastructure changes drawn on the charts, the candidates for what changed narrow inside the screen
  • Save the queries used during incidents. Improvised queries vanish where they were written, so name and store the recurring ones and link them directly from the runbook and the alert body. Sparing the responder from starting with a search cuts minutes off the first move
  • Write the normal baseline down in words. When the person who knows what this metric usually reads is away, a number alone does not tell anyone whether it is abnormal
  • Alert on symptoms. Hanging an alert on each candidate cause makes noise, and a new cause that was not on the list goes uncaught anyway

7. The Other Side - What Standardization and Auto-Instrumentation Actually Delivered

Reading this far and concluding that things were better before would be the wrong conclusion. Fairness requires putting the pre-standard world next to it.

Before standardization each vendor had its own agent and its own SDK. Changing observability tools meant touching application code, and that cost functioned as a lock. Metric names and tag conventions differed per service, so dashboards could not be reused even inside one company. When languages differed, request context broke at the boundary, and stitching cross-service requests was manual every time. Adding instrumentation meant editing app code, too. Today libraries and auto-instrumentation cover a large share of that.

This is not a small improvement. The author of the piece that prompted this opens by granting that the vendor-neutrality goal itself has been achieved. What he takes issue with is pace, not direction. The comment thread mixes the same verdict on neutrality with requests for better log support and reports that satisfaction varies sharply by language.

So: the standard did its part. What remains is the space a standard was never going to fill, and what to instrument and when to open it are decisions for people who know the domain. Pinning that expectation on a standard and coming away disappointed means the expectation was pointed the wrong way.

8. Adoption Order - Decide the Questions, Then Attach the Instrumentation

Turned into a sequence, everything above converges on one principle. The question comes first, not the instrumentation. Most teams run it in reverse: install the agent, turn on the default dashboards, confirm data is arriving, and only then think about what to look at. That leaves the empty space exactly where it was.

  1. Write the questions you must answer as sentences. Five to ten is enough. Forms that resolve to yes or no work best, such as whether payment failure rate is above baseline or whether latency worsened after yesterday's release
  2. Assign a signal to each question. Rates and trends are metrics, segment latency is traces, and the reason one thing failed is logs. A question that resists assignment is a question still stated too vaguely
  3. Attach only the minimum instrumentation that signal needs. Decide the dimensions you will split by in advance and put only those into labels. Labels added because they might be useful later account for most cardinality incidents
  4. Create one saved query per question. A one-to-one binding between a question and a screen is what gives the screen an owner
  5. Alert on symptoms only. An alert per candidate cause adds noise, and noise buries the real ones
  6. Every quarter, list what nobody opened. The targets are signals exposed in no dashboard and used by no alert, plus screens with no view history. Notify the owner before deleting, and delete when there is no reply
  7. Make new instrumentation PRs state the question. If you cannot write in one line which question this instrumentation answers, it is not needed yet. That line becomes the basis for the deletion decision later

The sixth item is the genuinely hard one, because deleting carries risk and no credit. So it has to be a scheduled procedure rather than an act of individual will. Once a quarter, at a fixed time, with at least the list generation automated.

  • No public statistics on dashboard view rates turned up. The claim that nobody opens them is not a measurement but a structural explanation drawn from cost asymmetry
  • There is no industry-standard number for appropriate retention per signal. It varies with regulatory requirements and incident cadence

Conclusion: Unread Data Is Not the Same as No Data

Unread data is worse than no data. Data you never collected costs nothing and fools nobody into thinking it exists. Unread data charges storage and hands you the illusion of being prepared on top. Opening a screen mid-incident and only then learning the dimension you need was never instrumented is exactly what that illusion produces.

The post that prompted this talked about supply-side pace. The user-side problem sits elsewhere. The standard fixed the shape of the data but not the question, and data collected without a question does not get opened. Once you accept that the three signals answer different questions, the rest is simple. Write the questions first, pick the signal that fits each one, and instrument only the minimum that signal requires.

Then, once a quarter, delete what nobody opened. Growth already has plenty of momentum behind it; the side that needs to be turned into a procedure is shrinking.
