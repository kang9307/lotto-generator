Learning Linear Algebra With Interactive Figures - An Immersive Linear Algebra Study Guide

Beyond static pictures - why it's hard, why it matters, and what order to learn it in

Jul 19, 2026 Tech & IT

Intro: Why Linear Algebra, Again, Right Now

A decade-old site recently resurfaced on GeekNews (Hacker News's Korean counterpart): Immersive Linear Algebra, a free online linear algebra textbook first published in 2015. The reason a ten-year-old resource is getting attention again is, ironically, simple. In 2026, with AI and machine learning now everyday tools, the math underneath them gets invoked more often than ever - yet most people only ever brushed past that math as rigid symbols back in a college classroom.

This post isn't just an introduction to one book. It lays out, in my own view, why linear algebra matters, why this particular subject feels like a wall to so many people, what "interactive visualization" actually does for learning, and what order to work through Immersive Linear Algebra alongside resources like 3Blue1Brown and MIT's course to maximize your chances of finishing. In my Machine Learning Self-Study Roadmap, I wrote that math should be learned "as much as needed, not to perfection." This post is the follow-up that goes one level deeper into exactly that Stage 1 prerequisite - linear algebra.

1. Why Linear Algebra Matters - The Shared Language of AI, Graphics, Data, and Engineering

1.1 The Language of Data: Vectors and Matrices

Linear algebra is the math of organized groups of numbers. A vector represents a single data point; a matrix represents a collection of data points, or a transformation applied to data. Tabular data, image pixel values, text turned into embeddings - almost everything a computer processes today is ultimately stored and computed on as vectors and matrices. Knowing linear algebra means having the language to read what "shape" that data takes and how it gets transformed.

1.2 Linear Algebra Inside the AI/ML Pipeline

A neural network layer is, at its core, an input vector multiplied by a weight matrix and passed through a nonlinear function. A transformer's attention mechanism is built from dot products and matrix multiplications between query, key, and value matrices. PCA, a dimensionality-reduction technique, comes down to finding the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a covariance matrix. Recommendation systems and lightweight fine-tuning methods like LoRA use singular value decomposition (SVD) to approximate a large matrix as a product of smaller ones. There's a real gap in debugging skill and applied judgment between using these components without understanding them and understanding why they were designed that way.

1.3 The Same Language in Graphics and Engineering

Linear algebra's usefulness isn't limited to AI. In 3D graphics, rotating, scaling, and translating an object are all matrix multiplications - notably, Tomas Akenine-Möller, one of Immersive Linear Algebra's co-authors, is himself a well-known researcher in real-time rendering. Robotics coordinate transforms, signal-processing filters, structural-engineering load calculations - the fact that such different fields share the exact same mathematical language is itself a strong argument for learning it.

2. Why So Many People Struggle With Linear Algebra

2.1 Symbols and Formalism Come First

Many courses and textbooks start by listing vector-space axioms, matrix operation rules, theorems, and proofs in sequence. A first-time learner is asked to memorize symbols before ever feeling why they were defined that way. Formal rigor matters, but when it becomes the first gate you have to pass through, a lot of people lose interest right there.

2.2 Numbers Without Geometric Intuition

If you only ever learn a vector as "a list of numbers" rather than "an arrow," operations like matrix multiplication stay purely procedural. But once you can picture a vector as an arrow in space and a matrix as a transformation that stretches, rotates, and skews that space, ideas like "a matrix with determinant zero has no inverse" or "an eigenvector is a vector whose direction doesn't change" start making visual sense first. This missing geometric intuition is widely cited as the single biggest reason linear algebra feels unusually hard.

2.3 Procedure-Heavy Curricula Missing the "Why"

Practicing Gaussian elimination by hand is necessary, but if you never learn what that computation actually means - beyond "solving a system of equations," how the transformation compresses space - you end up memorizing steps without gaining applied judgment. Plenty of people pass the exam and then freeze in front of a real data problem, unsure when or how to actually use what they learned.

3. What Interactive Visualization Does for Learning - From Manipulation to Intuition

3.1 Static Pictures vs. Manipulable Figures

A textbook's static diagram shows you a single snapshot. Dragging a vector with your mouse and watching the result change in real time is an entirely different experience. When you can watch, hands-on, how changing a matrix's values stretches and rotates space, or how changing the angle between two vectors changes their dot product, the concept stops being something to memorize and becomes something to observe.

3.2 The Learning Loop: Manipulation → Intuition → Concept

This is the core value of interactive learning. You first build concrete experience by manipulating a figure - "doing this causes that." That experience accumulates into an intuition for the pattern. Only then does the formal definition and theorem naturally sit on top of that intuition. This is exactly why Immersive Linear Algebra structures each chapter to "start with a concrete, intuitive example before generalizing into formal math."

3.3 The Limits of Visualization, Stated Plainly

Visualization alone doesn't complete the learning, though. As pointed out in the related Hacker News discussion, intuition-first learning tends to treat formal proofs and generalized theorems relatively lightly. One commenter argued that practitioners mostly need "a sense of when your understanding is good enough" - but for research or deep applied work, rigorous theory still needs to be filled in separately.

4. Introducing Immersive Linear Algebra - The World's First Fully Interactive Linear Algebra Book

4.1 Overview

Immersive Linear Algebra (immersivemath.com/ila) is an online textbook released in 2015 by J. Ström, K. Åström, and T. Akenine-Möller, describing itself as "the world's first linear algebra book with fully interactive figures." It launched with four chapters and expanded over the following years into its current ten-chapter form (v1.1, ISBN 978-91-637-9354-7), including a preface. It's free to read directly in the browser, with no install or payment required.

4.2 Chapter Structure

After an Introduction, the table of contents runs through Vectors, The Dot Product, The Vector Product, Gaussian Elimination, The Matrix, Determinants, Rank, Linear Mappings, and finally Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors - a natural climb from basic vector operations toward progressively more abstract concepts.

4.3 Who It's For

It's especially useful if you're meeting linear algebra for the first time and want to build geometric intuition quickly, or if you learned it once but the "why does this work" part never quite stuck and you want it back. If what you actually want is to dig deep into formal proofs and theory, this book alone won't be enough - pair it with a full course like the one below.

5. Representative Free Resources Worth Pairing With It

5.1 3Blue1Brown - Essence of Linear Algebra

Grant Sanderson's YouTube channel 3Blue1Brown runs the free Essence of Linear Algebra series, animating vectors, linear combinations and basis, linear transformations and matrices, matrix multiplication, determinants, inverse matrices with column and null space, dot and cross products, change of basis, and eigenvalues/eigenvectors. Like Immersive Linear Algebra, it puts geometric intuition first, so the two reinforce each other well.

5.2 Gilbert Strang - MIT 18.06 Linear Algebra

MIT's legendary professor Gilbert Strang's 18.06 Linear Algebra course is freely available in full through MIT OpenCourseWare - lecture videos and problem sets included. As a full university course, it covers systems of equations, vector spaces, eigenvalues, and positive-definite matrices with proper systematic theory. Strang capped a 61-year MIT teaching career with his final 18.06 lecture in 2023, which is also on OpenCourseWare. It's the first place to go once you want to cement intuition into theory.

5.3 Khan Academy's Linear Algebra Course

Khan Academy's linear algebra course offers short lessons paired with step-by-step practice problems and instant feedback - well suited to the stage where you've already seen a concept visually and want to drill it by hand until it sticks. All three resources are free and complete enough that there's no need to substitute a paid course.

5.4 How to Combine All Three

My recommended combination: build geometric intuition for a concept first with Immersive Linear Algebra or 3Blue1Brown. Then expand the same concept into systematic theory and problems with MIT 18.06. Finally, cement the computational feel by working through problems by hand on Khan Academy. Intuition, then theory, then repeated practice.

6. What Order to Learn It In - From Vectors to SVD

6.1 Vectors, Dot Products, Cross Products

Everything starts with vectors. Picture basic operations - addition, scalar multiplication - as arrows in space, then build up the dot product (a measure of similarity between two vectors) and, in three dimensions, the cross product (which produces a new perpendicular vector).

6.2 Matrices and Gaussian Elimination

A matrix is a bundle of vectors. Learn the procedure for solving systems of equations via Gaussian elimination, and understand what matrix properties like the determinant and rank actually mean - rank, for example, describes how much information a transformation preserves.

6.3 Linear Mappings - A Matrix as a Function

This is the view of a matrix not as a mere table of numbers but as "a function that moves one space into another." Once you understand that rotation, scaling, and projection can all be represented and composed as matrices, it becomes an obvious next step to see why each layer of a neural network is expressed as a matrix.

6.4 Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

An eigenvector is a special vector whose direction doesn't change under a given transformation - only its magnitude does, by a factor called the eigenvalue. Finding the direction along which data varies the most in PCA, or analyzing the stability of a graph, both reduce to an eigenvalue/eigenvector problem.

6.5 Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) - The Natural Next Step

It's not in Immersive Linear Algebra's table of contents, but SVD is the natural next topic once you've learned eigenvalues and eigenvectors. The idea that any matrix - not just square ones - can be decomposed into a product of three special matrices underlies matrix factorization in recommendation systems, image compression, and lightweight fine-tuning methods like LoRA. It's typically covered later in MIT 18.06 or in Strang's other lectures.

7. Connecting to Your AI/ML Study Path

In the prerequisites stage of my Machine Learning Self-Study Roadmap, I wrote that for linear algebra, it's enough to "picture how vectors and matrices represent data, and roughly why eigenvalues and eigenvectors are used in dimensionality reduction like PCA." This post is the follow-up that shows you concretely how to build that picture. It also fits the roadmap's "learn it when you get stuck" strategy well - rather than trying to finish all of linear algebra up front, you can dip into the relevant chapter of Immersive Linear Algebra or 3Blue1Brown whenever a specific concept like PCA or gradient descent trips you up. Conversely, if you'd rather build a solid linear algebra foundation first, working through this post's order - vectors → matrices → linear mappings → eigenvalues → SVD - before moving on to Stage 2 (classical ML) in the roadmap is also a good path.

8. Practical Study Tips

  • Manipulate the figures a little every day - dragging vectors and changing matrix values while asking yourself "why does this happen" sticks longer than memorization.
  • Sketch it by hand, then reproduce it in code - sketch a vector or transformation on paper, then write the same operation in NumPy or a similar library, and intuition connects to computation.
  • Proofs can come later - don't try to fully grasp formal proofs on the first pass; build intuition first, then reinforce with theory when you need it.
  • Meet the same concept in multiple resources - the same idea of an eigenvector gets explained from a different angle in Immersive's figures, 3Blue1Brown's animation, and Strang's lecture. If it doesn't click the first time, meet it again somewhere else.
  • Apply it to real data right away - visualizing a simple dataset with PCA, for example, makes "why I learned this" obvious almost immediately.

Conclusion: The Most Intuitive Free Way Into Linear Algebra

As of July 2026, the barrier to entry for learning linear algebra - or reviving your intuition for it - has never been lower. Immersive Linear Algebra is a free interactive textbook you can start the moment you open a browser, and 3Blue1Brown's animations, MIT 18.06's systematic lectures, and Khan Academy's drills are all free resources you can combine at zero cost. What matters isn't finishing any single resource perfectly - it's keeping the manipulation → intuition → concept order and building up from vectors to SVD one piece at a time. Whether you work in AI and machine learning or graphics and engineering, once you hold this language, there's a lot more you can understand on top of it.
