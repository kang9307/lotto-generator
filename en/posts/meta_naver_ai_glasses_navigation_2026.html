What was announced — the core of Meta's September 29, 2026 statement

On September 29, 2026, Meta said that together with Naver it plans to bring voice-guided walking directions based on Naver Map to its AI glasses, Ray-Ban Meta and Oakley Meta, within the year. The announcement builds the feature on the real-time walking directions that Naver introduced in June of the same year. By linking that navigation to the glasses, the idea is that a person walking can be guided by voice along a route, much like a car navigation system.

The way of using it is described simply. Once the glasses are paired with the Naver Map app, the guidance comes out of the glasses' open-ear speakers. The point is that there is no need to take the smartphone out of a pocket and stare at its screen. The explanation attributed to a Meta representative in the coverage points the same way: users should be able to get to a destination safely and conveniently without the bother of repeatedly checking the phone screen while finding their way.

The announcement names three bundles of functionality.

  • Route guidance. While the user is walking, the basic direction is announced by voice.
  • Walking details. Entrance and facility information and whether stairs or escalators need to be used are announced together.
  • Voice queries. The user can call the Naver Map voice agent by voice and ask about places nearby, such as cafes or hospitals.

Two statements concern timing and order. One is the introduction timing, "within the year." The other is the order: Android is supported first and iOS is applied in stages. Section 8 of this article takes these two expressions one sentence at a time and asks how far each can be pushed. For now one thing is enough to remember. The announcement informed the public of a plan, and it contains no statement that users could switch the feature on as of the announcement date.

Korean news outlets relayed the same content with broadly the same skeleton. Only the emphasis of the headlines differs. Some put the navigation itself first, and others put first the fact that this is Meta's first cooperation with a Korean company in the AI glasses field. English-language coverage rendered Naver as the first Korean internet company to work with Meta on AI glasses. The difference in wording is taken up in section 6.

What screenless guidance changes

Smartphone walking directions are designed on the premise that the user looks at a screen. The blue dot, the route line and the distance to the next turn are all information read with the eyes. Within that structure the walker alternates between two things: looking at the road and looking at the screen. When the glasses guide by voice, the intention is to reduce that alternation. The eyes stay on the road, and the guidance arrives through the ears.

What this change actually alters can be split into several layers. The list below separates what can be read directly from the announcement from what follows structurally.

  • Where the eyes are. The effect the announcement stresses is that the phone screen no longer needs frequent checking. The basic logic is that when guidance enters through the ears, the number of times the gaze drops to the screen goes down.
  • Freedom of the hands. Looking at a screen means lifting the device, while voice guidance uses no hands. That this makes a difference when carrying bags or holding an umbrella is an inference from the structure, not an example given in the announcement.
  • The form of the information. A map spreads the whole situation across one screen, whereas a voice delivers one sentence at a time. In voice navigation, deciding what to say and what to omit becomes the center of the design.
  • Being able to ask again. A screen can be looked at again, but speech flows away. A query path equivalent to "say that again" or "where next" is therefore needed alongside. The fact that calling a voice agent was introduced together as a feature fits this need. Whether that agent also handles re-routing, however, is not stated.
  • What failure looks like. When screen guidance is wrong, the map remains and users can correct themselves. When voice-only guidance is wrong, there are few clues for correction. This is not a claim in the source but a structural difference that appears when the medium changes, which is also why a report stating that accuracy is to be refined before launch deserves attention.

That the screen disappears does not mean the map itself becomes unnecessary. The source of the guidance is still map data. What changes is the way the map is delivered to the user, and the fact that the expression moves from a map to sentences is the technical core of this announcement.

One point where voice-guidance designs commonly diverge is the timing of the announcement. Speak too early and the user misses where to turn; speak too late and they have already passed it. A screen solves this by continuously showing the remaining distance, but with voice the moment of speaking has to be chosen. The announcement does not cover the design of announcement timing or how many times a prompt repeats. Since the comparison "like a car navigation system" was used, it is natural to read it as taking the long-refined approach of car navigation as the baseline, that is, announcing ahead and then speaking once more at the point. That reading is an interpretation drawn from an analogy, not a statement about the implementation.

Walking speed is also a variable. The speed of a car makes the interval between prompts easy to predict, while walking mixes in stopping, turning around and entering buildings. Even on the same route, the moment guidance is needed differs from person to person. For that reason, how densely position estimation is refreshed directly affects perceived quality in walking voice guidance. The announcement gave no figures on this point, and there is only a report to the effect that accuracy will be refined further before launch.

Entrances, facilities, stairs and escalators — the information walking guidance handles

The additional information the announcement lists for the guidance feature is entrance information, facility information, and whether stairs or escalators need to be used. These three are kinds of information that rarely appear in car navigation. A car runs on roads, while a person walks in and out of buildings. That the source took the trouble to list these items is a clue to where the quality of walking navigation is decided.

  • Entrance information. When the destination is a large building, the point the address refers to and the door one actually enters often differ. Announcing the entrance means narrowing the definition of the arrival point from the building to the door.
  • Facility information. Facilities encountered on the way are included in the guidance. The source does not list specific categories of facilities.
  • Stairs and escalators. Knowing in advance whether a route has stairs decides the choice of route itself for people using strollers, suitcases or wheelchairs. The announcement does not specify intended users, and it does not say whether route search is provided for users with particular mobility conditions.

There is a distinction worth drawing here. "Saying that there are stairs" and "finding a route that avoids stairs" are different functions. The former is a guidance sentence and the latter is a route search condition. The announcement wrote that it announces whether stairs or escalators are used, and it did not write that it offers a stair-avoiding route option. Readers who blur the two will expect a feature that is not described.

The completeness of the information is a separate matter. Entrance and stair information can be spoken only where that information has been entered in the map data. The announcement does not say to which regions and which facilities the data has been filled in. In voice guidance, a stretch with no information is left as silence or the prompt is omitted. On a visual map a blank looks like a blank, but in voice the blank does not show itself. The risk of mistaking the absence of guidance for "there is no problem" is the delicate part of voice navigation.

Open-ear speakers and voice queries — when both input and output are a voice

The announcement wrote that guidance comes out of the glasses' open-ear speakers. Open-ear means the ear is not blocked. Unlike earphones it does not seal the ear canal, so surrounding sound comes through as it is. In the scene of listening to directions while walking, this is a decisive condition, because the listener has to be able to hear traffic and the people around. That the announcement specified this approach as the output path fits the description of the glasses as a device that both guides and listens to the surroundings.

At the same time, an open speaker comes with structural limits. When the surroundings are loud the guidance can be drowned out, and in quiet places it can be overheard. The source does not address this problem, so the actual level of performance is outside the scope of what it says. That it is a variable to be tuned in design is, however, a general fact that follows from the form of the device.

The input side is also a voice. The announcement wrote that the Naver Map voice agent is called by voice. The glasses then become a device that talks with the user through only a microphone and a speaker instead of a screen. The questions that arise in this configuration can be split up.

  • How it is called. What the call phrase is, and how the glasses' own AI assistant and the Naver Map voice agent divide roles, is not stated.
  • The range of queries. The examples given are nearby cafes and hospitals. Whether other categories of places or business information can be asked about in the same way lies beyond the examples.
  • Delivery of results. If place search results are read out by voice, how many places are read in what order becomes the crux. With a screen one can skim a list, but voice is sequential.
  • What happens next. The announcement does not say whether there is a flow for setting a place found through a query directly as the destination.
  • Language. The reports do not state which languages are supported. It is easy to guess that Korean would be the default for a Naver Map based service, but that is not a statement from the source.

The three preceding sections all return to the same point. When sentences take over what the screen did, the amount of information that can be conveyed at one time and the way gaps in the information become visible both change. How well this difference is designed decides the quality of the feature. This announcement did not release the result of that design and only signaled the direction the design aims at.

From advertising to everyday services — scope of the partnership and who says "first Korean company"

The announcement describes this cooperation as a widening of the existing cooperation between Naver and Meta. English-language coverage reported that the two companies already cooperate through ADVoost, Naver's AI-based advertising solution. The solution was introduced as combining Naver's commerce and shopping data with Meta's AI targeting technology. The GeekNews summary likewise organized it as a flow that goes beyond an advertising solution and expands into everyday services. The move from advertising to navigation reads as the nature of the cooperation widening from the data and advertising domain into a service domain that users employ every day.

The word "first" has to be read by separating its attribution. The expression appears in several places, but the wording varies.

  • The Korean announcement and Korean reports wrote Naver as the first Korean company Meta cooperates with in the AI glasses field.
  • English-language coverage rendered it as the first Korean internet company to cooperate with Meta on AI glasses. A qualifier about the type of business is attached.
  • The GeekNews summary shortened it to the first Korean company to cooperate on AI glasses. In the shortening, the subject of the cooperation (the company Meta works with) and the business qualifier both dropped out.

The three statements point to the same fact but differ in strength. This article attributes the expression to the Meta announcement and the reports relaying it, and does not use it as a fact independently verified by the author. "First" is a claim carrying a scope condition, so its truth can flip depending on the baseline (all Korean companies, internet companies, or an official partnership). It is safer not to generalize beyond the scope the announcement itself took as its premise.

One line on the origin of the Meta glasses themselves is also worth adding. English-language coverage explained that Ray-Ban Meta and Oakley Meta were made through cooperation between Meta and the eyewear group EssilorLuxottica. The parties to this announcement are Meta and Naver, and statements about the eyewear manufacturing side fall outside its scope.

Set beside display glasses and map screens

Smart glasses divide broadly into two lines: display glasses that put text on the lens, and devices that listen and speak through a camera, microphone and speaker. That distinction was covered in section 6 of the earlier article Even Realities G2 review, titled "Two branches of the smart glasses market - camera type versus display type". This article does not explain the distinction again and uses it only to place this announcement on top of it.

The experience written into this announcement is guidance heard through open-ear speakers and voice queries. There is no statement that a route line is shown on the lens. In other words this feature moves navigation from sight to hearing. The starting point differs from display glasses, where navigation appears as an arrow on the lens. Meanwhile the announcement names two target devices, Ray-Ban Meta and Oakley Meta, and does not say which specific models are included under those names or whether models with a built-in display are included.

Another article that overlaps is A Map Is Not a Copy of the World. Its section 5, "Navigation is not a map", covered the point that navigation does not show the map as it is but re-expresses it to the extent a moving person needs. Voice navigation can be seen as a case where that re-expression goes one step further, because it does not trim the map on a screen but converts it into words altogether. This viewpoint is an interpretation and not content of the announcement.

  • Expressive medium. A screen map conveys the whole space, while voice guidance conveys the single next action.
  • Distribution of attention. A screen demands the eyes and a voice demands hearing. Which interferes less while walking depends on the environment of use.
  • Persistence of information. A screen stays and a voice passes. A way to hear it again has to be built into the design.
  • Device requirements. Because voice guidance works without a separate display, the device requirements are simple, which is a structural advantage. This too is not something the announcement claimed.

How to read "within the year" and "Android first" — disclosed scope and open questions

The remaining questions can be summarized as follows. They do not point out defects in the announcement. They are items that cannot be answered from statements made at the announcement stage.

  • When. At what point within the year, and with which versions of the app and the glasses software, will it be provided.
  • Where. Is it limited to Korea, or can it be used in other regions where Naver Map is offered.
  • On what. Which specific models among the target devices, what firmware requirements, and what Naver Map app version.
  • How much. What is the range of regions and facilities where entrance and stair information is filled in, and how does guidance behave when information is missing.
  • In which language. What are the languages of the voice guidance and the voice agent responses.
  • Under what terms. What notices exist on extra costs, account linking, and the handling of personal information such as location.

Location is an input that navigation inherently requires. In a structure where the glasses connect to the Naver Map app, to whom and in what form location information is passed is among the first things a user should check. The announcement has no statement on this, so the terms of use and privacy notice at launch become the things to check.

In the end, what can be read from this announcement is a direction. It is a direction of taking Naver Map walking directions out of the screen and moving them into the voice of the glasses, and of speaking walking information such as entrances and stairs along that route. When and how far that direction is completed as a product will be decided by the launch notices still to come.

What could not be confirmed

  • The original text of a separate Naver press release. Only the Meta announcement and the reports relaying it were cross-checked, and how Naver worded the feature in its own notice was not opened.
  • The exact sentences of the quoted remark in the full Meta announcement. Wording differs slightly from outlet to outlet, so this article conveys only the gist.
  • Release date, staged order of provision, regions and languages. The announcement has no statement.
  • What "Android first" refers to. Whether it is Naver Map walking directions themselves or the glasses integration is worded differently in different reports.
  • The specific models of the target devices and whether display models are included.
  • The range of regions and facilities for which entrance and stair information is provided.
  • Real-world usage experience and accuracy after introduction. There are no reports because it has not launched.
