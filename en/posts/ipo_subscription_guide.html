Korea IPO Subscription Guide - Allocation, Deposits, Refunds and Risks

How Korea's retail IPO subscription rules actually work, updated for July 2026

Jul 28, 2026 Finance & Economy

Introduction: IPO subscription is a rulebook, not a lottery ticket

There is no shortage of writing about subscribing to Korean IPOs, and a surprising share of it still describes rules that no longer exist. Advice such as put in a bigger deposit and you will get more shares dates from before equal allocation existed. Spread your application across several brokerages dates from before the duplicate-subscription ban. The famous first-day double-then-limit-up pattern refers to an opening mechanism that the exchange retired years ago. Since 2020, Korea has revised allocation methods, duplicate subscription, listing-day price limits and institutional lock-up rules in sequence, and further changes are working through the system in 2026. Acting on an outdated guide produces miscalculations that cost real money.

This article approaches the subject from the angle of how money and shares move under the rules, rather than how to get more shares. A single subscription sits at the intersection of at least four distinct rule sets: how the issuer and underwriter set the price (book building), how the total offering is split among investor categories (allocation quotas), how the retail slice is divided among individuals (equal and proportional methods), and how the price behaves after listing (reference price and price limits). Understand them separately and the three most common questions answer themselves: why did I get zero shares, why did the final price land above the indicative band, and why did the stock fall from the opening bell.

One caveat deserves to come first. An IPO share is a risk asset that can trade below its offering price after listing. The IPO and delisting reform package published by Korea's Financial Services Commission in January 2025 noted that institutional investors were net sellers on the listing day in 74 of the 77 companies that listed in 2024, roughly 96 percent. The reform effort began precisely because prices tended to spike on day one and drift down afterwards. This article promises no returns. Its purpose is to state the rules accurately and to point at the structures that carry elevated risk.

Everything here concerns the initial public offering system of the Republic of Korea. It applies to companies listing on the Korea Exchange's KOSPI and KOSDAQ markets, and the allocation mechanics differ substantially from IPO subscription rules in the United States, Japan or elsewhere.

1. The full timeline - from book building to listing day

The moment a retail investor presses the subscribe button falls in the middle of the process. Filing of the registration statement and institutional book building come before it; allocation, refund, payment and listing come after. Different information becomes public at each stage, so simply knowing what is visible when improves the quality of the decision.

StageTypical timingWhat happensWhat to check

Institutional book buildingShortly before subscription, over several daysInstitutions submit price, quantity and lock-up commitmentsWait for the result disclosure
Final price set, amended filingRight after book building closesThe offering price is fixed; demand and lock-up ratios publishedWhere in the band it landed; lock-up ratio
Retail subscriptionUsually two business daysDeposits are paid in under the equal and proportional methodsSubscription limit, fee, minimum lot

Refund and paymentCustomarily two business days after the closeUnused deposit returned, allocated shares paid forRefund account and actual credit

The timings above are customary practice, not values fixed by regulation. Subscription hours, minimum lot sizes and the moment allocation results appear vary by brokerage and by deal. Exact dates for a given company are printed on the cover of its registration statement and in the underwriter's notices, so treat this table as a skeleton and confirm the real calendar in the source documents.

2. Who gets how much - the allocation quotas

The entire offering does not flow to individuals. Article 9 of the Regulation on Underwriting of Securities, an industry rule administered by the Korea Financial Investment Association, sets quotas by investor category. The retail slice, formally the general subscriber category, is only one part of it.

Investor categoryQuotaNotes
Employee stock ownership association20 percent of the offeringMandatory for KOSPI listings; permitted for KOSDAQ and KONEX
General subscribers (retail)At least 25 percentThe slice individuals apply for
Transfer of unsubscribed employee sharesUp to 5 percent of the offeringAvailable when the employee association takes less than 20 percent
High-yield high-risk investment trustsAt least 5 percent of the offeringSo-called high-yield funds
Venture company investment trustsAt least 30 percent of the offeringFor KOSDAQ listings
Institutional investorsWhatever remainsAllocated among book-building participants

A frequent misreading needs correcting here. The 25 percent retail quota is a floor, not a ceiling. When the employee association under-subscribes, up to five percentage points can shift to retail, so the effective retail share often lands somewhere between 25 and 30 percent. Just as important, that quota applies to the offering as a whole. The pool at the single brokerage where you applied is far smaller, because the retail slice is divided again among the members of the underwriting syndicate. That is precisely why subscription competition ratios are published per brokerage rather than for the deal as a whole.

2.1 Why competition ratios differ between brokerages

The lead manager and each co-underwriter take different amounts of stock, and each firm has a different customer base. In the same deal, brokerage A might print a ratio of 2,000 to 1 while brokerage B prints 500 to 1. The catch is that these numbers are only final once subscription has closed, so nobody knows them for certain while the window is open. Live ratios on the final day are an indicator at best, and late swings are routine.

3. Equal versus proportional allocation, with a worked example

For companies that first filed a registration statement on or after 1 December 2020, at least 50 percent of the shares allocated to general subscribers must be distributed by the equal method under Article 9(11) of the Regulation on Underwriting of Securities. The remainder follows the older proportional method. The two operate on completely different logic.

  • Equal method: every subscriber who pays at least the minimum deposit receives an equal chance of allocation. Paying a larger deposit does not enlarge this portion.
  • Proportional method: shares are distributed in proportion to the quantity applied for, and therefore to the deposit paid. More applied for means more allocated.
  • Post hoc adjustment: if one method is under-subscribed while the other has excess demand, the shortfall may be shifted across.

3.1 A worked example with explicit assumptions

The numbers below are hypothetical and used only to show the structure. They do not describe any real offering.

Two lessons fall out of this. First, equal allocation does not guarantee a single share. When applications outnumber the equal pool, one share each is arithmetically impossible and a lottery decides, which is why zero-share results are ordinary in popular deals. Second, a proportional share costs more than people expect. At a ratio of 1,000 to 1, one share requires an application for 1,000 shares, and at a 50 percent deposit rate that locks up 10 million won. That money is unavailable for anything else until the refund date, which is a real opportunity cost.

One more detail. Proportional allocation produces fractional remainders, and the way those odd lots are handled can differ by brokerage. It is unwise to assume that 0.9 of a share automatically rounds up to one.

4. The duplicate-subscription ban - the rule since 2021

Applying to the same deal through several brokerages to improve the odds used to work. It no longer does. According to the Financial Services Commission, duplicate allocation to retail investors has been restricted for companies that first filed a securities registration statement on or after 20 June 2021, with the legal basis in Article 68(5)4-2 of the Enforcement Decree of the Financial Investment Services and Capital Markets Act. The restriction remains in force as of July 2026.

  • You cannot subscribe simultaneously through accounts at different brokerage firms for the same offering.
  • Where duplicate applications exist, only the one received first is eligible for allocation.
  • Brokerages carry a duty to check for duplicate applications; failing to check, or allocating on a duplicate, counts as an unsound business practice.
  • The verification system was announced as being built and operated by the Korea Securities Finance Corporation.
  • The employee stock ownership association keeps its allocation rights unchanged.

The practical consequence is straightforward: when several firms are underwriting, you must pick one at the outset. There is no moving to the less crowded brokerage once ratios become visible. Since subscription limits, which some firms tier by customer grade, and subscription fees differ between firms, both are worth checking in each firm's notices before the window opens.

Subscribe only through an account in your own name. Borrowing a family member's or acquaintance's account, or effectively operating a minor child's account yourself in order to enlarge an allocation, can breach Korea's Act on Real Name Financial Transactions and Guarantee of Secrecy and other laws. This article does not describe such methods, and discusses only conduct permitted within the rules.

5. Deposits, refunds and payment - how the cash moves

The cash sequence in a Korean IPO subscription runs deposit paid, shares allocated, refund and payment settled. Here are the points where people most often go wrong.

5.1 The subscription deposit

  • It is money placed with the brokerage in advance, functioning as a good-faith deposit on the application.
  • A 50 percent deposit rate is widely used for ordinary IPOs, but this is not a figure fixed in law. Some products, including special purpose acquisition companies, have been offered at 100 percent, so always check the terms for the specific deal.
  • The deposit is locked up for the duration. The opportunity cost of that cash is the real cost of subscribing.
  • As of July 2026, Korean regulators have announced that they are pursuing the payment of interest on subscription deposits, but the timing, mechanism and rate are not settled. Historical practice has been that no interest is paid.

5.2 Refund date and payment date

  • Customarily two business days after the subscription closing date serves as both the refund date and the payment date for allocated shares.
  • The refund equals the deposit minus allocated shares times the offering price, minus any subscription fee.
  • With zero shares allocated, the entire deposit less the fee comes back.
  • A subscription window late in the week pushes the refund across a weekend, so count business days rather than calendar days when planning cash flow.
  • An incorrectly designated refund account can delay the credit, so it is worth re-checking the account on the subscription screen.

5.3 Subscription fees

Online does not mean free. Brokerages charge a per-application fee, and both the amount and the waiver criteria, such as customer grade or assets held, differ from firm to firm. This article neither compares nor recommends particular brokerages. What matters is knowing that fee structures differ, because when the allocation is small, as it typically is under the equal method, the fee weighs relatively more heavily on the outcome. Exact amounts should be read from each firm's current notice.

6. Book building and lock-ups - the core of the 2025 to 2026 reform

The offering price is not a fair value blessed by the regulator. The underwriter runs a book build among institutional investors, collecting price, quantity and lock-up intentions, and the final price is agreed with the issuer on that basis. What incentives institutions face therefore feeds directly into both the offering price and the post-listing trajectory.

On 21 January 2025 the Financial Services Commission, together with the Financial Supervisory Service, the Korea Exchange and other bodies, published the IPO and delisting reform package. The diagnosis was blunt: a market dominated by short-term arbitrage overheats book building and produces prices that spike on listing day and then decline. The 74 of 77 companies with institutional net selling on listing day in 2024 figure cited earlier came from that document. The main planks are below.

Reform itemContentEffective
Tougher sanctions for breaking commitmentsPenalties for violating lock-up commitments made effective1 April 2025
Priority allocation to committed institutionsA minimum share of the institutional tranche reserved for institutions that commit to lock-ups1 July 2025
Priority allocation ratio30 percent in the first period (July to December 2025), rising to 40 percent from 2026Phased increase
Stricter book-building eligibilityRegistration period and assets under management requirements applied to private fund and discretionary managers1 July 2025
Cornerstone investors and pre-soundingCapital markets act amendment passed the National Assembly on 23 April 2026Six months after promulgation

6.1 What a mandatory lock-up commitment is

It is a promise by a book-building participant not to sell allocated shares for a set period, typically offered in tranches of 15 days, one month, three months or six months, with priority allocation going to those who commit. For an individual, the reason this matters is simple. A high commitment ratio means a larger portion of the institutional stock is locked away from the immediate post-listing market, and a low ratio means the opposite. Commitment ratios and their distribution by tenor are disclosed in the amended registration statement and prospectus after book building closes.

6.2 Stricter book-building eligibility

Under the FSC announcement, private fund managers and discretionary investment managers must now satisfy either registration for two years plus 5 billion won in entrusted assets, or 30 billion won in entrusted assets to take part in book building. The aim is to curb the practice of small houses bidding aggressively purely to secure stock.

6.3 Cornerstone investors - the 2026 variable

The capital markets act amendment that cleared the National Assembly on 23 April 2026 introduces cornerstone investors and pre-deal sounding. A cornerstone investor is an institution that receives an advance allocation in exchange for a lock-up of at least six months. According to the FSC, that allocation comes out of the institutional tranche rather than the retail quota, so it does not shrink what individuals can apply for. Pre-sounding is meant to gauge demand before the indicative band is set, improving the rationality of price discovery. The amendment takes effect six months after promulgation, with subordinate rules being prepared. In other words, the structure of Korean offerings may shift again in the second half of 2026. Check FSC announcements for the exact commencement date and detailed criteria.

7. Listing-day price limits - the 60 percent floor matters as much as the 400 percent ceiling

A Korea Exchange rule change effective 26 June 2023 altered how newly listed stocks are priced. Previously the exchange collected pre-opening orders and set an opening price within 90 to 200 percent of the offering price, and that opening price became the reference. Now the offering price itself is the listing-day reference price with no separate opening auction, and the intraday limit is 60 to 400 percent of that reference.

  • A stock priced at 20,000 won can trade anywhere between 12,000 and 80,000 won on day one.
  • The change means the old combination of a doubled opening price followed by a limit-up move, a phrase still repeated in Korean retail circles, no longer exists as a mechanism.
  • The 400 percent ceiling is widely known; the 60 percent floor is mentioned far less. A 40 percent loss on the first day is possible within the rules.
  • A wide limit band means high volatility, which is not the same thing as a high probability of gains.

Whether this remains current is time-sensitive. The text above rests on verification that the 60 to 400 percent band was in force as of July 2026, but exchange rules can be amended, so confirm against Korea Exchange rules and your brokerage's notices before trading.

8. What to check in the securities registration statement

The registration statement is free to anyone on Korea's DART electronic disclosure system. It runs to hundreds of pages, which is daunting, but the items a retail subscriber genuinely needs are limited. Working through them in this order is efficient.

  1. General information on the offering - the split between newly issued shares and existing shares being sold. Proceeds from existing-share sales go to selling shareholders, not into the company.
  2. Use of proceeds - whether the money funds facilities and research or repays debt and covers working capital. Ask whether the use connects to growth.
  3. Underwriter's valuation opinion - the basis for the offering price. It names the comparable companies, the multiples such as price-to-earnings, and the discount applied.
  4. Suitability of the comparable set - whether the businesses genuinely resemble the issuer. Including firms of very different scale or profitability can inflate the multiple.
  5. Use of forecast earnings - if valuation rests on projected future profit rather than realised results, ask whether those assumptions are attainable.
  6. Total share count - the proportion freely tradable immediately after listing. The larger it is, the greater the potential selling pressure early on.
  7. Shareholders and lock-up schedule - who is restricted, how much, and until when. Watch for expiries that cluster on one date.
  8. Investment risk factors - business, company and other risks. The core risks section is where the issuer itself sets out loss scenarios.
  9. Financial statements and audit opinion - revenue and operating profit trends, any going-concern language, and the scale of related-party transactions.
  10. Amended filings - a revised statement appears whenever the band or structure changes. Always read the latest version, and treat repeated amendments as information in themselves.
  11. Book-building results - where in the band the price settled, how many institutions took part, and the lock-up commitment ratio.
  12. Subscription terms - minimum lot, subscription limit, deposit rate, refund date and expected listing date.

The Korea Exchange's KIND disclosure portal carries listing-related filings as well. Reading both systems lets you cross-check the offering calendar against listing review information.

9. Risk signals - structures worth a second look

The items below are not predictions that a stock will fall. They mark places where selling pressure or valuation strain can build within the offering structure, and no specific company is named. The judgement remains yours.

  • An unusually large existing-share component - much of the proceeds go to selling shareholders rather than the company. Distinguish between raising capital for the business and providing an exit for incumbents.
  • A strong financial-investor exit flavour - if pre-IPO institutional backers are cashing out heavily through the offering, look at what remains and when they can sell it.
  • Short lock-ups or clustered expiries - stock unlocking all at once one or three months after listing creates a supply event on a known date.
  • A large free float from day one - the mirror image of the point above: little of the stock is restricted.
  • A final price above the top of the indicative band - a possible sign of overheated book building. Exceeding the band is not improper, but it raises the valuation hurdle.
  • A low lock-up commitment ratio - it can be read as institutions themselves declining to promise a holding period.
  • An arbitrary comparable set - inserting richly valued companies with weak business similarity pushes the derived price up.
  • Valuation built on forecast earnings - a structure that discounts profits not yet earned is fragile when the assumptions break.
  • Multiple amended filings - the disclosure needed correcting, so read the stated reasons for each amendment.
  • Proceeds largely earmarked for debt repayment - balance sheet repair may have been more urgent than growth investment.

The converse is equally true: a high commitment ratio and a small free float do not guarantee that a stock rises. Supply and demand is one factor among many, and after listing the direction of the broader market and the sector environment act on the price as well.

10. Selling on listing day - factors to weigh

This section does not recommend a selling point. Neither sell on day one nor hold is offered as advice. What can usefully be set down is the list of things worth confirming before deciding.

  • Did you set a rule before subscribing - judgement wobbles once the price starts moving. Deciding in advance what you will do under which conditions tends to help.
  • Have you accounted for the lower limit - the 60 percent floor on day one means a 40 percent loss is possible within the rules.
  • Institutional behaviour on listing day - on FSC figures, institutions were net sellers on listing day in the large majority of 2024 listings. That is a statistic, not a forecast for any single deal.
  • The lock-up expiry calendar - when additional stock becomes sellable.
  • Transaction costs - selling incurs securities transaction tax and brokerage commission, and the smaller the allocation, the larger their proportional bite.
  • Order type - on a volatile day a market order can fill far from the expected price, and a volatility interruption can halt trading temporarily.
  • What holding actually means - continuing to hold after listing day is no longer IPO subscription; it is ordinary equity investment, and the analytical framework has to change accordingly.
  • Your cash plan - if the money is needed soon, reconsider whether it belongs in a high-volatility asset.

11. Tax - what is taxed and what is not

Taxes around IPO shares fall into three groups: capital gains, transaction tax and dividends. Korean tax law changes often, so the summary below is stated as of July 2026 and should be confirmed with the National Tax Service or a tax professional before filing.

ItemTreatment and rateBasis and notes
Gains on listed Korean shares, small shareholdersIn principle not taxedOn-exchange disposals by individuals outside the large shareholder definition
Gains on listed Korean shares, large shareholders20 percent up to a 300 million won base, 25 percent aboveIncome Tax Act Article 104; separate rates apply in cases such as holding under one year

Securities transaction tax on saleRaised for trades from 1 January 2026KOSPI trades add a rural development surtax on top of the transaction tax
Dividend income14 percent withheld plus 1.4 percent local income tax, 15.4 percent in totalIncome Tax Act Article 129(1)2(b)

Several points deserve expansion. First, the overwhelming majority of retail subscribers are small shareholders, so a profitable sale after listing generally does not trigger capital gains tax. Second, the holding-value threshold in the large shareholder test has been raised and revisited repeatedly in recent years; in September 2025 the Ministry of Economy and Finance announced it would keep the existing scope, but the figure can change again through tax legislation, so anyone who might fall within it must confirm the current threshold with the National Tax Service. Third, securities transaction tax rates were increased for trades from 1 January 2026 and are composed differently by market, meaning the cost of selling a KOSPI-listed stock and a KOSDAQ-listed stock is not necessarily identical; confirm exact rates with the National Tax Service and your brokerage. Fourth, a separate taxation regime for qualifying dividends is reported to apply from 2026, but the eligibility conditions are detailed and individual application should be checked with the tax authority.

12. Eight common misconceptions

  1. Equal allocation guarantees at least one share - it does not. When applications exceed the equal pool a lottery intervenes and zero is a normal outcome.
  2. A bigger deposit increases the equal allocation too - it does not. Deposit size affects only the proportional portion.
  3. Splitting across brokerages yields more - since June 2021 duplicate subscription has been restricted and only the first application received is allocated.
  4. The offering price is a fair value approved by regulators - it is not. It is negotiated between underwriter and issuer using book-building results, and it warrants no market value.
  5. Stocks always rise on the first day - the rules allow a fall to 60 percent of the offering price, and trading below the offering price after listing is not rare.
  6. The old double-then-limit-up pattern is still available - the June 2023 reference price and price limit reform removed the opening mechanism it depended on.
  7. Subscription fees are the same everywhere - they vary by firm and customer grade, and matter more when the allocation is small.
  8. Subscribing to a SPAC is the same thing - it is not. A SPAC is a shell searching for a merger target, governed by separate escrow and merger rules. See the SPAC investing guide for detail.

13. Final checklist before subscribing

The items below gather what to verify immediately before applying. This list does not encourage subscribing to anything; it is a check for someone who has already decided to apply and wants to avoid the common omissions.

  • Have you read the latest amended filing on DART
  • Have you quantified the existing-share portion of the offering
  • Have you read the comparables, multiples and discount behind the price
  • Where in the indicative band did the final price settle
  • Have you checked the lock-up commitment ratio and its distribution by tenor
  • Have you checked the free float at listing and the lock-up expiry calendar
  • Have you read the core investment risks to the end
  • Have you confirmed the brokerage's subscription limit, minimum lot and deposit rate
  • Have you confirmed that firm's subscription fee and waiver terms
  • Have you planned for the cash being locked up until the refund date
  • Given the duplicate ban, have you chosen a single firm
  • Have you set a rule for what you will do after listing
  • Is the amount one you can lose without disrupting your life

Conclusion - knowing the rules calibrates expectations

Treat IPO subscription as a lucky-dip and every result feels arbitrary. Treat it as a system of rules and expectations become accurate. Three things compress what this article covered.

First, the ceiling on what you can receive is already fixed by rule. The retail quota is at least 25 percent of the offering, at least half of that is distributed equally, the equal portion is indifferent to deposit size, and duplicate subscription is blocked. Those four sentences answer most versions of why did I get so little.

Second, the risks are written in the registration statement, not revealed after listing. The existing-share proportion, the free float, the lock-up expiry calendar, the comparable set and the discount are all public before subscription closes. Much of what surprises investors afterwards was legible beforehand. Conversely, without reading it, the question of why the stock fell has no meaningful answer.

Third, the system keeps moving. Equal allocation in 2020, the duplicate ban in 2021, the reference price and price limit overhaul in 2023, lock-up priority allocation in 2025 and cornerstone investors in 2026 have reshaped the framework repeatedly in five years, and interest on subscription deposits is under discussion. This is a field where old guides go stale quickly. Before acting, re-check the primary sources: the Financial Services Commission, the Financial Supervisory Service, the Korea Exchange and the Korea Financial Investment Association.

Finally, allocation is not an end in itself. From the moment shares are allocated they are stock whose price moves in both directions, an asset that includes the possibility of loss. After listing, the analytical frame has to shift to company analysis.
