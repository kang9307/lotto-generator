Overseas Funds and How the Tax Works - The Account Sets the Tax, the Structure Sets the Cost

What differs from a domestic product, and where to check a rule that keeps changing

Opening: same index, and yet the tax is said to be different

You are comparing two products holding much the same assets, and someone says it. The tax is different on that one. And usually the explanation stops there. What differs, and how, does not survive the conversation. Only a vague sense that something is different does.

That is where a common misreading starts: the idea that tax is attached to the product. The actual structure does not work that way. Checking it shows that two products holding the same thing fall under different rules depending on which account holds them and what kind of legal vessel the product is. So there are two choices to make, not one. What to hold, and where to hold it.

This article is not an argument for investing in overseas funds. It is an argument for knowing what differs from a domestic product before going in. And most of what differs sits in tax and cost, which are set not by the return chart on page one but by the type of account and the structure of the product.

One more thing. This article keeps what was verified separate from what was not. The most dangerous move in a tax article is not a wrong number, it is writing an unverified thing as though it were settled. Items where a primary page was opened, items where only an official body's wording was seen quoted, and items that could not be confirmed at all are split into a table. Section 9 is that table.

Introductions to the asset classes themselves are not repeated here. Bonds are in bond investing basics, the structural comparison of index products is in index funds versus ETFs, and the nature of dividends is in dividend stock investing. This one digs into a single point: the structure, cost and tax handling of overseas funds.

1. The phrase covers more than one thing

The same words point to at least two different arrangements. Without this distinction first, everything after it blurs.

  • A fund established in Korea that invests abroad. An asset manager created it under Korean law, and what it holds sits overseas. Domestic vessel, foreign contents.
  • An offshore fund, established under foreign law. The vessel itself was made abroad. The contents are usually overseas as well.

Move to tax and the reason this matters appears. Opening the tax authority guidance page on the basis date showed three stated requirements for something to count as a collective investment vehicle: that it be a collective investment vehicle under the Financial Investment Services and Capital Markets Act, that it settle and distribute at least once a year, and that it take in money and return money.

The sentence that follows is the decisive one. The page carries wording to the effect that a trust established outside Korea is regarded as a collective investment vehicle even where it does not meet those requirements. Being a vessel built offshore, in other words, is not a route out of this framework.

Verification grade A. Those three requirements and the sentence about offshore-established trusts were confirmed by opening the tax authority guidance page directly on 31 July 2026. Note that a guidance page is a summary, and an actual determination turns on the statute and the specific facts. The treatment of a particular product must be confirmed with the distributor and a tax professional.

The same page yielded one more thing. It lists items not included when computing the gain, and on the basis date there were three.

Before carrying the familiar domestic instinct that trading gains do not get counted across to foreign assets, ask what the provision covers.

2. Where you hold it changes the tax

If you take one section from this article, take this one. Decide the account before choosing the product, because holding the same thing in a different account puts it under different rules.

The two ISA provisions are different in kind. One is a tax-free allowance and the other is deduction of losses. The statute states that losses arising are deducted when computing the aggregate of interest income and the like. That is the structural break from an ordinary account, and section 7 returns to it.

A common misreading on the pension side is worth naming. A tax credit is better understood as the timing and method of calculation moving, rather than tax disappearing. And this article did not confirm the treatment when money is taken out. Judging on the entry-side figures alone means seeing half the picture. The Korean accounts themselves are covered in the ISA account and pension savings and IRP.

Why the account comes first. Each account carries a contribution ceiling, a minimum holding period and a treatment for early withdrawal. Those constraints are harder to undo than a product choice. You can switch products, but a three-year contract requirement can only be met by time passing. Hence the order: account, then product.

3. How it is taxed - dividend income, withholding, and a threshold

In an ordinary account the chain runs like this. A gain arises in the fund, that gain is classified as dividend income, the financial institution withholds at source, and once that year's interest and dividend income together pass a threshold the amount is aggregated with other income.

Quoting figures here needs care, so what was confirmed on the basis date is set out by strength of verification.

ItemWhat was confirmed (basis 2026-07-31)Grade
Income category of a fund gainClassified as dividend incomeA tax authority guidance page opened directly
Withholding rate on dividend income14 out of 100 under the withholding rate provision of the Income Tax ActB official wording seen quoted, statute page not opened
Aggregation thresholdGlobal taxation once interest and dividend income together exceed 20 million won a year; below that, withholding settles the liabilityB tax authority wording seen quoted, page not opened
Local income tax surchargeCommonly described as applying on top, but no provision was confirmedC needs verification

Marking grades B and C serves a purpose. These figures appear on every blog, which makes them feel settled. But repetition is not evidence of accuracy. Rates and threshold amounts in particular are subject to amendment, so using them for an actual filing calls for checking the provision and its effective date on the law information centre.

What matters operationally is the nature of the threshold. Below it, the institution withholds and you are done. Cross it and you move into a world where the amount is computed together with your other income, and the answer then varies from person to person by income bracket. The filing itself is covered in the global income tax return.

If one sentence survives: do not look only at what the fund earned, look at your total financial income for the year. The threshold attaches to a person, not to a product.

4. Putting it on the same line as direct shareholding gets it wrong

The most frequent error deserves its own section. Buying and selling foreign shares directly and holding them through an overseas fund fall into different income categories. So carrying a rule from one side to the other produces a wrong answer.

So do not file on the strength of that column, open the tax authority guidance yourself. What this article can state firmly is not a number but the fact that the two regimes live in different boxes.

Why that matters in practice: the two things people most often carry across are the basic deduction and loss treatment, and neither can be carried. Applying a 2.5 million won figure to fund gains throws the whole calculation out, and assuming a loss will simply offset something needs its own basis. The Korean capital gains regime is covered in capital gains tax.

5. Currency - hedged and unhedged pick different risks

Hold a foreign asset and performance splits in two. One part is the price of the asset itself, the other is the value of the currency. Neither can be removed. So the product designer decides in advance how to handle the second.

  • Hedged. Designed to reduce the effect of currency movement. A choice to concentrate on the underlying asset's performance.
  • Unhedged. Currency movement sits directly on top of performance. Asset price and exchange rate move together.
  • Hedging is not free. It costs. Who bears that cost and in what form differs by product, so it has to be read in the document.
  • The hedge ratio may not be 100 percent. Partial hedging exists as a design. Reading a hedged class as unrelated to currency is not accurate.

Here is what this article cannot say. It does not say which side is better. That answer depends on the future path of exchange rates, which is forecasting. What is on offer here is the difference in structure, nothing more.

Grade C - currency gains and tax. How a gain arising from currency movement enters the tax base was not confirmed against a primary source in this research. This article does not fill an unverified gap with an estimate. If your decision needs it, ask the tax authority with the product type and account specified, or consult a tax professional. For a feel for the converted amount only, there is a currency calculator.

6. Cost - the headline expense ratio is not all of it

Cost is easier to verify than tax, because it is written down. The reason it still gets missed is that it is not summarised into one number.

How to read it: what leaves each year is not the headline ratio alone but that ratio plus other expenses plus brokerage costs, with sales and redemption charges sitting on top. Lining up headline ratios alone therefore understates the real drag.

The other thing to check without fail is the class code. Funds sharing a name can differ in distribution fee and charge structure by class. A comparison that skipped the class is not a comparison. Two identical names side by side may be two different products.

Grade C - the association disclosure screens. Fund-level fees and costs are said to be published on the industry association's electronic disclosure service, but in this research the site body did not render and its menu structure could not be confirmed. So no specific menu path is given here. What can be stated is that the cost items appear in the prospectus and management report the distributor provides, and both documents can be requested without opening an account.

To see a cost stretched over time, an interest calculator gives a rough feel. It only shows how a fixed rate compounds over years and does not compute the actual burden of any particular product.

7. When there is a loss - the edges of offsetting

Loss treatment has different edges in each regime. Those edges are one of the real reasons an account choice matters.

  • Confirmed for ISA. The statute provides, in effect, that losses arising are deducted when computing the aggregate of interest income and the like. Gains and losses are put together inside the account. (grade A)
  • Described for direct shareholding. Guidance describes netting capital losses and gains on domestic and foreign shares in the same tax year when filing. (grade B, primary page not opened)
  • Between funds in an ordinary account? Whether gains and losses across different funds can be offset was not confirmed in this research. (grade C, needs verification)

Put those three lines side by side and the point emerges. A loss does not offset something automatically. Whether it offsets is decided by which account it arose in and under which income category. So the expectation that last year's loss will lighten this year's bill needs a basis before it holds.

And a word on why the unconfirmed item is left as a blank. Loss offsetting is an item where the amounts diverge sharply. One sentence filled in by estimate here could turn into a real loss. So this article leaves the blank a blank. Whoever needs it filled should take their own account records to a tax office.

8. Redemption and the NAV date - not the price on the day you asked

This is the part that feels strangest to someone who has only handled domestic products. The calculation is not made at the price on the day you request redemption. The request date, the date whose net asset value applies, and the day the money arrives are three different dates.

The reason is simple. The assets the fund holds sit in a market in another time zone. While that market is closed the price of the asset is not fixed. So the procedure defers to the market's calendar.

  • The request date and the applicable NAV date differ. By how many days varies by product and is written in the prospectus.
  • The payment date is later still, because settlement follows once the NAV is fixed.
  • Overseas market holidays push the schedule out. Planning off the domestic calendar alone goes wrong; local and domestic holidays do not line up.
  • Price movement in the interval is outside the requester's control. The number on screen when you submit does not determine the outcome.
  • It interacts with the redemption fee window. Check which date the holding period counts from, or a difference of days can flip you into a different fee band.

Grade C - legal basis for the NAV date. The primary text setting the gap between request date and applicable NAV date was not confirmed in this research. So this article gives no specific number of days. That number differs by product, and the place to check is that product's prospectus and fund rules. It is an item to read off your own document, not to memorise as a general rule.

10. How to verify it yourself

What outlasts knowledge is the verification route. Rates and limits get amended; where you check them rarely changes. In order.

10.1 In the product documents

  • Established in Korea or abroad - the distinction in section 1
  • Target regions and assets, and the weight of each
  • Whether hedged, the hedge ratio, and how the hedging cost is borne
  • The annual total expense ratio and its components - management, distribution, trustee, administration
  • Other expenses and brokerage costs - the items outside the ratio
  • The class code and how charges differ by class
  • Whether the sales charge is taken on entry or exit
  • The redemption fee period and rate, and the date the period counts from
  • The applicable NAV date and payment date relative to the request
  • How a request during an overseas market holiday is handled
  • The settlement and distribution cycle
  • The basis date of the tax notes inside the document - it may be an old document
  • The risk grade and the maximum-loss description
  • How often the management report comes and where to read it

10.2 Where to ask about tax

  • For statute text, effective dates and amendment history, look the provision up by number on the law information centre. Checking amendment history matters because a summary page returned by a search may be on an old basis.
  • Guidance and forms by tax type are on the tax authority website.
  • Questions about your own situation belong at the tax authority helpdesk. Ask with your account and income structure specified, not in general terms.
  • Filing and payment happen on the national tax portal.
  • If the amounts are large or several income types are tangled, handing it to a tax professional is the cheaper form of safety.

10.3 Three habits when verifying

  • Write the basis date next to the number. A figure copied alone leaves no way to tell when it applied.
  • Distinguish the tier of the source. Statute, official guidance, a summary article and a product advertisement do not weigh the same.
  • Leave what you could not verify blank. A cell filled by estimate later reads as a confirmed fact.

Compressed into one sentence: most of what differs between an overseas fund and a domestic product is structure, not return. And structure is written down, so reading verifies it. Return is not written down and never will be.

12. Closing - account, structure, and the route to check

Three things are enough to keep.

  • The account sets the tax, not the product. An ordinary account, an ISA and a pension account sit under different rules, and the constraints attached to an account are harder to undo than a product choice.
  • Cost is not one number. Outside the headline ratio sit other expenses and brokerage costs, and above them sit sales and redemption charges. A comparison that skipped the class code does not stand.
  • Rules change, so the route to check outlives the knowledge. A rate memorised today becomes wrong information once amended, but the procedure of looking up a provision and its effective date keeps working.

And finally, why the unconfirmed items were left as a table. Tax articles usually read smoothly, without gaps. It is worth asking where that smoothness came from. Whether it is smooth because everything was verified, or smooth because the unverified cells were filled with plausible sentences, is not visible from the outside. The only defence a reader has is to ask for the source and the basis date. Ask this article the same question.

Frequently asked questions

How does an overseas fund differ from holding foreign shares directly, for tax?

Korean rules, checked on the basis date. The income category itself differs. Opening the tax authority guidance showed that gains from a collective investment vehicle are treated as dividend income, while profit from trading shares yourself is described as capital gains. That splits the collection method, who files, and the deduction rules. The figures on the direct-holding side were not confirmed on a primary page, so open the tax authority guidance before filing.

What changes if an overseas fund is held inside an ISA?

Article 91-18 of the Restriction of Special Taxation Act, read on the basis date, provides that interest and dividend income in the account is free of income tax up to a tax-free allowance of 2 million won, rising to 4 million won where conditions are met. The excess is taxed at 9 out of 100 and is not added to the global income tax base. Losses are deducted when computing the aggregate. The term is at least 3 years and the total ceiling is 100 million won. Whether a given product can be held is a question for the distributor.

Does holding a fund in a pension account make the tax go away?

Read it as timing and method moving, not tax disappearing. Article 59-3 of the Income Tax Act gives a credit rate of 15% where global income is 45 million won or less (or total salary of 55 million won or less for salary-only earners) and 12% otherwise. Guidance cites limits of 6 million won for pension savings and 9 million won counting retirement pension together. The withdrawal-stage rate was not confirmed here. Judging on the entry side alone is half the picture.

Should I choose a hedged or an unhedged share class?

This article does not say which is better, only what differs structurally. A hedged class is designed to reduce the effect of currency movement; an unhedged class lets it sit on top of performance. Hedging carries a cost, and the hedge ratio may not be 100 percent. The three things to check are whether it is hedged, the ratio, and how the cost is borne, all in the prospectus. How currency gains are taxed was not confirmed here.

Where do I check what a fund costs?

Three places. The prospectus for the headline expense ratio and its components, the sales charge and the redemption fee. The management report for other expenses and brokerage costs. And the class code, without which a comparison does not stand. Stopping at the headline ratio understates the real drag: what leaves each year is that ratio plus other expenses plus brokerage costs.

What the grades mean. A means the primary page was opened directly on the basis date. B means an official body's wording was seen quoted but the primary page was not opened. C means it could not be confirmed and was left blank. Do not file on grade B or C items, verify them yourself.
