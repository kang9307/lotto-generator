Korea's HWP Document Format in the AI Era — Accessibility and Open Standards

How a proprietary binary format that dominated Korean public administration for 30 years is hitting a wall in an age of AI data pipelines

2026-06-24 Tech

Introduction: The Document AI Cannot Read

As of June 2026, a significant portion of official documents produced by Korean public institutions still uses the HWP (Hangul Word Processor) format. Citizen petition forms, policy reports, official public notices — information that directly affects people's lives — are locked inside a proprietary binary file that cannot be fully opened without specific software. This structure took shape in the early 1990s when the Hangul word processor, developed by Hancom, aligned with government policies to protect the domestic software industry and became the de facto standard for public administration.

Now, the AI era is applying new pressure to this long-standing structure. In a time when large language models (LLMs) and automation pipelines treat documents as raw material, a format that machines struggle to parse has graduated from a mere inconvenience to a genuine data accessibility barrier. That is precisely why Korea's National AI Strategy Committee held an emergency session and the Ministry of the Interior and Safety issued formal policy. This article analyzes the specific challenges HWP faces in the AI era and examines the government's transition policy and international open standard alternatives in a balanced way. This is a fact-based analysis at the level of format and policy, not criticism of any particular company.

1. Historical Context — How HWP Came to Dominate Korean Public Administration

The HWP format is the native save format of the "Arae-a Hangul" word processor, released by Hancom in 1990. Amid a government policy climate favoring domestic software, its superior Korean-language processing capabilities quickly penetrated public administration, education, and media. Even as Microsoft Office became the global norm in the 2000s, Korean public institutions maintained HWP. This was not simple software inertia. It reflected the way decades of approval workflows, DRM systems, EDMS (Electronic Document Management Systems), and vast archives of legacy documents had been built around HWP. Switching the format would mean redesigning the entire administrative infrastructure.

1.1 Technical Characteristics of the Format

HWP is a proprietary binary format. Its internal structure is built on streams and records; extracting content requires reverse-engineering the format specification or using dedicated libraries. Hancom has released parts of the format specification since the 2010s, but the version-by-version structural variations and rendering dependencies have made complete third-party implementation elusive. Notably, line breaks are tied to the width metrics of Korean-specific fonts, meaning a font change can cause the entire layout to shift — a structural vulnerability that grounds the criticism that "the moment rendering fails, the right to edit an official document is tied to a specific piece of software."

1.2 Entanglement With the Public Administrative Ecosystem

The problem extends beyond the format itself. Public administrative infrastructure — the Onnara system, the unified civil servant email system, and others — was designed with HWP as the assumed format. Approval flows, digital signatures, and document retention regulations all operate around HWP. This systemic dependency means "just switch the word processor" is not a viable option. A format change is necessarily a redesign of administrative infrastructure.

2. Structural Constraints the AI Era Has Exposed

Before AI penetrated everyday work, HWP's format problems were managed as an interoperability nuisance. But as LLM-based analysis, summarization, and RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) pipelines have been introduced, this nuisance has been elevated to a technical barrier.

2.1 The AI Parsing and Training Data Wall

HWP files could not be submitted directly to major LLMs like ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini — until OpenAI announced direct HWP and HWPX support in April 2026. For public institutions trying to deploy AI summarization or analysis tools, documents in HWP format require a conversion step first, during which structural information and metadata can be lost. From a training data perspective, the situation is equally limiting. Korea's public documents are a primary source of policy, administrative, and legal data, but HWP makes it difficult to use this data in a machine-readable form. The fact that a significant portion of the data on Korea's public data portal is in HWP format is a clear inefficiency from an AI utilization standpoint.

2.2 International Collaboration and Interoperability

HWP is a format that is virtually unused outside Korea. When collaborating with foreign companies or research institutions, the other party needs to install HancomOffice or a dedicated viewer to open HWP files. This creates unnecessary friction in exchanging documents on international projects and naturally requires re-conversion to PDF or DOCX formats. Formatting, formulas, and tables often do not transfer cleanly in the process. A public document system that relies on a locally-used format rather than international standards places a subtle brake on the speed at which Korea integrates into the global digital economy.

2.3 Accessibility for People With Disabilities

Document accessibility for people with disabilities deserves a separate examination. Screen readers used by visually impaired users need to read the logical structure of documents — heading hierarchies, tables, alternative text, and so on. PDF/UA, EPUB Accessibility, and ODF-based documents have well-established specifications for compliance with accessibility standards (WCAG, ISO 14289). HWP file accessibility structuring, by contrast, is poorly standardized, and compatibility with assistive technologies falls short of major international formats. When public administrative documents are locked to a specific format, the accessibility limitations of that format become the accessibility limitations of public services themselves.

3. The Government's Policy Response — From HWP to HWPX

From 2026, the Korean government began addressing this problem in earnest. The data subcommittee of the National AI Strategy Committee formally acknowledged HWP's AI-unfriendly nature and laid out a transition roadmap.

3.1 Transition Schedule and Scope

Starting May 18, 2026, the Ministry of the Interior and Safety began restricting HWP file attachments in the Onnara government administrative system, with expansion to local governments underway. From October 2026, HWP attachments will be fully blocked in the unified civil servant email and Onmail systems. The Ministry is working with Hancom to display automatic conversion pop-ups when editing existing HWP files, nudging users toward HWPX. Vice Chairman Im Moon-young of the National AI Strategy Committee stated that the committee will "definitively implement the changes for public-sector data innovation in the AI era in cooperation with relevant ministries."

3.2 What Is HWPX?

HWPX is a format following OWPML (Open Word-Processor Markup Language), designated as Korean national standard KS X 6101. Its internal structure consists of XML files bundled in a ZIP container, similar to DOCX or ODF. The core difference from HWP is transparency. Because it is XML, reading content with a text editor, parsing with scripts, and connecting to AI pipelines is significantly easier than with HWP. The advantage of HWPX in AI training data processing and RAG integration stems from this structural difference.

3.3 Practical Challenges of the Transition

However, there is a gap between policy announcements and field implementation. The decades of HWP legacy documents cannot be converted in the short term. Redesigning approval, DRM, and EDMS systems to be HWPX-based carries significant cost and time. Hangul's unique font metrics and layout features are not perfectly reproduced in the conversion process, creating rendering differences that generate resistance in the field. Particularly for official document templates and civil petition forms with complex formatting, layout breaks after conversion create additional burdens that must be manually corrected.

4. The International Open Document Standard Landscape — ODF, OOXML, and Korea's Position

While transitioning to HWPX is the pragmatic choice for Korea, it does not mean full entry into international standards. Understanding the global open document standard landscape clarifies Korea's position.

4.1 ODF — The Genuinely Vendor-Neutral Standard

ODF (Open Document Format), ISO/IEC 26300, is a fully open format that any vendor can implement without patents or proprietary technology. LibreOffice, Apache OpenOffice, and other open-source software adopt it as their native format. Several European government agencies and Brazil have adopted ODF as the public document standard. ODF's advantages are no vendor dependency, excellent long-term preservation characteristics, and a well-defined accessibility structure.

4.2 OOXML — The Practical Mainstream With Ongoing Debate

OOXML (Office Open XML), ISO/IEC 29500, is rooted in Microsoft Office. DOCX, XLSX, and PPTX are all OOXML formats. It is the most widely used XML-based format in office environments worldwide, but debates persist about whether it qualifies as "truly open" due to implementation complexity and dependencies on vendor-specific features (VBA, legacy compatibility tags, etc.). In practice, however, OOXML has an overwhelming advantage in real-world interoperability and ecosystem breadth.

4.3 Where HWPX Fits

Where does HWPX sit in this landscape? It is a Korean national standard (KS X 6101), XML-based, and its structure is publicly available — all of which make it far more open than the old HWP binary. However, it is not an ISO/IEC international standard, and there is virtually no ecosystem for it outside Korea. Third-party tools that can process HWPX are very limited, and it remains difficult for international collaborators to open HWPX files directly. To be straightforward: the HWPX transition is a shift from a closed domestic format to an open domestic format — not an entry into the international standard ecosystem. For international interoperability, further transition to ODF or OOXML would still be required.

5. AI Companies' Response — Commercial Choices on Format Support

Meanwhile, AI companies have been expanding their HWP support, recognizing the scale and importance of the Korean market.

5.1 ChatGPT's Direct HWP Support

In April 2026, OpenAI announced that ChatGPT directly supports HWP and HWPX files. Reflecting the practical needs of Korean users, it is now possible to upload HWP files directly for analysis, summarization, and translation. Google Gemini is also moving toward expanding HWP support. However, this response is closer to a temporary workaround than a fundamental solution. The fact that an AI company adds format support does not resolve the structural openness problem of public data. A commercial service being able to read HWP and HWP files having standardized machine-readability are separate matters.

5.2 The Emergence of MCP Tools

In the developer community, open-source HWP-MCP (Model Context Protocol) tools have emerged that provide a bridge enabling LLMs like Claude to read HWP and HWPX files directly. While this ecosystem response has value, deploying such tools at ordinary public institutions without technical capacity is not realistic in practice. The resolution of format problems must occur at the format specification layer, not the tooling layer.

6. My Analysis — What Are the Real Challenges?

Here is my synthesis after researching this issue. There are several layers to this problem, and the discussion becomes muddied without distinguishing them.

6.1 HWP to HWPX Is a Necessary First Step, Not the Final Destination

The government's HWPX transition policy points in the right direction. Changing from a binary proprietary format to an XML-based structure alone brings real improvement in AI pipeline integration and data utilization. However, the fact that HWPX is a format used almost exclusively in Korea still leaves constraints in terms of international collaboration and interoperability. If the transition to international standards (ODF or OOXML) is not discussed in parallel over the long term, HWPX could occupy the same place that HWP does today a decade from now.

6.2 Ecosystem Transition Is Harder Than Format Transition

Changing a technical standard is far harder than changing the workflows, habits, and expertise built on top of it. Changing the practice of civil servants who have spent decades creating approvals, reports, and notices in HWP cannot be done with a single policy announcement. Electronic document solution companies like Synapsoft are strengthening their HWPX support, but redesigning approval systems, DRM, and EDMS to be HWPX-based comes at significant cost and time. The transition policy's success depends less on the technical standard specification and more on conversion tools, training, and legacy document handling guidelines.

6.3 Accessibility Challenges Must Be Treated as a Separate Track

The accessibility problem for people with disabilities must be addressed on a separate track from the AI pipeline problem. The fact that HWPX is XML-based does not automatically guarantee an accessibility structure (alternative text, heading hierarchies, logical reading order). Accessibility requires the trinity of format specification, content authoring guidelines, accessibility feature implementation in document creation tools, and compatibility verification with assistive technologies — all working together. If public institutions do not address accessibility requirements during the HWPX transition, the same problems will recur in the new format.

6.4 Domestic Software Protection and Open Standards Are Not in Conflict

In discussions about HWP, the framing of "domestic software protection vs. open standards" often appears. This binary is not useful. There is nothing preventing Hancom from building a word processor that fully supports international standards (ODF or OOXML). Software competitiveness should come from user experience, specialized Korean language processing features, and service quality — not from a proprietary format. Format openness does not conflict with software competitiveness. Competing on a better product built on open formats is the healthier long-term direction.

7. Conclusion — The Speed and Depth of Transition Both Matter

As of June 2026, Korea is pushing a public document transition from HWP to HWPX in earnest. This change is necessary and right for the AI era. But as this analysis has shown, the transition involves challenges at multiple levels.

First, HWPX is significantly better than HWP in machine-readability and AI integration, but remains limited in international interoperability. Second, changing the ecosystem (systems, practices, people) is far more difficult and time-consuming than changing the format. Third, disability accessibility issues require independent standards and verification frameworks separate from format transition policy. Fourth, the ultimate goal should not be HWPX, but full interoperability built on international standards.

For Korea's digital governance to integrate with the world in the AI era, document format openness is a prerequisite, not an option. The HWPX transition is meaningful as a first step. But the conversation about connecting to the international standard ecosystem — not stopping there — needs to begin now, in parallel.
