The Occasion — A Post of October 2026, a Ruling of December 2025

The piece introduced on GeekNews is Treachery in the Rodin Museum 3D Scan Verdict, which Cosmo Wenman, the plaintiff in this case, posted on his Substack on September 28, 2026. It opens with René Magritte's 1929 painting The Treachery of Images and refers to that painting in the text. The piece deals with the decision in which France's highest administrative court, the Conseil d'État (Council of State), did not grant a request for release of 3D scans held by the Rodin Museum.

The timing has to be stated precisely here. The decision was issued in December 2025. Commentaries by two law firms give decision number 487950 and the date December 23, 2025, while the file name of the copy of the decision published by the plaintiff side begins with 20251224. Both notations are given as they stand. By contrast, the post that drew attention is dated September 28, 2026, the GeekNews registration is October 4, 2026, and an online discussion the plaintiff takes part in (COMMUNIA Salon) is announced for October 13, 2026, from 14:00 to 15:00 Central European Summer Time. The ruling is not new. It became known again, about nine months after it was issued, through a party's post and an event.

The case is often introduced through the question of whether a museum must release 3D scans of public domain works. The question the court actually dealt with is narrower. It is whether scan files made by the museum are administrative documents that can be requested through France's administrative document access regime. That distinction is the axis of this whole explainer. Five questions are taken up below: what was requested and how the case proceeded, in what wording the court decided, what that wording does not say, how each source reads the same decision, and how a reader should keep the pieces apart when reading this ruling.

Which Files Were at Issue — Raw Data and Derived Files

The subject of the dispute was not one kind of file. A law firm commentary explains that the museum campaign produced two layers of files. One is the raw data, a set of three-dimensional coordinates (a point cloud, nuage de points) obtained from high-resolution scans, and the other is the derived files for visualization made from that data. The commentaries use different names for them.

According to the commentaries, the appeal in cassation at the Council of State stage was brought by the plaintiff, and the parts contested were the part closed without a ruling and the refusal concerning the raw data (the point cloud). The Council of State rejected the appeal. The commentaries contain no statement that the museum appealed the lower court's decision on the derived files, and the only appeal in cassation the decision records is the plaintiff's. The plaintiff side's post, on the other hand, writes of the museum and the ministry that "they did not appeal it, they ignored it" about the lower court's order. That is the plaintiff side's assertion, and the commentaries do not address it. This point connects to the difference in readings in section 7 below.

The commentaries also give the substance of the part closed without a ruling. According to a law firm commentary, during the proceedings the museum supplied the complete list of scanned works and all documents relating to the digitization campaign, including its correspondence with the chair of the Higher Council for Literary and Artistic Property, and the lower court cited this in closing part of the request. The commentary's explanation is that the Council of State held this assessment to be within the trial court's sovereign discretion and sufficiently reasoned.

There is one more thing to separate. The plaintiff side's post writes that point clouds are files in open plaintext formats commonly used in the cultural heritage field, and states that the museum told the court the scans could not be used or were incomplete. These are the claims of the parties to the dispute, and the commentaries do not say which party's explanation the court accepted as fact.

The Wording the Council of State Used — The Definition of Administrative Documents and the Collections

The framework of the decision as quoted in the commentaries is two provisions and one sentence.

  • The definition of administrative documents. Article L.300-2 of the Code of relations between the public and the administration (CRPA) treats as administrative documents those produced or received, in the course of their public service mission, by the State, local authorities and other public law persons or private law persons entrusted with such a mission. The provision as quoted in a commentary gives as examples files, reports, studies, minutes, statistics, instructions, correspondence, opinions, source code and decisions.
  • The status of the collections. Article L.451-5 of the Heritage Code provides that the property making up the collections of museums of France that belong to a public body forms part of that body's public domain (domaine public) and is therefore inalienable. According to the commentary, the Rodin Museum falls in the category of museums of France.
  • The key sentence. Three commentaries quote the same sentence. It says that les œuvres appartenant aux collections du musée Rodin, non plus que leur reproduction, même numérique, are not administrative documents. In English this can be rendered as: the works belonging to the collections of the Rodin Museum, nor their reproduction, even digital, are not administrative documents (a translation made here).

One law firm's commentary summarizes the logic of this sentence as follows. However technically sophisticated a digital reproduction is, it does not detach itself legally from the work, and whether it is a point cloud or a textured model, the reproduction remains inseparable from the original. The same commentary says the Council of State took this view only for the collection items and their digital reproductions, and that it drew a distinction under which files produced in the museum's administrative activity and distinct from the work can be administrative documents. The commentary does not explain in detail how far that last distinction extends. Another law firm's commentary summarizes the decision as separating documents that an administration produces or receives in the course of its public service mission from works in the sense of the Intellectual Property Code. The same commentary adds that the wording of the provision defining documents appears broad, so the decision may raise questions.

The sentence on the consequence, as quoted in the legal press commentary, runs as follows. These works and reproductions therefore cannot be supplied to anyone who requests them under the freedom of access to administrative documents implemented by the provisions of the Code. This wording shows that the framework of the decision is limited to the scope of the freedom of information regime.

Two Meanings of Public Domain — Copyright and Public Property

In the English-language material introducing the case, public domain ordinarily refers to the state of a work whose copyright protection has ended. The plaintiff side's post calls Rodin's sculptures cultural heritage works of this kind, and the question in the COMMUNIA announcement is whether 3D scans of works in the public domain should be freely available for reuse.

The domaine public of Heritage Code article L.451-5, cited as a ground of the decision, by contrast, refers to a legal category of property owned by public bodies in French law, and collection items in that category have the effect of being inalienable. Once translated, the same word looks like the same thing, but in the quoted context of the commentaries the two concepts are words at different levels.

  • Public domain in the copyright sense: the state in which the copyright term of a work has ended and anyone may use it. It was not cited as a ground of the decision.
  • Public domain in the property law sense: the category to which museum collections owned by public bodies belong. It was cited as the basis on which the decision treated the collection items and their reproductions as not being administrative documents.

There is one reason this distinction is needed. The intuition that the work is out of copyright and so the scan should also be available is at a different level from the question the ruling dealt with. The ruling neither denied nor granted that intuition; it answered within the scope of the freedom of information regime. General copyright concepts are covered in the copyright basics guide, and whether Korean copyright rules bear on this case has no basis in the material, so it is not judged.

What the Ruling Did Not Decide

Within the material consulted, the ruling dealt with one question: whether reproductions of museum collection items are administrative documents under the freedom of information code. The following are items that were not identified as subjects of the ruling.

  • Voluntary release or open policy by the museum. Nothing is written to the effect that the ruling prohibits or permits the museum releasing the scans of its own accord.
  • Conditions of reuse. Whether released material can be used commercially is not the question of this ruling. The COMMUNIA question includes reuse, but it does not appear in the wording of the ruling.
  • Copyright. A decision on the copyright status of the works was not identified.
  • Other museums. The legal press commentary points out that, because the sentence of the decision is written as the works in the Rodin Museum collections, whether the same conclusion applies to works in other French museums is an open question of interpretation. This is the commentary's question and not a conclusion of the ruling.
  • Documents about the work. The same commentary says that documents about a work, such as restoration reports or acquisition records, remain subject to requests.
  • Facts the Council of State did not examine. The plaintiff side's post says that after deciding the definition of documents the court considered it unnecessary to look further into the facts of the case.

The way the background to the issue is described also differs by source. The legal press commentary says that 3D scanning has become a valuable tool for conservation and research, but that it can also allow the commercialization of reproductions in competition with public museums, so supplying scans to third parties raises issues. The plaintiff side's post and the COMMUNIA announcement describe the same case as a matter of access to and reuse of cultural heritage. Depending on which description is chosen, the same ruling is introduced differently, so it is necessary to look at the source of the description together with it.

The conclusion a reader should take from this list is not that release was prohibited but that a determination was made that the scans cannot be demanded through the request route of this regime. What is possible through other routes, such as the museum's policy, open data rules or political decisions, lies outside the material consulted.

The commentator in Le Journal des Arts wrote that this restrictive analysis is regrettable, and that the Council of State gave priority to the museum's commercial mission over its public service mission

The two accounts are not necessarily contradictory. What the commentaries describe may be the legal state in which the part settled by the lower court remains, while what the plaintiff side describes may be an interpretation of the effect the Council of State's reasoning has on the practical force of that part. Even so, it is a fact that within the range consulted the two accounts give different impressions of the status of the lower court order, and since the text of the ruling itself could not be read directly, it is not possible to decide which reading is accurate.

There are also differences among the commentaries. One law firm describes CADA's 2019 opinion as confirming the museum's partial refusal, while the legal press describes it as finding the scans to be communicable administrative documents. The plaintiff side's post says it was an opinion favorable to him. These accounts are left under what could not be confirmed.

What to Keep Apart When Reading This Ruling

The sources themselves sit at different levels. The ruling comes first, commentary written by lawyers next, and a piece written by a party to the case is that party's account. The material confirmed in this case, in that order, is as follows.

  • The full text of the ruling: the original decision text (number 487950) on the Council of State's website was opened and checked. The wording follows that original.
  • Two law firm commentaries and one legal press commentary: they give the decision number, the date, the key sentence and the file distinctions. The legal press commentary includes the commentator's questions.
  • The plaintiff side's post: it has the most detail on the course of events and the hearing, but it is a party's account and argues against the ruling.
  • The COMMUNIA announcement: as an event notice it summarizes the outline of the case and the question. Announced speakers are the plaintiff, the lawyer in the case, an expert from INNO3 and an expert from Creative Commons.

Next, the points at which readers easily go wrong in a piece introducing a legal decision are set out as they apply to this case. This list is not an evaluation of the ruling but a way of reading.

  • Keep the ruling and the party's post apart. The plaintiff's post is written by a party to the eight-year lawsuit and argues against the ruling. The positions of the museum and the ministry are not directly contained in the material consulted.
  • Keep what the court decided apart from what was claimed. The facts the court found and the facts each party claimed (whether the scans can be used, whether they could be used for counterfeiting, and so on) are not the same.
  • Keep the time of the ruling apart from the time of the coverage. The decision is from December 2025 and the post that drew attention is from September 2026. Legal commentaries (February and April 2026) appeared in between.
  • Keep apart the translation of legal concepts. Public domain and domaine public, and document and document administratif, are not the same words.
  • Keep apart the routes of the system. A freedom of information request, a museum's open policy and reuse rules are different routes.
  • Keep countries apart. To apply French provisions and a French decision to another country's law, there must be a separate basis in that country's statutes. Application in Korea is not a matter judged here.

To follow the discussion around this case further, the copy of the decision linked from the plaintiff side's post, the originals of the legal commentaries and the materials of the COMMUNIA discussion are the closest to primary material. To quote or translate the wording of the decision, the original text of the decision (number 487950) on the Council of State's website must be checked directly.

What Could Not Be Confirmed

  • [Confirmed] The full text of the Council of State decision and its date. The original decision text (conseil-etat.fr) begins with "Lecture du mardi 23 décembre 2025" and ends with "Rendu le 23 décembre 2025". December 23 is the date the decision was delivered, and December 24 is the file name of the plaintiff side's copy.
  • The positions of the museum and the ministry. The museum's side could not be confirmed beyond what is indirectly quoted in the plaintiff side's post and the commentaries.
  • Whether the 2023 lower court order has been carried out and its current state. The commentaries consulted contain no statement on carrying out the order. The plaintiff side's post says the museum and the ministry ignored the order; that is a party's assertion, and the museum's side could not be confirmed.
  • The content and date of CADA's opinion. One law firm commentary gives June 6, 2019 and the legal press commentary gives February 7, 2019, and the assessments differ too.
  • The timing of the 2023 lower court judgment. Two commentaries give April 21, 2023, while the plaintiff side's post gives December 2023 and an appeal in late 2023.
  • [Confirmed] The nature of the order to pay 3,000 euros. Article 2 of the decision has the plaintiff pay 3,000 euros to the museum on the basis of article L. 761-1 of the Code of Administrative Justice (the provision on bearing litigation costs). The decision does not describe it as damages.
  • [Confirmed] The parties at the Council of State stage. The only parties named in the decision are the plaintiff as an individual (M. B...) and the museum (the public establishment musée Rodin), and the co-plaintiffs at the lower court, such as Communia, do not appear in the decision. The Minister of Culture is listed only as a recipient of notification.
  • The content of the COMMUNIA discussion. The event is scheduled for October 13, 2026, so what is discussed cannot be confirmed.
  • The scope of application to other French museums and to other countries. No interpretation of Korean law is given.
