Diabetic Retinopathy - Why You Get Checked While You Still See Well

When to start, how often to go back, and which signs mean an eye clinic the same day

Sep 1, 2026 Health & Wellness

There is a scene that repeats itself in the eyes of people who have lived with diabetes for a long time. They read the eye chart without difficulty. Nothing bothers them, they say. And then the pupils are dilated, the retina comes into view, and the tiny vessel outpouchings and dot haemorrhages are already scattered across it.

What makes diabetic retinopathy frightening is not that it belongs on the list of blinding diseases. It is that the warning arrives last. Most illnesses hurt or inconvenience you into a clinic; this one withholds that signal until it is well advanced. That is why the ordinary rule of thumb, go when you cannot see, fails specifically here.

Turned around, that is also the good news. Found while there are no symptoms, the disease is usually caught in the window where sight can still be protected. Blood glucose management itself belongs in the piece on reversing high blood sugar, so this one stays on the eye.

This is general health information, not a diagnosis or a treatment plan; those are decisions your clinician makes. The timings and intervals here are the general recommendations of clinical practice guidelines, and the interval that applies to any one person shifts with the state of the retina and other conditions present. Do not use this article to adjudicate your own symptoms; if something here describes you, confirm it at an eye clinic.

The disease whose warning arrives last

The patient education points in the American Academy of Ophthalmology's diabetic retinopathy practice guidance contain, word for word, the sentence you end up saying most often in clinic: effective treatment depends on timely intervention despite good vision and the absence of any ocular symptoms.

The Korean Diabetes Association's 2025 clinical practice guideline lands on the same spot. Because proliferative diabetic retinopathy and macular edema can both be present without symptoms, it says, detecting and treating them early through regular screening is what matters for preventing vision loss. The fact that it names the proliferative stage and macular edema specifically is the striking part. Those are the stages that actually take sight away, and even there a person may feel nothing.

The scale is not small either. In an analysis of the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, the share of people with diabetes who had retinopathy rose from 16.6% in 2008 to 24.6% in 2020, and the number of patients went from roughly 470,000 to roughly 1.23 million, with a projection of 38.8% by 2040. Yet of 3,717 people with diabetes aged 40 and over, only 1,109, or 29.5%, had been screened for it within the past year.

  • The list of factors linked to going unscreened, from the same study, says a lot about the disease. People without eye disease were the ones who skipped it (adjusted odds ratio 1.59)
  • Age 80 and over (2.96), rural residence (1.32) and education of elementary school or below (1.64) also went unscreened more often
  • So did current smokers (1.80) and those not managing diabetes through diet and exercise (1.60)
  • The authors summed the cause up as low concern and complacency about one's own health. It is what happens when the absence of symptoms is read as a safety signal

What actually breaks - leaking, blocking, growing

The retina is a thin sheet of neural tissue lining the back of the eye, and it depends entirely on a network of very fine vessels. Sustained high blood glucose weakens the walls of those microvessels. As the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency's national health information portal describes it, small vessel outpouchings called microaneurysms form at those weakened points, and haemorrhages and exudates follow.

What comes after is easiest to hold as three events. The first is leaking. The barrier function of the vessel wall fails and blood components and fluid escape into the retinal tissue; hard exudates, visible as yellow deposits, are the trace of that. The second is blocking. When capillaries close off, that patch of retina stops receiving oxygen, and cotton-wool spots are the marker of it.

The third is growing, and it is the accumulated result of the first two. Retina starved of oxygen sends out a signal to build new vessels, and new vessels grow. The problem is that these vessels are structurally abnormal and fragile. They do not do the job, and they rupture easily. Fibrous tissue grows alongside them, and when it contracts and pulls on the retina, tractional retinal detachment follows.

  • Microaneurysms — small outpouchings of dilated capillaries. Among the earliest findings visible on fundus examination
  • Retinal haemorrhages — blood escaped from weakened vessels. Their shape hints at location and depth
  • Hard exudates — yellow deposits, lipid left behind after leakage. The sign that says something is leaking
  • Cotton-wool spots — soft white lesions marking focal ischaemia of the nerve fibre layer. The sign that says something is blocked
  • Venous beading — veins alternately thickening and narrowing like a string of beads. A signal that ischaemia has progressed
  • Neovascularisation — new vessels grown on the optic disc or the retina. Their presence changes the staging itself

Nonproliferative and proliferative - crossing the line changes the kind of risk

The dividing line is simpler than people expect: are there new vessels or not. Without them it is nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy; with them it is proliferative diabetic retinopathy. Within the nonproliferative range it is further graded mild, moderate and severe by how much haemorrhage and ischaemia is present.

The line matters because the kind of risk changes across it. In the nonproliferative range the main threat to sight is macular edema. Once the disease turns proliferative, vitreous haemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment and neovascular glaucoma are added on top. The first sort worsens gradually; the second sort arrives on a single day. That is exactly why the Korean Diabetes Association guideline recommends referral to an ophthalmologist for panretinal photocoagulation once the disease has become proliferative.

The third column of that table stands in for the point of this article. The top two rows are the range in which a person has essentially no way of knowing, and the bottom row is the range where, by the time they do know, the event has already happened. Screening exists to fill the gap between them.

Macular edema runs on its own axis

This is where explanations of the disease get flattened most often. Macular edema is not something that appears once the stage becomes severe; it is a separate axis that does not travel in step with the stage. It can appear in mild nonproliferative disease, and conversely the macula can be intact in proliferative disease.

Yet this is usually the axis that touches vision directly. The macula is a very small region carrying central vision, so fluid pooling there shows up immediately as blur and distortion. That is why the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency material names diabetic macular edema as a leading cause of vision loss.

So an ophthalmology record carries the severity of the retinopathy and the presence and location of macular edema as two separate entries. The American Academy of Ophthalmology management table crosses those two on a grid to set the follow-up interval. Whether the edema involves the centre of the macula is the next fork, and when it does, one month becomes the standard interval regardless of severity.

  • Within the same moderate nonproliferative stage: no edema means 6 to 12 months, non-center-involved edema 3 to 6 months, center-involved edema 1 month
  • So the retinopathy is mild and the sight is safe are not the same statement. The edema entry has to be checked separately
  • Optical coherence tomography is the test that reads this axis. Looking at the fundus alone makes it hard to quantify a change in thickness
  • When edema is present but vision is still excellent, observing rather than treating immediately is an option. But observing means looking often, not leaving it alone

Why symptoms arrive so late

Once you know the anatomy, the long silent stretch makes sense. Most of what we experience as seeing is carried by the macula. Reading the chart, recognising letters, telling faces apart all happen in that small patch. Microvascular damage, by contrast, spreads widely across the whole retina. The periphery can be badly damaged and the chart still reads fine as long as the macula is untouched.

A second mechanism layers on top. When part of the visual field drops out in one eye, the other eye fills that space during ordinary two-eyed life. So a change in one eye often goes unnoticed until someone happens to cover the other one.

The upshot is that the first symptom a person notices is usually not a warning sign but an event that has already occurred. A sudden shower of floaters may be a vitreous haemorrhage; blur at the centre or straight lines that look bent may be macular edema. Symptoms are not this disease's early warning system. The only thing that fills that role is the calendar of examinations.

When to start and how often to come back

This is the most practical part of the article. The screening recommendation in the Korean Diabetes Association's 2025 clinical practice guideline for diabetes, ninth edition, is short and unambiguous. People with type 1 diabetes should be examined within five years of diagnosis, and people with type 2 diabetes at the time of diagnosis, with a fundus examination that includes the retinal periphery plus a comprehensive eye examination. After that it is annual, and where there is no retinopathy and glycemic control is good, the interval may be one to two years.

Each starting point has its own basis. In type 1, retinopathy with visual impairment was almost never seen within three to five years of diagnosis, which is what makes the five-year allowance hold. Type 2 is presumed to have been preceded by a substantial stretch of undiagnosed hyperglycaemia, so it is treated as though retinopathy may already be present on the day of diagnosis and looked at straight away.

Pregnancy is managed separately. The guideline says a woman with diabetes who is planning a pregnancy should have an eye examination beforehand, and that once pregnant she should be examined and counselled within the first three months, then followed every three months and up to one year after delivery. The reason it does not end at delivery is the observation that the increase in risk persists for twelve months postpartum. Conversely, gestational diabetes first found during pregnancy is stated not to carry an increased risk of retinopathy through to delivery, so no eye examination is required for it.

The tests - each one answers a different question

Several tests of quite different character sit inside the single phrase eye examination. They answer different questions, so having had one does not substitute for another.

The base is the dilated fundus examination. The qualifier in the Korean Diabetes Association recommendation, including the retinal periphery, is the load-bearing part. Without dilating the pupil the view is too narrow to see the periphery properly, and this disease begins at the periphery. The American Academy of Ophthalmology examination elements likewise call for stereoscopic examination of the posterior pole after dilation, with the peripheral retina and vitreous examined by indirect ophthalmoscopy or slit-lamp biomicroscopy.

Fundus photography documents severity, the presence of new vessels and the response to treatment, and widefield imaging shows the overall burden at a glance. Optical coherence tomography quantifies retinal thickness, tracks macular edema and identifies vitreomacular traction. Decisions about whether to inject, whether to switch agents and whether to operate rest in large part on those findings.

Fluorescein angiography is a different animal. The Academy's guidance states explicitly that it is not indicated as part of the regular examination of patients with diabetes. It is used to distinguish macular edema from other macular disease, to work up unexplained vision loss, to identify macular capillary nonperfusion as the reason treatment is not working, and to explain neovascularisation persisting after previous laser.

What raises the risk

The top of the list is not in dispute. Duration of diabetes is the strongest predictor of onset and progression. The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency material puts numbers on it: the frequency of diabetic retinopathy is 1.9% at the time of diagnosis and 40.1% at eleven years or more of disease duration. However well control is managed, duration keeps accumulating, which is why the reasoning that says my control is good so I need not look does not hold.

Glycemic control comes next. Keeping glucose near normal from early on has been shown in large prospective trials to prevent or delay onset. Blood pressure points the same way, with one caveat: the additional benefit of pushing systolic pressure below 120 mmHg was not demonstrated in the ACCORD-EYE study.

  • Duration — the strongest factor. It accumulates regardless of how well things are managed
  • Glycemic control — control from early on is established as effective for preventing or delaying onset
  • Blood pressure — control is effective, but extra benefit from a lower target has not been confirmed
  • Dyslipidaemia — considered to affect progression; two large trials found a particular lipid-lowering agent slowed it
  • Coexisting diabetic kidney disease — named alongside, because the microvascular damage shares a root
  • Smoking — appears both in the risk factor list and in the management items. The case for quitting on account of the eyes is already inside the guideline
  • Pregnancy — with pre-existing diabetes it can worsen progression. In type 1, puberty is named too
  • Aspirin — large trial evidence confirms that use for cardiovascular prevention does not increase the risk of retinal haemorrhage. Meaning there is no ocular reason to stop it; whether to take it is set by the prescribing clinician

Signs that mean an eye clinic without delay

If any of the following appears suddenly, be seen at an eye clinic that day. It may be a situation like vitreous haemorrhage, retinal detachment or neovascular glaucoma, where time is sight. Do not try to sort out which one it is yourself.

  • A sudden drop in vision — even in one eye only, including waking up with one eye blurred
  • A sharp increase in floaters — not the usual specks slightly multiplied, but a sudden shower of them, or a darkening that spreads like ink through water
  • A field defect like a curtain coming down — a dark area closing in from above or the side
  • Repeated flashes of light — photopsia, often most obvious in a dark room
  • Eye pain with redness — especially alongside reduced vision, or with headache and nausea

This list is not a diagnosis; it is a threshold for not postponing. If it describes you, be seen that day and leave the adjudication to the examination.

Changes that come on gradually are worth not letting slide either. Central blur, straight lines that look bent, difficulty seeing at night, letters dropping out while reading: none of that is an emergency, but there is no reason to wait for the next scheduled screening. Simply having the fact that a symptom appeared written into the record changes the next decision.

Treatment - not reversal, but stopping the progression

It is better to settle the purpose before describing the treatments. The main aim in diabetic retinopathy is not to recover sight already lost but to prevent the sight that would be lost next. Without that premise, disappointment follows treatment, and the disappointment is what pushes the next appointment back.

Panretinal photocoagulation destroys peripheral retina with laser, lowering that region's oxygen demand and thereby suppressing neovascular proliferation. In the early large trial it reduced the risk of severe visual loss in proliferative retinopathy by 60% at two years after treatment. The cost is equally plain. The Korean Diabetes Association guideline records the harm of the procedure as peripheral visual field loss occurring in 100% of cases and not recovering. It is a trade in which the periphery is given up to keep central vision.

Intravitreal injection delivers an agent that suppresses vessel growth signalling directly into the eye. It is the standard option where macular edema is present, and it can be used instead of panretinal photocoagulation at the proliferative stage, though the guideline places that substitution as a limited recommendation because the number of treatments rises and cost-effectiveness falls compared with laser.

Vitrectomy is surgery. It is performed when vitreous haemorrhage does not clear, when tractional retinal detachment has developed, or when bleeding recurs. If the first two treatments hold back progression, this one is closer to cleaning up after the event has happened.

The evidence that treatment preserves sight is old and solid. The representative result is that laser treatment halved the risk of visual loss in clinically significant macular edema. Fifty per cent does not mean it is cured; it means the probability of losing sight is cut in half, and that half applies only to people found in time and treated in time.

Dropping blood glucose fast can worsen the eyes temporarily

This is not folklore; it is a phenomenon with a name. The basis is the DCCT analysis published in Archives of Ophthalmology in 1998. Among 1,439 patients with insulin-dependent diabetes split into intensive and conventional treatment arms with fundus photographs every six months, 13.1% of the 711 on intensive treatment showed early worsening of retinopathy at the six-month or twelve-month visit. In the conventional arm it was 7.6% of 728, an odds ratio of 2.06.

Stopping there leads to the wrong conclusion. What the same study reported next was this. By the eighteen-month visit, 51% and 55% respectively had recovered. Two cases progressed to high-risk proliferative retinopathy and three to clinically significant macular edema, and all responded well to treatment. The investigators' conclusion is unambiguous: the long-term benefits of intensive insulin treatment greatly outweighed the risks of early worsening.

The risk factors are known as well. Early worsening appeared more often the higher the HbA1c at the start and the larger the reduction in that value over the first six months. Recent literature sets out the same risk factors, higher baseline HbA1c, a greater reduction, longer duration and the severity of pre-existing retinopathy, while noting that the proposal to add quarterly eye monitoring for those at highest risk is not yet supported by formal guidelines.

The practical conclusion is one line. This is emphatically not a reason to delay glycemic control. It is a reason to get an eye examination beforehand and tighten follow-up for a while afterwards when someone whose control has long been poor, or who already has retinopathy, makes a large change in control. The DCCT investigators themselves recommended precisely that. Reversing the order into slowing down glucose lowering out of fear for the eyes takes on a far larger long-term risk.

Correcting the common misreadings

A handful of sentences come up again and again in clinic. Why each fails is already in the sections above, so here it is in short form.

  • My vision is fine, so I am fine — the eye chart tells you about the macula only; it says nothing about what is happening in the peripheral retina
  • Control the sugar and the eyes take care of themselves — the strongest predictor is duration, and it accumulates regardless of control. And since a sharp intensification of control is itself associated with temporary worsening, that is a period for looking at the eyes more, not less
  • I was checked once and told it was fine — one to two years applies only when there are no findings and control is good; once findings appear it is annual at minimum, three to four months by severity, and as tight as one month for center-involved edema. One normal result is normal as of that day
  • Eye supplements like lutein prevent it — the large clinical trials that tested lutein and zeaxanthin were designed around age-related macular degeneration and cataract, and I found no guideline recommendation putting them forward for the prevention of diabetic retinopathy. What guidelines do offer for prevention is control of glucose, blood pressure and lipids, plus stopping smoking
  • If I go, they will find something to do to me — the Korean Diabetes Association guideline notes that fundus photography, optical coherence tomography and similar are non-invasive with almost no anticipated harm, and that the only things to weigh are the time and the cost
  • Vision correction surgery sorts it out — a different layer entirely. Refractive surgery changes how light lands on the retina; the disease of the retina itself remains. Cataract and glaucoma are separate conditions again, each followed on its own terms

How the exam actually happens in Korea

There is a practical trap here. The National Health Insurance Service general health screening consists of consultation and examination, anthropometry, vision and hearing tests, blood pressure, chest radiography, blood tests, urinalysis and a dental check. A fundus examination is not on that list. A vision test is, but as section five explained, a vision test does not catch this disease early.

So having had the national screening every year does not amount to having had the retina looked at. A screening report stamped with no diabetes-related abnormality is a statement about blood values.

There is a route through local clinics as well. Primary care chronic disease management for hypertension and diabetes moved from pilot to full programme on 30 September 2024, and participating patients get a tailored management plan built on test results, plus education and counselling on a one-year cycle. The patient share for the integrated chronic disease management fee, related test fees and follow-up consultation fees is reduced from 30% to 20% (as of September 2026). Whether a fundus examination sits inside that programme's test cost support, though, is something I could not confirm, so it is more accurate to ask the clinic directly.

  • General health screening is every two years by default, and yearly for non-office workers among employee subscribers. Neither includes a fundus examination
  • The fastest route is to tell the clinic managing your diabetes that you want your retina checked because of it and ask for an ophthalmology referral
  • Since the pupils will be dilated, it is better to book a day with no driving and no close detailed work scheduled
  • Bring previous records if you have them. Judgement in this disease leans more on the direction of change than on the state today
  • Fundus photographs from another hospital are comparison material too. Images of the same eye separated in time are worth a lot

Summary

Diabetic retinopathy progresses in a sequence: high blood glucose damages the microvessels, they leak, they block, and eventually abnormal new vessels grow. The moment new vessels appear, the name of the stage changes and so does the character of the risk, moving from something that worsens gradually to something that happens on a single day. Alongside all of that sits macular edema on its own axis, and that is usually what actually lowers vision.

Three things are worth keeping. Type 2 gets the eyes looked at from the day of diagnosis. Type 1 starts within five years of diagnosis. The interval stretches to one or two years only when there are no findings and control is good; otherwise it is annual at minimum and tightens to a matter of months with the findings.

And one last line. In this disease, symptoms are not an early warning system. A sudden drop in vision, a surge of floaters, a field closing in like a curtain, eye pain with redness: those are signals that an event has already happened, and they mean being seen that day. What gets the eyes examined before such a day arrives is not discomfort but a calendar. A screening rate of 29.5% is bad news, but turned around it also means most of the sight at stake is still in the place where it can be saved.

Once more: this is general information, and diagnosis and treatment follow a clinician's judgement. The choice of agents and procedures, when to perform them and whether to repeat them are all matters decided in clinic, and this article does not stand in for that judgement. If you have symptoms, do not use this piece to adjudicate them; get an eye examination.
