The short answer, once your constraints are fixed

Picking a video model by asking 「which one is best」 leads nowhere. Visual quality is not verifiable from documentation, and every sample reel a vendor publishes is the take that came out best. What is written down as numbers and sentences is length, resolution, whether audio is generated, the billing unit, and the commercial-use terms. Narrow the field on those five axes, then run the two or three survivors on your own material.

What changed since May, starting with the names

The first edition of this article, written in May 2026, compared four things: Sora 2, Veo 3, Runway Gen-4 and Pika 2.0. Three months later, three of those four names are stale.

  • Veo 3 is now marked deprecated. On the Gemini API pricing page, Veo 3 and Veo 2 sit under a deprecated heading with a shutdown date of 30 June 2026. The current line is Veo 3.1.
  • Runway shipped Gen-4.5. The primary text- and image-input model in the API list is gen4.5; the Gen-4 generation survives as gen4_turbo for image input.
  • Pika moved to 2.5. Every tier on the pricing page is labelled Pika 2.5, and the free tier is capped at 480p.
  • Sora 2 now bills by the second, explicitly. What used to be described in terms of consumer subscriptions is a dollars-per-second table on the API pricing page.
  • Chinese and late-arriving vendors are now documented enough to sit in a table. Seedance 2.5, Wan 2.7, Hailuo 2.3 and HappyHorse 1.1 either did not exist in May or had documentation too thin to cite.

Generational turnover in video models runs faster than in language models. The language-model landscape is laid out separately in the 2026 AI model comparison, and there deprecation notices arrive months ahead. On the video side, a new version ships and the old version quietly acquires a deprecated label. Any internal manual with a tool name hard-coded into it should be assumed to break every quarter.

The single most useful thing in that table is that the same Veo 3.1 spans an eightfold price range across its own tiers. Standard at 4K is $0.60 per second; Lite at 720p is $0.05. Render only the delivery master on Standard and every draft on Lite, and the same number of finished pieces costs a fraction. Running every generation on the top tier is the most common form of waste in this workflow.

The second is that Veo 3.1 through Runway is sometimes cheaper than Veo 3.1 direct from Google. Runway's veo3.1 is 40 credits per second with audio, which is $0.40 and matches Google Standard, but silent output is 20 credits, or $0.20. The Google price sheet has no separate silent rate. If a lot of your shots do not need sound, that gap compounds fast.

The judgement here is clean. If the piece has dialogue, use Veo 3.1, Wan 2.6 or above, or Seedance 2.5. Seedance 2.5's 30 images, 10 videos and 10 audio clips is an order of magnitude away from everything else. Three reference images, as in Veo 3.1, covers one character's front, profile and outfit. The operationally critical row is Google's split between unpaid and paid. The Gemini API terms state that on the unpaid tier the content you submit and the responses generated are used to provide, improve and develop Google products, that human reviewers may annotate them, and that you should not submit sensitive, confidential or personal information. On the paid tier, prompts and responses are not used to improve products. If client material is going into the prompt, the free tier was never an option.

The same terms carry a clause that you may use only paid services when making API clients available to users in the European Economic Area, Switzerland or the United Kingdom. For an agency with European clients, that one line decides the plan.

Runway's terms open ownership and commercial use generously, and in exchange the company takes a non-exclusive, irrevocable, perpetual, worldwide, royalty-free, fully paid, transferable and sublicensable licence over inputs and outputs, for purposes that include model improvement. Owning a thing and being able to stop someone else from using it are two different questions.

  • If the client contract forbids training use of deliverables, check the paid or enterprise tier before you start.
  • Never deliver something rendered on a free tier. Pika stamps a watermark and blocks commercial use outright.
  • Ownership clauses and training-use clauses carry separate meanings. Read both.
  • Where the terms page will not open, get the clause in writing before signing.

  • For a service aimed at mainland China, start with providers that name a mainland region.
  • For a service aimed at Europe, read the terms first; extra clauses attach.

Watermarks and provenance marking

A logo burned into the corner is a pricing-tier question. An invisible mark is not. Google documents that video created by Veo is watermarked with SynthID, and that applies on paid tiers as well as free ones.

  • Veo 3.1, every tier — documentation states that videos created by Veo are watermarked using SynthID.
  • Pika free — downloads carry a watermark and commercial use is not permitted.
  • Pika paid — no watermark from Standard upward.
  • Runway paid — no watermark from Standard upward.
  • Everyone else — watermark policy could not be confirmed from documentation.

The visible watermark and the invisible one exist for different reasons. The first is a lever to move free users onto paid plans. The second is a mechanism for establishing later that a clip was machine-generated. Building a workflow that assumes the second can be stripped out tends to break the terms of use, and on some platforms disclosure is a regulatory requirement rather than a courtesy. The broader direction of provenance and disclosure in generated media is covered in how multimodal AI is transforming content creation.

OpenAI Sora 2, per-second billing and 16 or 20 seconds

Sora 2's character fits into one line of the price sheet. sora-2 is a single 720p tier at $0.10 per second, and above it sora-2-pro steps up to $0.30 at 720p, $0.50 at 1024p and $0.70 at 1080p. Because the rate jumps in steps with resolution, an unfixed delivery resolution makes the budget unstable.

Length comes in exactly two values: 16 and 20 seconds. You do not specify an arbitrary duration. Building a 15-second spot means rendering 16 and cutting one second, and that second is billed. For social work built on 6-second cuts, this structure is a poor fit.

  • Billing — dollars per second, and the batch route is exactly half.
  • Resolution — 720x1280 portrait and 1280x720 landscape by default, with 1024 and 1080 classes added on Pro.
  • Inputs — text is required, images may be jpeg, png or webp, video is used for extension and editing.
  • Endpoint — v1/videos only; every other endpoint is marked unsupported.
  • Audio — the API documentation says nothing about audio generation.

Twenty seconds at 1080p on Pro is $14 per render. The same twenty seconds on sora-2 at 720p is $2. Two options that differ sevenfold sit under one product name. Using Pro during exploration is nearly always waste.

Google Veo 3.1, three tiers with a wide gap

Veo 3.1 has two defining traits: audio is on by default, and the tiers are far apart on price. The documentation marks audio as always on for all three tiers, and the published rates include it. Nothing extra has to be attached for speech.

Standard$0.40$0.40$0.60Supported

Rates come from the Gemini API pricing page and capability differences from the Veo documentation, both checked 2026-08-03. Look only at 720p and Standard costs eight times what Lite costs. On an 8-second clip that is $3.20 against $0.40. Across a hundred drafts it is $320 against $40.

  • Length — 4, 6 or 8 seconds. Arbitrary durations are not accepted.
  • Aspect ratio — 16:9 by default, 9:16 supported.
  • Reference images — up to three.
  • Extension — Standard and Fast support video extension, Lite does not.
  • Watermark — SynthID is applied.
  • Older generations — Veo 3 and Veo 2 appear as deprecated on the pricing page with a shutdown date of 2026-06-30.

The working rule falls out cleanly. Explore on Lite, review on Fast, render the master on Standard. Because Lite does not extend, any shot that runs past 8 seconds has to start on Fast or above. Miss that structure and run everything on Standard, and the budget leaks by a factor of eight.

Licensing and output rights in practice

Rights language in this field reads generously and is narrower than it looks. Three provisions do most of the damage in real contracts: who owns the output, who may train on it, and which plan you were on when you rendered it. The first two live in the terms document; the third lives on the pricing page, and the pricing page is the one people actually read.

Runway grants the widest ownership language in this comparison and takes the widest licence back. Google splits behaviour by whether you pay. Pika ties commercial rights to the plan tier rather than to a clause. None of this is unusual as software licensing goes; what is unusual is that the deliverable itself is the licensed artefact.

  • Ownership — Runway and Google both state they do not claim ownership of generated output. Neither statement addresses third-party rights in the underlying material.
  • Training use — Runway's licence back to the company covers model improvement, with no opt-out short of enterprise terms.
  • Paid versus unpaid — the Gemini API terms allow human review of unpaid-tier content and warn against submitting sensitive material.
  • Plan-gated commercial use — Pika blocks commercial use on the free tier outright; Luma lists commercial use from Plus upward.
  • Territorial clauses — Gemini API terms require paid services for clients serving the EEA, Switzerland or the UK.
  • Watermarking — SynthID applies to Veo output on every tier, paid included, and is not a plan feature.
  • Client indemnity — none of the consumer-facing pricing pages examined offered an indemnity for output-related claims.
  • Documented gaps — OpenAI, ByteDance, Alibaba, MiniMax and Kuaishou published no accessible clause on any of the above.

The order to check things in before you commit

Image quality is not verifiable from documentation. So once the field is down to two or three, there is no substitute for measuring on your own material. The order goes like this.

  1. Fix the delivery specification first. Length, resolution, aspect ratio, audio or no audio. Nothing filters until these are set.
  2. Check the length quantum. Building a 15-second spot on a 16-second unit wastes a second every single render.
  3. Run the same prompt five times on every candidate. One result is a sample of one.
  4. Keep the failures separately. The shape of the failures is a better decision input than the average.
  5. Count the retries. A cheap per-second rate stops being cheap after the third re-render.
  6. Time the generations. On a tight deadline, queue time weighs more than unit price.
  7. Read the commercial-use clause before signing, not after delivery.
  8. Share the training-use clause with the client. Once their material goes into the prompt, this is a negotiation item.
  9. Confirm what watermarking is applied — visible and invisible alike.
  10. Archive the model ID and prompt alongside the render. Without them, a revision request starts from zero.

Steps three and five carry the weight. The per-second rate is what the table says; the real cost is how many seconds you burned to get one usable piece. A gap of three times or more between those two numbers is not unusual. The second number is the one to choose on.

## 9. Measuring for yourself once the field is narrowed

1. Fix the delivery specification first: length, resolution, aspect ratio, audio or none
2. Check the length quantum. A 16-second unit wastes a second on every 15-second render
3. Run the same prompt five times on every candidate. One run is a sample of one
4. Keep the failed runs separately. Failure shape is the real decision input
5. Count the retries. Three re-renders make the cheap model the expensive one
6. Time the queue. On a short deadline this outweighs unit price
7. Measure credit consumption on a free tier. Only then does a credit total mean anything
8. Archive the model ID, prompt and reference files alongside the render
