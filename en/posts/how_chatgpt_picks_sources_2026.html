How ChatGPT Actually Picks Its Sources

What one logged-in Pro account, 1,240 source records, and two days of traffic reveal about the query-to-citation pipeline - and what it means for GEO

Jul 17, 2026 Tech & IT

Intro: What the Final Answer Never Shows You

Ask ChatGPT something and the answer arrives with a handful of source links attached. What never shows up anywhere on screen is how those links got picked - why one site gets cited while another gets scraped repeatedly yet never mentioned at all. A recent analysis went straight at that black box. SEO consultant Suganthan Mohanadasan kept his browser's DevTools Network panel open for two days on a single logged-in ChatGPT Pro account and read roughly 1,240 source records across a few dozen searches, straight from the raw traffic. This was not packet sniffing - it was simply watching, through tools anyone can open, the HTTP responses the ChatGPT client exchanges with its own servers.

This post uses that analysis as a starting point for three things. First, it generalizes how generative AI search and answer engines pick sources into a five-stage pipeline: query classification → query generation → candidate gathering → ranking and selection → citation. Second, it offers my own reading of what the traffic actually revealed. Third, it draws out what this means in practice for GEO (Generative Engine Optimization) - especially for a small, static-HTML site like this one. Everything here reflects the situation as of July 2026, and rather than repeating the original numbers verbatim, I've rebuilt what the structure itself is telling us, in my own words.

1. What Was Actually Measured - A Methodology Summary

1.1 What Was Collected

The method was simple. Using a logged-in ChatGPT Pro account, the author asked real questions while keeping the browser's Network tab open, logging the raw response JSON exchanged between client and server. Over two days and a few dozen searches, about 1,240 source-related records piled up, and fields like result_source, turn_use_case, personal_sources, and local_results_limit stood out. None of these fields are ever exposed in the citation cards a user actually sees - they are internal labels sitting entirely behind the curtain.

1.2 What This Method Can and Cannot Tell You

The author was explicit: "one person, one logged-in Pro account, a few days of traffic, not a population study." Structural findings - which fields exist and what values they can take - are relatively trustworthy. But rates and percentages came from a sample skewed toward SaaS and tech questions, so they should be read as direction, not measurement. I keep that distinction throughout this piece.

2. The Five-Stage Pipeline Behind Source Selection

Combining this traffic analysis with broader GEO literature, the process generative AI search uses to pick sources can be generalized into roughly five stages.

2.1 Query Classification - Deciding Whether to Search at All

ChatGPT first sorts a question into an internal bucket called turn_use_case. Six values were observed: instant search, shopping, text, local, thinking, and image generation. The striking part is that when a question lands in the text bucket, the model skips web search entirely and answers purely from training data. Of 10 deliberately time-sensitive questions the author tried, 3 were handled exactly this way with zero search - one of them asking about current treatment guidelines. The more important finding is that wording decides the bucket, not the topic. "Good coffee near me" triggered the local pipeline; "best 4K TVs to buy" triggered shopping; but "best 4K TVs with reviews" ran as a normal search. The same underlying topic can be routed through an entirely different pipeline depending on a single phrase.

2.2 Query Generation - One Question Becomes Many

Once search is triggered, the model rarely fires the original question as-is. On a reasoning model comparing products, a single question spawned 15 to 40 sub-queries. It probed vendor pages directly with site: operators, guessed a price and then searched to confirm it, and used browsing tools to read HTML server-side. The industry calls this query fan-out, and other reports similarly describe a single query typically decomposing into 8-12 parallel sub-queries. What looks like one question to the user is, behind the scenes, dozens of independent searches running at once.

2.3 Candidate Gathering - Where the Pages Come From

Every gathered candidate document carries a result_source label, and four values were observed. serp draws on open-web search results and was prominent for news queries. labrador was limited to a small set of publishers - Reuters, The Guardian, WSJ, FT, Wikipedia, arXiv - with unusually long snippets around 1,080 characters. The author read this as "a licensed tier, suggesting several of those publishers have signed content deals with OpenAI." bright (the Bright Data scraper) dominated shopping, finance, weather, and local queries, and oxylabs appeared to cover regional press and the remaining open web.

2.4 Ranking and Selection - Why Only Some Documents Survive

Only a fraction of gathered candidates actually make it into the answer. In one visible reasoning trace, the model examined a SaaS product's pricing page and concluded, "the pricing doesn't show up directly, possibly hidden with JavaScript," then decided "I should use citations from G2 where appropriate." When a page renders key facts only via JavaScript, the model simply cannot read it - and authority quietly shifts to a third-party site that has repackaged the same information. Local results were capped by a local_results_limit value of 2, meaning "you're in the top 2 or you aren't there at all." The actual ranking formula, beyond the model's own narration, never surfaces anywhere in the traffic - it stays entirely server-side.

2.5 Citation - Fetched, Mentioned, and Cited Are Not the Same Event

The most practically useful distinction in this analysis is between fetched, mentioned, and cited. Being fetched only means a document entered the model's context - the user never sees it. Being cited means a specific sentence is bound to a clickable footnote. Reddit and YouTube were both fetched at similar, heavy rates - 278 and 201 times respectively - yet Reddit was cited 11 times while YouTube was cited zero times. The reason is mechanical: a citation has to bind to text the model actually pulled, and fetching a YouTube page in search returns metadata, not the video's transcript. Content that cannot be excerpted as text never becomes a citation, no matter how often it gets referenced.

3. What This Analysis Reveals - My Own Reading

From here on, this is my interpretation, going beyond the raw facts of the source analysis.

3.1 The Fact That Search Sometimes Doesn't Happen Is a Big Deal

Many site owners assume that if their content is current, it will simply get searched and reflected whenever relevant. Section 2.1 shows that assumption can be wrong. If a question lands in the text bucket, search never runs at all, no matter how well a site is maintained. Before any content optimization even begins, the exact phrasing a user happens to type sets a hard ceiling on whether your content ever has a chance to be seen.

3.2 JavaScript Rendering Is Structurally Disadvantaged

Rendering key facts - price, specs - only via JavaScript has long been a known SEO problem, but this analysis shows the cost is more direct under GEO. If the model can't read it, the original source quietly drops out, and a third-party site that repackaged the same data inherits the authority instead. Sites that expose core facts in static HTML already hold a real advantage in this competition.

3.3 Text-Based Content Is Inherently Citation-Friendly

Flip the Reddit/YouTube contrast around and it's a favorable signal for blogs and documentation sites. Content clearly written out as HTML text is, by construction, the kind of thing that's easy to excerpt and bind to a citation. Video- and image-heavy content, however popular, is structurally at a disadvantage in the race for citations.

3.4 A Licensed Allowlist May Be a Wall You Simply Cannot Climb

If a contractual fast lane like labrador exists, no amount of structural polish lets a small site into that tier. That's exactly why GEO shouldn't be sold as a cure-all. My conclusion: don't compete for territory locked up by large publishers' content deals - concentrate instead on the terrain you can actually contest: structure, clarity, and excerptability.

4. The GEO Lens: What Content Actually Gets Cited by AI

Mapping this traffic analysis's findings onto GEO's five familiar axes - freshness, structure, authority, clarity, excerptability - looks like this.

4.1 Freshness

Multiple sources note that AI search leans toward recent content. In my earlier controlled GEO experiment analysis, regularly refreshed pages were more likely to be picked as citation candidates than neglected ones. That piece measured a before/after citation-rate change from a specific intervention; this piece targets a step earlier - the pipeline that decides how a citation happens in the first place - which is what makes the two genuinely complementary rather than redundant.

4.2 Structure

The fact that a single query fans out into 15-40 sub-queries implies documents need to be self-contained at the section level so any one of them can be a hit for a different sub-question. That's exactly why clear H2/H3 headings, definition-style opening paragraphs, and facts organized into lists and tables show up as consistent recommendations across GEO guides.

4.3 Authority

Most sites will never enter a contractual fast lane like labrador, so they need other ways to build trust signals. Industry research associates including a direct quote with roughly a 27.8% citation lift, a statistic with about 25.9%, and citing a source with about 24.9%. The general direction is consistent across multiple sources: sentences carrying concrete numbers, dates, and sourcing get picked over vague claims.

4.4 Clarity

Platforms differ, too. Claude tends to synthesize rather than quote directly, favoring logically well-organized structure; Gemini is deeply tied to Google's search infrastructure, so existing SEO performance tends to carry straight over into visibility. A definition-style sentence that states the conclusion first and the reasoning after is a safe default regardless of platform.

4.5 Excerptability

This is the axis most directly confirmed by this piece. Exposing key facts in static HTML rather than hiding them behind JavaScript rendering, and describing facts in text rather than video or images, translate into a real, measurable edge in the race for citations.

5. How GEO Differs From Traditional SEO

SEO's target is ranking and clicks on a results page; GEO's target is being mentioned or cited inside an AI-generated answer. The success metric itself shifts from clicks to citation and mention rates. Still, the two aren't fully separate. All three channels observed here - serp, bright, oxylabs - effectively draw their candidate pool from the existing web index, reconfirming that GEO is difficult to achieve without a solid SEO foundation. Some industry research suggests the overlap between top Google links and the sources AI actually cites has fallen from roughly 70% to under 20%. Rankings and citations are increasingly becoming different games - so the practical approach isn't SEO here, GEO there, but layering GEO-specific structuring work on top of an existing SEO foundation.

6. A Practical Checklist for Blog and Site Owners

These are items worth checking today, and they apply just as much to running this blog (BrainDetox) as to any other site.

  • Expose key facts in static HTML - put prices, figures, and conclusions directly into HTML text rather than relying on JavaScript rendering. A purely static site already has an edge here.
  • Make every section self-contained - structure each H2/H3 so it functions as its own question-and-answer, since any single sub-query might land on just one section.
  • Use FAQ schema and clear structure - offer question-and-answer pairs phrased the way a real user would ask them. The FAQ section at the bottom of this post follows the same logic.
  • State concrete figures and sources - build trust signals with statistics, dates, and sourcing instead of vague claims.
  • Check robots.txt - make sure you aren't unintentionally blocking crawlers like GPTBot. No optimization matters if the crawler can't get in.
  • Update content regularly - lean into the tendency for recently refreshed pages to be picked as citation candidates over neglected ones.
  • Build topical clusters with internal links - connect related posts so that when one question fans out into many sub-queries, your site as a whole can still cover it.

7. Limits of the Methodology - How Much Should You Trust This?

This analysis is valuable precisely because real-world reverse-engineering like this is rare, but its limits need to be stated plainly. First, it's a single account, single observer - the author's own usage patterns and question style are baked into every result. Second, the sample skews toward SaaS and tech; for domains like shopping, where single observations contradicted each other, the author himself flagged the conclusions as "unsettled." Third, the observation window was just two days. Fourth, AI models are updated on a weekly cadence and mix in personalization signals (past conversations, email, files tied to a logged-in account), so there's no guarantee today's observations still hold next month. In short: structural findings - which fields exist and how the mechanism works - carry real reference value, but figures like "X% comes through this channel" are safest read as direction only.

8. What This Means for Users and Marketers

Ordinary users should internalize that "a ChatGPT answer isn't necessarily built on a search that just happened." For time-sensitive questions - medical guidelines, legal rules, pricing - it's safer to phrase questions precisely and to check whether the answer actually carries a source footnote. For marketers and content operators, a two-track strategy is realistic: rather than competing head-on for territory locked up by large publishers' content deals, concentrate resources on the axes you can actually control regardless of scale - structure, clarity, excerptability. None of that requires a large budget; it comes down to the basics of static HTML, clear headings, and fact-grounded sentences.

Conclusion

What 1,240 traffic records reveal isn't some flashy new technology - it's the plain fact that even ChatGPT runs on a handful of mechanical stages: classify, search, select, cite. But important operational details hide inside that plain fact. The wording of a question decides whether search happens at all. JavaScript can quietly block a citation. Only content that can be excerpted as text ever gets bound to a footnote. GEO isn't a trick for magically bypassing this pipeline - it's closer to understanding these mechanical constraints and focusing on the parts you can actually control: structure, clarity, excerptability. Given the limits of a single-account, single-snapshot observation, this analysis is best read not as a definitive algorithm manual but as a map pointing in a direction - and the direction it points toward, static, clear, easily excerptable content, is something worth applying right now regardless.
