An AI Engineer Claims to Have Decoded Linear A — Possibilities and Scholarly Skepticism

A self-taught engineer tackled one of humanity's oldest linguistic mysteries using AI tools — here is what was claimed, and why extreme caution is warranted

2026-06-22 Tech
Important notice: The claim by Tom Di Mino discussed in this article remains unverified by the academic community as of June 2026. Expert review at Rutgers and Cambridge is reported to be ongoing, but no official conclusions have been published and no formal peer-reviewed paper exists. This article does not assert that Linear A has been deciphered, and presents both possibilities and skepticism in a balanced manner.

Introduction: An AI Challenge to One of Humanity's Oldest Mysteries

In June 2026, an unusual claim attracted attention across AI and linguistics communities. Tom Di Mino, a self-described autodidact AI engineer and amateur linguist, announced that he had deciphered Linear A — one of the oldest and most persistently undeciphered writing systems in human history. The claim reached GeekNews, where it garnered 25 points and triggered a substantial discussion thread.

If true, the implications would be revolutionary. But that conditional — "if true" — carries enormous weight. Linear A has resisted over a century of concentrated effort by the world's top linguists and archaeologists. A claim that an amateur with AI scripting tools cracked the puzzle in a matter of months demands both genuine open-mindedness and rigorous skepticism. This article aims to provide exactly that: a fair summary of what was claimed, an honest assessment of the methodology, and a clear account of why the scholarly community is cautious.

1. What Is Linear A? The 3,500-Year Silence

To evaluate the claim, it is necessary to understand what Linear A is and why it has proven so resistant to decipherment.

Linear A was the writing system of the Minoan civilization, the Bronze Age culture centered on the island of Crete that flourished from approximately 2000 to 1450 BCE. The Minoans built large palace complexes at Knossos, Phaistos, Malia, and Zakros, and maintained extensive trade networks across the eastern Mediterranean. Linear A was their primary script for administrative, economic, and likely religious records — inscribed on clay tablets, libation vessels, and various artifacts.

Discovered in the early 20th century by archaeologist Arthur Evans at Knossos, the script comprises around 70 syllabic signs supplemented by ideograms and numerals. Approximately 7,500 individual characters have been identified across roughly 1,500 inscriptions. The script spread widely: artifacts bearing Linear A have been found not only on Crete but also on Rhodes, Thera, and across the Cyclades, reflecting the breadth of Minoan trade.

1.1 The Relationship Between Linear A and Linear B

Linear A has a close structural relationship with Linear B, the script used by the Mycenaean Greeks who dominated the Aegean after around 1450 BCE. The two systems share approximately 70% of their signs, which means the phonetic values of Linear B signs can be provisionally applied to Linear A.

This is precisely where Linear A's core puzzle lies. You can read Linear A aloud using Linear B phonetics. The sounds can be produced. But those sounds encode a completely unknown language — and without knowing the language, the sounds remain meaningless. Linear B was deciphered in 1952 by Michael Ventris, who correctly hypothesized that it encoded an archaic form of Greek. Linear A encodes something else entirely, and that something else is what remains unknown.

1.2 Why Decipherment Has Resisted All Attempts

The decipherment of ancient scripts has historically required a critical ingredient: a comparative anchor. Egyptian hieroglyphics yielded to the Rosetta Stone's bilingual text. Cuneiform was cracked via the trilingual Behistun inscription. Linear B relied on the hypothesis that it encoded Greek. Linear A has none of these advantages.

The dominant scholarly position in 2026 treats Minoan as a language isolate — a language with no demonstrated connection to any known living or extinct language family. This is not a minor technical obstacle. It means there is no established comparative linguistic framework from which to derive plausible word meanings. Scholars have proposed connections to Luwian, Semitic languages, Etruscan, and Uralic families over the decades, but none has survived rigorous scrutiny.

The corpus size compounds the difficulty. Around 7,500 characters is a remarkably small dataset for linguistic analysis. A standard English novel contains roughly 100,000 words. With such a limited corpus, it is both difficult to falsify a hypothesis and dangerously easy to find apparent confirmations of almost any theory.

2. Tom Di Mino's Claim — What Was Done and How

Tom Di Mino, based in Hudson Valley, New York, reports spending approximately seven years studying Linear A as a self-taught pursuit before launching a systematic decipherment effort in January 2026. He states his key insight arrived on May 22, 2026.

2.1 The Core Hypothesis

Di Mino's claim rests on two central pillars. First, he argues that Linear A encodes an extinct Semitic language that was an ancestor of Biblical Hebrew, Arabic, and Aramaic — analogous to the relationship between Latin and the Romance languages. Second, he identified what he describes as a prayer inscription formula in which all words except the first were already known, and linked that unknown first word to the Semitic root nawaya, meaning "to dwell" or "to inhabit," a root attested in Hebrew, Akkadian, and other Semitic languages.

From this starting point, he reportedly assigned phonetic values to 40 signs (including 13 previously unassigned ones) and compiled a 408-word lexicon. A draft manuscript has been shared with linguistics specialists at Rutgers University and Cambridge University for informal review, though no formal conclusions have been published as of June 2026.

2.2 The Role of AI: Tool, Not Solver

A crucial clarification is necessary about AI's actual role in this case. Di Mino used Claude Code to build a suite of Python scripts for querying, cross-referencing, and organizing the digitized Linear A corpus. AI served as a data processing and hypothesis-testing accelerator. The core linguistic insight — the proposed Semitic family connection and the specific identification of the nawaya root — came from Di Mino himself.

This distinction matters enormously. The headline "AI decodes Linear A" significantly overstates the AI's contribution. The more accurate description is "an amateur linguist used AI-assisted scripting tools to systematically test a Semitic decipherment hypothesis against the corpus." The distinction is not merely semantic: it determines whether this represents a new mode of AI-driven discovery or a traditional amateur linguistic investigation conducted with modern computational tools.

3. AI and Ancient Script Decipherment — Genuine Possibilities and Hard Limits

Before this specific claim, AI had already shown genuine utility in the study of ancient writing systems. Understanding where that utility is real and where it hits fundamental walls is important context.

3.1 What AI Does Well

Machine learning and large language models have contributed meaningfully to ancient script research in specific domains. Pattern recognition and frequency analysis across large corpora can be performed orders of magnitude faster than manual analysis. AI has assisted in restoring damaged inscriptions, mapping phonetic correspondences between structurally related scripts, clustering similar signs, and generating candidate translations when at least one language in a bilingual text is known. MIT researchers demonstrated in 2019 how neural decipherment algorithms could identify correspondences between Ugaritic and related Semitic scripts, achieving promising results precisely because the language family relationship was established.

3.2 The Hard Limit

The fundamental constraint is this: AI statistical pattern extraction cannot, by itself, assign linguistic meaning. Patterns are only interpretable when there is an established anchor — a known language family, a bilingual text, or a set of words whose meaning is independently established. Without such an anchor, a pattern that looks linguistically significant may simply reflect the statistical noise of a small dataset.

A linguist commenting on the Hacker News thread put it sharply: "With a corpus this small, you can make almost any language family match look plausible. Take an English text, look for surface resemblances to Semitic roots, and you will find them — because the corpus is small enough that exceptions are rare and confirmations are easy to cherry-pick."

4. Scholarly Skepticism — The Specific Problems

As of June 2026, the academic response to Di Mino's claim is characterized by caution and skepticism. No official rebuttal has been published, but the concerns that specialists and informed commentators have raised are substantive.

4.1 The Corpus Size Problem

The entire Linear A corpus — approximately 7,500 characters across 1,500 inscriptions — fits on a single screen at readable font sizes. This is not a minor statistical inconvenience; it is a fundamental analytical problem. With a corpus this small, statistical tests of significance are unreliable. A Hacker News participant demonstrated this by showing how a surface-level mapping of English words onto Semitic roots produces a superficially convincing list. Any hypothesis applied to 7,500 characters can find supporting examples while the opposing examples remain sparse and easy to dismiss.

4.2 The Methodological Transparency Problem

Di Mino has publicly demonstrated one example of his method — the nawaya root identification — while claiming 300-plus translations and a 408-word lexicon. The full methodology for all 40 sign assignments and all lexical entries has not been made publicly available. Without this, independent verification is impossible. One commenter on Hacker News analyzed the demonstrated example and noted that the reasoning involved taking the sign sequence A-TA-I-*301-WA-JA, retaining only W and J, assuming *301 begins with N, and declaring a match to a Semitic root — while the A-TA-I portion of the sequence was simply set aside. Matching one-fifth of one word while ignoring the rest is not a robust decipherment demonstration.

4.3 The Precedent of Previous Semitic Hypotheses

The claim that Linear A encodes a Semitic language is not novel. The American linguist Cyrus Gordon proposed substantially the same hypothesis in 1957. His work received serious scholarly attention and was ultimately rejected after extensive review. The fundamental problems that led to rejection have not obviously been resolved: if Minoans spoke a Semitic language and sustained contact with Greek speakers for centuries after 1450 BCE, we would expect to find Semitic loanwords embedded in ancient Greek vocabulary. They are largely absent.

4.4 The Structural Mismatch

Semitic languages are characterized by a consonantal root system — meaning is carried by consonantal skeletons with vowels as secondary modulation (the root k-t-b in Hebrew conveys the concept of "writing" across multiple word forms). Semitic scripts historically omit vowels, representing only consonants. Linear A, however, appears to be a full syllabary that encodes both consonants and vowels explicitly. Writing a Semitic language in a full syllabary is not impossible in principle, but it represents a structural mismatch that requires a compelling explanation — one that Di Mino's publicly available material has not yet provided.

4.5 Informal Review Is Not Peer Review

The fact that specialists at Rutgers and Cambridge are reviewing Di Mino's manuscript is meaningful as a sign that the work is being taken seriously enough to examine. It is emphatically not the same as passing peer review. Informal expert review, with no timeline, no requirement for publication-standard rigor, and no formal verdict, is where many incorrect claims sit for extended periods before formal evaluation occurs.

5. How to Read AI Science Claims

Di Mino's case exemplifies a broader challenge in the AI era. Democratized access to powerful computational tools means that amateur researchers can now conduct data analyses of a scale and speed that were previously impossible without institutional resources. This is genuinely positive. It expands who can contribute to difficult problems.

But it also creates a new class of risk: the conflation of AI-assisted data processing with AI-validated conclusions. The use of AI tools does not confer epistemic authority on the conclusions drawn from those tools. What matters is the rigor of the underlying reasoning — in this case, the linguistic logic of the proposed Semitic connection and the completeness and consistency of the sign-value assignments.

When evaluating claims at the intersection of AI and established disciplines — whether linguistics, physics, biology, or mathematics — the appropriate questions are: Has the methodology been fully and publicly documented? Has it been subjected to independent replication? Does it resolve the known objections that caused similar prior claims to fail? If the answer to these questions is no, the appropriate response is interested skepticism, not either dismissal or acceptance.

6. Why Linear A Still Matters Enormously

The prevalence of skepticism should not obscure why Linear A decipherment would be one of the most significant discoveries in modern archaeology and linguistics.

If Minoan were to be identified within a language family, it would reshape our understanding of pre-Indo-European language diversity in the Aegean and Mediterranean world. Currently, the Minoans are understood primarily through their material culture — their architecture, pottery, frescoes, and trade goods. Access to their written records in translated form would reveal the substance of their administrative systems, religious practices, and possibly their cosmology and social structure. It would open a direct window into a civilization that shaped the Bronze Age Aegean and influenced subsequent Greek and Mycenaean cultures.

The Minoan question is genuinely open. There is no theoretical reason Linear A cannot eventually be deciphered. The history of decipherment suggests that the breakthrough, when it comes, will likely depend on either a bilingual text discovery — the Minoan equivalent of the Rosetta Stone — or a compelling structural argument that places Minoan in a known language family with sufficient rigor to survive specialist scrutiny.

Conclusion: An Interesting Hypothesis at the Claim Stage

To summarize: Tom Di Mino's AI-assisted Linear A decipherment claim is an interesting and ambitious hypothesis that deserves serious examination. The use of computational tools to systematically test linguistic hypotheses against the corpus is a methodologically sound approach. The proposal itself is not unreasonable on its face.

However, as of June 2026, this remains a claim — not a decipherment. The corpus size problem is severe. The methodology has not been fully published. The Semitic hypothesis has a prior history of scholarly rejection that has not been demonstrably overcome. The structural mismatch between Semitic language typology and Linear A's syllabic character requires a compelling resolution. And informal expert review is not the same as formal peer evaluation.

The appropriate response is to watch with informed interest — and to wait for the evidence before updating our picture of Bronze Age Minoan civilization.
