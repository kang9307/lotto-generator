Panic Disorder Symptoms and How to Tell When You Should Get Checked

An attack is a symptom, a disorder is a diagnosis - from ruling out physical causes to the signals that mean it is time to seek care

Any conclusion about whether panic disorder is present belongs to the clinician who evaluates you, and this article contains no scoring sheet or checklist that assigns a diagnosis. Why an all-clear on the tests does not make the fear go away

The sentence people hear most often after a panic attack is that the tests came back normal. And they are still frightened. A normal ECG and the memory of a heart that felt ready to burst on last night's train simply refuse to be reconciled.

That gap is what this article is about: what a panic attack actually is in the body, and how having one or two attacks differs from being diagnosed with panic disorder. That distinction is the spine of the piece. Blur it and you go wrong in both directions, either pinning a diagnosis on yourself after a single attack and spending months braced for the next one, or watching your life quietly narrow while telling yourself you have always been the anxious type.

One thing to state up front. There is no self-check tool here that adds up points and hands back a diagnosis. What such a table actually delivers is either false reassurance or a label, and both delay care. And because chest pain and palpitations overlap with cardiac problems, the step of clearing the heart first comes before any discussion of mental health.

The mechanics of paced breathing and the general principles of cognitive behavioral therapy are not re-explained here. Those are covered in the Anxiety and Stress Management Complete Guide. This piece stays on one specific diagnosis.

1. What is actually happening in the body during an attack

A panic attack is less an emotional outburst than an alarm system misfiring. Korea's national mental health portal describes panic disorder as the body's alarm going off in the absence of any real threat, producing fear intense enough to shut down clear thinking. That is the right framing. The problem is not that the alarm sounded. It is that it sounded with no fire.

Once the alarm sounds, the body shifts into a state built for real danger. The sympathetic nervous system engages, the heart speeds up, breathing quickens, fingertips go cold and sweat appears. All of that is a normal response. The problem is that it switched on inside a train car with nothing to run from.

Then a second loop starts turning. The bodily changes themselves get read as fresh evidence of danger.

  • A rising heart rate is interpreted as a heart problem, which raises the fear, which raises the heart rate again
  • Faster breathing blows off carbon dioxide, producing dizziness and tingling in the hands, feet and around the mouth, and those sensations get read as further danger signals
  • A sense of unreality arrives and gets interpreted as losing one's mind, amplifying the fear of losing control
  • Through all of this no actual physical damage occurs. The NHS states that although panic attacks are frightening, an attack will not cause you physical harm

That is a description of a mechanism, not reassurance. Reciting the words during an attack will not stop it. But it does explain why the tests find nothing while the experience feels overwhelming.

2. Symptoms and course - it peaks within minutes

The most distinctive feature of a panic attack is not which symptoms appear but the shape of the curve over time. DSM-5-TR defines a panic attack as an abrupt surge of intense fear or discomfort that reaches a peak within minutes, with four or more of thirteen listed symptoms appearing during that window. Seoul National University Hospital describes symptoms peaking within about 10 minutes and easing over 20 to 30, and the NHS puts most attacks between 5 and 20 minutes.

This is where a misreading creeps in. People see four out of thirteen and start counting their own symptoms. That number is a definition clinicians apply during an interview, not a rule handed to readers for labelling themselves. Four symptoms produced by hyperthyroidism and four produced by an attack do not sort themselves out by counting.

System Commonly reported during an attack What is going on physically
Cardiovascular Pounding or racing heart, chest pain or tightness Sympathetic activation raises heart rate and output while chest muscles tense
Respiratory Shortness of breath, a smothering sensation, a feeling of choking Breathing accelerates, and hyperventilation clears out carbon dioxide
Autonomic Sweating, chills or heat sensations, trembling, nausea and stomach upset Blood flow shifts toward the muscles while digestion is pushed aside
Neurological Dizziness, faintness, tingling in the hands, feet or around the mouth Falling blood carbon dioxide from hyperventilation is a common background
Cognitive Unreality or feeling detached from oneself, fear of losing control, fear of dying Threat interpretation strengthens, so bodily sensations are read as danger again

Some things about the course are worth noting.

  • There is a peak. It does not simply keep escalating; it tops out within minutes and comes down
  • Something holding at the same intensity for hours does not match the typical course. That is itself information to bring to a visit
  • Attacks often arrive without warning. Korea's mental health portal notes that a first attack frequently occurs spontaneously, with no identifiable trigger
  • After it passes, exhaustion and residual tension often linger for hours

3. A panic attack and panic disorder are not the same thing

If one sentence survives from this article, let it be this. An attack is a symptom; a disorder is a diagnosis.

NIMH draws the line explicitly: a one-time or occasional panic attack is not in itself a mental disorder. Plenty of people have one during an overloaded week or after a major loss and never have another.

The diagnosis is narrower. Two conditions have to hold together.

  • Unexpected attacks recur. The key is not attacks tied to one specific situation but attacks that keep arriving without warning
  • For a month or more afterwards, worry about further attacks persists, or behavior changes in order to avoid them
  • And it must not be better explained by something else - a physical illness, a substance or medication, or another psychiatric condition

Why a month? Because being anxious for a few days after an attack happens to almost everyone. What the diagnosis targets is not the attack but the state in which life begins reorganizing itself around it.

4. Anticipatory anxiety and the loop that avoidance builds

What makes panic disorder hard is not the intensity of the attacks but the time between them. After one bad episode, fear of the next one settles in. That is anticipatory anxiety.

The real trouble is the behavior it produces. People avoid what frightens them, and avoidance feels better immediately. That instant relief reinforces the avoidance. The radius of a life shrinks one notch at a time.

  • An attack on the train ends train travel, then buses, then driving through tunnels
  • Places that are hard to leave mid-way - cinemas, salons, meeting rooms - land on the list first
  • Being alone becomes frightening, so going out hardens into something done only with a specific person along
  • Anything that raises the heart rate gets dropped: exercise, saunas, coffee. Bodily sensation itself has become the trigger

A lengthening list of this kind is the path toward agoraphobia. What matters here is that avoidance is learned behavior, not a shortage of willpower. From the brain's point of view, every avoided situation files away another success. That is why resolve alone does not undo it, and why treatment exists.

5. Clear the heart first - physical causes and emergency signals

The most dangerous sentence in any article about panic is that it is only panic, so it is fine. Chest pain and palpitations are hallmark symptoms of a panic attack and also symptoms of acute myocardial infarction, arrhythmia, hyperthyroidism and asthma. The Merck Manual lists hyperthyroidism, acute coronary syndrome, arrhythmias, asthma and COPD among the physical conditions to rule out. Korea's national mental health portal likewise notes that cardiovascular, respiratory, neurological and endocrine disorders must be distinguished.

So there is an order to this. Chest pain you are feeling for the first time is not written off as panic. That goes double if you have risk factors such as high blood pressure, diabetes, abnormal lipids or smoking, or a family history of cardiac problems at a young age.

Overlapping condition What resembles a panic attack Observations that help separate it
Acute coronary syndrome Chest pain, cold sweat, breathlessness, a sense of impending death Pressure that does not let up, pain radiating to arm, neck, jaw or back. Emergency response
Arrhythmia Palpitations, dizziness, faintness Irregular pulse or near-fainting episodes. Confirmed by ECG or Holter monitoring
Hyperthyroidism Pounding heart, tremor, sweating, anxiety Weight loss, heat intolerance, persistent tachycardia. Confirmed by blood tests
Asthma and airway disease Breathlessness, chest tightness, choking sensation Wheeze, cough, identifiable triggers. Lung function testing is used
Substances and medication Palpitations, agitation, insomnia Timing against caffeine, stimulants or some cold remedies, or alcohol withdrawal

Some signals belong to emergency care. The NHS advises calling emergency services immediately for chest pain in the following circumstances.

  • Sudden chest pain or discomfort that does not go away
  • Pain that spreads to one or both arms, the neck, jaw, stomach or back
  • Chest pain accompanied by sweating, nausea, light-headedness or shortness of breath
  • Consciousness clouding, or an episode of actually collapsing
  • If this is the first chest pain you have had, verification comes before your own interpretation

The opposite mistake also happens: repeating the same cardiac tests after they have already come back clear. Once physical causes are excluded, the next step is not another cardiology appointment but a mental health one. Either way, the checking happens in a clinical setting.

6. Signals to seek care, and how a diagnosis is actually made

Telling when to get checked is not the same as assigning yourself a diagnosis; it means deciding whether to book the appointment. Any single item below is reason enough. There is nothing to count.

  • Unexpected attacks have happened more than once
  • Worry about the next one has run past a month
  • You have started cutting back on places you go or things you do to avoid attacks
  • There is real interference with daily function - work, school, driving, relationships
  • Attempts to suppress the anxiety with alcohol or medication have increased
  • You are thinking about suicide or having urges to harm yourself. In that case do not wait, reach out now

What happens in the consulting room is an interview, not a scoring exercise. When it started, what situations bring it on, what the periods in between are like, how caffeine, alcohol, sleep and current medications figure, what physical conditions are in your history. Blood tests or an ECG may be used to check for physical causes, and the clinician looks for co-occurring anxiety disorders or depression. If a questionnaire is used, it supports the interview rather than serving as a verdict.

If a psychiatric clinic feels like too big a first step, community mental health services can be a place to start. Diagnosis and prescribing, however, happen in a medical setting.

7. Treatment - there are approaches with evidence behind them

Something has to be said alongside all of this: panic disorder is a treatable condition and approaches with established evidence exist. Left alone, avoidance widens. It also moves in the other direction.

  • Cognitive behavioral therapy: NIMH presents it as the standard psychotherapy, including exposure work aimed at bodily sensations. Evidence has accumulated through clinical trials
  • Medication: Korea's mental health portal notes that antidepressants and anti-anxiety agents are used to reduce attacks and anticipatory anxiety. What is used and how much is decided at a visit
  • Duration: the same source stresses continuing medication for a period even after symptoms settle. Cutting back or stopping on your own once you feel better can be dangerous
  • Lifestyle factors: caffeine, alcohol and short sleep are commonly cited aggravators. They are managed alongside treatment, not instead of it
  • Co-occurring problems: depression or another anxiety disorder is often present, and the plan is built to cover that too

The mechanics of paced breathing and relaxation are set out in the Anxiety and Stress Management Complete Guide. Those techniques do not substitute for treatment. If depressive symptoms are also present, the Understanding and Managing Depression guide is worth reading.

8. What the person beside you can do

Witnessing an attack is frightening too, which is why people either raise their voice in alarm or minimize the whole thing. Neither helps.

  • In a calm voice, repeat briefly that you are staying with them. Do not argue or explain
  • If the place is crowded, move together to somewhere more open. Somewhere to sit is better still
  • Once it eases, looking up an appointment together is the most practical help there is
  • If it is a first attack, or if chest pain or fainting is prominent, do not hesitate to call emergency services

And what to avoid.

  • Anything that frames it as a willpower problem. Avoidance is learned behavior, not a flaw of character
  • Breathing into a paper bag or forcing breath-holding. It can make things worse and delays finding a physical cause
  • Deciding on their behalf that it is not bad enough for a doctor. That judgment belongs to clinicians

9. Common misconceptions

Here are the things people say most often, set against what is actually the case.

What people say What is actually the case
A panic attack could actually kill you The NHS states that an attack will not cause physical harm in itself. Physical illnesses producing the same symptoms are a separate matter to check
It happens to people who are weak-willed A misfiring alarm system plus learned avoidance is the core. Willpower does not explain it, and treating it that way delays care
One attack means panic disorder NIMH states an occasional attack is not a mental disorder. Diagnosis looks at recurrence together with a month or more of change
Normal test results mean nothing is wrong It means physical causes are excluded, not that symptoms are absent. The specialty changes; the care does not end
Once you start medication you take it forever Duration varies and is set at a visit. Sources agree that stopping on your own after improvement can be risky

What could not be verified

These are the items that could not be sourced, gathered in one place, with the reason each was left out.

  • Current prevalence in Korea: the national portal cites a 0.5% lifetime and 0.2% twelve-month prevalence from a 2016 survey. No updated figure from a later survey was confirmed
  • Treated-patient counts: claims that the number is rising are widespread, but year-by-year figures were not confirmed in primary sources, so none appear here
  • Response and relapse rates: international ranges exist, but no cohort figure was confirmed. That is why no specific number is given

Frequently asked questions

Q. I had one panic attack. Does that mean I have panic disorder?

No. NIMH states that a one-time or occasional panic attack is not a mental disorder in itself. Panic disorder is considered when unexpected attacks recur and are followed by at least a month of ongoing worry or avoidance behavior. Knowing where that line sits does not let you assign yourself a label; that call belongs to a clinician who has evaluated you.

Q. How long does a panic attack usually last?

DSM-5-TR defines it as an abrupt surge reaching a peak within minutes. Seoul National University Hospital describes symptoms peaking within about 10 minutes and easing over 20 to 30, and the NHS puts most attacks between 5 and 20 minutes, with some reported up to an hour. Something holding at the same intensity for hours does not match the typical course, and is worth reporting at a visit.

Q. My chest hurts. Can I just assume it is panic?

No. Chest pain and palpitations also occur in acute coronary syndrome, arrhythmias, hyperthyroidism and asthma. Never write off chest pain you are feeling for the first time. The NHS advises calling emergency services immediately if the pain does not go away, spreads to an arm, the neck, jaw, stomach or back, or comes with sweating, nausea, light-headedness or breathlessness.

Q. Is panic disorder treatable, and what happens first?

There are approaches with established evidence. NIMH describes cognitive behavioral therapy as the standard psychotherapy, and Korea's mental health portal lists antidepressants and anti-anxiety medication alongside it. Which comes first is decided at a visit. Starting, changing or stopping medication is a decision to make with the prescriber, and stopping on your own can be dangerous.

Q. Can I use a self-check questionnaire to find out?

Not as a way of assigning yourself a diagnosis by score. The symptom list in a diagnostic manual is a definition clinicians apply in an interview, and the same symptoms can come from a physical illness, so counting does not separate them. What you can check is whether to seek care: recurring attacks, worry past a month, and a shrinking radius of activity are enough.
