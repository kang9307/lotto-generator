Introduction: When Hand Numbness Is More Than "Just Tired Hands"

Waking up at night with a numb hand, or feeling a tingling jolt in the thumb and index finger after a long stretch at the keyboard - these experiences are common, but when they recur and worsen, carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is worth considering. CTS is a peripheral nerve disorder caused by compression of the median nerve as it passes through a narrow passageway in the wrist called the carpal tunnel, and it is widely regarded as one of the most common nerve compression conditions in adults.

With computer work, smartphone use, and repetitive hand motions now part of daily life for many people, hand numbness is often dismissed as ordinary fatigue and left untreated. Left unaddressed, however, sensory loss can become persistent and thumb muscle weakness can make everyday tasks - buttoning a shirt, opening a jar lid - difficult. As of July 2026, this guide draws on publicly available medical literature to cover CTS symptoms, causes and risk factors, diagnostic methods (Phalen's test, Tinel's sign, nerve conduction studies), treatment options from splinting to surgery, self-care and prevention, and how to distinguish CTS from other conditions. This article is not a substitute for diagnosis or treatment - its purpose is to give you background so you can have a more informed conversation with your doctor.

1. What Is Carpal Tunnel Syndrome - The Median Nerve and the Carpal Tunnel

1.1 What the median nerve does

The median nerve originates in the neck, travels down the arm, passes through the wrist, and enters the hand. It carries sensation from the thumb, index finger, middle finger, and the thumb-side half of the ring finger, and also supplies motor function to part of the thenar muscles at the base of the thumb. The little finger, by contrast, is served by a different nerve (the ulnar nerve), so in CTS the little finger is typically spared from numbness. This "little finger spared" pattern is an important clue that helps distinguish CTS from other causes of hand numbness.

1.2 The structure of the carpal tunnel

The carpal tunnel is a narrow passage on the palm side of the wrist, bounded on the bottom and sides by the carpal bones and roofed by the transverse carpal ligament - a relatively rigid, confined space. The median nerve and nine finger-flexor tendons all pass through this same tunnel. Anything that raises pressure inside the tunnel or narrows the available space can compress the median nerve and produce CTS.

2. Symptoms - Where and When Numbness Occurs

2.1 Distribution of numbness and altered sensation

The most common symptoms are numbness, tingling, or a burning sensation in the thumb, index finger, middle finger, and half of the ring finger, typically sparing the little finger. Symptoms may stay confined to the fingertips or radiate up into the wrist and forearm.

2.2 Nighttime worsening and functional decline

Many people report being woken from sleep by numb hands, which is thought to happen because the wrist is easily held in a bent position during sleep. Shaking out the hand often brings temporary relief. As the condition progresses, grip strength can weaken, fine motor tasks - buttoning clothes, opening jar lids, picking up small objects - can become clumsy, and people may drop things more often. In more advanced cases, the thenar muscle at the base of the thumb can visibly waste away.

3. Causes and Risk Factors

3.1 Repetitive motion and wrist anatomy

Repetitive wrist bending, forceful gripping, and the use of vibrating hand tools have been linked to CTS. Wrist fractures or dislocations, and bony changes from arthritis, can also narrow the carpal tunnel. People who are naturally born with a smaller carpal tunnel may develop symptoms more readily under the same conditions.

3.2 Systemic conditions and hormonal factors

Diabetes, hypothyroidism, and inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis are well-recognized risk factors for CTS. During pregnancy, fluid retention can raise pressure inside the carpal tunnel and trigger symptoms, though pregnancy-related CTS often improves after delivery. Obesity is also a significant risk factor - a recent large-scale study found a markedly higher rate of CTS among obese adults without diabetes compared with non-obese adults, suggesting obesity's effect is largely independent of diabetes. Rates also tend to be higher in postmenopausal women.

3.3 Occupational and lifestyle factors

Office workers who spend long hours on a keyboard and mouse with the wrist held in an extended or flexed posture may see symptoms worsen. Higher rates have also been reported in occupations involving repetitive hand use, such as assembly-line work, cleaning, and food preparation. A family history of CTS has also been associated with increased risk.

4. Diagnosis - History, Physical Tests, and Nerve Conduction Studies

4.1 History-taking and physical examination

Diagnosis begins with a history of the symptom pattern. A physician may then perform Phalen's test (holding the wrist in a flexed position for about a minute to see if it triggers numbness) and check for Tinel's sign (lightly tapping over the wrist to see if a tingling sensation radiates into the fingers). However, more recent research indicates these two tests alone have limited diagnostic accuracy: one analysis found Phalen's test to have roughly 50% sensitivity and 33% specificity, and Tinel's sign roughly 47% sensitivity and 56% specificity - meaning neither should be relied on alone to confirm a diagnosis.

4.2 Nerve conduction studies and EMG

A nerve conduction study (NCS) measures how quickly electrical signals travel along the median nerve, objectively confirming whether and how severely the nerve is compressed at the wrist. It plays a central role in confirming the diagnosis, grading severity, and deciding whether surgery is warranted. Electromyography (EMG) may be performed alongside it to assess muscle involvement.

4.3 Clinical scoring systems and ultrasound

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) notes strong evidence that structured clinical scoring systems combining history and exam findings, such as CTS-6, can diagnose CTS in many cases without routine electrodiagnostic testing. That said, nerve conduction studies remain an important tool for confirming diagnosis, grading severity, and guiding surgical decisions. Ultrasound imaging of the median nerve can also be used as a supportive tool. The right combination of tests is a decision for your physician.

5. Treatment - From Conservative Care to Surgery

5.1 Wrist splints and activity modification

For mild to moderate cases, wrist splints that hold the wrist in a neutral position (particularly worn at night) along with reducing activities that trigger symptoms are commonly recommended as first-line treatment. That said, the evidence for the effectiveness of nighttime splinting is described as weak, and the optimal duration of use is unclear, so individual results can vary.

5.2 Medication and corticosteroid injections

Anti-inflammatory medications may provide supplementary pain relief. Corticosteroid injections into the carpal tunnel have shown relatively strong evidence of short-term symptom relief in placebo-controlled trials. However, the benefit is generally temporary - one study following patients who received combined splinting and injection found that only about 10 percent maintained symptom relief long-term. In other words, steroid injections can calm symptoms but do not eliminate the underlying nerve compression.

5.3 Surgical treatment - carpal tunnel release

When conservative treatment fails or nerve conduction studies show moderate-to-severe nerve damage, surgery (carpal tunnel release) may be considered. The procedure involves cutting the transverse carpal ligament that roofs the tunnel to create more space for the nerve, and it is one of the most commonly performed surgical procedures in the United States. It can be done via an open incision or endoscopically; the choice is made together with your surgeon. Surgery is generally regarded as the definitive treatment because it directly addresses the underlying nerve compression, though recovery time and outcomes vary by individual.

6. Self-Care and Workstation Ergonomics

6.1 Stretching and nerve gliding exercises

Gentle wrist and finger stretches, along with nerve gliding exercises designed to help the median nerve move smoothly through the tunnel, are often suggested as supportive self-care. The type and intensity of exercise should stay within a pain-free range and is best done under professional guidance.

6.2 Improving your mouse, keyboard, and workstation setup

If you do a lot of computer work, adjusting keyboard and mouse height so your wrist stays in a natural, straight line, and using a wrist rest to avoid excessive bending, can help. Taking short, frequent breaks to avoid staying in the same posture for long periods is also recommended.

7. Prevention

There is no way to guarantee prevention of CTS, but certain habits can lower the risk: taking breaks during repetitive wrist tasks, avoiding prolonged bending or extension of the wrist, and arranging your desk, keyboard, and mouse so your wrist is not under constant strain. Managing systemic risk factors such as diabetes, thyroid disease, and obesity can also contribute to lowering long-term CTS risk.

8. Distinguishing CTS from Other Conditions

Not all hand numbness is carpal tunnel syndrome. Cervical radiculopathy - nerve compression in the neck - can cause numbness radiating through the entire arm and sometimes the shoulder, often involving the whole hand including the little finger, a different pattern from CTS. Diabetic peripheral neuropathy tends to appear symmetrically in both hands and feet, in a "glove and stocking" distribution. De Quervain's tenosynovitis is a tendon condition marked more by pain on the thumb side of the wrist than numbness. Because the pattern of numbness and accompanying symptoms can point to different causes, an accurate diagnosis requires examination and any necessary testing by a qualified physician.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. What is carpal tunnel syndrome?

It is a peripheral nerve disorder caused by compression of the median nerve as it passes through a narrow passage in the wrist called the carpal tunnel. It causes numbness and tingling in the thumb, index, middle, and half of the ring finger, typically sparing the little finger. It is considered one of the most common nerve compression conditions in adults.

Q2. Can a wrist splint alone cure it?

For mild cases, nighttime splinting combined with activity modification is recommended as first-line treatment, but the evidence indicates the benefit is modest and the ideal duration of use is unclear. If symptoms persist or worsen, additional treatment may be needed, so it is best to monitor progress with a physician.

Q3. Do steroid injections work?

Placebo-controlled studies show relatively clear short-term symptom relief. However, the effect is generally temporary, and some research found long-term relief in only a minority of patients. Because it does not eliminate the underlying nerve compression, whether to repeat injections or consider surgery should be discussed with your doctor.

Q4. Is a nerve conduction study always necessary?

Because Phalen's test and Tinel's sign alone have limited diagnostic accuracy, nerve conduction studies serve as an important basis for confirming diagnosis, grading severity, and determining the need for surgery. Some cases are diagnosed using clinical scoring systems that combine history and exam findings, so the need for testing is determined by your physician.

Q5. Does surgery fully cure carpal tunnel syndrome?

Carpal tunnel release is considered the definitive treatment because it directly relieves nerve compression, and most patients see substantial improvement. However, recovery time and outcomes can vary depending on the degree of nerve damage, underlying conditions, and individual factors, so surgical candidacy and expected outcomes should be discussed with a specialist.
