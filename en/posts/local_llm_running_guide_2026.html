Running LLMs Locally - A Practical Guide to Hardware, Quantization, and Tooling

Running today's open-weight LLMs on your own machine for privacy, cost, and offline use - from the tooling stack to the pitfalls to avoid

Jul 6, 2026 Tech & IT

Intro: Why Local LLMs Deserve a Serious Look Right Now

As of July 2026, open-weight LLMs sit in a different place than they did just a couple of years ago. Families like Qwen, DeepSeek, and GLM now show up near the top of coding and reasoning benchmarks, and even OpenAI - long the standard-bearer for closed models - has shipped its own open-weight release, gpt-oss. The quality gap between "an API you pay for" and "a model you run on your own machine" has narrowed enough, for plenty of workloads, that it's no longer something you can wave away. One project that captures this shift well is jamesob/local-llm, a repository published by developer James O'Beirne. It lays out, in concrete and unvarnished detail, the hardware builds and serving stacks needed to run genuinely current models locally - everything from a roughly $2,000 two-GPU rig to a workstation with four GPUs costing tens of thousands of dollars.

This piece isn't a summary that just repeats that repository. Rather than copying one specific parts list, I want to lay out, from my own vantage point, why going local makes sense, what the real bottlenecks are, and what criteria to use when picking a tool, a quantization level, and a model. Hardware prices, VRAM capacity, and open-weight leaderboard rankings all shift on a timescale of months. So this piece deliberately avoids pinning down any one model's absolute performance numbers, and instead focuses on the things that hold up over time - what actually eats memory, and what you're trading off against what.

1. Where Local LLMs Stand Today - Open Source Is Not the Same as Open Weight

1.1 Why Everyone's Suddenly Talking About Local Models

Just two or three years ago, "local LLM" was a hobbyist's corner - barely getting a small model to run at all, with a wide gap versus cloud APIs for any real work. That's changed. Open-weight models have reached a practical level for coding, reasoning, and agentic tasks, and at the same time, growing consumer-GPU VRAM, an active secondhand hardware market, and unified-memory architectures like Apple Silicon have converged to make "run a reasonably heavy model on my own desk" a realistic option rather than a stretch. That's exactly the nerve jamesob/local-llm touched: it didn't lean on exotic datacenter gear, but showed, with a concrete build, that last-generation parts available on the secondhand market can run a genuinely capable model.

1.2 Open Source and Open Weight Are Different Words

Terminology needs sorting out here. Strictly speaking, the phrase "open-source model" is usually inaccurate. True open source would mean releasing the training data and training code as well, but most models you run locally today are open-weight: only the finished weight files are released, while the data, code, and pipeline that produced them often stay private. This distinction matters in practice because of licensing. Not all open weights come with the same terms. Meta's Llama family ships under a community license that attaches separate conditions once a service crosses a certain scale. Qwen, by contrast, uses Apache 2.0, giving relatively free commercial use, and much of the DeepSeek and GLM lineup ships under an MIT license with wide latitude for modification and redistribution. If you're evaluating local deployment, checking the license terms comes before checking performance.

2. Mapping the Stack - llama.cpp, Ollama, LM Studio, and vLLM

2.1 The Engine and the Experience Layer Are Different Things

These four names are easy to mix up when you're new to local LLMs. Here's how they sort out. llama.cpp is the inference engine itself, written in C++. Ollama and LM Studio are experience layers that wrap that engine (or MLX, on Apple Silicon) with easy installation, model management, and a chat interface. vLLM is a different animal altogether: it was designed from day one as a production serving system for many concurrent users, using techniques like PagedAttention and continuous batching to substantially raise throughput under simultaneous requests. Comparing all four as if they were interchangeable options is itself the wrong frame.

2.2 When to Reach for Which

In practice, pick based on your purpose.

3. Hardware Reality - VRAM Decides Almost Everything

3.1 A Back-of-the-Envelope Formula: Parameters x Bytes = Memory

The first wall you hit with local LLMs is VRAM. The rough math goes like this: FP16 (native precision) needs about 2 bytes per parameter, Q8 quantization needs about 1 byte, and Q4 needs about 0.5 bytes. For a 7-billion-parameter model, that's roughly 14GB at FP16, about 7GB at Q8, and about 3.5-4GB at Q4. Here's what beginners commonly miss: that math covers only the model weights - the KV cache, which grows in proportion to context length, is added on top, separately. That's exactly why VRAM headroom shrinks faster than expected once you push context length up, especially when you feed in whole documents or run a long conversation.

The real-world example in jamesob/local-llm backs this map up. On roughly a $2,000 budget, pairing two secondhand RTX 3090s (48GB total) runs a 27B-class model with real headroom to spare; at the other end, roughly $40,000 spent on four RTX 6000 Pro cards (384GB total) serves a very large model at production scale. What matters is that somewhere between those two points sits the spot that fits your own workload.

3.3 The Other Path: Unified Memory

A discrete GPU isn't the only answer. Apple Silicon shares one memory pool between CPU and GPU, so if you configure 64GB or 128GB of memory, most of that capacity is available for loading a model. Each new M-series generation brings more memory bandwidth, and a 128GB configuration can hold a 70B-class model. The accurate way to think about it: unified memory has lower bandwidth than a discrete GPU, so tokens-per-second tends to be slower, and you're trading peak throughput for large capacity plus quiet, power-efficient operation. A similar idea has shown up on the x86 side too - AMD's latest unified-memory APU platform shares 128GB between CPU and GPU, and has been credited with bringing 100GB-class local AI within reach of a mini PC under $2,000. If a discrete-GPU stack feels like too much, the unified-memory route can be a realistic alternative.

4. GGUF Quantization - Choosing Between Q4 and Q8

4.1 What Quantization Actually Does

GGUF is the quantized model format used by the llama.cpp family. Quantization reduces the number of bits used to represent weights, trading a bit of precision for memory and speed. In practice, Q8_0 sits close to the original FP16 and is widely regarded as near-lossless. Q4_K_M is the point the community widely cites as the practical sweet spot - cutting memory to less than half of FP16 while reportedly keeping quality loss limited. Drop to Q3 or below, though, and the loss curve steepens sharply, with reports of shakier reasoning, more repetition, and noticeably worse instruction-following.

4.2 What Should Guide Your Choice

The practical rule is simple. For precision-sensitive work like coding or complex reasoning, it's safer to use Q6 or higher - Q8 if your VRAM allows. For work where mistakes are cheap, like conversational summarization or drafting, Q4_K_M is often plenty. If you're torn between "a bigger model at lower quantization" and "a smaller model at higher quantization" on the same memory budget, community experience generally favors trimming parameter count slightly to reduce quantization loss, for the sake of consistency in output. That said, this isn't an absolute rule - it varies by workload, so comparing directly on a few dozen of your own real tasks is the most accurate approach.

5. Picking an Open-Weight Model - A General Map of Families

5.1 What Families Are Out There

As of mid-2026, here's a general map of the families that come up most often for local use. Llama (Meta) is the original Western open-weight line, with a thick ecosystem and documentation, but you need to check the commercial restrictions in its community license. Qwen (Alibaba) ships under Apache 2.0, giving relatively free commercial use, and is generally regarded as consistently strong on multilingual and reasoning work. DeepSeek ships under MIT and is known for reasoning and math specialization. GLM (Zhipu) ships under MIT with a clear lean toward coding and agentic work. And the fact that even gpt-oss - from a lab that long held to a closed-model policy - now exists as an open-weight release is worth reading as a signal that open weights are no longer the exception, but part of mainstream strategy.

5.2 A Golden Set Beats a Leaderboard

Here's a principle I want to keep hammering on. Public leaderboard rankings flip on a timescale of weeks. Whatever counts as "the top open-weight model" as I write this is likely to have already changed by the time you're reading it. That's exactly why this piece avoids asserting any specific model's absolute rank or score. Instead, there's one method I recommend: build a golden set of 50-100 tasks you'll genuinely repeat, and run 2-3 candidate models head-to-head at the same quantization level. A leaderboard only points a direction - the right answer for your workload lives only in your own data.

6. Versus Cloud APIs - Trade-offs and the Break-Even Point

6.1 Where Local Wins

Three things are clear. First, privacy: your prompts and data never leave the device, which is decisive when handling sensitive code or documents. Second, offline operation: you can work in an environment with no network, or where external connectivity is restricted. Third, marginal cost at high volume: unlike cloud APIs billed per token, once you own the hardware locally, the marginal cost of additional use is low, which can favor workloads that run large volumes on a constant basis.

6.2 Where Cloud Wins

Cloud clearly wins on the opposite side too. You can start immediately with no upfront investment, you get access to whatever the best current model happens to be at that moment, and there's zero operational burden around GPU maintenance, driver updates, or power management. For a light individual user, cloud pay-per-use often stays cheaper once you factor in hardware depreciation.

6.3 The Realistic Answer Is Hybrid

The answer isn't either-or. It's realistic to route sensitive or repeatedly high-volume work locally, while keeping occasional but quality-critical tasks - a complex refactor, reviewing an important document - on a cloud flagship. This split isn't fixed either; as local model quality rises and your own confidence in it builds, it's safe to gradually shift more weight toward the local side.

7. Practical Tips for Setting This Up

A few practical points worth flagging for anyone actually building this out. First, if you're running a GPU under sustained heavy load - especially alongside air conditioning in summer - your power bill can climb more than expected under a tiered residential electricity structure like Korea's, so it's worth roughly estimating your usage pattern in advance if you're planning always-on operation. Second, the secondhand GPU market is active but includes listings with parallel-import or warranty issues, so verifying provenance matters, especially for datacenter-class cards bought by an individual. Third, for organizations under strict data-handling or network-segregation rules, running locally can turn out to be the answer that satisfies compliance requirements rather than merely an option - the simple fact that data never leaves the premises helps with audits. Fourth, documentation and community discussion around local LLMs still skew heavily toward specific hubs, so a language or regional barrier can slow the first few weeks, though local community resources keep accumulating over time, so there's no need to rush. Finally, if you're running a GPU continuously in a home setting, summer heat and fan noise are real operational variables worth planning around.

8. Common Pitfalls

Here's a rundown of where people commonly trip up starting out with local LLMs. First, counting only model weights in your VRAM math and forgetting the KV-cache share that grows with context length, leading to an unexpected out-of-memory error. Second, trusting a benchmark number captured at one point in time as if it still holds months later - open-weight rankings change often. Third, over-trusting quantization and handing precision-critical work to a low-bit model anyway. Fourth, putting off security updates for your serving framework or GPU drivers - going local doesn't mean security stops mattering. Fifth, buying hardware based on a spec sheet alone without validating it against your own real workload first. Renting a cloud GPU for a day to test the same conditions before you buy is a far cheaper way to verify.

9. Conclusion - Three Things You Can Apply Today

A local LLM is no longer a hobbyist's pastime - it's become an option worth seriously weighing whenever privacy, cost, or offline operation matter for your workload. That said, I want to stress once more that the hardware prices and model rankings covered here keep moving. Here are three things worth trying today.

9.1 Run the VRAM Math Yourself

Take a candidate model's parameter count and your desired context length, and apply the back-of-the-envelope math from above directly. Confirming, in actual numbers, whether it fits on your GPU and how much headroom shrinks as you extend context is the first step.

9.2 Pick Tools by Purpose, Quantization by Workload

Start with Ollama or LM Studio if you're using it solo, and look at vLLM once you move to a service with multiple simultaneous users. Push quantization higher for precision-critical work, and lower it to save VRAM where the cost of a mistake is low.

9.3 Choose a Model by Your Own Golden Set, Not the Leaderboard

Use public rankings as a reference, not gospel - compare candidate models directly on roughly 50 tasks you'll genuinely repeat. That result is a more accurate basis than any leaderboard.
