Turning Division Into Multiplication Is Something the Compiler Already Does - The Three Places a Human Still Wins, and What They Cost

The new thing is not the multiplication but three pieces of information: domain, reordering, identity

2026-09-09 Dev & Infra

Almost every conversation about arithmetic optimization arrives at the same sentence. Division is expensive, so replace it with multiplication. The sentence is true. But carrying only that sentence forward drops the most important part of it. If the divisor is a constant fixed at compile time, the replacement has already happened without anyone asking for it. The major compilers do not emit a divide instruction there.

So where does a hand-written optimization actually win? That is a far narrower and far more useful question. The answer lies not in multiplication but in information. What a compiler lacks is not capability. It lacks knowledge of how far the input actually ranges, the freedom to choose a shape for the expressions, and awareness of an algebraic identity that holds only in this particular problem.

Code that splits a timestamp into hours, minutes and seconds is nearly an ideal test case for that line. The divisors are the constants 60 and 3600, the domain is finite, and a naive reference that gives the right answer fits in three lines. So you can count exactly what belongs to the compiler, what belongs to the human, and how much collateral is pledged against the human part.

There are no cycle counts or latency figures here. Those values shift with microarchitecture, operand width and compiler version, and a number stripped of its conditions becomes false the moment it is repeated. What follows are properties, conditions and verification methods instead. The numbers for your environment come out of your environment.

The trigger - code that splits one day of seconds into three

The problem on the table is narrow. Take a single count of seconds since midnight and produce three values: hour, minute and second. Dates are out of scope. Leaving leap seconds aside, the input runs from 0 to 86,399 and the output never exceeds 23:59:59.

The textbook code for this comes in two shapes. They produce the same answer, but the dependency relationships between the expressions are different.

/* Sequential - each step waits on the result of the one before it */
unsigned hour   = t / 3600;
unsigned rem    = t % 3600;
unsigned minute = rem / 60;
unsigned second = rem % 60;

/* Independent - three values computed separately, at the cost of more divisions */
unsigned hour   = t / 3600;
unsigned minute = (t / 60) % 60;
unsigned second = t % 60;

The sequential form uses fewer operations but strings them into one long chain. Nothing can start on minute before rem exists, and the final subtraction waits on minute. The independent form breaks the chain but repeats the same division. Reducing the operation count and breaking the chain pull in opposite directions, and that tension defines the character of the problem.

All of that is old ground. What is newly on the table is the observation that an arrangement exists which breaks the chain without adding operations, plus two techniques that replace the remainder operation itself with something else. Sorting those techniques into the right boxes is the body of this article.

Why integer division alone behaves differently

Addition, multiplication and division all get filed under arithmetic, but they behave differently in hardware. Division is expensive because it is not a circuit that produces a result in one shot, but an iterative structure that settles one digit of the quotient at a time. Fixing any digit requires the digit above it to be fixed already.

Three consequences follow from that property.

  • Full pipelining is hard. An adder or a multiplier can accept the next operation before the previous one finishes. An iterative core cannot overlap that freely, so a line of divisions waits on itself.
  • The cost is not a fixed number. For the same instruction, some implementations take longer depending on operand width and even on the magnitude of the values. A 32-bit divide and a 64-bit divide do not carry the same price tag.
  • The situation differs by architecture. Some instruction sets have no integer division instruction at all. There a division becomes a library call, and the cost stops being a story about one instruction.

The third point gets forgotten most often. The claim that division is expensive is not true to the same degree on every chip. Which is why no figure here should travel without its conditions attached.

That last row is the premise of the next section. The fact that division is expensive is not by itself grounds for an optimization. It becomes grounds only when the division is actually present in the machine code.

A constant divisor already becomes a multiply in the compiler

This is the heart of the article. Plenty of people believe that writing t / 3600 puts a divide instruction into the machine code. Mostly it does not.

The principle is simple. Dividing by a constant D is the same as multiplying by 1/D. Integer arithmetic has no fractions, so the code multiplies by an approximation of 1/D scaled up by a power of two, then shifts the scale back out afterwards. That is fixed-point reciprocal multiplication, and this transformation for invariant divisors was worked out in the 1990s and became a standard item in compiler optimization.

The rule for producing the constant is just as simple. At a width of b bits, the multiplier is the quotient of 2b by D, plus one. Every constant that appears in time-of-day conversion comes out of exactly that rule.

  • 71582789 — the quotient of 232 by 60, plus one
  • 1193047 — the quotient of 232 by 3600, plus one
  • 2443359173 — the quotient of 243 by 3600, plus one
  • 37283 and 2185 — the quotients of 227 by 3600 and of 217 by 60, each plus one

What matters is that these constants are not hand-invented values but outputs of a rule. The compiler builds its own constants from the same rule. It simply computes under one disadvantage a human does not have: it must be correct across the entire type. If the input is a 32-bit unsigned integer, the compiler has to pick a multiplier and a shift width that hold for every value from 0 to 4,294,967,295, and so it works at a wider intermediate width than a person would need to choose.

Why that constraint does not lift is equally clear. A compiler sees the type of a variable, not the history of the values that variable actually holds. Looking at one function alone, there is no telling what an argument will be, and even with every caller in view the range is settled only by following the callers of those callers. So the compiler always allows generous room on the safe side. That is not laziness but a requirement to be correct, and it is exactly why a place for the human to win exists at all.

The boundary between what a compiler does and what it does not turns out to be sharper than expected. The author of the article that prompted this observed the following. Two widely used compilers will fuse t / 60 / 60 into t / 3600, but only when the code is written with subtraction and multiplication rather than the remainder operator. A third compiler failed to perform that optimization in every case tested, and one just-in-time compiler did not recognize the reordered form of the expressions at all.

Which means the same source becomes a different program depending on the tool. The author reaches the same conclusion: since the approach leans on the compiler being clever and that is not always so, writing it in the plainly reordered form is likely preferable.

The author also notes that in an ideal world the compiler would handle all of this, adding that a hint about the valid input range would let the compiler do the rest. But that hint is not a standardized contract. The GCC documentation defines the unreachability builtin purely as a marker meaning the program is undefined if control flow reaches this point, and does not describe it as a way to communicate a value range. A widely used idiom and a documented guarantee are two different things.

Then where does the human win

Invert the previous section and the answer appears. What a compiler lacks is not arithmetic capability but information, and there are only three kinds a human can sell.

  • Domain — the fact that the input only ever arrives inside a far narrower range. One day of seconds has 86,400 possibilities. A compiler has no way to learn this, so it emits code that fits the whole type. A human can write code that is correct only over the narrow range and shorter in exchange.
  • Reordering — laying the expressions out so the three results do not wait on each other. Mathematically it is an equivalence, so nothing is risked, but which compilers recognize that equivalence varies. In other words, the human advantage here comes not from mathematics but from variance between tools.
  • Identity — an algebraic fact that holds only in this problem. The transform that turns a remainder into a bit mask belongs here. There is no reason for a compiler to know such an identity, and no reason it should have to.

The important part is that the three have different characters. Reordering is free. The expressions are equivalent, so the answer does not change for any input. Domain and identity, by contrast, pledge collateral. They are correct only under the condition that values arriving at that function keep a promise. That promise is not written in the code; it usually lives in someone's head.

Which splits the order of work in practice. Reordering can be done up front; domain and identity wait until there is a reason to measure. Not because the first is reversible and the second is not, but because the second sends an invoice to future callers.

Classifying the techniques against that standard

The article that prompted this presents three forms. Dropping each into the three boxes above separates what is new from what was already there.

The first - pure reordering

/* Two divisions made independent of each other; the rest is a multiply and a subtract */
unsigned tmin   = t / 60;      /* total minutes since midnight */
unsigned hour   = t / 3600;
unsigned second = t - tmin * 60;
unsigned minute = tmin - hour * 60;

No constants, no magic. The two divisions no longer wait on each other, and once both are available the remaining two values each finish with one multiply and one subtract. The author describes this shape as an arrangement that does not come to mind immediately, because the minute is now computed entirely from two intermediate results rather than directly from the time. That is an accurate diagnosis. People generally try to derive each output straight from the input.

The value of this form is that it assumes nothing at all. It gives the same answer as the naive implementation for every input. It is the only improvement here obtained without pledging collateral.

The second - fixed point bought by narrowing the domain

/* 32-bit fixed point. The high half is the quotient, the low half is
   the fractional progress toward the next whole step.
     1193047  = (2^32) / 3600 + 1
     71582789 = (2^32) / 60   + 1                                      */
uint64_t hprd = (uint64_t) t * 1193047u;
uint32_t hlow = (uint32_t) hprd;
uint32_t mlow = (uint32_t) ((uint64_t) t * 71582789u);

unsigned hour   = (unsigned) (hprd >> 32);
unsigned minute = (unsigned) (((uint64_t) hlow * 60) >> 32);
unsigned second = (unsigned) (((uint64_t) mlow * 60) >> 32);

The idea runs like this. In the 64-bit result of multiplying by the scaled reciprocal, the high half is the quotient and the low half is a fraction expressing how far along you are toward the next whole step. Multiply that fraction by 60 and take the high half again, and out come the remaining minutes or seconds. Rather than computing the remainder separately, this recycles the discarded half of a multiplication that already happened.

The cost is the domain. There is an upper bound below which this computation holds at 32-bit width, and above it the answers change. The valid range the author states runs from 0 to 2,255,818. That is far wider than one day of seconds, so it is generous for the original purpose, but the bound appears nowhere in the code.

The third - an identity that turns the remainder into a bit mask

/* Identity:  x mod D  ==  (x + c*(x/D)) mod (D + c),   with c = 4     */
/* 60 plus 4 is 64, so the remainder operation becomes & 63            */
uint32_t tmin = (uint32_t) (((uint64_t) t * 71582789u) >> 32);
uint32_t hour = (uint32_t) (((uint64_t) t * 1193047u)  >> 32);

unsigned second = (t    + 4 * tmin) & 63;
unsigned minute = (tmin + 4 * hour) & 63;

To get a remainder modulo 60, multiply the quotient by four, add it, then take the remainder modulo 64 and you land on the same value. Since 64 is a power of two, that remainder is a single mask keeping the low six bits. And the shape x + 4*y expresses as one instruction on both of the major architectures, through an address computation instruction on one and a shifted add on the other.

This identity does not hold for arbitrary values. It is true only inside a range where the quotient and remainder keep a particular relationship, and the range the author states runs from 0 to 2,257,198. The fact that this bound differs from the previous technique's bound matters shortly.

The classification table

Read the table down its first column and the conclusion appears. Turning division into multiplication is not where the human wins. That slot is already filled. The human wins in the four rows below it, and of those, only one wins without an assumption. The rest are all obtained by pledging the domain as collateral.

The last row deserves separate attention. When vector instructions are in play, widening an intermediate result to 64 bits reduces how many values fit in one vector. So on a vector path, a narrower constant beats a more accurate one. Using a narrow multiplier shrinks the valid range considerably in exchange for keeping the lanes. On architectures with wider vectors, the author writes, the earlier forms win again. Which code is best on which architecture is not a fixed ranking.

The cost - what gets pledged as collateral

Thinking of the cost of an optimization as merely the opposite of speed misses half of it. Most of what gets paid here is billed in the future.

CostConcretely whatWhen the bill arrivesHow to head it off
Domain assumptionA narrow multiplier returns different values above its boundWhen the function gets reused for another purposeNail the bound into the function name and a comment, and add a range assertion
Width of the intermediatesThe product of the multiplier needs somewhere to liveWhen porting to a 32-bit target or into vector codeState the intermediate width in the types and compare exhaustively there
PortabilityWhether the high half of a wide multiply is cheap differs by architectureWhen porting to a different chipKeep the naive path as an alternate and choose at build time
Verification cost for the readerWhy a mask equals a remainder is not settled from the code aloneEvery time that code has to be changedLeave the identity and the range in a comment, tests next to it
Tool dependenceThe same source becomes different machine code per compilerWhen the toolchain or optimization level changesRe-examine machine code and measurements per toolchain

The fourth row is the most underrated. Losing the reader's ability to verify is also a cost. Naive code shows its mistakes to the eye. Masks and multipliers can be wrong without showing anything, so whoever comes to change it either leaves it alone or changes it and cannot tell. This cost is billed repeatedly for as long as the code lives.

An invoice of the same shape shows up elsewhere. In an implementation squeezed to an extreme budget, deleting a data structure also deletes the validation that structure was quietly performing, and that pattern shows up more sharply in the attempt to fit an interpreter into 1,024 bytes. One thing was removed and two were lost, which is the same shape.

What happens at the first value past the range

The danger of a domain assumption stays abstract if it is left as a phrase. Because the domain is finite, you can count exactly what happens at the value just past the stated bound. And the result differs by technique.

TechniqueStated boundValue past itTrue answerWhat the technique returnsCharacter of the failure
Narrow-width fixed point2,255,8182,255,819626:36:59626:37:59Silent. All three fields are legal, so no assertion catches it
Remainder as a bit mask2,257,1982,257,199626:59:59627:63:59Visible. A minute of 63 is caught by a single range assertion
Narrow multipliers to keep lanesA little past one day115,20032:00:00A different valueThe bound is far lower. Reuse it for elapsed time and you meet it early

The difference between the second row and the first is where practice splits. A failure that puts 63 in the minute field gets caught somewhere, without fail. Display code padding to two digits goes strange, a validator rejects it, or a person notices it on screen. A time one minute off, by contrast, goes into the log as is, gets stored as is and gets aggregated as is. Both violated the same kind of assumption, yet discovery comes months apart.

One practical rule falls out of this. A function that assumes a range should state that range as an assertion. If the person who knows the bound at which the optimization holds does not leave that bound in the code, the knowledge disappears the moment that person leaves the team. Write down a value without the condition attached to it, and later the condition quietly falls off on its own.

The third row shows the reuse trap. A function that splits a time of day is very tempting to reuse for splitting an elapsed duration. The shape is identical. But a constant chosen on the premise of one day of seconds collapses on a duration spanning several days. What traps the clock itself already carries when elapsed time is involved is laid out separately in the piece that separates monotonic clocks from wall clocks.

What it takes for the sentence it got faster to hold

A speed claim becomes a proposition only when conditions are attached. A number passed along without them is generally false by the time it reaches the next person. At minimum four things travel together.

What must be presentWhat happens without itConcrete form
Correctness comparisonCode that is fast and wrong gets called fastCompare the whole domain against a naive implementation. Since the domain is finite, do it exhaustively rather than by sampling
Machine code comparisonYou cannot tell whether the division left the source or left the machine codeUse a compiler explorer or a disassembler to check for divide instructions, at each optimization level
Stated environmentIt does not reproduceCompiler and version, optimization flags, target architecture, operating system
Which axis is measuredYou measure throughput while calling it latencyFor latency, feed each result into the next computation as an input so parallel execution is blocked

That last item collapses most often on this topic. Run several mutually independent computations inside a loop and the execution units overlap them. The figure that comes out then is not how long one operation takes to finish, but how many finish per unit of time. The two can move in opposite directions. An optimization that breaks a chain can gain a great deal on latency while gaining little or even losing on throughput.

The measurement method in the article that prompted this addresses that distinction explicitly. For latency it feeds each result into the next computation through an exclusive-or to block parallel execution, and measures throughput separately. Random values are loaded into memory in advance, several runs are made and the median is taken. On the vector path it attempts auto-vectorization, and the author notes that one compiler emitted vector code and the other did not. This is about the same source.

Three kinds of machine were used: a recent laptop-class processor, a high-performance desktop processor, and a small 32-bit board. The last one carries real methodological weight, because the ranking changes where the high half of a 64-bit multiply is not cheaply available. Without those three axes, a conclusion that fits one environment gets carried away as a general proposition.

On the board where the original was shared, a short report appeared saying that in another language the compiler erased the differences. No conditions were given, so it cannot be treated as established, but the direction matches the previous section. Which tool does what on your behalf is what flips the result.

What a finite domain means

This topic has a property most optimizations lack. The input is finite, and small enough to count. One day of seconds has 86,400 possibilities. The extended ranges above sit in the low millions. Which is to say that exhaustive comparison, not sampled verification, is available.

/* The domain is finite, so compare all of it rather than a sample */
for (uint32_t t = 0; t <= LIMIT; t++) {
    hms fast = fast_hms(t);
    hms ref  = { t / 3600, (t / 60) % 60, t % 60 };   /* the naive one is the answer */

    if (fast.h != ref.h || fast.m != ref.m || fast.s != ref.s) {
        printf("first mismatch at %u\n", t);
        return 1;
    }
}
/* Set LIMIT generously above the claimed range so you also see how the
   first value past the boundary goes wrong. A test that only walks the
   passing range says nothing about whether the function is safe to reuse. */

This property raises the whole standard of verification. On a finite domain, running a sample and calling it correct is lowering your own grounds. The article that prompted this is clear on the point. The repository states that its tests compare the full valid range of each algorithm against a naive reference, and for the leap-second variant the author writes plainly that there is no formal proof of the mathematics and that it was checked exhaustively over one day's range.

Leaving that admission in place is the most worth-copying thing in the piece. Exhaustive comparison is not a proof. On a finite domain the two become practically the same, but the moment the domain changes, a comparison guarantees nothing. Code that marks that boundary for itself and code that does not have different reuse safety.

There is a rule about the order of verification too. Verify before changing values, and do not change them again after verifying. Introduce an optimized path while also touching input normalization and there is no telling which side was the cause. What becomes void when that order is inverted is set out in the piece on normalization and validation order.

Where it pays and where it does not

The same optimization is meaningful in some code and meaningless in other code. What decides is not the elegance of the technique but how many times that code gets called.

Where it pays:

  • Log formatting — a time stamped on every line. In a system writing tens of thousands of lines per second, this becomes a constant cost on the logging path.
  • Bulk time series conversion — a batch turning millions of timestamp rows into readable form. The same function is called once per row.
  • Rendering and serialization — one table stamping tens of thousands of times. Being called far more often than what is on screen is common.

Where it does not pay:

  • Paths called a handful of times per request — the gain is smaller than the measurement noise. Trading naive code for masks and multipliers here leaves only the cost.
  • Paths already blocked on something else — trimming arithmetic in code that waits on storage or the network only lengthens the waiting.
  • Paths where the next stage is heavier — after hours, minutes and seconds are computed, the digits still have to become characters written into a buffer. If that stage is larger, shrinking the front barely moves the whole. Which side is larger is not settled before measuring.

The third item catches people most often on this topic. Looking at the whole function that turns a time into a string, the division is only one part of it. To optimize a part and claim the whole got faster, the whole has to be measured.

And code that handles time often has problems that come before arithmetic cost. Which zone and which rules interpret the same second value changes the result, and those rules are updated as data rather than as code. The way a time zone data update also changes how past timestamps read is that axis. Move on to days of the week or to dates and the character of the computation changes entirely, which is covered separately in the piece on putting a calendar into code.

Measure first, then change

Arranged as a sequence, it comes to five steps. Not a list of techniques but an order of judgment.

  • First, measure whether that path is near the top. Fixing a function that does not stand out in a profile is a hobby, not an optimization. Skip this step and the remaining four are all waste.
  • Second, look at whether a division survives in the machine code. If it does not, the work at that spot is already finished. With a constant divisor, it usually ends here.
  • Third, apply the improvement that assumes nothing. That is the reordering that breaks the dependency chain. It pledges no collateral, so nothing is lost if it has to be undone.
  • Fourth, if that is still not enough, mine the domain. Collateral is pledged from here on, so write the valid range into the code and put the exhaustive comparison into the repository alongside it. A narrow constant that goes in without tests is a defect with a scheduled date.
  • Fifth, measure again. Compare before and after under the same conditions, and look at latency and throughput separately. Without this step, the previous four were just work that made the code harder.

The step most often skipped is the second. Knowledge that division is expensive is widespread; the habit of checking whether that division is already gone is not nearly as widespread. So people redo work the compiler had already done, and take on a fresh domain assumption as the price.

Put together, it comes to this. What the article that prompted this actually demonstrated is not that multiplication is cheaper than division. It is that the only point at which a human beats a compiler is holding information that is not written in the code, and that the moment you use that information, an obligation to write it into the code arrives with it. Left unwritten, the optimization becomes a timer set for whoever comes next.

What could not be confirmed

  • Cycle counts, latency and throughput figures — no vendor optimization manual was consulted, so no such number was used. What remains is the qualitative statement that integer division costs more than addition or multiplication and that the cost varies with architecture and operand width.
  • The emitted machine code — the forms above were not compiled to see which instructions come out. The account of how compilers handle constant divisors rests on the widely known transformation rule and on the original author's observations. The result on your own toolchain is settled only by compiling it there.
  • Reproducing the original benchmarks — the repository was not built to see whether the same figures appear. The measurement conditions above restate the methodology the original states.
  • A standard way to give a range hint — a widely used idiom exists for telling a compiler about value ranges, but the way the GCC documentation defines that builtin stops at marking unreachability. Whether the assumption attribute added in recent standards actually draws out this kind of optimization was not compared.
  • Why the leap-second variant holds — the author of the original states plainly that there is no formal proof of the mathematics and that it was checked exhaustively over one day's range. Nothing beyond that is claimed here either.
  • Reproduction in other languages and runtimes — one short report exists on the board, but no compiler version, flags or measurement method were given, so it was not used as evidence.
  • How large a share this conversion takes in a real system — what percentage of a logging or time series path this function occupies differs by system, and there are no grounds for offering a general figure. So what appears above is only the order in which to measure that share.
