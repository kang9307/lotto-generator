Designers Are Fighting the Wrong Battle - Where Real Value Is Moving in the AI Era

It's not time to sharpen the knife (craft) - it's time to pick up the gun (strategy). Problem framing and judgment are the last skills left.

Jul 24, 2026 Trends & Culture

Intro: Which Way Are Designers Actually Pointing Their Knives?

In July 2026, an essay from UX Collective made the rounds on hada.io (GeekNews) and shook up design-industry timelines. The title alone is provocative: "Designers are sharpening knives for the wrong fight." Author Dan Maccarone argues that at a moment when AI can spit out images, UI, and even working prototypes in seconds, the entire industry is still obsessing over the old question of "how do we build well."

This piece isn't a summary of that essay. Taking Maccarone's metaphor as a starting point, I want to work through, in my own analysis, what battle designers are actually fighting right now, why that battle may be missing the point, and where real competitive value is genuinely shifting. My conclusion up front: I agree with much of Maccarone's diagnosis, but with one important caveat attached.

1. What the Original Piece Argues - the Knife and the Gun

1.1 Execution Is the Knife, Strategy Is the Gun

Maccarone's metaphor is simple but sharp. The knife (craft) stands for execution skill - code quality, visual polish, UI finish, production standards. It's a close-range weapon a team can hone and control internally. The gun (strategy), by contrast, is the strategic call on who you're building for, what, and why - a long-range weapon aimed at the real opponent: the market. He notes that craft only ever fights at close range, while the market never gets any closer. Polishing execution to perfection doesn't matter if the aim was wrong to begin with.

1.2 The Paradox of AI Making Building Nearly Free

When building was expensive, "you get one shot" was a constraint that automatically forced strategic scrutiny. But once AI drops implementation cost close to zero, a paradox kicks in. Endless iteration becomes possible, every output looks polished enough to feel like progress, yet the pressure to decide what to stop building - and what to cut - quietly disappears. Maccarone sums this up in one line: the model will perfectly build something nobody wants.

1.3 What Three Case Studies Show

The original piece backs this with examples. One organization spent two and a half years rebuilding a voice interface, a web app, and a problem-solving engine four separate times, each rebuild more polished than the last - and never found a customer. By contrast, Maccarone's own bar business, constrained to $150,000 and six weeks, forced strategic thinking: he personally visited six neighboring bars and found a genuine gap in the market - a living-room-like space where neighbors could actually linger. The media startup Skift under Rafat Ali tells a similar story: it started with four pillars (aggregation, curation, syndication, original reporting) but narrowing down to two, based on what actually resonated, was itself the strategy.

2. The Battle Designers Are Actually Fighting Right Now

Overlay this diagnosis onto current design-community discourse and it feels familiar. As of 2026, designer reactions to AI generally fall into three camps.

2.1 Tool Rejection and a Renewed Emphasis on "Craft"

Criticism that AI-generated concepts feel "soulless" or "all look the same" keeps recurring. In response, some designers push hand-drawn sketches, manual prototyping, and artisanal detail back to the front of their identity - treating execution texture as the last line of defense.

2.2 Copyright and Training-Data Protests

Protests that image- and UI-generation models were trained on work scraped without consent remain fierce. In the U.S., lawsuits by artists and photographers against companies like Stability AI are still working through the courts, and demands to shift to opt-in consent continue. These are legitimate grievances.

2.3 Debates Over Standards and Process

There's also active debate over how to adapt design systems, component standards, and review processes for the AI era - questions like "on what basis do we approve AI-generated output" fall here.

All three camps have their reasons. But borrowing Maccarone's metaphor, all three largely stay within the territory of the "knife" - how to build well, how to defend execution - rather than moving toward the "gun."

3. Why This Fight May Be Missing the Point

3.1 Why Taste Becomes the Scarce Resource

Why might the execution-focused fight miss the point? Because the more AI eliminates the cost of execution, the more the sole remaining bottleneck becomes the eye for deciding what's worth building at all - taste and judgment. This isn't unique to design. Wes McKinney, creator of the pandas data library, reached a strikingly parallel conclusion writing about the era of AI coding agents. When agents generate "accidental complexity at machine speed," he argues, the ability to hold a system's conceptual coherence in your head and decide what to add and what to leave out becomes the scarce resource once labor constraints vanish. Doers multiply; deciders don't. The same structure repeats across both design and coding.

3.2 Five Places Where Value Is Actually Moving

Here is how I'd map the shift.

  • Problem framing - deciding what counts as the problem worth solving. Perfectly solving the wrong problem is waste.
  • User understanding - primary research and interpretive skill about who actually wants what, in what context.
  • Systems and information design - coherently structuring an entire journey and its data/feature architecture, not just one screen.
  • Decision-making - choosing which of many options to push forward and which to cut, and persuading the organization of the reasoning.
  • Taste and judgment - the eye to sort the genuinely worth-building signal out of the flood of "good enough"-looking AI output.

None of these five are execution skills for drawing a screen - all of them are judgment that wraps around execution, both before and after it.

4. Where Value Lands When Tools Get Cheap - Same Structure as Yesterday

Interestingly, this structure isn't unique to design. It shares an identical skeleton with two pieces we published the day before.

4.1 Parallel With "The AI Wrapper Is Dead"

As covered in The AI Wrapper Is Dead, a startup that layers a thin prompt and UI on top of a model API loses its defensibility for the exact reason it was easy to build in the first place. What survives is the team that owns the entire workflow and designs what to automate versus what to leave to humans. Design is no different: drawing one pretty screen gets replaced by AI in seconds, but designing which journeys to automate and which decisions to leave to people does not.

4.2 Parallel With "Data Is Your Only Moat"

The argument in Data Is Your Only Moat tracks the same logic. As models and features flatten out, defensibility comes from what accumulates on top - judgment data built up through use, repeated feedback. Translate that into design: the more AI mass-produces concepts, the more defensibility comes from the accumulated standard of "what counts as good design for this organization" - and the people who know how to set that standard.

4.3 One Structure Running Through All Three Pieces

The pattern is singular. The more AI erases cost at the execution layer, the further upstream the priced seat moves - toward "deciding what to do." Whether it's startup strategy, data moats, or design, whenever tools get cheap, value always converges on the point where judgment intervenes.

5. What Designers Should Actually Change

5.1 Absorb AI as a Tool, Not a Threat

It's more realistic to absorb generative AI as a tool for fast drafts than to treat it as something to reject. Cut the time spent on repetitive concepts, variations, and minor component production, and reinvest the time saved into problem framing and user validation.

5.2 Move Engagement Upstream

Staying purely in downstream execution - drawing screens - carries the highest replacement risk. Engaging earlier and more deeply in research design, product strategy discussions, and systems design builds defensibility. This isn't so much a job expansion as a return to how good design used to get made in the first place.

5.3 Manage Craft and Judgment as Separate Muscles

Execution quality (craft) and strategic choice (judgment) are different muscles. Craft can be substantially augmented by AI tools, but judgment can atrophy without deliberate practice. Building the habit of asking "what happens if we don't build this" before committing to a project - the exact discipline Maccarone emphasizes - is the practical training that keeps that muscle alive.

6. The Counterargument - Copyright and Labor Are Still Legitimate Fights

Some balance is needed here. Stretching Maccarone's argument into "craft debates and copyright protests are meaningless" would be a misreading. Training-data consent, fair compensation, and job displacement are not secondary issues irrelevant to industry survival - they are serious fights in their own right. The fact that the U.S. Copyright Office has ruled that fully AI-generated work isn't eligible for copyright, and that artist groups keep pushing for a shift to opt-in consent, reflects real, legitimate stakes. What I want to flag isn't "fight this instead of that" - it's a matter of priority: getting locked into only this fight risks missing the bigger question that actually determines the industry's survival. The two fights can, and should, run in parallel.

Conclusion: Keep the Knife, But Pick Up the Gun Too

As of July 2026, the debate around designers still centers on "how much will AI replace our jobs." But layering Maccarone's diagnosis with my own observations points to a more precise question: as execution cost approaches zero, who inside an organization actually knows how to judge what's worth building? Craft that upholds standards and texture is still necessary, but it's no longer the only weapon. Legitimate protests over copyright and labor should continue, but they alone won't secure the industry's next decade. Real competitive value is shifting toward problem framing, user understanding, systems design, decision-making, and the taste to know what to cut. Keep sharpening the knife. But pick up the gun too - that's the only way to win the fight that actually matters.
