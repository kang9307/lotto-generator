Asking AI About Money - The Six Fields That Decide the Answer, and the US-Default Trap

Blank fields get filled by the model, quietly, and its default is usually the US tax code

Aug 3, 2026 Finance

Introduction: The answer sounds right, but you cannot tell whether it fits you

Ask an AI assistant "I am in my thirties with a salaried job, how should I handle my money?" and you will get a fairly consistent reply. Build an emergency fund, use your tax-advantaged retirement accounts, diversify, hold for the long run. None of that is wrong. It is arguably better than what most people actually do.

The trouble starts one step later. Nothing in that conversation lets you check whether the answer fits you. The model filled in everything you did not say: what retirement age it assumed, what return it penciled in, what interest rate it imagined on your loan, and which country's tax rules it treated as normal. Those values are not printed anywhere in the reply, and unless you ask, they never surface.

So this article is not about picking products. It is about constructing the question: what to include so the answer becomes usable, what to omit at your peril, and where to check the answer once you have it. The short version is this. In AI financial advice, the biggest variable is not the model. It is the input. And for anyone outside the United States there is a second one on top: leave out your jurisdiction and the answer drifts into US retirement law.

1. The question, not the model, drove the result

"Is AI financial advice any good?" has been argued about for years, and a 2026 paper moved the argument onto a different axis. "AI Financial Advice: Supply, Demand, and Life Cycle Implications" by Taha Choukhmane, Tim de Silva, Weidong Lin and Matthew Akuzawa treated the variable of interest not as model capability but as the questions real people write.

The design is clever. The researchers had 1,000 adults write their own prompts asking a large language model for spending and investing guidance. Not researcher-polished questions, but the way ordinary people actually phrase things. The resulting advice was then fed into a life cycle model of financial decisions covering ages 22 to 89.

The headline result is favourable. Following the AI's advice moved people closer to the standard life cycle benchmark than their observed real-world behaviour. Saving during working years went up, participation in diversified equity funds went up, and equity exposure came down after age 45. Nothing there contradicts long-established financial planning practice.

The interesting part is the dispersion. The same models produced measurably different outcomes depending on who wrote the question.

Who wrote the promptWealth difference at 60PercentageWhat the study attributed it to
Lower financial literacyabout $50,000 less4.11%How much information the prompt carried
No prior experience asking AI for financial helpabout $100,000 less5.71%How the question was structured
Prompts written by womenabout $60,000 less than men's4.10%Lower recommended equity exposure. Roughly two thirds of the gap traced to phrasing

All three rows are input differences, not model differences. The last row matters most. If two thirds of the gender gap came from phrasing, then two thirds of it is recoverable by rewriting the question. That is a variable an individual actually controls.

One more finding from the study is immediately practical. Liquidity appeared in 83 percent of LLM responses but in only 6 percent of the prompts people wrote. The model almost always cares about when and how much you can withdraw. The person asking almost never mentions it. The model closes that gap with its own default.

The research was equally clear about limits. The AI advice handled abrupt shocks such as job loss poorly and did almost no active rebalancing, leaning instead on simple savings heuristics. Both of those come back in the section on where humans have to take over.

2. The six fields a good question has to carry

Translate the study's notion of a "structured" prompt into actual sentences and you get six items. None of them are hard. They are simply the things people do not bother writing down.

#FieldWhat to writeWhat the answer becomes if you leave it blank
1Age and target dateCurrent age, intended retirement age, large planned expenses in betweenArbitrary defaults slip in, such as retirement at 65 and life expectancy of 85
2IncomeMonthly take-home pay, employment type, variability from bonuses or freelance workA savings rate premised on a stable salary
3DebtBalance, interest rate, repayment structure, remaining termThe model cannot order repayment against investing, and recommends investing even over high-rate debt
4When the money is neededSix months or twenty years. Say whichA generic portfolio indifferent to horizon
5Risk toleranceNot "I can handle risk" but a number: would you sell at a 30% drawdown or hold?The allocation tilts either too aggressive or too defensive
6Tax and regulatory jurisdictionCountry of residence, currency of income, accounts you actually holdA US-shaped answer. Section 4 covers this in detail
+LiquidityHow much you must be able to pull out, and within how many daysThe item users omitted most in the study. The model substitutes its own default

Of the six, the two that go missing most often and change the answer most are number 3 and number 4. Without a debt interest rate the model has no benchmark to compare an expected return against. Without a spending date, money you need for a deposit in three months gets a long-horizon allocation. Both failures produce answers that read perfectly well, which is exactly what makes them dangerous.

Field 5 lives or dies on wording. "I can tolerate risk" conveys nothing to the model. Write "I can sit through a 30 percent drawdown within a year. At 40 percent I think I would sell" and the allocation in the reply genuinely changes.

3. Bad questions and the specific defects they produce

Sorting the failures by type is faster than arguing them one by one. The six below look different on the surface, but they fail for the same reason: too many blanks left for the model to fill.

  • 1. "What should I buy right now?" — No conditions at all. You get either a list of generalities or, worse, a plausible-sounding product name. Individual product and security recommendations are not a job for a chatbot in the first place; they sit inside a regulated activity
  • 2. "How should I invest $100,000?" — An amount with no horizon and no purpose. The same sum needs opposite allocations if it is spent next year versus left alone for twenty. With no distinction offered, the model picks the middle
  • 3. "How do I earn a return safely?" — The requirements contradict each other. The reply lists deposits and equity products side by side with no ordering. You finish reading no better informed
  • 4. "I am a salaried thirty-something, how should I manage my money?" — This is the shape people actually used in the study. It has an age band and nothing else: no debt, no liquidity, no horizon. Someone with zero emergency savings gets a long-term investing plan
  • 5. "Which pension account is the better deal?" — No jurisdiction. Asked from outside the US, you can still get an answer built on 401(k) and Roth IRA mechanics. If the reader does not notice, the whole plan inherits the error
  • 6. "This is right, isn't it?" — A conclusion already decided, seeking agreement. Models tend to carry forward whatever premise the user supplied, so this amplifies rather than checks. If you want a real test, ask in the opposite direction

Number 6 is the one people miss most. Replace "is this plan okay?" with "if this plan fails, what are the three most likely reasons?" and the same conversation yields entirely different content. Flipping the direction of the question is how you obtain a counterargument. If you want to sharpen prompt structure generally, there is a separate collection of prompt templates by task.

4. Where it breaks hardest outside the US: the default jurisdiction

Here is why field 6 deserves its own section. Given the composition of English-language training data the outcome is unsurprising: a financial question with no stated jurisdiction leans toward US structures. The problem is that this bias does not present as a wrong answer. It presents as an answer that would be correct somewhere else, which is far harder for a reader to catch.

Typical US-framed adviceDoes it hold in Korea?What to check instead
"Always capture the full 401(k) employer match"No account with that structure existsWhich retirement pension type you are in (DB, DC or IRP) and your actual enrolment
"Contribute after tax to a Roth IRA and withdraw tax free"No direct counterpart accountKorean pension accounts give a credit on contribution and tax the payout. See pension savings and IRP
"Use an HSA to shelter medical costs"No equivalent schemeNot applicable
"Fund a 529 plan for education"No equivalent schemeNot applicable
"Delay Social Security to 70"Different systemNational Pension eligibility age and deferral rules
"Hold long term for the lower capital gains rate"Different tax architectureDomestic financial income taxation. Above 20 million won of annual financial income, comprehensive taxation applies
"Build three to six months of emergency cash first"HoldsClose to a border-independent principle

The last row is the important one. It is not all wrong. Life cycle saving, diversification and cost minimisation travel across borders fine. The breakage begins the moment a scheme has a proper name. So point your verification there: doubt the proper nouns, not the principles.

5. Writing your accounts and tax rules into the prompt

Stating your jurisdiction means more than typing "I'm in Korea." It means giving both the scheme names and your own status. The Korean items below measurably change the reply, and the figures come from institutional guidance.

  • Pension account tax credit. Per National Tax Service guidance, a pension savings account is capped at 6 million won per year, and the amount within that 6 million combined with retirement pension contributions is capped at 9 million won per year. The credit rate is 15% where aggregate income is 45 million won or below (55 million won of gross salary) and 12% above that
  • Rolling a matured ISA into a pension account. National Tax Service guidance describes an expansion of the credit ceiling equal to 10% of the transferred amount, capped at 3 million won. AI rarely raises this route unprompted. You have to ask
  • How an ISA is taxed. Per Financial Services Commission guidance, gains and losses are netted inside the account, and amounts above the tax-free allowance are subject to 9.9% separate taxation. Eligible holdings are also restricted. See the ISA account guide
  • Comprehensive taxation of financial income. Per National Tax Service counselling guidance, annual financial income above 20 million won becomes subject to comprehensive taxation; at or below that level withholding settles the matter. Spousal income is not aggregated
  • Fund costs. Per the Korea Ministry of Government Legislation's plain-language legal portal, sales commission is capped at 2% of the subscription or redemption amount and sales fees at 1% of the annual average value of collective investment assets. That gives you a basis for checking whether an AI's return projection included costs

Do not let these numbers be settled inside the chat. Put your own situation into the prompt, then re-check any figure the answer cites against the originals listed at the end. Tax rules get amended while a model's knowledge is frozen at some date. There is a separate write-up on verifying AI output as a process rather than a skill.

Choosing between account types is outside this article's scope. It is covered separately in pension savings funds, ETF basics and retirement withdrawal rates.

6. Eight checks to run on the answer

Read the reply against these in order and most defects will surface. Verification cost rises as you go down, so if something fails near the top you can stop there.

#What to checkThe warning signWhere to compare
1Which jurisdiction it assumedUS-style scheme names, or no stated assumptionAsk the answer to state it explicitly
2Values you never suppliedRetirement age, life expectancy or return appears out of nowhere"List every assumption this answer used"
3Expected return as a numberOnly phrases like "rises over the long run"Demand a figure and the period behind it
4Sources for rates and capsNumbers with no year attachedTax authority and financial regulator originals
5Costs subtractedGross return only, no fees or taxesStatutory caps on sales commission and fees
6Liquidity in the mathsNo mention of when or how much you can withdrawAsk about early exit and withdrawal terms explicitly
7A bad scenarioNo drawdown or job loss caseThe study's identified weak spot. Ask separately, always
8Whether named products existPlausible fund or product namesOfficial disclosure and product comparison databases

Number 8 is both the most dangerous and the fastest to settle. Product names, fund names and tickers are where generated text goes wrong most reliably. When a plausible name appears, look it up in a disclosure database, not a search engine. Search shows you similar names; disclosure answers whether the thing exists.

Number 2 pays off heavily in practice too. A single line — "list every assumption this answer used" — drags the hidden defaults into view. Usually retirement age and expected return alone dominate the entire reply, and if those two do not match your situation, every calculation below them has to be redone.

7. Where AI ends and a person begins

There is a public benchmark worth borrowing when drawing this line. Korea's Financial Services Commission put an AI guideline for the financial sector into effect on 22 June 2026, built on seven principles: governance, legality, auxiliary status, reliability, financial stability, good faith, and security. On auxiliary status the text reads:

"At the present stage artificial intelligence is an auxiliary means for work, so final decision-making and the accompanying responsibility are carried out by staff."

It governs financial institutions, not individual users. Still, the fact that a regulator draws the line there even for finance professionals makes it a reasonable reference for anyone handling their own money.

The most underrated row is the second. Using AI as a question-refiner rather than an answer-machine is the highest-yield application available today. Most of the gap the study measured came from question quality, and refining a question is something models do well with almost no downside.

8. A prompt template you can paste

It is quicker to see what a six-field question actually looks like. Copy the block below and swap in your own values. The numbers are illustrative and must be replaced.

[SITUATION]
- Jurisdiction  : resident of South Korea, income in KRW, answer under Korean tax rules
- Age / goal    : 34, targeting retirement at 60
- Income        : 3.8 M KRW net per month, permanent employee, variable bonus
- Spending      : 2.2 M KRW fixed monthly outgoings
- Debt          : 20 M KRW personal loan (6.2% p.a., level repayment, 3 years left)
- Emergency cash: 3 M KRW now (target: 4 months of living costs)
- Accounts held : pension savings yes, IRP no, ISA no
- Horizon       : no plan to touch this money for 15+ years
- Risk tolerance: I can sit through a 30% drawdown in a year. At 40% I would sell

[REQUEST]
1. Under these conditions, repay the debt first or invest first, with the deciding criterion
2. List every assumption used (expected return, inflation, years in retirement) as numbers
3. Attach the year to any tax rule or cap you cite; if you are unsure, say you are unsure
4. Name the 3 most likely reasons this plan fails
5. Show how the plan changes if income stops for 6 months
6. Do not name specific products or securities. Answer at the asset class level only

Items 2 and 6 in the request block do most of the work. Item 2 forces the hidden defaults into the open; item 6 pre-empts the category of answer that is hardest to verify. Item 3 measurably reduces tax-related fabrication — there is a real difference between having the instruction "say you are unsure" and not having it.

A few follow-ups worth keeping on hand as the conversation continues.

  • "State in one line which country and tax system this answer assumed"
  • "Give me the strongest case for the opposite conclusion under the same conditions"
  • "Where in this calculation are fees, ongoing charges and taxes reflected?"
  • "What information did I fail to give you, and how would it change the answer?"
  • "Flag anything in this advice that does not apply under my country's rules"
  • "How does the answer change if I need this money in three years instead?"

9. How much personal data to hand over

Better answers require more information, and that information is personal data. This is the practical compromise.

  • Never enter. National identification numbers, bank account numbers, card numbers, brokerage login details, digital certificate material
  • You do not need to enter. Employer name, exact date of birth, home address. "Manufacturing, permanent contract", "age 34", "metro area" loses nothing in answer quality
  • Ratios work fine. If absolute figures feel uncomfortable, "income 100, fixed costs 58, saving 25" still supports a valid allocation discussion
  • Check the service settings. Whether conversations feed model training and whether history is retained varies by provider, and can often be turned off
  • Minimise other people's data. If a spouse's income matters to the maths, a combined figure is enough

In short: be precise about structure, vague about identity. What determines the quality of financial advice is not who you are but what shape your finances are in, so separating the two lets you share most of what matters safely.

10. What this article could not confirm

Leaving the gaps visible.

  • Whether the same gaps appear in non-English prompts. The study's sample is US and UK adults writing in English. An equivalent experiment in Korean, against Korean rules, was not found
  • Model-by-model differences. The research covered several commercial models, but this article does not rank them. The variable that separated outcomes was on the input side
  • The survey methodology behind the "more than half of US and UK adults" figure. Sample composition and question wording were not confirmed
  • Accuracy of AI advisory services offered by individual financial institutions. No per-service evaluation is included here
  • Subsequent amendments to the tax figures. The caps and rates cited follow each institution's guidance pages; confirm at the source before filing or opening an account

11. Frequently asked questions

Q. Does filling all six fields make the prompt uncomfortably long?

Yes. That length is precisely the difference the study measured. It is also a template you build once and reuse, so store it in a notes app and change only the values. The cost lands entirely on the first attempt.

Q. Can I just follow the allocation the AI gives me?

Not advisable. What the study showed is that the advice is better on average than what people actually do, which is not the same as optimal for you. Abrupt shocks such as job loss, and rebalancing, were named weaknesses.

Q. Do paid models give better answers than free ones?

Beyond what this article can answer. What the cited research found is that the dominant variable was the person writing the question, not the model. Changing the question comes before changing the model.

Q. Is it acceptable to ask an AI about tax rates and contribution caps?

As a starting point, yes. But settle them at the source. Tax rules change while a model's knowledge has a cut-off date, and that date rarely appears in the answer.

Q. Why is asking for product names risky?

Two reasons. Names that do not exist can be generated, and even for real ones, individual product recommendation is a regulated activity. Ask at the asset class level and pick actual products from official disclosures yourself.

Conclusion: Change the question before you change the model

Debate about AI financial advice usually collapses into "can you trust it or not." The evidence so far does not sit on that axis. The same model gives one person usable advice and another person a misfitting plan. Most of that difference came from the question.

Which makes the task concrete. Fill the six fields, do not omit liquidity, and state your jurisdiction. When the answer comes back, force the assumptions into view, check the figures against institutional originals, and confirm product names in disclosure databases. For the areas where AI is demonstrably weak, such as a job-loss scenario, route to a person from the start.

One last thing. Even with all of the above, the model is a calculator and an organiser, not a decision-maker. The line a financial regulator draws for regulated firms is "auxiliary means." This article is for information only and does not recommend buying or selling any specific product. Investment decisions and their consequences are your own responsibility. That fact is the best reason of all to build the question properly.
