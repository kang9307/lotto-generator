After the SKT USIM Hacking Incident: Is Bank App Re-authentication Really Necessary?

A comprehensive guide to what happened, what you need to do after replacing your USIM, and how Korean banks are responding

2025-06-03 Finance

Introduction: Seeking Clear Answers Amid the Anxiety

The suspected USIM-related data breach at SK Telecom (SKT) that occurred in April 2025 caused widespread anxiety among subscribers. The long lines snaking outside SKT retail stores for USIM replacements and the flood of online reservation requests were a clear reflection of that fear -- along with growing concerns about the safety of personal financial information.

In the midst of all this confusion, the question most people are asking is: "I replaced my SKT USIM because of the hacking incident -- do I really have to go through the hassle of re-authenticating my banking apps and other important services?"

This article aims to provide a clear, fact-based answer to that question. We will offer a comprehensive analysis of the specifics of the SKT breach and the company's response measures, the impact of USIM replacement on smartphone authentication systems, and -- most importantly -- how Korea's major banks are handling the situation, so you know exactly what steps to take after replacing your USIM.

1. Understanding the SKT USIM Hacking Incident: What Was Leaked and Why It Matters

How the Incident Unfolded

The incident began on April 18, 2025, around 6:09 PM, when a 9.7 GB file transfer was detected on an SKT server. Later that evening, around 11:20 PM, malware was discovered on a billing analysis device, along with evidence of file deletion. On April 19, at approximately 11 PM, SKT recognized signs of USIM-related data leakage caused by the malware and immediately deleted the malicious code and isolated the compromised equipment. SKT then reported the security breach to KISA (Korea Internet & Security Agency) on April 20, notified the Personal Information Protection Commission on the morning of April 22, and made a public announcement the same day. However, the roughly four-day gap between the initial detection of anomalies and the public disclosure has drawn criticism regarding the speed of the initial response.

What Was Actually Leaked

According to the first investigation results from the joint government-private investigation team (announced April 29), the data leaked in this breach included:

  • Confirmed leaked data: Four types of information that could be used for USIM cloning -- subscriber phone numbers, subscriber identity keys (IMSI), USIM serial numbers (believed to be ICCID), and authentication keys (Ki values). An additional 21 types of SKT internal management data were also leaked.
  • Data confirmed NOT leaked: The device's unique identifier (IMEI), which had been a major source of concern, was confirmed not to have been compromised. SKT also stated that resident registration numbers, financial information, and email addresses were not leaked.

The Real Danger: SIM Swapping

The most alarming aspect of this breach is the potential for SIM swapping attacks.

  • What is SIM swapping? SIM swapping occurs when a hacker uses leaked core USIM data such as IMSI and Ki values to create a cloned SIM that functions identically to the victim's original USIM. When this cloned SIM is inserted into another phone (a so-called "clone phone"), the hacker can intercept SMS messages and phone calls intended for the victim.
  • Why it's dangerous: Many financial apps, cryptocurrency exchanges, email services, and social media platforms use SMS verification codes for identity verification. If a SIM swap succeeds, the hacker can steal these verification codes to bypass two-factor authentication (2FA), take over accounts, or attempt financial fraud. Financial regulators have also warned about the risk of mobile identity verification bypass and unauthorized financial transactions, urging financial institutions to stay vigilant.

Some experts note that the leaked information alone would not be enough to directly drain money from financial accounts, since additional security layers such as account passwords, OTPs, and joint certificates are in place. However, the ability to intercept SMS verification codes alone could lead to serious secondary damage including identity theft and account takeovers -- making this breach a fundamentally different kind of threat compared to a typical personal data leak. The risk is further amplified when the leaked USIM data (IMSI, phone numbers, etc.) is combined with other personal information already circulating on the dark web (such as names and resident registration numbers), enabling targeted attacks against specific individuals.

In short, the core issue of this SKT hacking incident is that sensitive data directly tied to network authentication -- IMSI and Ki values -- was leaked, theoretically opening the door to SIM swapping, which allows attackers to hijack the communication channel itself. This represents a far more direct and severe security threat than a typical personal data breach, and it is the primary reason behind the surge of public anxiety. The gap between SKT's initial reassurances and the actual technical risks drove users to seek the most tangible solution -- physically replacing their USIMs -- which in turn triggered massive demand and led to a logistics crisis.

2. SKT's Response: USIM Protection Service and Free Replacements

Along with issuing a public apology, SKT pledged to cooperate with the government investigation, conduct system inspections, and strengthen measures to block unauthorized USIM device changes and abnormal authentication attempts. Two concrete measures were offered to subscribers: enrollment in the "USIM Protection Service" (Yusim Boho Service) and free USIM replacements.

Option 1: USIM Protection Service (Free)

  • What it does: This free add-on service pairs a customer's USIM information 1:1 with a specific device (smartphone), preventing the USIM from working in any other device. Essentially, it pre-links the USIM with the device's unique identifier (IMEI, etc.).
  • How it works: If someone creates a cloned SIM using the leaked data and inserts it into a different phone, SKT's network detects that the device information does not match the registered device. In this case, voice, text, and data services are immediately blocked on both the original user's phone and the phone containing the cloned SIM. This allows the user to immediately notice something is wrong, while the hacker is unable to use the cloned SIM.
  • How to enroll: You can sign up for free through the T world app or website.
  • SKT's guarantee: SKT pledged to take 100% responsibility and provide compensation if any damage occurs to customers enrolled in the USIM Protection Service. Thanks to this guarantee and the convenience of enrollment, a large number of subscribers reportedly signed up.
  • Note: The service may be automatically canceled or need to be temporarily deactivated when using international roaming.

Option 2: Free USIM Replacement

  • Who qualifies: All SKT customers (including eSIM users) who were active subscribers as of April 18, 2025. Secondary devices such as tablets and smartwatches activated before that date are also eligible. Customers who paid for USIM replacements between April 19 and April 27 can receive retroactive reimbursement.
  • How to replace: Visit any T world retail store or airport roaming center. Due to extreme demand, online reservations (care.tworld.co.kr) were strongly recommended before visiting. You need to bring your ID, phone, and existing USIM.
  • The reality: A nationwide "USIM crisis" erupted shortly after the service began. Many users waited for hours or made wasted trips due to stock shortages. While SKT announced significant replacement and reservation numbers, criticism arose over inadequate initial stock levels and the lack of delivery options (e.g., no mail-in replacements).
  • NFC transit card note: If you were using your NFC-enabled USIM as a transit card (T-money / Cashbee, etc.), it is best to use up the balance or request a refund through the respective app before replacing your USIM. For postpaid transit cards, you need to delete the old USIM data and re-register on the new USIM.

From a technical standpoint, the USIM Protection Service is specifically designed to defend against SIM swapping -- the core threat. By blocking the use of USIM information on any unauthorized device, it can provide protection that is comparable to, or even more immediate than, a physical replacement. Nevertheless, the widespread preference for physical replacement can be attributed to the psychological comfort of a tangible hardware change, combined with distrust stemming from SKT's initial response and communication. Ultimately, SKT's failure to anticipate the demand for USIM replacements and its logistics missteps created a secondary wave of confusion and user frustration on top of the original security breach. This underscores how, separate from the technical effectiveness of a solution, managing user perception and trust during a crisis is critically important.

3. USIM Replacement Basics: What Changes and What Stays the Same

Your Data Is Safe: Don't Worry

First, the most important point: replacing your USIM chip does not delete any data stored on your smartphone itself. Photos, videos, installed apps, notes, downloaded files, and other content are stored in the phone's internal storage, SD card, or cloud services (Google Drive, iCloud, etc.), and have no direct connection to the USIM. The USIM primarily stores subscriber information and cryptographic keys for network access -- it is not a storage medium for user content.

What the USIM Actually Does

The USIM serves several critical functions:

  • Network authentication: It authenticates your smartphone to connect to the mobile carrier's network (like SKT). The USIM's unique identifiers (IMSI, Ki, etc.) are used to verify identity between the carrier and the device.
  • Subscriber identification: The physical USIM card or eSIM profile links a specific phone number to a subscriber.
  • SMS reception channel: Through the network connection, the USIM enables receipt of text messages (SMS), which many online services rely on for delivering one-time passwords (OTPs) used in identity verification.

Why Re-authentication Is Required

Why do so many apps require re-authentication after a USIM replacement?

  • Change detection: When a new USIM is inserted, the smartphone and the mobile network detect that the USIM's unique identifiers (such as ICCID and IMSI) have changed.
  • Security signal: Security-sensitive apps, especially financial apps, may treat this USIM change as a potential security threat -- for example, considering the possibility that someone has hijacked the phone number by inserting the SIM into a different phone. Some apps may also simply recognize it as a device change and prompt the user for verification.
  • Rebuilding trust: From the app's perspective, it needs to re-confirm: "Is the person using this new USIM really the owner of this account?" To do so, it may require steps such as re-entering a password, biometric authentication (fingerprint, face, etc.), ARS phone verification, or receiving an SMS verification code on the new USIM.

In essence, replacing your USIM is like changing the "network access key" linked to your phone number. Financial apps and other services treat this key change as a significant security event and require you to prove that you are still the legitimate account holder. This has become an even more critical security measure because SIM swapping -- the hijacking of the communication channel via a cloned USIM -- is a real-world threat. So while re-authentication after a USIM change may be inconvenient, it should be understood as an essential safeguard designed to protect your accounts from exactly that kind of SIM swapping attack.

4. How Korean Banks Are Responding: Strengthened Security and Re-authentication Requirements

In the wake of the SKT USIM hacking incident, Korea's financial sector (banks, credit card companies, insurance companies, etc.) moved quickly. They broadly tightened their security posture and made authentication procedures more stringent in preparation for the potential misuse of leaked USIM data. Financial regulators, including the Financial Supervisory Service (FSS), also directed financial institutions to strengthen their Fraud Detection System (FDS) monitoring and to inform consumers of precautions, aiming to prevent secondary damage.

Major Bank Responses (Re-authentication Details)

While the specific measures vary by bank, they all moved in the same direction: requiring additional authentication when detecting "abnormal access attempts" or "device changes." In particular, biometric authentication methods such as facial recognition were actively adopted.

Other Financial Institution Measures

  • Credit card companies (KB Kookmin, Lotte, Samsung, etc.): Recommended that customers enroll in the USIM Protection Service or replace their USIMs, and encouraged the use of app-based authentication (push notifications, simplified authentication, etc.) instead of SMS verification. They also raised their FDS detection thresholds.
  • Insurance / Capital companies (KB Life, NH NongHyup Life, KB Capital, etc.): Some companies temporarily suspended login or service access via SMS-based identity verification for SKT users.

This comprehensive response across the financial sector demonstrates how seriously the industry views the risk of secondary damage from the SKT USIM hack -- particularly the danger of financial fraud via SMS authentication bypass. While the introduction of additional authentication steps such as facial recognition may cause some inconvenience for users, it also means that financial institutions are taking proactive, aggressive measures to protect customer assets. Ultimately, this incident has accelerated the shift across the financial industry away from reliance on SMS-based authentication and toward more secure methods such as biometric verification and app-based authentication.

5. Digital Certificates (Joint Certificates / Financial Certificates) and USIM Replacement

Beyond bank app re-authentication, another common concern after a USIM replacement is the impact on "joint certificates" (gonging injeungseo, formerly known as public certificates) and "financial certificates" (geumyung injeungseo).

Certificate Types and Storage Methods

  • Joint certificates (Gonging Injeungseo): Traditionally stored as files on a PC hard drive, USB storage device, or in a secure storage area within the smartphone (such as a secure folder or the USIM's Secure Element area).
  • Financial certificates (Geumyung Injeungseo): A more recent type where the certificate itself is securely stored on the cloud servers of the Korea Financial Telecommunications & Clearings Institute (KFTC). Users can access and use it from multiple devices via password or biometric authentication.

Impact of USIM Replacement

  • Certificate files themselves: Joint certificate files stored in the smartphone's internal storage are unlikely to be deleted by a USIM replacement alone, since the USIM and the phone's storage are separate. Financial certificates stored in the cloud are not directly affected by a USIM change at all.
  • The issue is the "link with the app": Even if the certificate file is intact, the banking or brokerage app that uses it may invalidate the previously registered certificate information or require re-registration during the post-USIM-change re-authentication process. In other words, the app wants to re-confirm: "Is the person using this new USIM really the owner of this certificate?"
  • Possible re-registration or re-issuance: In most cases, you can continue using your existing certificate by completing a simple identity verification process within the app and re-registering (re-linking) it. However, depending on bank policies or if issues arise during re-authentication, you may in the worst case need to issue a brand new certificate. Re-issuing a joint certificate may require identity verification procedures similar to a new issuance (document submission, non-face-to-face authentication, etc.). For financial certificates, issuing a new one automatically revokes the previous one.

Therefore, after replacing your USIM, you should log into each financial app to check whether your certificate is recognized properly, and follow the app's instructions to re-register or, if necessary, go through the re-issuance process. Since certificate types and bank policies vary, the most accurate approach is to follow each financial institution's specific guidance. Having to deal with certificate issues on top of the USIM replacement itself adds another layer of burden for users, and it highlights the complexity of the digital authentication ecosystem.

6. Practical Steps and Final Recommendations

If you have decided to replace your USIM or have already done so, it is important to proceed through the following steps calmly.

Post-USIM Replacement Checklist

  • Reboot: Restart your smartphone after inserting the new USIM.
  • Launch apps and re-authenticate: Open your frequently used banking, brokerage, mobile payment apps (KakaoPay, Naver Pay, etc.), and messenger apps like KakaoTalk one by one. Most sensitive apps will prompt you to re-authenticate.
  • Complete verification procedures: Calmly follow the authentication steps each app requires -- password entry, biometric verification, ARS verification, facial recognition, etc. The exact method may differ by bank (see Section 4).
  • Check your certificates: Within each financial app, verify that your joint certificate or financial certificate is working properly. If needed, follow the app's instructions to re-register your certificate, or go through the re-issuance process via the bank or certification authority.
  • Review other settings: App notification settings may have been reset, so adjust them as needed.

Reconsidering the USIM Protection Service

If you have not yet replaced your USIM or are still waiting, you may want to reconsider enrolling in SKT's free "USIM Protection Service" (Yusim Boho Service). This service can be an effective alternative for preventing SIM swapping without the hassle of a physical replacement. Of course, you can also take both measures.

User Experience and Precautions

Throughout this incident, many users experienced long wait times and stock shortages when trying to replace their USIMs, and some even took the extreme step of deleting their banking apps out of anxiety. Online communities were set up where affected users gathered to share information and prepare for class-action lawsuits. Misinformation and conspiracy theories about USIM replacement also spread.

Conclusion: Re-authentication Is Necessary and Should Be Expected

In conclusion, if you replaced your USIM after the SKT hacking incident, there is a very high likelihood that you will need to go through re-authentication procedures for banking apps and other major financial and authentication-related services. This is because financial institutions have used this incident as an occasion to strengthen security, treating USIM changes as a significant security event and adding identity verification steps. While it may feel inconvenient, these are essential measures designed to protect your assets from potential financial fraud enabled by the leaked data.

Final Recommendations

  • Protect your carrier account: If you haven't taken action yet, replace your USIM at an SKT T world store or online, or -- for a simpler and highly effective option -- enroll in the "USIM Protection Service" (Yusim Boho Service).
  • Prepare for re-authentication: After replacing your USIM, be ready for re-authentication prompts when opening key apps (financial, mobile payment, social media, etc.). Check your passwords in advance and make sure biometric authentication features are working properly.
  • Verify your certificates: Check the status of your digital certificates (joint / financial) in each financial app, and follow re-registration or re-issuance procedures as needed.
  • Watch out for phishing: Never respond to phone calls, texts, or emails impersonating SKT or financial institutions that use USIM replacement assistance or damage compensation as a pretext to request personal or financial information. All procedures should only be conducted through official apps, websites, or authorized retail stores.
  • Upgrade your authentication methods: Where possible, switch from SMS-based verification to stronger two-factor authentication methods such as app-based OTPs, push notifications, or biometric authentication. You may also consider enrolling in identity theft prevention services such as M-Safer (Myeongui Doyong Bangji Service).

The SKT USIM hacking incident has served as a powerful reminder of the importance of personal data protection and financial security in our digital society. Although it may be somewhat inconvenient, reviewing and strengthening your security settings according to the recommended procedures is the surest way to protect your valuable assets.
