Why Lisp Still Matters in 2026 - Macros, REPL, and the Bipolar Programmer Paradox

Beyond the parentheses: the mindset of fitting a language to a problem, and why a brilliant language never became mainstream

Jul 12, 2026 Dev & Infra

Intro: Why Lisp Is Still Talked About

In July 2026, two essays about Lisp landed on GeekNews (news.hada.io) within days of each other. One was "A Road to Lisp: Why Lisp" by developer Elia Scotto; the other was "The Bipolar Lisp Programmer" by Mark Tarver, creator of the Qi language. Neither is a rehash of old talking points. The first explains, from a language-design angle, why Lisp is still worth learning. The second asks why such a technically excellent language never became mainstream - and looks for the answer not in the language but in the people it attracts. That "why it's great" and "why it failed" get asked about the same language in the same week says a lot about where Lisp actually stands as of July 2026.

Designed by John McCarthy in 1958, Lisp is often called the second-oldest actively used programming language after Fortran. Yet almost no commercial team starts a new project in Lisp today. And yet its core ideas - macros, the REPL, the condition system - keep flowing into other languages: decorators, macro experiments, structured exception handling, and even recent discussions of AI agent design. Old but not dead; not mainstream but not ignored either. This piece treats both essays with equal respect and traces the intersection between them - the paradox that power and marginalization share the same root - through my own lens.

1. Two Lenses: Technology and People

Scotto's piece looks inward at the technology. His core claim is that Lisp's essence is not its parenthesis-heavy syntax. He sums up what macros do in a short phrase: "language toward your problem." You grow the language toward the problem, then write the program in that grown language. That single phrase compresses the technical core this article works through.

Tarver's piece looks outward, at people. He observes a type that shows up disproportionately in the Lisp community - people with sharp intellect who can't tolerate boredom and are drawn to new ideas more than to finishing things - and argues Lisp attracts this type unusually strongly. His conclusion is terse. He opens with the line "no problem with Lisp" and closes by saying the issue was never the language but what people make of it. Not a flaw in the language itself, but the social behavior patterns of the people it draws in, that makes standardization and collaboration hard.

Put side by side, the two essays form an interesting symmetry. Scotto explains why Lisp is powerful; Tarver explains why that same power becomes a weakness. This article walks through both lenses in turn, then brings them back together in the context of AI-era coding.

2. The Core of Lisp: A Language Where Code Is Data

2.1 Homoiconicity - Why All Those Parentheses

Lisp source code is made of nested lists called S-expressions. To a newcomer this looks like an unusually parenthesis-heavy syntax, but that structure is exactly Lisp's identity. The property that Lisp code takes the same shape as Lisp's core data structure - the list - is called homoiconicity. Code can be read as a list, manipulated as a list, and evaluated back as code. There is almost no separate parsing step standing between syntax and data structure.

In most languages, making code manipulable by code requires a separate reflection API or an AST-manipulation library - genuinely hard work. In Lisp it's the default. Programs reading and writing programs is the everyday case, not the exception, which is why homoiconicity is not a mere syntactic quirk but the foundation that makes macros and metaprogramming, discussed next, possible at all.

2.2 Macros - A Tool for Extending the Language

A function's arguments are evaluated before the call happens. Macros are different: they take unevaluated code (a list) as-is and transform it into other code at compile time. If Common Lisp has no built-in "while" loop, a programmer can write one as a macro and bolt it directly onto the language. New syntax is added not by the language designer but by the language's own users.

This is the substance behind Scotto's phrase about growing the language toward the problem. In most languages, a new control structure or DSL means waiting for the next language version or reaching for an external tool like a preprocessor. In Lisp, that extension happens inside the language, with the same syntax, as a first-class capability. AutoCAD handing extensibility to users through AutoLISP, and Emacs doing the same through Emacs Lisp, both trace back to this macro capability.

2.3 The REPL - A Conversation With a Living Program

Most languages follow a cycle: write code, compile, run, look at the result, go back to the code. Lisp's REPL (Read-Eval-Print Loop) removes that cycle. Fix one function and it is immediately redefined inside the running process; the next call picks up the new definition without ever restarting the program from scratch. This approach, spiritually close to Smalltalk's image-based development, feels less like "building" a program and more like "teaching" and "evolving" one.

The difference sounds minor but feels large in practice. You can explore and develop a program with a long initialization or heavy data load without restarting it every time, keeping the live process state and swapping only the code. What hot reload and notebook-based development environments in many modern languages are trying to imitate today, Lisp had as a design default from the start.

2.4 Conditions and Restarts - Recovery That Goes Beyond Exception Handling

Common Lisp's condition system goes a step further than exception handling (try-catch) in most languages. Ordinary exception handling unwinds the stack immediately when something goes wrong and throws upward; the execution context at the point of failure is already gone. Common Lisp's condition system calls a handler right at the point the condition is signaled, without unwinding the stack, and the handler can choose among several pre-defined restarts to keep execution going.

The practical difference this creates is a separation between "offering recovery options" and "deciding which recovery to use." A low-level function can simply register several recovery strategies as restarts, while an upper-level caller - or even the end user - decides at runtime which one to actually use. Instead of killing the whole program when a file can't be found, you can present options like trying another path, falling back to a default, or asking again, all without stopping execution. This is a fundamentally different idea from most languages, which treat an exception purely as a notice of failure.

3. The "Why Lisp" Mindset: Fitting the Language to the Problem

What stays with me most from Scotto's piece is not any single feature but an attitude. Most development asks, "how do I solve this problem with the tools this language gives me?" Lisp thinking flips the order: it asks first "what would the ideal language for expressing this problem look like," builds that language with macros, and only then solves the problem in it. The tool is fit to the problem, not the other way around.

This mindset is not unfamiliar in practice. Config-file DSLs, query builders, routing rules, state-machine definitions - these already exist as "internal DSLs" in many languages. But most languages need a separate parser, framework, decorators, or code-generation tooling to build such a DSL. In Lisp, a single built-in feature, the macro, does the job. The answer to "why Lisp" ultimately comes down to this: Lisp offers as syntax what other languages have to fake with frameworks.

4. So Why Didn't It Go Mainstream? The Bipolar Programmer Paradox

This is where Tarver's essay steps in. Why didn't a language this technically powerful get as widely used as Java, Python, or C++? Tarver looks not for a flaw in the language but for an answer in the temperament of the people it attracts.

4.1 Power Breeds Fragmentation

The flexibility that lets you solve the same problem many different ways is a strength - and it is also exactly what makes standardization hard. The concept of the "Lisp curse," first articulated by Rudolf Winestock, explains this paradox well. In other languages, technical limitations force convergence onto a single standard library; in Lisp, individuals can so easily build their own solution that convergence often never happens at all. A commonly cited example: in the 1990s, the Scheme community produced a proliferation of competing object-oriented extension packages. Easy to build means everyone builds their own, and everyone building their own means nothing becomes standard.

4.2 The Tension Between Individualism and Collaboration

The programmer Tarver describes - brilliant but prone to swings - is motivated more by chasing a new idea than by finishing a polished result. Documentation, testing, and maintenance - boring but necessary for collaboration - get pushed to the back. Tarver points out that languages like C and C++, precisely because of their complexity and lower-level control, tend to naturally force a culture of disciplined, collaborative work among many people. The more freedom a language grants an individual, the harder it becomes to bind that freedom back into organizational discipline.

4.3 The Symbol of the Lisp Machine

A case that quietly overlaps in both essays is the Lisp machine. Dedicated Lisp hardware built by companies like Symbolics in the 1980s represented, technically, the best development environment of its era. But it could not compromise with where the market was headed - general-purpose workstations, then PCs - and it disappeared. Tarver's bitter diagnosis is that when technical ideals and market demand diverge, the Lisp camp has generally chosen not to bend toward the market.

5. Where the Two Views Meet

The freedom Scotto describes - fitting the language to the problem - and the diagnosis Tarver offers - that same freedom obstructing organization - are really two sides of one coin. That anyone can build their own dialect with macros is both a technical blessing and a social curse. In places where one person or a small team needs to build a tool that fits a problem fast - personal projects, research, prototypes - Lisp remains top-tier. In large commercial software maintained by hundreds of people over years, that very same freedom to extend the language however you like becomes a risk to codebase consistency. Why Lisp has quietly survived in academia, research labs, and small elite teams while never rising to the mainstream of commercial development can only be explained by looking at both the technical layer and the social layer together.

6. What Lisp Teaches the AI-Coding Era

As of July 2026, the central topic in software development is AI coding agents. Interestingly, the structure of an agent that plans, generates, edits, and executes code on its own overlaps with a problem Lisp has worked on for decades. An agent treating its own execution plan as data, then turning that plan back into code to execute, is a variation on the "treat code as data" problem homoiconicity has been solving for over sixty years. Recent research has even begun wiring LLMs into persistent, Lisp-based metaprogramming loops - a signal that AI agent design and Lisp-style thinking are meeting not by coincidence but by structure.

The condition and restart system also deserves a fresh look. If an AI agent could, right at the point a tool call fails, choose a recovery path - try another tool, ask the user, fall back to a default - without unwinding the stack, that is something Lisp already had at the language level, which most agent frameworks today crudely approximate with retries and fallbacks. REPL-style development - talking to a running program, swapping code without restarting it, keeping state alive - also resonates emotionally with today's practice of iteratively refining code in conversation with an AI.

But Tarver's warning should not be forgotten here. In an era where AI generates code, anyone can churn out their own solutions and patterns at scale as easily as writing a macro. The risk that flexibility and generation speed accelerate codebase fragmentation and non-standardization means the Lisp curse could reappear at a new scale in the AI era. What Lisp teaches is not only the power of metaprogramming but also a warning about what is lost when that power isn't organizationally controlled.

Conclusion: Is Lisp Worth Learning?

My conclusion agrees with both essays while landing somewhere between them. Few developers will pick Lisp as their daily driver language. But homoiconicity, the mindset of extending a language with macros, REPL-based conversational development, and the flexible error recovery that conditions and restarts demonstrate are ideas worth learning regardless of which language you use day to day. These ideas are already absorbed into modern languages under names like decorators, macro systems, hot reload, and structured exception handling. At the same time, Tarver's diagnosis cannot be dismissed. The lesson that a powerful tool, without the discipline of the people and organization wielding it, ends in fragmentation applies not just to Lisp but to every team today whose code-generation speed AI has dramatically raised. Lisp is still talked about not out of nostalgia, but because the question it raised - whether to fit the language to the problem, and how to organize the freedom that comes with it - remains unresolved in 2026.
