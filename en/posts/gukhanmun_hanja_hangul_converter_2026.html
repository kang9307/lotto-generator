Gukhanmun - A Rust and JS Library Converting Korean Mixed Script to Hangul

Turning hanja-laden old documents, newspapers, and records into hangul - the technical difficulty of conversion and its value for archiving and NLP

2026-06-16 Dev & Infra

Intro: the headache of old Korean text mixed with hanja

Up through the mid-20th century, a large share of Korea's official documents, newspapers, and scholarly works were written in mixed script (gukhanmun): nouns and Sino-Korean words in hanja (Chinese characters), with particles and endings in hangul. Digitizing this material hits a wall. Most modern readers cannot read a hanja string like 行事場所 at a glance, and search engines and NLP models struggle to treat a mix of hanja and hangul consistently.

In June 2026, an open-source library aimed squarely at this problem, Gukhanmun, drew attention on GeekNews. It converts mixed-script Korean into hangul-only text, and ships for both Rust and JavaScript. Rather than restate the news, this article analyzes, in my own view, what the library actually solves, why hanja-to-hangul conversion is a surprisingly hard problem, and what role it plays in digital archiving and Korean NLP. Specs are based on the public Gukhanmun docs and repository (as of June 2026), and the Korean orthography parts were cross-checked separately.

1. What Gukhanmun is - Seonbi's successor, focused on hanja to hangul

Gukhanmun was built by software engineer Hong Minhee (dahlia), a developer with a focus on East Asian languages and Chinese-character processing. He previously made Seonbi, a Korean text preprocessor. Where Seonbi bundled several typographic transforms (including mixed-script to hangul-only, plus quote curling and more), Gukhanmun positions itself as a successor that narrows down to the single hanja-to-hangul pipeline.

1.1 What it takes in and what it outputs

The input is mixed-script text combining hanja and hangul. The default output is hangul-only, but that is not all. It also offers parenthetical glossing (hangul with the hanja in parentheses, or the reverse), an HTML ruby mode that sets the hanja as a small annotation above, and a mixed mode that selectively keeps some hanja. This variety of output modes is not mere feature padding; it ties directly into a core tradeoff discussed below: whether to discard the original or preserve it.

1.2 The decision to bundle dictionaries

A notable design choice is that it does not fetch dictionaries externally but ships them compiled in. The National Institute of Korean Language's Standard Korean Dictionary and the open Open Korean Dictionary (Urimalsaem) are built into FST (finite-state transducer) and CDB form inside the package. That means conversion works immediately, with no separate download or network dependency. It pulls the thorniest distribution and version-consistency problem of dictionary-based conversion inside the library and solves it there.

2. Why hanja-to-hangul conversion is hard - the trap of the lookup table

On the surface it looks like you just attach a hangul reading to each hanja and you are done. In practice it is one of the trickier problems in NLP. Break it into three layers.

2.1 Homophony and polyphony - many readings, many meanings

A single hanja can have multiple readings (polyphony), and conversely many entirely different hanja words crowd into the same hangul reading. Without context you cannot fix which reading or which word it is. Given that over half of Korean vocabulary is Sino-Korean, this ambiguity is not an edge case but a pervasive condition spread throughout the text. Character-level mapping alone sees its accuracy collapse fast.

2.2 Word boundaries - where you cut changes the reading

Hanja strings often run without spaces, so where you cut the word is decisive. Gukhanmun's documentation gives an intuitive example. 行事場所 must split into 行事 (event) + 場所 (venue). If you greedily grab the longest word first and split as 行事場 + 所, the result breaks. This is a well-known hard problem in Korean, Chinese, and Japanese word segmentation, and a wrong cut throws off all the downstream reading conversion.

2.3 Orthography - the initial sound law, liaison, phonology

Even with segmentation and readings fixed, you are not done. South Korean orthography has the initial sound law (dueum beopchik), which changes a word-initial consonant. The same hanja takes a different reading word-initially: word-initial 'r' drops or shifts to 'n', and 'n' drops before 'i' or a glide. An interesting paradox is that the initial sound law actually increases homophones: Sino-Korean words with different starting sounds collapse into the same hangul form. Add liaison and phonological change, and accurate hangul demands segmentation, dictionary, and orthographic rules meshed into one pipeline.

3. Gukhanmun's approach - lattice segmentation and initial-sound handling

How Gukhanmun answers those difficulties is the heart of the library.

3.1 Lattice-based segmentation, not greedy matching

Instead of grabbing the longest word first, Gukhanmun uses lattice-based segmentation. It lays out all possible word candidates over the input as a lattice, then chooses the most plausible split path based on its dictionaries. That is how it correctly splits 行事場所 into 行事 + 場所. It is a classic technique proven in morphological analysis and Japanese kana conversion, and what fundamentally separates it from greedy methods is that it searches for a globally optimal path over candidate routes.

In my view this is the single most important decision shaping the library's accuracy. The quality gap between hanja-to-hangul tools comes almost entirely from how well they segment. Anyone can do character mapping and dictionary loading similarly; the hard part is resolving ambiguous boundaries using dictionary evidence, and that is where the class of a tool is decided.

3.2 Combining orthographic rules

Once segmentation is set, it applies South Korean orthography such as the initial sound law to produce the final hangul. Dictionary-based conversion and rule-based post-processing flow as one. The dictionary supplies words and standard readings; the rule layer refines spelling for word-initial position and phonological environment. Keeping these two layers separate is reasonable for maintenance: the dictionary updates as data, and orthography is managed as rules.

3.3 The pros and cons of bundled dictionaries

Bundling the Standard Korean Dictionary and Urimalsaem as FST/CDB cuts both ways. The upside is clear: it works on install, guarantees a consistent dictionary even offline and on embedded targets, and avoids version mismatch. The downside is also clear: carrying dictionaries makes the package large, and updating a bundled dictionary requires a new library release. Still, given that standard dictionaries are not frequently changing data, I find this tradeoff broadly reasonable.

4. The meaning of dual Rust and JS - one core, many runtimes

Gukhanmun keeps a Rust core and extends it to JavaScript and a CLI. This is not bragging about language count; read it as a design that matches real usage scenarios.

4.1 Rust core - performance, safety, embedded

The reason to keep the core logic in Rust is plain. Segmentation and dictionary lookups are operations repeated across large volumes of text, so performance matters, and Rust delivers memory safety and speed without a GC. With no_std support, it reaches embedded environments that lack the standard library. In a pipeline grinding through hundreds of thousands of records in an archive, these performance traits translate directly into cost.

4.2 WebAssembly - no server, in the browser

Compiling the same Rust core to WebAssembly (@gukhanmun/wasm) runs conversion in the browser. It converts instantly on the client with no server round trip, so it drops straight into an old-document viewer or an educational web tool. Since the text never leaves the device, it also wins on privacy and cost. From my own vantage of running a hub of in-browser tools, this "zero-server, client-side processing" pattern is an especially attractive deployment form.

4.3 Node-API and CLI - backends and pipelines

For a Node.js backend, the Node-API native addon (@gukhanmun/napi) carries native speed over directly. Because it binds the same Rust core rather than a pure-JS reimplementation, it keeps result consistency and performance together. The CLI fits archiving jobs that batch-convert many files via shell scripts, and with HTML and Markdown adapters it can convert while preserving document format. In short, it is a multi-target structure where one core branches to the optimal path per environment with no code duplication.

5. Value in digital archiving - old newspapers and records

Gukhanmun's most direct use is digital archiving. A large volume of Korea's modern newspapers, gazettes, official records, and scholarly collections is in mixed script. Digitizing them as-is preserves them but leaves them underused.

5.1 Solving search and readability at once

Converting to hangul-only solves two things together. First, readability: even readers who cannot read hanja can reach the content. Second, searchability: users usually search in hangul, and if the original is indexed only in hanja it never surfaces. Indexing a hangul conversion alongside it raises hit rates. Since an archive's value ultimately rests on "can you find it," this effect is not small.

5.2 The constraint of preserving the original

But archiving carries a scholarly constraint. Converting the original to hangul-only loses the original information. For researchers, which hanja it was can matter, and once converted there is no basis to verify a mistaken conversion. This is where Gukhanmun's parenthetical and ruby output modes shine. Read it in hangul while keeping the original hanja in parentheses or ruby, and you get both readability and preservation. In my view, a serious archiving project should default not to hangul-only but to the glossing or ruby mode.

5.3 Coupling with OCR and error propagation

Real old-document digitization usually has an OCR step extracting text first. Recently, AI OCR that reads regular and semi-cursive hanja at high accuracy has appeared, but it is not perfect. The trap to flag is error propagation. If OCR misreads a hanja, that error flows straight into Gukhanmun's conversion and hardens into wrong hangul. So an OCR-then-convert pipeline must be designed with per-stage validation and a human correction loop. A good conversion tool still yields wrong output from wrong input.

6. Value in Korean NLP - normalization preprocessing

The second use is Korean NLP preprocessing. Most modern Korean models are trained on hangul-centric corpora. Old text with embedded hanja is, to a tokenizer or embedding, an input with a different distribution.

6.1 Simplifying the input distribution

Normalizing mixed script to hangul brings the downstream model's input closer to its training distribution. Hanja tokens are low-frequency and tend to become out-of-vocabulary or be split inefficiently; converting to hangul reduces this. In tasks like search, classification, and summarization, notation consistency translates into performance. Text normalization is unglamorous but a foundation of pipeline quality.

6.2 The risk that normalization erases information

Yet normalization also erases information. Hanja is itself a cue that disambiguates homophones. Converting to hangul-only can merge different Sino-Korean words into the same form, making meaning harder to tell apart. For precision-sensitive work like scholarly search or semantic analysis, a design that preserves both the hangul normalization and the original hanja information is safer: hangul for search and matching, hanja as metadata for sense identification. Fortunately, Gukhanmun's output modes support such a parallel design.

6.3 Is preprocessing still valid in the LLM era?

One might ask whether preprocessing is needed at all now that strong LLMs understand hanja-laden text to some degree. My answer is "still valid." First, a deterministic, verifiable conversion suits archiving better than an LLM's probabilistic output: it gives the same result for the same input, is explainable by rules, and is clear about where to fix a mistake. Second, in large batches an LLM call is costly and slow. A dictionary-and-rule library runs fast and cheap offline. See it not as competition with LLMs but as a division of labor.

7. Limitations and caveats

Listing only upsides would be dishonest. There are limits worth naming explicitly.

7.1 Out-of-dictionary words and proper nouns

The weakness of dictionary-based segmentation is words not in the dictionary. Old documents are full of archaic forms, proper nouns like personal and place names, and special terms absent from modern dictionaries. Such words can missegment or take a wrong reading. Bundling large dictionaries like the Standard Korean Dictionary and Urimalsaem helps, but they cannot hold all the vocabulary of historical text. Domain-specific material needs dictionary augmentation or human correction alongside.

7.2 The South Korean orthography premise

Initial-sound handling presumes South Korean orthography. For North Korean spelling that does not apply the initial sound law, or for pre-reform spelling conventions, results may differ from intent. Conversion always targets a particular notational norm, so use it conscious of which norm you are converting toward.

7.3 The GPL 3.0 license

Gukhanmun is licensed under GPL 3.0. It is strong protection for free software, but the copyleft clause is a constraint for organizations that want to statically link it into a closed commercial product and distribute it. For internal tools or open-source projects there is no issue, but if you are considering commercial integration, check the license impact first. This is not a technical flaw but an item to verify before adoption.

8. Conclusion - a small but honest tool

Gukhanmun is not a flashy AI model. But I like tools like this. It is a deterministic, verifiable tool built to properly solve one clear problem: converting mixed-script text to hangul. It tackles the core difficulty of hanja-to-hangul conversion, the word-boundary problem, head-on with lattice-based segmentation; it combines orthography like the initial sound law; and it simplifies distribution by bundling dictionaries.

From a practitioner's standpoint, I land on three moves.

8.1 For archiving, default to glossing or ruby

If you digitize old newspapers or records, do not use hangul-only mode alone; default to hanja glossing or ruby to preserve the original. Keeping a hangul version for search and readability alongside the original hanja for verification and research is the safer long-term design.

8.2 For NLP preprocessing, divide labor with the LLM

Insert it into the normalization stage of a Korean NLP pipeline, but rather than pitting it against an LLM, split the work. Deterministic, high-volume, offline conversion is fast and cheap with a dictionary-and-rule library. Preserve the hanja information that normalization erases as separate metadata.

8.3 Pick the target that fits your deployment

Choose WASM for a browser tool, napi for a Node backend, and Rust or the CLI for large batches. Since one core branches per environment, you can take the optimal path while keeping result consistency. But before adopting, be sure to check OCR-stage error propagation, out-of-dictionary vocabulary, and the GPL 3.0 license.
