Introduction: Why Japanese Stocks Are Drawing Attention Now

Since 2023, a rally that pushed the Nikkei 225 to record highs, pressure from the Tokyo Stock Exchange on listed companies to manage with greater awareness of capital cost and share price (widely known as the "value-up" push), and a prolonged period of yen weakness have combined to sharply increase interest in Japanese stocks among Korean retail investors. As of 2025, foreign investors reportedly held a record 34.7% share of the Japanese stock market.

But rising interest has also led many investors to jump in without fully understanding how the market works. The Tokyo market differs from Korea's in several structural ways: the unit-share trading convention (typically 100 shares per lot), two distinct benchmark indexes (Nikkei 225 and TOPIX), a unique shareholder-benefit culture, and Korean tax rules that apply specifically to overseas stock investing. This guide, current as of July 2026, covers what a first-time investor from Korea should know: opening an account and converting currency, market structure, individual stocks versus ETFs, dividend and shareholder-benefit culture, taxes, and FX risk. It does not recommend any specific stock or guarantee any return, and details that vary by country or over time are presented as general principles rather than fixed facts.

1. Getting Started - Accounts and Currency Conversion

The first step in investing in Japanese stocks is deciding where to open an account.

1.1 Account Opening Routes

For most Korea residents, the most practical route is the overseas (Japan) stock trading service offered by a Korean brokerage. Most major Korean securities firms support trading on Tokyo Stock Exchange-listed shares. By contrast, opening an account directly with a Japan-based broker such as SBI Securities as a non-resident often requires a Japanese address or a local bank account, making it a higher barrier to entry for most Korean residents.

The general process is: (1) open an account with a Korean brokerage, (2) apply separately for the overseas stock trading service, (3) convert Korean won into Japanese yen, and (4) place orders on Tokyo-listed shares through the broker's app or trading platform. Online commission rates are commonly cited around 0.2-0.3%, but they vary meaningfully by broker, promotion, and time period, so always confirm the current fee schedule with your broker before trading.

1.2 Yen Conversion and Trading Mechanics

Most brokers support real-time currency conversion within their app, or an integrated margin service that automatically converts won when you place a won-denominated order. Conversion involves a spread on top of the reference rate, and relationship discounts or promotional rates can reduce this cost. The Tokyo Stock Exchange's regular trading hours are a morning session from 9:00 to 11:30, a lunch break, and an afternoon session from 12:30 to 15:30 Japan Standard Time (extended by 30 minutes starting in November 2024, and subject to further change). Korea and Japan share the same standard time, so there is no time difference to manage — a practical convenience.

One thing that often surprises first-time investors is Japan's unit-share (tan-gen kabu) system: most Japanese listed stocks trade in lots of 100 shares, meaning a single trade in a higher-priced stock can require a substantial amount of capital. That said, some brokers now offer odd-lot (single-share) trading services, so if you want to start small, check whether your broker supports fractional trading.

2. Understanding the Market - Nikkei 225, TOPIX, and Market Segments

Understanding the basic structure of the Tokyo market makes it much easier to interpret news and research reports.

2.1 Nikkei 225 vs TOPIX

The Nikkei 225 is calculated by the Nikkei newspaper and tracks 225 representative stocks on the Tokyo Stock Exchange Prime Market using a price-weighted method (each stock's price is multiplied by an adjustment factor and simply averaged), with up to three constituent changes made in regular reviews each April and October. TOPIX (Tokyo Stock Price Index) is the official index calculated directly by the Tokyo Stock Exchange, using a market-capitalization-weighted method across a broader universe of listed shares, giving larger companies more influence. The two indexes generally move in the same direction, but differences in methodology are reported to produce differing annual returns in some years. Since actual performance can depend on which benchmark a fund tracks, always check whether an ETF or index fund follows the Nikkei 225 or TOPIX before investing.

2.2 TSE Market Segments and Notable Companies

In April 2022, the Tokyo Stock Exchange reorganized its previous five market segments into three: Prime, Standard, and Growth. The Prime Market targets large, well-established companies expected to maintain constructive dialogue with global institutional investors, and its listing-maintenance requirements — such as tradable share market capitalization and float ratio — are stricter than those for Standard or Growth. Reports indicate that after transitional measures end in March 2026, companies that fail to meet the tightened criteria may be designated for monitoring and could eventually face delisting.

Large companies across a range of industries are listed on the Prime Market, including Toyota Motor, Sony Group, Mitsubishi Corporation, Fast Retailing (operator of Uniqlo), and SoftBank Group. These names are cited only to illustrate market structure and are not a recommendation to buy any specific stock.

3. Individual Stocks vs ETFs - How to Invest

There are two broad approaches to accessing Japanese stocks: buying individual shares directly, or investing through a diversified ETF.

3.1 Direct Stock Investing

Investing directly in a specific company and receiving dividends and shareholder benefits firsthand is the main appeal of individual stock investing. However, the unit-share system described above can require substantial capital depending on the stock, and corporate disclosures and financial reports are mostly provided in Japanese, which reduces information accessibility compared to Korean stocks. You should also keep in mind that exchange rate movements pass through directly to your returns.

3.2 Diversifying Through ETFs

Korea-listed ETFs that track major Japanese benchmarks such as the Nikkei 225 allow diversified investing in Korean won starting from a small amount. These ETFs come in currency-unhedged and currency-hedged versions. With an unhedged ETF, exchange rate movements are added directly to returns — if the index rises but the yen weakens, the KRW-denominated return can end up lower than the index gain, and vice versa if the yen strengthens. A hedged ETF minimizes this FX impact but incurs a separate hedging cost. ETFs are generally considered more accessible for beginners due to lower capital requirements and built-in diversification, though the right choice depends on your investment goals and personal circumstances.

4. Dividends and Shareholder Benefits Culture

An essential feature of Japanese stock investing is the evolving dividend landscape and the country's distinctive shareholder-benefit system.

4.1 Dividend Trends and Recent Change

Japanese companies have traditionally been viewed as having lower payout ratios than their Western counterparts. However, as the Tokyo Stock Exchange has pushed listed companies toward capital-cost- and share-price-conscious management, more companies are reportedly increasing dividends and share buybacks. At the same time, as foreign institutional investors increasingly call for fair cash dividends over in-kind shareholder benefits, some large companies have reduced or discontinued their shareholder-benefit programs in favor of higher cash dividends. This shift varies in pace and direction from company to company, so it is difficult to generalize.

4.2 The Shareholder Benefit (Kabunushi Yutai) System

Shareholder benefits are a distinctly Japanese practice said to have originated with railway companies in the Meiji era, which gave shareholders complimentary travel passes. Under this system, shareholders who hold a certain number of shares as of a designated record date receive, in addition to dividends, company products, discount coupons, regional specialty goods, or gift cards. It has long served as a marketing tool to attract retail investors and encourage long-term holding.

Although foreign investors reportedly held a record 34.7% of the Japanese market as of 2025, receiving physical shareholder benefits often requires a Japanese mailing address or is otherwise unavailable for overseas delivery, so an investor residing abroad may not actually receive the benefit, or may need to complete additional procedures. Unlike dividends, shareholder benefits are generally not paid automatically regardless of where your brokerage account is located, so if you are considering a purchase specifically for the benefit, verify the company's eligibility conditions and whether your broker facilitates delivery in advance.

5. Taxes - Capital Gains and Dividend Tax (General Principles for Korea Residents)

Taxes are the most confusing part of overseas stock investing. The following reflects general principles applicable to Korea residents as of July 2026; tax law can change, and treatment can vary by individual circumstances.

5.1 Capital Gains Tax

Capital gains from overseas stocks, including Japanese shares, are subject to Korea's capital gains tax. A flat 22% rate (including local income tax) generally applies to gains above an annual basic deduction of 2.5 million KRW. There is no preliminary filing; taxpayers generally file a final return and pay the tax between May 1 and May 31 of the following year. Gains and losses across domestic and overseas stocks in the same year are reportedly allowed to offset each other, but the specific application and procedure should be confirmed against that year's tax law amendments.

5.2 Dividend Tax and Double Taxation Relief

Dividend income can be subject to overlapping taxing rights between the two countries. Under the Korea-Japan tax treaty, dividends paid to an ordinary individual (portfolio) investor are generally subject to a 15% withholding tax in Japan (a reduced 5% rate applies only to a special case of directly holding 25% or more of the voting shares, which does not typically apply to individual retail investors), while Korea's dividend income tax is based on a rate of 14% (15.4% including local income tax). Tax already withheld in Japan can be partially offset against Korean tax through the foreign tax credit.

However, exactly how a brokerage handles withholding and tax adjustment can vary by trading platform and product structure. In addition, if your annual financial income (interest plus dividends) exceeds 20 million KRW, you may become subject to comprehensive financial income taxation, so investors with significant dividend income should consult a tax professional.

6. Yen (FX) Risk and Opportunity

In Japanese stock investing, the yen exchange rate is a variable just as important as the stock price itself.

6.1 Unhedged vs Hedged Exposure

With direct stock holdings or currency-unhedged ETFs, both stock price movement and exchange rate movement are reflected in returns. If you buy when the yen is weak and it later strengthens, a currency gain adds to any stock price gain. Conversely, if you buy when the yen is strong and it subsequently weakens, currency losses can offset stock price gains, or even turn a nominal gain into a loss once converted back to won.

6.2 What Drives the Yen Exchange Rate

One of the key variables affecting the yen against the won or the dollar is the interest-rate gap between the Bank of Japan (BOJ) and the U.S. Federal Reserve. The BOJ is reported to have gradually raised its policy rate after ending its long-standing negative interest rate policy, but market expectations about the pace and future path of these moves differ across institutions and over time. Indeed, forecasts made around the same period have simultaneously predicted both a reversal toward yen strength and continued yen weakness. This illustrates that exchange rates are driven by a complex mix of factors beyond interest rate differentials alone — including trade balances, global risk sentiment, and capital flows — and are very difficult to predict in the short term.

Key takeaway: rather than trying to time currency direction speculatively, deciding your hedged-versus-unhedged exposure in advance and taking a diversified, long-term approach is generally considered the safer principle.

7. Common Pitfalls for Beginners

  • Information asymmetry: Japanese corporate disclosures and IR materials are mostly published in Japanese, so it helps to build a habit of using translation tools or English-language IR materials alongside the originals.
  • Different trading conventions: There is no time difference, but the midday trading halt and other market conventions differ from Korea's exchange.
  • Capital requirements from unit shares: The 100-share lot convention can require more capital than expected for certain stocks.
  • Delisting and monitoring risk: Reports indicate that tighter Tokyo Stock Exchange listing standards may result in some stocks being designated for monitoring or eventually delisted.
  • Conversion timing and fees: Actual returns can be affected by conversion spreads and timing, so it's worth comparing FX terms across brokers.
  • Tax filing obligations: Don't forget that overseas stock capital gains require a separate final return the following May.
  • Cross-check information sources: Avoid trading based solely on confident predictions from a single community or video creator; cross-verify across multiple sources.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. How do I start investing in Japanese stocks from Korea?

For most Korea residents, using a Korean brokerage's overseas (Japan) stock trading service is the most practical route. You apply for the service in the broker's app, convert won to yen, and place orders on Tokyo-listed shares. Opening a Japan-based broker account directly as a non-resident is generally harder due to address and local bank account requirements. Fees vary by broker, so check current disclosures.

Q2. What is the difference between the Nikkei 225 and TOPIX?

The Nikkei 225 is a price-weighted index of 225 stocks calculated by the Nikkei newspaper; TOPIX is the official market-cap-weighted index calculated by the Tokyo Stock Exchange. They generally move together but can diverge in annual returns due to methodology, so check which benchmark a fund tracks before investing.

Q3. Should beginners choose individual Japanese stocks or ETFs?

There is no fixed answer. Individual stocks let you focus on a specific company and receive dividends and benefits directly but can require significant capital due to unit-share lots and Japanese-language disclosures. Korea-listed ETFs enable small, diversified investing and are generally viewed as more accessible, though FX impact differs between hedged and unhedged versions. Choose based on your capital and experience.

Q4. How are dividends and capital gains from Japanese stocks taxed for Korea residents?

As a general rule, capital gains face a flat 22% tax (including local tax) above an annual 2.5 million KRW deduction, filed the following May. Dividends face roughly 15% Japanese withholding, with Korea's 14% (15.4% including local tax) dividend tax partly offset via the foreign tax credit. Handling varies by broker and product, so verify current details.

Q5. How does yen appreciation or depreciation affect my investment returns?

Unhedged holdings add currency movement directly to stock price movement. Buying when the yen is weak and later seeing it strengthen can add a currency gain; the reverse can offset stock gains with currency losses. Hedged products minimize this but add a cost. Since exchange rates are very hard to predict, a diversified, long-term approach is generally advised.

Conclusion

Japanese stocks have become an attractive market for Korean retail investors, driven by the Nikkei 225's record-high rally, expanding dividends and buybacks tied to the value-up trend, and the practical convenience of geographic proximity with no time difference. However, investors must also weigh factors that don't apply to domestic Korean stocks: the unit-share system, a largely Japanese-language information environment, restrictions on physically receiving shareholder benefits, a tax structure spanning two countries, and a yen exchange rate that is notoriously hard to predict.

If you're just starting out, it may help to first compare account-opening routes and fee structures, confirm whether a product tracks the Nikkei 225 or TOPIX, and consider building familiarity through a Korea-listed ETF with lower capital requirements before expanding into individual stocks. Always remember the following May tax filing deadline, and approach the yen exchange rate from a diversification standpoint rather than trying to call its direction.
