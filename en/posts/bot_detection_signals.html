Is an Empty User-Agent a Bot - What Works and What Does Not in Detecting Automated Signups

A one-line rule turns away people before it turns away bots - reading signals in combination and grading your response

Introduction: a judgement that starts with one blank field

A handful of accounts appear on the member list at around the same time every day. They never post and never log in again. You open the access log and the User-Agent field on the requests that created them is empty. One thought usually follows. If the User-Agent is empty, reject it.

That single line really does reduce ghost accounts. It also quietly pushes some genuine users out of the service. People who get blocked do not file a support ticket, they close the tab, so the dashboard keeps a record of blocks and no record of the people you lost.

This article covers what you should know before adding that line: why a value the client sends cannot separate people from automation, what to look at instead, and why grading your response by the cost of being wrong rather than by detection accuracy is the design that survives in production. Attacker techniques are out of scope.

1. An empty User-Agent is not evidence of a bot

RFC 9110, which defines HTTP semantics, says in section 10.1.5 that A user agent SHOULD generate a User-Agent header field. SHOULD, not MUST. Omitting it is not a violation.

The same section adds that a user agent MAY allow its User-Agent value to be configured by the user. Letting the user change the value is behaviour the specification anticipates. This field was designed from the start as a slot the client may fill or leave empty, which is why it comes up blank or odd for many reasons in real traffic.

What you see in the logCommon causeIs it automation
No valueConnections from native apps and mobile SDKs that are not browsersNo. These are your app users
No valueHeader normalisation by a corporate proxy or security gatewayNo. This is enterprise traffic

The last row is the conclusion of this section. An empty value is not evidence of automation, and a complete value is not evidence of a person. Both directions have collapsed, so this one field cannot decide anything either way.

  • Sending it is a recommendation, not an obligation - a request without it is a normal request
  • The content is chosen by the client - changing it through user settings is anticipated behaviour
  • The protocol gives the server no way to verify the value - there is no original to compare against

2. Freely forged - a claim, not evidence

The third item is the crux. The User-Agent is a self-description the client writes about itself, and the server has nothing to check it against. The same reasoning behind why client-supplied values must not cross the trust boundary applies here. For the reason you never trust a price field in a request body, you cannot trust an identity claim in a request header.

The people who write browser documentation discourage branching on this value as well. MDN puts a warning at the top of the User-Agent page pointing readers to why serving different content to different browsers is usually a bad idea, and notes that this header can identify a particular user agent and therefore be used for fingerprinting.

The consequence is a shrinking value. Browsers are deliberately reducing what the header carries and directing servers that need more to ask through a separate hints mechanism. Any rule that distinguishes something using this string loses resolution over time.

2.1 What the value is still good for

That does not make it useless. It cannot be the sole basis for allowing or denying access, but the value still does work in three places.

  • Statistics and auditing - understanding what client mix reaches the service. Because it is not used for decisions, forgeability does not matter
  • Recognising crawlers that announce themselves - search crawlers usually write a name and an informational URL into the value, but the confirmation has to come from another axis. Google documents a reverse lookup confirmed by a forward lookup, and matching against published address ranges
  • Narrowing candidates - a hint about where to look harder. The decision belongs to the axes that come after

In short - a bot that announces itself can be recognised by name, and automation that has chosen not to announce itself cannot. User-Agent rules create an asymmetry that filters only the honest side. For honest crawlers, declaring policy in robots.txt beats hand-maintaining a rule list.

3. The cost of false blocks - the people you lose quietly

The number people usually look at when judging a block rule is the count of blocked requests. That number does not tell you whether the rule is working correctly. What you need to know is how many humans were in that set, and standard logs do not carry it. False blocks are dangerous because they are quiet.

  • Bots come back after failing. Retry is the default in automation, so blocking is less effective at reducing total automated volume than it feels
  • People do not come back after failing. Few users who are refused once on a signup screen try again from another browser
  • Complaints never reach you. Contacting support usually requires a login, and someone blocked at signup has no account
  • Shared addresses hide crowds. Carrier-grade address sharing and corporate proxies can put hundreds or thousands of people behind one source

Accessibility overlaps here. Assistive technology, low-end devices, narrow links, and users of autofill and password managers naturally produce patterns that do not look human. The more refined the detector, the more this group tends to get caught first. It is the same objection raised repeatedly about verification getting heavier.

So there is a step to take before enabling any rule: observation mode. Do not apply the rule, only record which requests it would have caught. Skim a day or two of that list and you will usually see immediately whether your own app users or enterprise customers are in it. A rule enabled without this step never tells you what it cut away.

4. Combining signals - behaviour, network, time

So what do you look at? There are three broad axes, and the premise is that all three are weak on their own.

4.1 Behaviour - the speed and order of input

  • People do not fill a form top to bottom in one pass. They go back and correct, and the gaps between fields are uneven
  • Typing and pasting are mixed, and focus often leaves the page and returns
  • Automation is usually uniform. But uniformity also comes from password managers and autofill

4.2 Network and connection characteristics

  • Repeated creation requests from one source in a short window. This shows up in batches, not in individual requests
  • A mismatch between what is declared and what happens. If a complete browser string arrives but none of the subresource requests a browser always makes follow, that inconsistency is the observation
  • No solo decisions here either. One source address does not mean one person

4.3 Time distribution - a signal visible only in aggregate

  • Human signups trace a daily curve. They rise in the morning and fall overnight, and weekdays have a shape too
  • Creation repeating at exactly the same hour and minute is not a human rhythm but a scheduler rhythm. It only emerges when you overlay several days
AxisWhat you look atWhy it is weak aloneWhat it means when it overlaps
Declared valueUser-Agent and the shape of request headersThe client chooses it freelyMeaningful only when it conflicts with another axis
BehaviourInput order and the distribution of gapsAssistive tech and autofill make the same shapeWith time distribution, the odds rise
NetworkRequest batches per source, declared versus observedMany people sit behind one addressSame source plus same clock pattern is strong

No signal is a basis on its own, and they only get strong when several axes point the same way. That getting strong does not mean 「you may block」 is the subject of the next section.

5. Where false positives are expensive and where they are cheap

Bot handling usually fails not on detection accuracy but on the response being binary. Pass or block, and every judgement becomes a death sentence. That forces you to keep the rules loose, and loose rules stop nothing. The first thing to build is not a threshold but tiers.

Signal strengthResponseWhat a wrong call costs the userCost to undo
WeakLog onlyNothingNothing
Weak to mediumDelay listing visibility, exclude from indexingTheir post appears laterFlip the visibility flag back
MediumDefer some capability on new accountsLink posting or invites come laterLift the deferral
Medium to strongSend to a human review queueThey wait for approvalReview turnaround time
StrongRefuse signup or suspend the accountThey cannot use it and usually leaveUndoing it does not bring them back

The last column is the whole design. The top three rows can be undone when you are wrong; the bottom row cannot be repaired by undoing it. Exhaust the reversible responses before you reach for the last row.

This is not an invention. In the OWASP catalogue of automated threats, bulk account creation is listed as OAT-019, and one recommended countermeasure is limiting the functionality and/or capacity available to all newly created accounts. Not picking out the bad ones and restricting them, but lowering the initial privilege of every new account.

5.1 Separate scoring from the decision

When signal evaluation and the decision sit in one lump, changing the criteria requires a deploy, and you cannot replay past requests against new criteria.

// Conceptual example - written to show the shape, not executed or verified.
// Weights and thresholds differ per service, so do not copy them as is.

score = 0
if (ua_absent)            score += 1   // weak. Never decides anything alone
if (source_burst)         score += 2   // repeated from one source in a short window
if (schedule_like_timing) score += 2   // only visible when days are overlaid
if (declared_vs_observed) score += 3   // claims to be a browser, no subresource requests

decision =
    score >= 6 ? "queue_for_human_review"   // a person looks at it
  : score >= 3 ? "limit_new_account"        // reversible restriction
  :              "log_only"                 // record only

// Blocking is not on this list. The strongest automatic outcome is handing it to a human.

The last comment is the point. If the strongest automatic decision is 「hand it to a human」, the detector's false positives never reach the user directly.

6. What to log today so you can decide later

Bot decisions are usually made after the fact. A strange account catches your eye, and then you open the log. Whether you can answer at that moment is set by what you kept today. It is a question of structure, not volume.

  • Timestamps down to the millisecond. Second granularity is not enough to read a time distribution. Check that clocks across servers agree
  • A correlation key joining requests to events. If you cannot tell which request produced an account, you cannot reconstruct the context. This one key turns a multi-day investigation into a few minutes
  • Rule id, score and decision together. Keeping only the outcome means you can never fix the rule. Which rule gave how many points and what it decided belong on the same line
  • Shadow verdicts from observation mode. Records marked 「would have blocked」 without blocking
  • Headers as shape, not as values. Which headers were present and what the set looked like is what detection uses. Storing full values only adds personal data

What not to keep is equally clear. Never keep the input content itself. What you need is the distribution of gaps, not what was typed. The same criteria used when deciding log levels apply here. Decide the question first, then keep only what answers it.

7. Privacy - detection data is personal data too

Values collected to catch bots are not exempt from privacy rules. If anything they are more sensitive, because raising detection power and raising identifying power point in the same direction.

The EU General Data Protection Regulation says so directly in recital 30. Natural persons may be associated with online identifiers provided by their devices, tools and protocols, including internet protocol addresses, cookie identifiers, and those traces combined with other information may be used to create profiles and identify them.

Korean law points the same way. Article 3 of the Personal Information Protection Act requires a clearly defined purpose and collection of only the minimum personal data necessary for that purpose, and bars use beyond it. Article 21 requires destruction without delay once the data becomes unnecessary, with measures preventing recovery or reproduction.

7.1 Four things this becomes in design

  • Write the purpose down. Keep it narrow, such as preventing automated signups, and never repurpose the values for personalisation or marketing
  • Cut the retention period. A few weeks is usually enough to read a time distribution. There is rarely a reason to hold raw values for a year
  • Lower the resolution. Buckets instead of exact milliseconds and network ranges instead of exact addresses still show you whether something repeats
  • Put it in the privacy notice. If you cannot write down what you collect, why and for how long, that is a signal to revisit the collection

Pushing fingerprint precision ever higher runs straight into these rules. That tension is exactly what makes watching behaviour across a whole session contested.

One practical test - each time you add a signal, first answer 「what harm reaches users if this value leaks」. If the answer is 「their device and habits are exposed」, that signal is expensive relative to the detection power it buys.

8. Fix the flow before you fix the signals

Everything so far has been about detection. This section is about needing less of it, and it is usually far cheaper.

Open a service that produces automated signups in bulk and you often find account creation sitting ahead of any explicit user action. An anonymous account is created when the app launches or the first screen opens, and details are filled in later. Convenient, but it has a price. Everything that opens a link becomes a member, including preview generators and indexers. That is not an attack, it is ordinary web behaviour.

The fix is not attaching a detector but changing the order. Move account creation behind an explicit agree-and-start gesture and automated visits stop creating accounts. The population to classify disappears, so the signals no longer need to be accurate. This is a measurement problem before it is a security measure. Inflated signup counts make conversion and active-user numbers wrong. Signups falling after the fix is not a loss but the numbers becoming honest.

Generalised: do not put state-changing actions where an automatic request can pass through them by accident. Screens that increment a counter on view, flows that send a notification on a preview fetch, and designs that consume an invite merely by opening a link all carry the same problem.

In August 2026 a three-week investigation of this problem was posted to a Korean developer community. The operator of an anonymous community added a rule blocking crawler names and then a rule rejecting empty User-Agents, hit the side effect of blocking real app users, and finally solved it by changing the flow so that no connection or account is created until the agree button is pressed. The value of that write-up is that the conclusion was a flow change, not a longer signal list.

9. What I could not verify

  • The share of traffic that is automated, and detection rates for particular methods, are not in this article. Published figures use inconsistent measurers, definitions and denominators, and many exist to show a vendor's own product working
  • Commercial bot management products are not compared. I could not find public measurements taken under identical conditions independently of the vendors
  • Whether a given realtime communication library sends a User-Agent depends on the client implementation and transport. I treated it only as a case from a published operational write-up, not as general library behaviour
  • The code fragment is a conceptual example written to show the shape, and it was not executed or verified

10. Frequently asked questions

Can I treat an empty User-Agent as a bot?

No. The HTTP specification only recommends the header, and it anticipates that users may change the value. An empty value is not 「this is a bot」 but 「there is no browser-shaped self-description here」, and it means something only when it overlaps with another axis.

Then is the User-Agent useless?

It cannot be the sole basis for allowing or denying access. It is fine for statistics and auditing, and it helps recognise crawlers that announce themselves. Confirmation, though, has to come from a reverse lookup or a match against published address ranges.

How many signals must line up before I block?

It is not a counting problem. Reversible responses such as logging only or deferring a new account capability can fire on weak signals. Responses that make a user leave on the spot, like refusing a signup, belong only where several axes point the same way.

Are the addresses and headers I collect for detection personal data?

Treat them as such. EU rules state that protocol addresses and cookie identifiers can be used to identify people, and Korean law requires minimum collection and destruction without delay once the data is unnecessary. Write the purpose narrowly, cut retention, lower the resolution.

What is the cheapest way to reduce automated signups?

Move the point where accounts are created before you attach any detector. Push creation behind an explicit user gesture and automated visits stop creating accounts, which shrinks the population you would otherwise classify.

11. Conclusion - three decisions for today

First, run your existing block rules in observation mode for a single day. Do not block; just collect the list they would have caught and read it. If your own app users or enterprise customers are in that list, the rule was already cutting people.

Second, create one tier between pass and block. Lowering the initial privilege of new accounts takes the least work. Once that tier exists, a clumsy detector no longer hurts users.

Third, check in the code where accounts are created. If it sits ahead of an explicit user action, move that first. It usually removes more problems with less code than any detector you could add.

The most common illusion in bot detection is that finding an accurate signal ends the problem. Values sent by the client cannot be verified, and behaviour and timing have plenty of human exceptions. The perfect signal is not coming. What you can build today is a structure that tolerates being wrong. That is the order that lasts.
