Claude Opus 5 Released - Near-Fable 5 Intelligence at Half the Price

The effort dial opens an era of cost control - and the announced numbers need careful reading

Jul 25, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: Opus Returns, and This Time the Price Tag Is the Story

On July 24, 2026, Anthropic released Claude Opus 5. The focus of this launch is not a new performance peak but intelligence per dollar. According to the company, Opus 5 aims to deliver intelligence close to its top flagship, Claude Fable 5, at half the price. It is priced at 5 dollars per million input tokens and 25 dollars per million output tokens - the same as the previous-generation Opus 4.8. Measured against Fable 5 list pricing (10 dollars input, 50 dollars output), that is half the per-token cost.

The notable addition this generation is the effort dial. A single model can be tuned from low to max across several levels, trading performance against token consumption - a different shape from the single-model choice of the chatbot era. This piece lays out the announced specs and benchmarks, explains what the effort dial means in practice, considers which workloads Opus 5 fits, and asks how far the self-reported figures should be trusted, from a working developer's angle. Worth noting: as the fourth model in under two months after Mythos 5, Fable 5, and Sonnet 5, this launch is also a statement about Anthropic's release cadence itself.

1. What Shipped - The Specs

Cross-checking the announcement and follow-up coverage, here are the essentials. All figures are as reported by the company.

1.1 Price and Position - Higher Intelligence at the Same Cost as Opus 4.8

Opus 5 lists at 5 dollars per million input tokens and 25 dollars per million output tokens, identical to Opus 4.8. Anthropic's logic, then, is that it kept the price flat and raised the intelligence. On placement, it is the new default model on Claude Max and the strongest model available on Claude Pro, with a context window described in the million-token range. Developers can call it as claude-opus-5 on the API.

1.2 The Effort Dial - One Model, Five Levels

The most striking change this generation is the effort setting. According to the announcement, it can be set to low, medium, high, xhigh, or max, with Anthropic recommending low or medium for most tasks and xhigh for coding work. Raising effort has the model spend more reasoning and tokens to lift quality; lowering it conserves tokens and cost for faster, cheaper answers. In effect, the center of gravity shifts from choosing a model to choosing the strength of the same model.

1.3 Fast Mode and Supporting Features

According to the announcement, the new Fast mode runs at roughly 2.5 times the standard speed but charges double the base price - a way to buy speed with money for interactive tools where latency shapes the experience or for tight deadlines. The release also brings API features such as automatic fallback routing and swapping tools mid-conversation without busting the prompt cache. All of these read as aimed less at single-response polish and more at the operability of long-running agent workflows.

2. What the Effort Dial Actually Means

2.1 The Problem of Predictable Cost

One of the biggest headaches when wiring an LLM in at scale is cost predictability. Even with the same prompt, token consumption can swing several-fold depending on how long the model reasons. The effort dial can be seen as a way for operators to bound that variability up front: cap low-risk, high-volume work at low or medium to hold down unit cost, and reserve xhigh or max for hard coding and design judgment - a policy you can enforce at the code level.

2.2 Pulling Model Routing Inside the Model

In practice, cost optimization has hinged on routing - swapping between a cheap small model and an expensive flagship depending on the situation. The effort dial is an attempt to handle much of that routing inside a single model. That can cut the quality variance and integration burden of moving between separate models, but genuinely cheap high-volume processing may still fit a Haiku-class small model better, so it is premature to treat one dial as a replacement for every tier.

4. Where It Sits Against Rivals

4.1 Within Anthropic's Own Lineup

Opus 5's most direct comparison is with Anthropic's own models. Priced the same as Opus 4.8, it is effectively a free upgrade; against Fable 5, it narrows the intelligence gap while halving the price. In summary:

4.2 Compared to OpenAI and Google

Rival models are mentioned only on the same dry terms. OpenAI splits its GPT-5.6 family into tiers from the top-end GPT-5.6 Sol down to the low-latency, low-cost GPT-5.6 Luna, and as the table shows, Sol is reported higher than Opus 5 on some coding metrics such as DeepSWE v1.1. Google has recently leaned into low-latency small models like Gemini 3.6 Flash and Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite. The three approaches differ: Anthropic is putting a dial on a high-performance model to widen its cost range, Google is strengthening small, fast tiers, and OpenAI is slicing the tiers themselves more finely. Rather than declaring one approach superior, the practical move is to first see which axis your own workload is sensitive to.

5. A Practical Lens - Where to Use It

5.1 Where Opus 5 Fits Well

Given the effort dial and a million-token-class context, Opus 5 fits especially well for work that needs deep reasoning within a single call - agentic coding over large codebases, multi-step refactoring, and complex document or data analysis. The real benefit is covering, at half the price, the band where you needed Fable 5-class quality but the budget stung. For coding, you can start at xhigh as Anthropic suggests and dial down once quality is sufficient to cut cost.

5.2 Where to Be Cautious

Conversely, for low-difficulty, high-volume tasks with a clear correct answer - classification, routing, summarization - it can be cheaper to run Opus 5 at low or medium rather than max, or to use a Haiku-class small model outright. Fast mode is also double the price, so it makes sense to switch on selectively only where speed truly drives revenue or experience. The point to remember is that effort and Fast are not free; they are handles that buy performance and speed by adding cost.

6. A Critical Read - The Limits of Self-Reported Numbers

The part to read most carefully in this launch is the benchmarks. Most of the scores are vendor-measured under the vendor's own conditions, and it cannot be ruled out that the comparison set, prompts, and effort settings were arranged favorably. Indeed, metrics where rivals score higher, such as DeepSWE v1.1, coexist in the same release, and some coverage notes there were benchmarks where pushing effort to the maximum raised cost but lowered the score. Anthropic itself also said the model remains behind top models in security areas such as vulnerability exploitation. In other words, the one-line summary of Fable 5-class at half the price is an average impression, not a guarantee that holds on every task. If you are evaluating adoption, it is safer to measure quality, cost, and latency on your own task samples rather than a marketing table, then expand from low-risk use cases outward.

Conclusion

The core of Claude Opus 5 is not a new high score but the price tag and the dial. By raising intelligence at the same cost as Opus 4.8 and widening the cost-and-speed range with five effort levels and Fast mode, this launch shows the center of competitive gravity shifting from who is smartest to who sells the same intelligence more cheaply and predictably. Set alongside Google pushing small, fast tiers and OpenAI slicing finer tiers, the directions differ but the destination is similar. The practical move for developers is clear: treat the announced figures as a reference line, split your workload by difficulty and tune the effort dial on real data, and verify for yourself the conditions behind the half-price claim. As of July 2026, AI model competition has entered a phase contested not over spec sheets but over cost handles.
