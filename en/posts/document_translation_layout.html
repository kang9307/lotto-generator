Why Translated Documents Fall Apart - Layout Is Harder Than Text

The moment the same meaning becomes a different length, fixed-layout documents break

Intro: the translation is done, but the document is unreadable

You drop a twelve page contract into a translation tool. Every sentence comes back in the target language. Then you open the file and the last column of the second table runs past the right margin, a footnote has landed in the middle of a body paragraph, and one subheading wrapped onto a second line and pushed everything after it a page down. The translation itself is not bad. The document is unusable.

Most people respond by switching translation engines. The same thing happens again, because the cause was never on the translation side. Translation is an operation that preserves meaning, not length, and once length changes something has to move on a fixed page. Layer on a format like PDF, where the concept of a paragraph does not exist at all, and the engine ends up translating whatever the extractor guessed was a paragraph.

So the problem has two floors. The lower floor is recovering structure; the upper floor is re-seating changed lengths on a page. The causes and the fixes differ, but from the outside both look identical: it broke when we translated it. This article separates the two floors. It does not tell you which product to buy. Once you know the shape of the problem you can pick tools on your own terms, and more importantly you learn which parts cannot be fixed at all before you spend time on them.

1. Translation changes the character count

Start by accepting one fact: moving the same content into another language changes how much room it needs. That is a property of languages, not a sign of a weak translator.

The W3C internationalization article Text size in translation puts numbers on it. It cites IBM guidance that expected expansion depends on the length of the original string when English is translated into European languages. Strings of ten characters or fewer can reach 200 to 300 percent of the original, strings of eleven to twenty characters land around 180 to 200 percent, and strings longer than seventy-one characters still come out near 130 percent. The article also gives a concrete case: the English word views becomes 2.6 times longer in Portuguese and 3.0 times longer in Italian.

The number to take away is not any single percentage. It is the rule that shorter strings expand the most. And in a document the shortest strings are almost always sitting in the narrowest boxes: table headers, button labels, chart legends, figure captions. The strings with the highest expansion factor live in the places with the least slack.

1.1 Shrinking is a problem too

Some pairs contract. English prose translated into Korean or Chinese often comes out with fewer characters. That does not mean you gained room, because character count and rendered width are different numbers. The same W3C article notes that Chinese, Japanese and Korean characters take up more space even at the same character count. This gap is sharpest in fixed-width typesetting and terminals, which I covered separately in the piece on East Asian width.

The vertical dimension moves as well. The article points out that different scripts need different line heights and gives Thai as an example, noting it requires around 150 percent of the vertical space Latin does. The line count can stay identical while the paragraph gets taller, and a taller paragraph pushes down everything beneath it.

1.2 Why the web survives this and documents do not

The same expansion usually leaves a web page intact. Boxes grow with their contents, following elements shift down, and the worst outcome is a longer page. That is exactly what the W3C article recommends: the more flexibly you can design your layout, the better. Avoid fixed-width containers, avoid text baked into graphics, keep presentation separate from content.

A fixed-layout document cannot take that advice. The page size is decided, every element is pinned to coordinates, and overflow has nowhere to go. Re-seating a translation therefore means recomputing the layout itself. That is the actual body of this problem.

2. A PDF does not store paragraphs

Here is why PDF is uniquely painful. There are no paragraphs inside a PDF file. Strictly speaking there are no sentences either, and words are not guaranteed.

Text in a PDF is a list of instructions saying draw this here. The structure is plain in clause 9 of ISO 32000-2: set a text matrix, move the position, show a string. One of the showing operators takes an array that mixes strings and numbers so that individual glyph positions can be nudged. Character spacing and word spacing are separate state values, and horizontal scaling is specified as a percentage of the normal glyph width.

Three consequences follow from that design.

  • The paragraph is something your eye assembled. Nothing in the file says these twelve lines belong together. A human infers it from leading and indentation
  • A space may not be a space character. Word gaps are sometimes real spaces and sometimes just a small coordinate jump that produces the same visual gap. In the latter case the extractor has to guess word boundaries from spacing
  • Drawing order is not reading order. The order the operators run in is the order things were painted, not the order a person reads them

So the phrase extracting text from a PDF is misleading. What actually happens is inferring structure back out of coordinates. Positions and sizes are examined to decide which glyphs form a line, which lines form a paragraph, what is a heading and what is body copy. If any of those judgements is wrong, the translation engine receives sentences that were already stitched together incorrectly. This is the first place a layout problem turns into a translation quality problem.

2.1 Tags change everything

PDF does have a way to carry logical structure: tagged PDF, defined in the document interchange clause of the same standard. Roles such as paragraph, heading, table and list are attached as a structure tree, and there is a replacement text entry for supplying text that differs from what is visually shown. The standard describes this structure as the basis for text extraction and reflow.

The catch is reality. Tagged PDFs are a minority. Files exported for print, scanned files and older files usually have nothing, so most PDFs you meet in practice are documents whose structure has to be guessed. Adding tags is the same investment as accessibility work, so if you are on the side that produces documents, it is also the cheapest way to prepay your translation bill.

3. Reading order - obvious to a human, inferred by a machine

Reading order is where structure recovery fails most often. The W3C PDF accessibility technique PDF3 states that reading order is determined primarily by the tag order of document elements, and gives a concrete failure: in a two column document a screen reader may read straight across both columns, interpreting them as one column. The same document notes that pages with complex layouts involving graphics, tables, footnotes, side bars and form fields may not convert into the correct reading order automatically and need manual repair.

In a translation pipeline this failure is even more visible than in accessibility. When the order is wrong, a sentence gets spliced into the middle of another sentence, and what comes out is a faithful translation of nonsense. The reader concludes the translation is bad. In fact the input was already broken.

The places machines get confused are fairly predictable.

  • Multi-column layout. Papers, magazines, leaflets. A line ends in the left column and continues at the top of the right one, but by coordinates the first line of the right column sits at the same height as the first line of the left
  • Footnotes and endnotes. Smaller type at the bottom of the page. Splice them into the flow and the paragraph breaks; drop them and the evidence disappears
  • Headers, footers and page numbers. Repeated at the same spot on every page. Fail to filter them and the translation contains the same sentence twelve times
  • Sidebars, boxes and figure captions. They sit beside the body but are not part of it, and where they attach differs from document to document
  • Tables. A table is a grid, not a sequence. Flattening it into one stream destroys the relationships between cells

A human makes these calls instantly, reading whitespace, rules and type size all at once. A machine can use the same signals, but they are conventions rather than rules, and they vary by document. There is no complete automation in this stretch. There are only ways to raise accuracy and ways to notice errors cheaply.

4. Why sentence-by-sentence translation ruins documents

Say structure recovery went well. The common next design is to cut the text into sentences and feed them in one at a time. It parallelizes nicely, caches well, and lets you retry only the failed pieces. Engineering-wise it is attractive. It costs three things in document quality.

4.1 Broken context

A lone sentence leaves too much undecided. What the pronoun refers to, which register to use, whether a noun is singular or plural, whether the sentence is part of a conditional clause. In languages where the sentence ending carries politeness, the tone of the whole document wobbles sentence by sentence. That is how you get a contract that suddenly turns conversational in the middle.

4.2 Inconsistent terminology

When the same term appears twice and comes back as two different renderings, that is close to inevitable in sentence-level processing, because each call knows nothing about the others. In technical and legal documents it is fatal. Wobbling terminology tells the reader there are two distinct concepts.

4.3 Broken self references

Documents contain sentences that point at themselves. See the table above. Discussed in section 3 below. As shown in figure 2. These hold only if that table and that section land in the same place in the translated file. But as section 1 showed, translation changes lengths and changed lengths move things. If the table above slides onto the next page, above is now a lie. Numbering suffers too: merge or split one table and 3 becomes 4.

This is why the translation industry has long refused to treat a document as a flat list of sentences. The design intent shows in XLIFF 2.1. The segment is the smallest container holding source and target together, but above it sits a unit, and a unit must contain at least one segment. Formatting codes are not mixed into the text; they are kept as original data and linked by reference, and there is dedicated machinery for codes whose span crosses several segments.

The rule to take from this is simple. A segment is a storage unit, not a decision unit. Splitting for storage and splitting for translation are different things. In practice the rule is honoured roughly like this.

  • Send the neighbouring sentences and the owning heading along with the segment. Translate one thing, but supply wide evidence
  • Fix the glossary first and enforce the same rendering across the document. That part is achievable without context
  • Flag self-referential sentences and re-check them at the very end, after typesetting
  • Pull numbers, symbols, formulas and code out of the translatable set, replace them with placeholders and restore them afterwards

5. Text inside tables and images

Body text that grows just gains a line. Tables and images do not get off that easily.

5.1 A table decides its widths first

Column widths are settled before the content goes in, so expanded text has nowhere to go. One of three things happens: the cell wraps and the row gets taller, the text spills outside the cell, or it is clipped and disappears. The last case is the dangerous one, because clipped text does not look like an error.

Now stack the rule from section 1 on top. Table headers are the shortest strings in the document, and the shortest strings expand the most. In other words the narrowest cell in a table expands the hardest. There is a reason translated tables look broken so often.

5.2 Text inside an image is not text

Words in a screenshot, a chart image or a scanned diagram are pixels, not character codes, so changing them means redrawing. Recognising them and re-seating another language in the same spot are wildly different difficulties: you have to erase the background, match the font, and fit expanded text into a small box. That is why the W3C article recommends not baking text into graphics. For anything that will be translated, that advice is worth several times its usual value.

Vector graphics are the middle ground. In a diagram the boxes and arrows are shapes while the labels are often live text. You can change the text, but the box is fixed, so a longer label breaks out of its container. That is the same class of problem as a table and deserves the same handling.

5.3 Strings that must not be translated

Documents contain strings you must not touch. Fail to filter them and the document breaks regardless of how good the translation is.

  • Formulas. A formula is layout, not text. Superscripts, subscripts and fractions are assembled by coordinates, so meaning evaporates the moment an extractor flattens it into one line
  • Code and commands. Comments are translatable, identifiers are not. Miss the distinction and you ship examples that no longer run
  • Proper nouns, units and currencies. Decide once for the whole document whether to localize them, and never alter the values. Changing a value changes a fact
  • Signatures, seals and form field names. For strings with legal weight, keeping the original alongside is the safe move

6. Fonts and character sets - missing glyphs are not drawn

Sometimes structure is recovered, the translation is fine, and the page is full of boxes and blanks. That is not an encoding problem. It means the font does not contain those glyphs.

PDF can embed fonts, and in practice a subset containing only the glyphs actually used is the norm, for file size reasons. The font clause of the standard cited above states that when a font descriptor describes an embedded font, all of its fields apply to that specific embedded font program, full or subset. Subsetting is the normal shape of things.

That collides with translation. A subset contains only the characters that appeared in the source. New characters introduced by translation are simply not there. What happens next depends on the processor, but the outcomes rhyme.

  • A blank or a box is drawn. The default glyph used for characters the font does not define
  • Another font is substituted. It looks better, but glyph widths change. Different widths move every line break in the block, and the expansion problem from section 1 happens a second time
  • Only the weight or slant disappears. If the regular face is available but the bold is not, emphasis quietly vanishes

The size of the problem depends on the language pair. Latin-only fonts contain no Hangul, kana or Han characters whatsoever, so translating English documents into East Asian languages is best treated as a full font swap. The reverse direction hurts less, since East Asian fonts usually include Latin. In exchange they carry tens of thousands of glyphs, so embedding them inflates the file noticeably.

Licensing deserves a note at the level of principle. A font is software, and the right to install it and the right to embed and redistribute it inside a document are different rights. Whether embedding is allowed at all, and in what form, is decided per font. A translated file is a new document you are distributing, so permission does not carry over automatically from the source. Checking an individual font means reading its licence text, and I am not judging any specific font here.

7. What to measure - quality and layout are separate metrics

A common mistake in this space is trying to collapse the result into one score. Whether the translation is good and whether the document is intact have different causes, different owners and different fixes. Merge them and you can no longer tell which side dragged the number down.

Translation quality already has established evaluation practice: human assessment, comparison against reference translations, error typologies. Layout does not have a widely agreed metric set. So what follows is not a standard but a list of things I consider cheap to count in practice. They share two properties: they can be automated, and they narrow down where a human has to look.

That last row is the round trip check. Pull the text back out of the translated file and line its structure up against the source, and you catch most large failures before a human opens anything. It is cheap and effective, and it is rarely wired into pipelines.

8. Practice - where to put the review gates

Compressed into decision rules, the article so far says this.

8.1 If the source file exists, work from the source file

This is the single biggest win. A PDF is output. If whatever produced it still exists, translating there and re-exporting is dramatically cheaper. The structure recovery stage vanishes entirely, the typesetting engine absorbs expansion, and you pick fonts for the target language from the start. If the reason you cannot do this is that nobody kept the source, that is a process problem rather than a technical one.

8.2 If it does not, split the job in two

Doing structure recovery and translation in one shot leaves you unable to tell which one failed. Separate them and put one human checkpoint in between.

  1. Structure recovery. Rebuild paragraphs, tables and reading order, and keep the intermediate result in a form a person can look at
  2. Structure review. Check table counts, column order, footnote placement, header removal. This gate is the cheapest one, because you have not spent the translation budget yet
  3. Translation. Run it with document-level context and a fixed glossary
  4. Typesetting. Decide in advance how expansion is absorbed: recompute column widths, shrink type, or allow more rows
  5. Final review. Count overflows, font substitutions and reference numbers by machine, and have a human open only that list

8.3 Classify the unfixable first

The biggest time sink is trying to make impossible things work. Separate these out before you start and decide a policy for each.

  • Scanned images. They require recognition, and recognition errors get amplified by translation
  • Handwriting, signatures and seals. Keep the original. Translating them changes what the document is
  • Text inside images and complex formulas. Without a budget to redraw, preserve them whole and put the translation alongside
  • Legally operative wording. State in the file that the translation is for reference and keep the original next to it

Which repairs are possible when you try to edit a PDF directly, and which never were, is laid out separately in the piece on the boundaries of PDF editing. Much of the advice here assumes you have already accepted those boundaries.

9. Language-specific rules that bite

Line breaking is where general layout advice runs out and language rules take over. English readers meet this least often, because English has the most forgiving rules: spaces mark word boundaries and hyphenation offers extra break points. Almost every other script is stricter, looser or simply different.

9.1 Break opportunities are not universal

The Unicode line breaking algorithm UAX #14 assigns each character a class and decides break points from adjacent pairs. Breaks come in four kinds: mandatory, direct, indirect and prohibited. Ideographic characters can break between almost any two characters, while Latin letters cannot break between letters unless something else provides the opportunity. The report is explicit that the algorithm expects tailoring, noting that considerable variation is expected for regional and stylistic preference, and that scripts without marked word boundaries need morphological analysis.

The practical consequence for a document pipeline is blunt. The same sentence folds differently in different engines. A table cell that fit perfectly in the source can become two lines somewhere else, and the row height changes with it. If your validation compares rendered output, it must render with the same engine you ship with. Bidirectional scripts such as Arabic and Hebrew go a step further: mixed with Latin they change how a line is composed, not merely where it breaks.

9.2 Korean as a worked example

Korean is a useful case because its rules are written down. The W3C document Korean Layout Requirements states that Korean words are in principle separated by spaces, but that there is no space between a Latin word and the Korean particle that follows it. Two things follow from that single line.

  • Breaking gets harder. The Latin word and its particle form one glued chunk, so in a narrow cell the whole chunk drops to the next line
  • Placeholders collide with grammar. If a value is injected into the sentence, the particle cannot be fixed in advance, because its form depends on the preceding word. In document translation the safe move is not to solve this grammatically but to rewrite the sentence so no particle is needed

The same document adds that when Hangul falls at the end of a line, the break unit is a choice between the character and the word. In other words Korean line breaking is a setting, not a fixed rule, which is precisely the tailoring UAX #14 anticipates.

9.3 Other things that catch people out

  • Words with no break points. A German compound noun can be longer than the column it sits in with no space anywhere to save you, while Thai and Khmer mark no word boundaries at all, so break points require analysis rather than lookup
  • Han characters do not identify a language. Detecting stray untranslated text by character class runs into this immediately, which I unpack in the piece on detecting foreign script
  • Normalization. The same Hangul syllable can be stored precomposed or decomposed, and the difference breaks search and length checks. Boundary by boundary in the NFC and NFD piece
  • National document formats. In some markets public documents circulate in a locally dominant format, and that becomes a wall at the opening stage, before translation. Background in the piece on Korea's public document format

10. This article is its own example

This article is written in Korean and published in English, Japanese and Chinese alongside it. So it runs into everything above. The damage is milder, and the reason is exactly section 1.2: a web page has fluid layout and simply absorbs expansion.

Some things survive anyway.

  • Table headers and contents entries land at different lengths per language. Section titles and table labels are short strings, which puts them in the high-expansion band. Which language version wraps to two lines on a narrow screen differs by language
  • The examples have to be swapped. The Korean particle discussion in section 9 means nothing to a Chinese reader as written. Each language version has to cite the places that actually bite in that language. That is not translation, it is rewriting
  • Structural counts must match. If the number of sections, tables or list items differs between versions, that is not translation, it is omission. Lengths may differ; counts may not

That last point previews the conclusion. What you have to preserve is not the appearance, it is the structure. Lengths will change regardless. If the container holding the changed length is flexible the problem disappears, and if it is fixed the problem stays.

What I could not verify

The following were left out of the body as figures or firm claims because I could not pin down a source.

  • Layout preservation accuracy of specific tools or services. I did not measure how well any product preserves layout, and this article does not compare products
  • Expansion rates involving Korean. The figures quoted come from W3C-cited guidance for English into European languages. I could not verify measured rates for pairs involving Korean
  • Whether those rates apply to long prose. The cited guidance assumes interface strings. Whether the same ratios hold across multi-page prose is unverified
  • How common tagged PDFs actually are. I found no figure I trust for the share of PDFs that carry tags, so the body says only that they are a minority
  • Font-specific judgements. Embedding permissions vary per font, so I judged none, and merging extra glyphs into an existing subset depends on the implementation, so I did not generalize
  • A standard for layout preservation metrics. The list in section 7 is a proposal, not a standard. I found no widely agreed metric set, and I could not cover how well formula-preserving pipelines work either

Conclusion: drop the fixed layout and half the problem goes away

Document translation is hard not because translation is hard. It is hard because translation changes length while the document has length nailed down. Add a format like PDF, where the concept of a paragraph does not exist, and even deciding what to send to the engine becomes an inference performed anew every time.

The right order of decisions follows from that. Work from the source file if it exists. If it does not, split structure recovery from translation and put one human checkpoint between them. If you are on the producing side, add tags, keep text out of graphics, and stop crowding short labels into narrow cells. A large share of translation cost is decided before any translation happens.

One last thing. When you evaluate the result, measure translation quality and document integrity separately. Merge them and the causes blend; blend the causes and nobody can be assigned the fix.
