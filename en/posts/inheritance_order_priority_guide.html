The Complete Guide to Korea's Inheritance Order and Statutory Shares

Who Inherits and How Much - Order and Share Calculations Under the Korean Civil Code

Jul 26, 2026 Law

Legal information notice: This article provides general information based on Korea's Civil Code and is not legal advice on any specific matter. Inheritance outcomes vary substantially with family relationships, the makeup of the estate, and the moment the succession opens, and the rules can change through amendments and court rulings. For an actual case, consult a qualified lawyer or judicial scrivener.

Introduction: Inheritance Is a Question of Order and Share

When a close family member passes away, a practical question follows almost before the grief settles: who inherits, and how much? Inheritance can feel like a matter of emotion, but legally it is structured fairly clearly along two axes - who becomes an heir (the order) and how much each receives (the statutory share). Where there is no will, or a will does not dispose of the entire estate, what remains is divided according to the order and shares set by the Civil Code.

This article walks through Korea's inheritance order, the spouse's special position, statutory shares and the spouse's 50 percent add-on, worked share calculations, substitute inheritance and the simultaneous-death presumption, and finally the legal reserve that protects an heir's minimum portion. Keep in mind that what follows are general principles; in an individual case, variables such as contributory portions, special benefits, disqualification, or renunciation can change the result. This article is informational and does not substitute for legal advice on a specific matter.

1. The Inheritance Order - Who Becomes an Heir

The Civil Code sets a clear order for blood-relative heirs. The governing principle is that if even one higher-ranking heir exists, lower ranks do not inherit. The order is as follows.

  • First - lineal descendants: children, grandchildren, and further generations below. Where children exist, grandchildren generally do not inherit.
  • Second - lineal ascendants: parents, grandparents, and generations above. They inherit only when there are no lineal descendants at all.
  • Third - siblings: they inherit only when there are neither lineal descendants nor lineal ascendants.
  • Fourth - collateral relatives within the fourth degree: uncles, aunts, cousins, and the like. They inherit only when no higher-ranking heir and no spouse exist.

Where several people share the same rank, they become co-heirs. If there are three children, for instance, all three are first-rank heirs together. A nearer degree takes priority over a more distant one (children before grandchildren), and where the degree is equal, several people inherit jointly.

2. The Spouse's Special Position

The spouse is treated separately from the blood-relative ranking. The spouse becomes a co-heir with lineal descendants if any exist, or with lineal ascendants if there are no descendants. If there are neither descendants nor ascendants, the spouse inherits the entire estate alone. In effect, the spouse always ranks ahead of siblings and fourth-degree collateral relatives and is, practically speaking, always in the position of an heir.

Here, spouse means a legally married spouse. A de facto (common-law) relationship may be handled under other doctrines such as property division, but is generally understood not to be included among the automatic heirs for the statutory shares described here, so individual confirmation is needed.

3. Statutory Shares and the Spouse's 50 Percent Add-On

Shares among co-heirs are, in principle, equal. Where there are several children, each takes an equal portion. The spouse, however, has a special rule: when inheriting jointly with lineal descendants or ascendants, the spouse receives a 50 percent add-on over their share. If one child's portion is counted as 1, the spouse is counted as 1.5.

Co-heir compositionShare ratio
Spouse + 1 childSpouse 1.5 : child 1 (= 3/5 : 2/5)
Spouse + 2 childrenSpouse 1.5 : child 1 : child 1 (= 3/7 : 2/7 : 2/7)
Spouse + 3 childrenSpouse 1.5 : each child 1 (= 3/9 : 2/9 each)
Spouse + parents (no children)Spouse 1.5 : each parent 1
Spouse alone (no lineal relatives)Spouse 100 percent

As the table shows, when a spouse and two children inherit, the whole is divided into 7, with the spouse taking 3 and each child taking 2. The 50 percent add-on applies only to joint inheritance with lineal descendants or ascendants; when the spouse inherits alone, the add-on concept is not needed at all.

4. Worked Statutory-Share Calculations

Let us confirm this with concrete numbers. Suppose the estate is 700 million KRW and a spouse and two children inherit jointly.

HeirShareAmount (of 700 million KRW)
Spouse3/7about 300 million KRW
Child A2/7about 200 million KRW
Child B2/7about 200 million KRW

If there are no children and the heirs are the spouse and the deceased's parents, the ratio becomes spouse 1.5 : father 1 : mother 1, dividing the whole into 3.5. The spouse takes 1.5/3.5 (= 3/7) and each parent 1/3.5 (= 2/7). Because the denominator changes with the composition of heirs, the correct sequence is to first fix who the heirs are (the order) and only then calculate the shares.

Case-by-Case Calculations - Change the Heirs and the Denominator Changes

Let us compare how the same estate divides across five different sets of heirs. To keep the comparison clean, assume in every case that the estate is 700 million KRW and that there are no debts, no will, no contributory portions, and no special benefits.

Case ASpouse + 2 childrenSpouse 3/7, each child 2/7Spouse 300M, each child 200M KRW
Case BSpouse + parents (no children)Spouse 3/7, each parent 2/7Spouse 300M, each parent 200M KRW
Case C3 children (no spouse)1/3 eachabout 233.3M KRW each
Case DSpouse alone (no lineal relatives)1/1the full 700M KRW
Case E2 siblings (no spouse or lineal relatives)1/2 each350M KRW each
  • Case A (spouse + 2 children): the denominator is 1.5 + 1 + 1 = 3.5; multiply both sides by 2 to clear the decimal and you get 7. The spouse takes 3/7 = 300 million KRW and each child 2/7 = 200 million KRW.
  • Case B (spouse + parents): parents become second-rank heirs only if there is not a single child. The denominator, 1.5 + 1 + 1 = 3.5, is the same as in Case A, so the resulting ratio is likewise 3/7 : 2/7 : 2/7. If only one parent survives, the denominator becomes 1.5 + 1 = 2.5, giving the spouse 3/5 and the parent 2/5.
  • Case C (3 children, no spouse): the spouse has predeceased or the couple divorced. With no one eligible for the 50 percent add-on, the three children split the estate equally at 1/3 each. A child's gender, marital status, or whether they lived with the deceased does not affect the statutory share.
  • Case D (spouse alone): with neither lineal descendants nor lineal ascendants, the spouse inherits everything alone. Siblings rank below the spouse, so in this situation they receive nothing at all.
  • Case E (siblings only): third-rank siblings become heirs only when there is no spouse, no lineal descendant, and no lineal ascendant. Being of the same rank, they divide the estate equally by head count.

As these five cases show, the sequence is not to compute amounts first but to fix the list of heirs and then build the denominator. In a real case, however, estate debts and funeral costs, contributory portions and special benefits, and the terms of a will are layered on top, so the amount actually received can differ.

5. Substitute Inheritance - The Share of a Predeceased Heir

Substitute inheritance applies when an heir who would be a lineal descendant or a sibling has already died before the succession opens (the deceased's death) or has become disqualified. In their place, that person's lineal descendants or spouse become heirs. A common example: if the father passes away before the grandfather, the share the father would have taken passes to the grandchildren (and to the father's spouse, that is, the daughter-in-law).

In substitute inheritance, the total the substitutes receive is, in principle, the same as the share the deceased heir would have taken had they lived, and that share is then divided among the substitutes according to their own shares. If there are several grandchildren, they split the father's share equally; where the daughter-in-law is also present, it is divided under the 50 percent add-on principle.

6. The Simultaneous-Death Presumption and Substitute Inheritance

Where several people die together and it cannot be established who died first - in an accident, for instance - the Civil Code presumes they died simultaneously. The rationale is that treating deaths as simultaneous when their sequence cannot be proven is the fairest result. Among people presumed to have died at the same time, no inheritance passes between them as a rule.

There is, however, a point to watch in relation to substitute inheritance. Korean courts recognize substitute inheritance even where the deceased and the substituted person (for example, parent and child) are presumed to have died simultaneously. So if a grandfather and a father are presumed to have died together in the same accident, the grandchildren may inherit the grandfather's estate by substituting for the father. Such cases are frequently disputed, so obtaining a professional assessment is the safer course.

7. The Legal Reserve - An Heir's Minimum Portion

The deceased may freely dispose of assets by will or lifetime gift, but completely excluding a particular heir could threaten the remaining family's livelihood. To prevent this, the law recognizes a legal reserve - a minimum guaranteed portion - for a defined range of heirs. The commonly cited reserve ratios are as follows.

HeirLegal reserve ratio
Lineal descendants1/2 of the statutory share
Spouse1/2 of the statutory share
Lineal ascendants1/3 of the statutory share
SiblingsAbolished by the 2024 unconstitutionality ruling

The notable change concerns the siblings' legal reserve. In April 2024 Korea's Constitutional Court struck down the provision granting siblings a legal reserve, so for successions opening after that ruling, siblings can no longer claim one. The court also found it constitutionally deficient that there was no provision for forfeiting the reserve or for reflecting contributory portions, and related legislation has been under revision - so the legal reserve is an area of significant recent change. 8. The Inheritance Process - Acceptance, Renunciation, and Qualified Acceptance

Succession opens automatically the moment the deceased dies, with no separate procedure. The complication is that the estate may include debts (negative assets). Here the heir has three choices.

  • Simple acceptance: inherit both assets and debts as they are. This can be deemed to occur once the deadline passes without any filing.
  • Qualified acceptance: repay the deceased's debts only up to the value of the inherited assets. Used where debts may exceed assets.
  • Renunciation: be treated as never having been an heir, inheriting neither assets nor debts.

Qualified acceptance and renunciation must be filed with the family court within the set period after you learn that the succession has opened. Missing the deadline can result in simple acceptance, meaning debts pass to you in full, so managing the deadline is critical. The exact period, procedure, and documents vary by case, so confirm with the court, a judicial scrivener, or a lawyer. For the fuller picture, see our Renunciation and Qualified Acceptance Guide and Inheritance and Gift Tax Guide.

9. Deadlines That Start Running Once Succession Opens

The most dangerous thing in an inheritance is a deadline missed because nobody knew about it. From the moment succession opens (the death), several procedures start running at once, each with its own starting point and length - and missing the deadline for renunciation, qualified acceptance, or a tax filing is hard to undo. The main deadlines are summarized below.

ProcedureDeadlineCounted from
Death report (family registration)within 1 monththe day you learn of the death
Renunciation / qualified acceptance filingwithin 3 monthsthe day you learn succession has opened
Safe Inheritance one-stop service requestwithin 1 yearthe last day of the month of death
Inheritance tax return and paymentwithin 6 monthsthe last day of the month succession opens
Acquisition tax (real estate, vehicles, etc.)within 6 monthsthe date succession opens
  • Death report, 1 month: the Act on Registration of Family Relations requires a report within one month of learning of the death, with a medical certificate or post-mortem examination report attached; a late report without good cause can draw an administrative fine.
  • Renunciation and qualified acceptance, 3 months: the filing must reach the family court within three months of the day you learn succession has opened. This window is called the deliberation period, and where circumstances warrant, an extension can reportedly be requested from the court before it expires.
  • Special qualified acceptance: if, without gross negligence, you did not learn within the three months that the debts exceed the assets, an exception allows a qualified acceptance within a set period after you do learn of it. The requirements are strict, so confirm with a professional.
  • Inheritance tax, 6 months: the return and payment go to the competent tax office within six months of the last day of the month in which succession opens. Longer periods are set where the deceased or an heir was domiciled abroad.
  • Acquisition tax, 6 months: where you inherit an asset subject to acquisition tax, such as real estate or a car, the return and payment are due within six months of the date succession opens (nine months where an heir is domiciled abroad).
  • Registration of inherited property: no fixed statutory deadline is specified, but delaying registration blocks disposal and the creation of security interests, and if an heir dies in the meantime the ownership picture grows tangled - so handling it alongside the tax filings is the safer course.

Deadlines and starting points can change with amendments, and exactly when you are treated as having learned of something is itself often disputed. If the date arithmetic is ambiguous, do not decide alone - confirm with the family court, the tax office, or a professional.

10. How to Identify the Heirs and Trace the Estate

Knowing the order and the shares gets you nowhere if you do not know what you are actually inheriting, because you cannot decide whether to accept or renounce. For this, the Korean government runs the Safe Inheritance one-stop service (integrated property search for the deceased and wards), which searches financial transactions, land, vehicles, taxes, and pension enrollment in a single request. You can apply in person at a city or district office or a community service center, or online at Gov.kr after simple or certificate-based authentication; filing it together with the death report makes the process considerably simpler.

Below is a checklist to work through in order once succession opens. The items that matter vary by case, so use it to spot what you may have overlooked rather than as a script to follow literally.

  • Obtain plenty of copies of the death certificate or post-mortem examination report - many institutions require originals.
  • File the death report with the competent local office within one month of learning of the death.
  • Submit the Safe Inheritance one-stop service request at the same time to start the property search.
  • Obtain the deceased's basic certificate, family relation certificate, marriage relation certificate, and removed family register to fix the range of heirs.
  • Check for overlooked heirs such as children born outside marriage, acknowledged children, adopted children, or children from a previous marriage.
  • Check whether any child or sibling predeceased the deceased and, if so, identify who qualifies for substitute inheritance.
  • Take the financial search results and sort deposits, insurance, and securities against loans and card debt into positive and negative assets.
  • For real estate, obtain the certified copy of the register and check encumbrances such as mortgages and provisional attachments.
  • Check for unpaid national and local taxes and for any suretyship obligations of the deceased - guarantees rarely surface in a standard search and are the classic blind spot.
  • Look for easily forgotten titled assets such as vehicles, construction machinery, memberships, and intellectual property rights.
  • Check whether money is still owed to the estate, such as unclaimed insurance proceeds, severance pay, or survivor pension benefits.
  • Confirm whether a will exists. Depending on the form - holograph, notarial deed, and so on - a court probate step may be required.
  • If the debts appear to exceed the assets, weigh renunciation or qualified acceptance within the three-month window.
  • Remember that if all higher-ranking heirs renounce, the debts can pass down to the next rank, so decide together with the lower-ranking heirs in view.
  • Consider whether there is room to argue special benefits where an heir received a lifetime gift, or a contributory portion where an heir gave exceptional care, support, or help in building the estate.
  • Once agreement is reached, draw up an estate division agreement and have every heir seal it - unanimity is the rule.
  • File the inheritance tax return within six months of the last day of the month succession opens, and the acquisition tax return for real estate and vehicles within six months of the date succession opens.
  • Complete the registration and title transfer alongside the tax work, and if agreement breaks down, consider a family court adjudication on estate division.

11. Contributory Portions and Special Benefits - The Variables That Adjust Statutory Shares

The statutory shares discussed so far are a starting point, not the final answer. To keep things equitable among co-heirs, the Civil Code provides two adjustment mechanisms - and a large share of real-world inheritance disputes is fought over precisely these two.

  • Special benefits: where a co-heir received a lifetime gift or a bequest from the deceased, that value is treated as a share received in advance. The calculation is described as (value of the estate + each heir's special benefits) x that heir's share ratio - the value of the special benefit the heir received.
  • Contributory portion: recognized for an heir who, over a substantial period, gave exceptional support through cohabitation or nursing care, or contributed exceptionally to maintaining or increasing the estate. The calculation is described as (value of the estate - the contributory portion) x each heir's share ratio + the contributory portion for the contributor - that is, the portion is carved out first and the remainder divided.
  • Valuation date: in computing the specific share, the estate and the specially benefited property are, as a rule, valued as of the time succession opens. When property gifted long ago should be valued is a frequent point of contention.
  • Agreement and adjudication: the contributory portion is set by agreement among the co-heirs, and failing agreement the family court fixes it, weighing the timing, method, and degree of the contribution and the state of the estate. Whether the contribution was exceptional is the crux, so objective records - care logs, transfer histories, proof of involvement in a business - matter a great deal.

In short, the number produced by the statutory shares is not fixed in stone: the specific share, reflecting special benefits and contributory portions, is the actual basis for distribution. This area turns heavily on disputed facts and varies case by case, so where the sums at stake are large, getting professional help early is the better course.

12. Conclusion - Fix the Order First

The first task in any inheritance is to fix who the heirs are - the order. Only once the order is set is the denominator determined, and only then can you apply the spouse's 50 percent add-on to calculate each person's statutory share. In short: first confirm the order from first-rank lineal descendants to fourth-rank collateral relatives; second, identify whom the spouse inherits with and apply the add-on; third, consider substitute inheritance if an heir predeceased; and fourth, weigh the legal reserve if an heir was excluded, or qualified acceptance and renunciation if debts are heavy.

In an actual case, however, variables such as contributory portions, special benefits, disqualification, the validity of a will, and unsettled legislation often intertwine, making it hard to reach a conclusion from these general principles alone. If you face a significant decision about property, use this article only to sketch the big picture, and always consult a lawyer or judicial scrivener for the specific judgment.
