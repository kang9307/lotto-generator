Being sick alone is really rough

The first time I had a high fever while living alone was brutal. I wanted to go to the doctor but couldn't move, had no medicine, nothing to eat... That's when I realized I need to be prepared.

This is not just my story. Single-person households keep growing, which means more and more people are experiencing "being ill with nobody around" for the first time in their lives. The problem is that it does not stop at feeling sorry for yourself. When you live with someone, they catch the signals for you: "your face looks off", "your speech is slurred". Alone, there is no observer. Your temperature can climb to 40°C (104°F), dehydration can blur your thinking, and the only person left to notice and decide is the version of you whose judgment is already slipping.

So for someone living alone, preparation is less about "how well stocked is my medicine cabinet" and more about "what did I decide in advance, before my judgment got worse". Searching after you are already sick is too late. If you have ever tried to look up "clinics open on Sunday near me" while running a 39°C fever, you know exactly what this means. That is why this guide is ordered the way it is: first the signs that mean call an ambulance right now, then what to prepare, how to decide whether to go in, how to manage food and fluids during recovery, and finally how to ask for help.

The most dangerous moments when you're sick alone

The real danger of being ill alone is not "being in a lot of pain". It is losing the ability to call for help. If your consciousness clouds, you cannot dial. If your breathing gets short, you cannot finish a sentence. If you are badly dehydrated, your judgment collapses. So the line that matters most for someone living alone is not "how bad does it hurt" but "am I still able to help myself". If you are about to cross that line, call before you cross it.

Sepsis, where an infection spreads through the body, is a good example: it can show up as fever or chills along with an abnormally fast pulse, shortness of breath, clouded consciousness, and a noticeable drop in how much you urinate, and it moves fast (Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency, National Health Information Portal). Do not dismiss the feeling of "this is strange for a cold". The table below splits common symptoms into three tiers, and the rule is simple: if anything in the right-hand column applies, ignore everything to the left and call emergency services now.

Three thoughts that stop people from calling

  • "Isn't it a waste of their time to call for this?" — Emergency lines are not only for dispatching ambulances. In Korea, since 1339 was folded into 119, the same number provides 24-hour medical advice and directions to clinics and pharmacies, so "I don't know whether this counts as an emergency" is itself a valid reason to call. Most countries have a non-emergency medical advice line too; find yours in advance.
  • "I'll sleep on it and decide in the morning." — Problems involving consciousness and breathing get worse overnight. If your judgment was already fading when you went to bed, there is no guarantee you will be able to make a call when you wake up.
  • "I'll just take a taxi." — Chest pain, fainting, clouded consciousness and severe breathing difficulty are the four situations where getting worse in transit means nobody is there to act. For those, call an ambulance instead of travelling yourself.
  • "The fever broke, so I'm fine." — A fever coming down with medication is not the same as the cause going away. If the fever climbs back to the same height every time the medication wears off, that pattern alone is a reason to be seen.

1. Prepare in Advance

The goal of preparing is not to build a perfect first-aid kit so you never have to see a doctor. It is to reach a state where, even with your judgment dulled by illness, you only have to reach out your hand rather than think. That makes where you keep things and when you check expiry dates more important than what you buy. Put your medicines in one drawer you can reach from bed, keep the thermometer and water next to it, and arrange everything around the path you actually take when you're ill.

  • Cold medicine: All-in-one cold medicine

When every pharmacy is closed: overnight options

Your fever starting to climb at 11pm with every nearby pharmacy shut is a more common scenario than people expect. In Korea, the Ministry of Health and Welfare designates 13 "safe household medicines" that 24-hour convenience stores may sell: 5 fever/pain reducers, 2 cold remedies, 4 digestive aids and 2 medicated patches. As of 2026 there is ongoing discussion about expanding that list. Other countries handle this with 24-hour pharmacies or supermarket general-sale medicines — the useful move is knowing yours before midnight.

  • Convenience-store medicine is not a replacement for a pharmacy — it is a stopgap for the hours a pharmacy is closed. Follow up properly the next morning.
  • There are limits on how much can be sold at once, so you cannot stock up there.
  • Only registered stores carry it, not every branch. Find out which shop near you qualifies while you are healthy.
  • Buying a cold remedy and a fever reducer together can mean doubling up on the same ingredient. Always read the ingredient panel.
  • If over-the-counter options are not touching the pain or fever, that in itself is a signal you need to be seen.

Emergency Food

  • Instant porridge or soup pouches
  • Electrolyte drinks
  • Bottled water
  • Ready meals (microwaveable)

When you're sick, even cooking is impossible. Stock up on things you just need to heat up.

The criterion for emergency food is not taste — it is how many steps stand between the package and your mouth. The moment you have to pull out a pot and boil water, you have already lost. Aim for two minutes in the microwave, or lift-the-lid-and-eat. And because this is food for when you are ill, greasy, heavily spiced ready meals are better left out. Plain rice porridge, thin gruel, clear broth, oral rehydration salts and bottled water are what actually earn their shelf space.

Storage location matters just as much. Not the back of the freezer — build a dedicated "sick box" at eye level in a cupboard, or in whichever storage is closest to your bed. Checking expiry dates once per season (four times a year) is enough. If you already keep your fridge organised, this gets much easier; the fridge management part of this series pairs well with it.

2. Cold/Flu

Colds and flu mostly resolve with time, but for someone living alone the problem is who is watching your condition while that time passes. So add exactly one habit to the basics below: take your temperature twice a day at the same times and write it down. Two numbers a day let you judge whether you are improving or deteriorating from evidence rather than feel. It also helps enormously at the clinic, because you can say "it was this high starting on this day" instead of "a few days, pretty hot".

Basic Care

  • Rest well (seriously, take a day off work)
  • Drink lots of fluids
  • Stay warm
  • Take cold medicine

Each of those four has a reason behind it. Rest gives your immune response room to work. Fluids replace what you lose far faster than usual through breathing and sweating while febrile. Warmth saves the energy your body would otherwise burn on temperature regulation during chills. Medication suppresses symptoms enough to let you sleep. Just remember that cold medicine treats symptoms rather than the cause, and that formulations differ between products and can overlap with other medicines you take. A quick word with a pharmacist before taking anything is the safest route.

When You Have Fever

  • Check with thermometer (take fever reducer if above 38°C/100°F)
  • Cool down with lukewarm cloth
  • See a doctor if 39°C/102°F persists

Avoid cold water and alcohol rubs. A cold shock can make you shiver, which pushes your temperature up rather than down. A lukewarm cloth over the neck, armpits and groin — where larger blood vessels run — is about right. And when you take a fever reducer, log the time along with the temperature. Alone, "did I already take one?" gets fuzzy fast, and double-dosing is genuinely dangerous.

What matters more than the number is what comes with it. At 38.5°C, if you can hold a conversation and drink water, watching is reasonable. At 37.8°C, if you are short of breath or foggy, that is a different problem entirely. In hot weather, remember that a fever might be heat illness rather than infection, so factor in whether you were out in the heat.

What to Eat

  • Porridge, soup
  • Warm water, honey water
  • Electrolyte drinks
  • Fruit (for vitamins)

Losing your appetite during a fever is normal. Sipping small amounts often beats forcing down a full meal. When nothing goes down, "food you can drink" — warm broth, thin gruel — is the realistic format. But if you cannot even keep water down for a sustained stretch, that is no longer a watch-at-home situation.

3. Stomach Bug

Basic Care

  • Preventing dehydration is most important
  • Sip electrolyte drinks frequently
  • Take anti-diarrhea if severe
  • Avoid spicy/greasy food

In acute gastroenteritis, what actually endangers people is not the diarrhea itself but dehydration. Korea's National Health Information Portal (KDCA) explains that for acute bacterial gastroenteritis, patients who can drink are rehydrated with standard oral rehydration therapy, while those with severe dehydration or frequent vomiting that prevents oral intake may need intravenous fluids. In other words, "cannot keep fluids down" is a hospital situation, not a home one.

One note on anti-diarrheals: using them on your own when diarrhea comes with fever or blood is not advisable. Depending on the cause it can work against you, so in that situation get seen before reaching for medication. If spoiled food is a plausible cause, read alongside food poisoning: what to do right now, and note down what you ate and when — a timeline helps enormously at the clinic.

What to Eat

  • Plain rice porridge
  • Bananas
  • Plain toast (no butter)
  • Water, electrolyte drinks

What these share is being low in fat, lactose, fibre and irritants. While your gut is inflamed, food that takes effort to digest drags symptoms out. On the other side, drinks with very high sugar content can worsen diarrhea, so if you use a sports drink, dilute it with water — or use pharmacy oral rehydration salts instead.

Food and fluids during recovery — what and how much

Managing the recovery phase matters as much as managing the illness. People living alone tend to make one of two mistakes: going straight back to normal meals the moment the fever breaks and relapsing, or living on plain porridge for days until the lack of energy stretches recovery out. A simple framework avoids both.

Fluids: how much, and how

  • Small amounts often, not a lot at once. After vomiting, a few sips every 15–20 minutes stays down far better than draining a glass.
  • Plain water alone may not be enough. Diarrhea and vomiting cost you electrolytes such as sodium and potassium as well as water. Oral rehydration salts are formulated to replace that balance.
  • Check your urine. Pale yellow and about as frequent as usual is generally reassuring; dark and infrequent means you are behind.
  • Park coffee, energy drinks and alcohol until you are recovered. Diuretic effects and stomach irritation do not help.
  • If you have barely managed any fluid for 8+ hours, this is no longer self-care territory. Get seen.

Getting back to normal food in three stages

  • Stage 1 (symptoms at their peak) — fluids and electrolytes only: thin gruel, clear broth, oral rehydration salts. Do not force solid food.
  • Stage 2 (symptoms roughly halved) — small portions of soft, bland carbohydrates: plain rice porridge, banana, boiled potato, dry toast.
  • Stage 3 (appetite returning) — start adding protein: tofu, white fish, steamed egg, anything cooked with little fat.
  • Bring back last: fried food, spicy food, dairy and alcohol. After gastroenteritis in particular, temporary trouble digesting lactose is common, so rushing back to milk can restart the diarrhea.
  • Do not skip stages. Leaving roughly a day between each one measurably cuts the chance of relapse.

Sleep matters as much as food during recovery. Fatigue lingering for one to two weeks after the fever breaks is not unusual. But if that fatigue is getting worse rather than better, or arrives with breathlessness, weight loss or night sweats, that is not ordinary slow recovery and is worth having checked.

4. When to See a Doctor

Go Immediately If

  • High fever (39°C+/102°F+) for more than a day
  • Blood in vomit or stool
  • Severe persistent stomach pain
  • Confusion or disorientation
  • Difficulty breathing
🚑 These five are not "keep an eye on it" items
Of the list above, clouded consciousness and difficulty breathing mean you should not attempt to travel yourself — call emergency services immediately. Treat crushing chest pain lasting more than 5 minutes, slurred speech or weakness down one side, and dehydration severe enough that you have produced almost no urine for 8+ hours at the same level. If you live alone, the key is making the call while you still can. When you cannot decide, do not sit on the decision — ring the medical advice line and ask.

When You Can't Get There Alone

  • Call emergency services
  • Contact someone nearby
  • Use telehealth for minor symptoms

The option people most often forget is the advice call itself. In Korea, since the emergency medical information line 1339 was merged into 119 in June 2012, a single number handles 24-hour medical advice, directions to clinics and pharmacies, and ambulance dispatch (Ministry of Health and Welfare). Many countries run an equivalent non-emergency nurse or medical advice line. Questions like "this probably isn't ambulance-level but I don't know what to do" are exactly what those lines exist for.

Deciding whether to go in or ride it out

The hardest cases are the ones that are neither "call an ambulance" nor "just a cold" — the middle ground. Rather than trying to name the illness, split the question along three axes and it gets much cleaner.

The three axes

  • ① Time — how long has this been going? Most acute symptoms show their direction within two to three days. If three days pass with no sign of improvement, or you improved and then slid back, that leans toward getting seen.
  • ② Slope — improving or deteriorating? Direction beats absolute severity. If today is worse than yesterday, and this evening is worse than this morning, that pattern is the signal.
  • ③ Function — can you still do the basics? Can you drink water, get to the bathroom unaided, hold a phone call? If any one of those fails, you are past the ride-it-out zone.
  • Supporting axis — is this a kind of symptom you recognise? "I've had plenty of colds and this doesn't feel like one" is a surprisingly reliable instinct. Unfamiliar patterns deserve a lower threshold for care.

Living alone means setting the bar one notch lower

When someone who lives with family says "let's give it one more day", a person living alone should not copy that decision, because nobody is watching. If any of the following apply, get seen earlier than you otherwise would.

  • You have a chronic condition or take medication that suppresses immunity
  • You had recent surgery or a procedure, or a wound that has not healed
  • You are pregnant or might be
  • You spend long stretches alone and can go more than a day without contacting anyone
  • A weekend or long holiday starts tomorrow — when it is borderline the day before a closure, going in is closer to the right answer

5. Know Your Local Medical Services

Research in Advance

  • Nearest clinic/doctor
  • Hospital with ER
  • Late-night/weekend pharmacy
  • 24-hour convenience store (emergency supplies)

These four are only useful if you write down the address and phone number too. "Somewhere around there" in your head is worthless when you are actually ill. Make one note in your phone called "When sick" and record the name, address, phone number and walking time for the nearest clinic, the nearest hospital with an ER, a late-night pharmacy, and a store that sells basic medicines. Doing it once right after you move covers you for years.

6. Tips When Sick

Work/School

  • Don't push through - resting one day helps recovery
  • Don't feel bad about calling in sick
  • Telehealth can provide sick notes too

Begrudging a single day off and then being ill for three is extremely common. If what you have is contagious, showing up is also an act of passing it to colleagues, so resting is the better outcome for the team too. When you call in, keep it factual and short — "I have a fever, so I'll work from home / take today off" beats a detailed account of your suffering. If you need paperwork, check which document your employer actually wants before the appointment; a medical certificate and a simple proof-of-visit differ in purpose and cost.

Mental Health

  • Easy to feel down when sick alone
  • At least call family/friends
  • If it's serious, don't hesitate to ask for help

Feeling low while ill is closer to a normal side effect of your body diverting resources into recovery than a character flaw. Alone, though, that mood slides easily toward "nobody is here for me". A five-minute call or a one-line message usually breaks the loop. If low mood persists for more than two weeks after you have physically recovered, that is a separate thing worth attention — the mental health part of this series covers it.

How to ask for help — alone without being on your own

The most valuable thing someone living alone can stock is not medicine but a structure where somebody notices if you go quiet. Thirty minutes of setup while healthy, and it works later without you doing anything.

The safety net to build in advance

  • Name two emergency contacts. Ideally one family member and one person who lives nearby. Say out loud: "if you don't hear from me for a couple of days, please check on me."
  • Set up the medical ID on your phone's lock screen. Most phones can show blood type, allergies, medications and an emergency contact without unlocking.
  • Share where the spare key is with exactly one trusted person. It removes the delay of nobody being able to open the door.
  • One sheet of paper on the fridge with conditions, medications, allergies and a contact number. If you are unconscious, that paper speaks for you.
  • Have a regular clinic. Somewhere that already holds your records makes telemedicine and follow-up visits far smoother.
  • Check what your local authority offers single-person households. Depending on the area there may be wellbeing check-ins, hospital escort or temporary care support.

What to say when you call for an ambulance

When you are ill you ramble and lose the thread. Memorising the order halves the call and gets responders to you faster. Location comes first, above everything: even if you fade mid-sentence, help can still arrive if the address got through.

Frequently asked questions

What over-the-counter medicine should I stock up on before I actually get sick living alone?

It's worth keeping a basic kit on hand: fever/pain reducers, an all-in-one cold medicine, digestive aids, anti-diarrheal medicine, bandages with antiseptic, and a thermometer to check your temperature. On top of that, stocking instant porridge pouches, sports drinks, bottled water and ready-to-eat meals as emergency food means you only need to heat something up when you are too sick to move. Which specific products to pick, though, should be decided after telling a pharmacist about your conditions, current medications and allergies.

Is there a clear line for when a fever means I should go to the hospital?

Check your temperature with a thermometer. If it is 38°C (100.4°F) or higher, taking a fever reducer and wiping down with lukewarm water is the usual first response. If a fever of 39°C (102.2°F) or higher lasts more than a day, you should be seen. Blood in vomit or stool, ongoing severe abdominal pain, confusion or difficulty breathing all mean going in right away. The accompanying symptoms and the direction of travel matter more than the number itself.

What should I eat if I have an upset stomach or gastroenteritis?

Preventing dehydration matters most, so sip fluids a little at a time and avoid anything spicy or irritating. Plain rice porridge, bananas and dry toast are easy-to-digest choices, and anti-diarrheal medicine is one option if diarrhea is severe. However, if there is fever or blood in the stool, do not self-medicate — get care, and if you cannot keep even water down, go in immediately.

What should I do if I'm too sick to get to the hospital on my own?

If you are too weak to move, call emergency services or reach out to someone nearby first. For milder symptoms, telemedicine can even produce a medical certificate, so rather than pushing through and going to work, it is better not to feel guilty about a day off — it is the faster route to recovery. In Korea, first-time telemedicine consultations are available at night (after 6pm on weekdays) and on public holidays.

What hospital and pharmacy information is worth knowing ahead of time?

Know which clinic is closest, which hospital has an emergency room, and which pharmacies open at night or on weekends. Emergency medical information apps find the nearest ER, pharmacy locators show what is open, and a 24-hour convenience store is a backup for basic supplies. In Korea, the E-Gen portal run by the National Medical Center lets you search open clinics and pharmacies by district, department and date without signing up.

Wrap-up

Being sick is the hardest part of living alone. Being prepared makes a huge difference. And if it gets really bad, don't hesitate to ask for help.

If there are three sentences worth keeping from this article, they are these. First, consciousness, breathing, chest pain and severe dehydration are not things to watch — they are call-now situations. Second, the core of preparation is not objects but the thresholds and contacts you fixed before your judgment got worse. Third, when it is ambiguous, round one column to the right: living alone means setting the bar a notch lower than everyone else.
