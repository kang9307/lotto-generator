A Model Cannot Go Beyond What It Read

What the grade-five corpus experiment exposed — composition, not volume, draws the ceiling on capability

Aug 17, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: the same model, collapsing on only certain questions

Two people use the same model. One calls it remarkable, the other calls it useless. Neither is lying. The difference is that one asked questions landing in a region the model read thickly and the other asked questions landing in a region it read thinly.

Parameter count does not explain this. Two models of identical size, fed different material, end up strong and weak in entirely different places. Yet when people compare models, they compare parameters, context length and benchmark scores. The axis that moves outcomes most, the composition of the training data, is usually undisclosed and therefore drops out of the conversation.

An experiment published in August 2026 attacked that axis head on. A corpus of 88 billion tokens was assembled to match U.S. elementary school material, explicitly excluding concepts, facts and vocabulary taught above Grade 5, and a model was trained on it from scratch. The point was not to build a small model but to watch, under controlled conditions, how a line drawn in the data transfers to a line drawn in capability.

Five things to take away.

  • What exactly disappears when hard text is absent — four things go at once, not one
  • Volume and distribution are separate axes — that the same token count yields different results is already measured
  • What web corpora are actually made of — what is overrepresented, what is scarce, and why specialist domains thin out
  • Which cells synthetic data fills and which it does not — the model collapse debate is unreadable without its conditions
  • How to predict where answers will be weak — the part practitioners actually use

Why a model declines to answer is a different question. That arises above the data layer, in knowledge cutoffs, refusal scope and abstention training, and it is separated out in Models started saying they do not know. This piece stays upstream of that: what the model was fed.

1. What the thought experiment actually asked

The design is simple. Filter web text down to what fits an elementary curriculum, strip out everything above that line, train a model from scratch on the result, and then find out whether anything done afterwards can cross the line.

The important part is not the filtering. It is that three post-hoc levers were tried in sequence: making the model bigger, applying reinforcement-style additional training, and putting examples in the prompt so the model learns in context. Those are the three levers the industry actually reaches for when it wants more capability.

The published results point the same direction for all three. In-scope ability improved; out-of-scope ability did not improve meaningfully. Scaling raised performance inside the elementary range without crossing out of it, fine-tuning refined what was already there, and in-context learning failed to unlock what was absent. The framing the authors used is that the pretraining filter sets the effective capability ceiling.

How to read that result splits opinion. It was called a restatement of the stochastic parrot thesis, and that criticism has a point: the conclusion that a model cannot do what was never in training is not new. What is new is not the conclusion but the way the boundary was drawn. Because the limits are mapped onto human-readable curriculum items rather than a single aggregate benchmark number, you can point at which item is empty and why.

Where generalisation stops. What this produces is evidence about what happens under a hard filter, not evidence about the mixtures commercial models were actually trained on. Those mixtures are largely undisclosed and cannot be inferred from this. The corpus was also not yet released at publication, so third-party reproduction is still outstanding.

2. Four things that vanish along with difficult text

Reading this as no hard reading means no hard words captures less than half of it. Removing high-difficulty text takes at least four things with it.

  • Technical vocabulary and the concepts it bundles. The model does not merely lack a word; the conceptual package that word carries is absent. Being unable to use the term jurisdiction and having no concept of jurisdiction are different deficits
  • Sentence structure itself. Sentences with nested subordinate clauses and stacked conditions are nearly absent from easy prose. The ability to read a regulation with layered conditions and follow the exception to the exception comes from having seen many such sentences
  • Multi-step reasoning chains. This one matters most. The form of moving several steps from premise to conclusion is only present in quantity in hard writing: papers, judgments, technical documents. Easy prose states conclusions without unfolding the path
  • Domain conventions of form. The limitations paragraph of a paper, the contraindication notation of a clinical guideline, the proviso clause of a contract, the timestamp convention of an incident report. These are not knowledge but format, and without them correct content still comes out in a shape the field does not use

The first two are conspicuous when missing. The trouble is the last two. Surface fluency is largely secured by easy text alone. So you get answers that read smoothly while the conclusion does not follow from the grounds, and answers whose form looks plausible until someone in the field reads it. Because the exterior is intact, verification gets skipped. That makes these the more expensive gaps.

3. Volume and distribution are different problems

Token count is a single number, which makes it easy to compare. That is why data discussions keep collapsing into volume discussions. But that the same token budget yields different outcomes is measured, not speculated.

DoReMi, from Xie and colleagues in 2023 (arXiv:2305.10429), shows this cleanly. A 280M proxy model optimised domain weights first, and the corpus was resampled at those weights to train an 8B model, thirty times larger. Against a baseline using The Pile default weights, the result was a 6.5 percentage point gain in average few-shot downstream accuracy and 2.6 times fewer training steps to reach baseline accuracy. Nothing was added. Only how much came from where changed.

One counterintuitive detail sits inside that paper. It reports cases where a domain was downweighted and its perplexity improved anyway. Domains are not independent buckets; structure learned in one carries into another. So the simple mapping of to raise this ability, add more of that domain does not hold.

For a practitioner the conclusion of this section is one line. An announcement that training data grew tells you almost nothing about performance by itself. Without knowing what grew and what shrank in relative terms, there is nothing to judge. And that information is usually not published.

4. What the textbook-quality result proved, and what it did not

The most dramatic push of the claim that composition can beat volume was phi-1 in 2023. Gunasekar and colleagues (arXiv:2306.11644) trained a 1.3 billion parameter model on 6 billion tokens of textbook-quality text selected from the web plus 1 billion tokens of textbooks and exercises generated with GPT-3.5, reaching 50.6% pass@1 on HumanEval and 55.5% on MBPP. For the time, that was code performance comparable to far larger models.

What the result genuinely demonstrates is clear. When the target domain is narrow and you can manufacture high-quality data for it, you can cut scale enormously and still be strong inside that domain. There is a reason the small-model line of work started being taken seriously after this paper.

Extending it into quality is all you need, size is irrelevant makes a claim the paper does not. Three things need saying.

  • The domain is narrow. phi-1 specialises in Python code generation, and lacking the broad domain knowledge of larger models was noted alongside the result as a limitation. Nothing in the paper licenses transferring a narrow win into general capability
  • Evaluation contamination follows the method around. The authors anticipated the objection, pruned training files similar to HumanEval in a strong form of decontamination, and built fresh problems outside the training distribution for additional evaluation. That a response existed is not the same as the concern being eliminated
  • Code is a special domain. Correctness is machine-checkable and synthetic problems can be mass-produced. There is no guarantee the same strategy transfers to law, medicine or history, where the answer is out in the world and cannot be generated

So the textbook line is evidence that composition is a powerful lever, not evidence that scale is meaningless. Blurring those two is where the small model beat the big model style of overstatement comes from.

5. What web corpora are actually made of

So what fills a real web corpus? Part of this is answerable from public material. FineWeb, released by a Hugging Face team in 2024 (arXiv:2406.17557), published the full construction process for a Common Crawl-derived corpus along with the reasoning behind each design choice.

One number in it says a great deal. To build FineWeb-Edu, documents were scored 0 to 5 for educational value using Llama-3-70B, and discarding everything below 3 removed 92% of the dataset, leaving 1.3 trillion tokens. Inverted: most web text is filtered out on a knowledge-density criterion. Models trained on the surviving 8% under a matched 350B token budget went from 33% to 37% on MMLU and from 46% to 57% on ARC.

Read those figures with one caveat. They are controlled ablation comparisons, not absolute performance. Same token budget, filter swapped. It does not mean a 37% model exists. The meaning is that changing composition alone moves the needle by that much.

DomainThickness on the public webWhy it lands there
Casual conversation, social, forumsVery thickCheap to produce and public by default

Standards and specificationsThinOften sold rather than posted, and revisions do not persist on the open web

Why specialist domains thin out reduces to one line. Knowledge density and probability of publication run in opposite directions. The denser the text, the more likely it is paid, internal or on paper. What a crawler can take is the other side. So the more you grow data by scraping the web, the more the total rises while the relative share of specialist material falls.

What can be taken is also a rights question, not only a technical one. That axis is handled separately in the legal status of AI training data, and this piece goes no further than noting that it is being litigated.

6. What synthetic data fills and what it does not

As the open web yields less, synthetic data becomes the next candidate. Discussion tangles here because synthetic data is not one thing. The cells that fill and the cells that do not are clearly separable.

  • Fills well — form and variation. Problem types, register, instruction-response pairs, restatements of the same content at different difficulty. This rearranges what the model already holds, and it works
  • Fills well — verifiable domains. Where correctness is machine-checkable, as in code or mathematics, you can attach a loop that discards wrong generations. That is why the phi-1 line worked in code
  • Does not fill — new facts about the world. A model generating a fact it does not know produces a sentence, not a fact. Which regulation changed when, which body announced what, cannot be manufactured
  • Does not fill — the tail of the distribution. Generation pulls toward the probable. Rare phrasings, minority dialects and unusual cases are the first to disappear from generated text

That last item is where the model collapse debate starts. Shumailov and colleagues showed in Nature in 2024 that repeatedly training on generated output and generating again erodes the distribution from the tail inward until the model degrades. The paper was widely cited, and the objection that its conditions are narrow arrived quickly.

The dividing line is precise: replacing the original with generated data, versus accumulating generated data on top of it. Gerstgrasser and colleagues (arXiv:2404.01413) showed that when real data is retained and synthetic data accumulates, the same collapse does not appear, and later work backed that mathematically. Replace and the risk diverges; accumulate and it does not. There is even a position paper (arXiv:2503.03150) arguing that the term as commonly used departs from what the original paper claimed.

So translating this debate into synthetic data is dangerous or it turned out to be fine gets it wrong both ways. The accurate statement is that the conditions of repetition determine the outcome, and which conditions large commercial training runs actually satisfy cannot be determined from public information.

7. How to read the running-out-of-data forecast

Behind the synthetic data conversation sits a projection about the total stock of public human text. The estimate from Villalobos and colleagues (arXiv:2211.04325) puts the effective stock of public human-generated text at roughly 300 trillion tokens, with a 90% interval from 100 trillion to 1,000 trillion, and projects that stock being fully used somewhere between 2026 and 2032 if current trends hold.

Two misreadings recur.

  • Treating the date as fixed. It is a conditional forecast. An earlier estimate placed exhaustion of high-quality text at 2024 and was subsequently revised later. The width of the interval is itself a statement about the nature of the estimate
  • Reading it as data disappears. Nothing disappears. The growth rate of new text fails to keep pace with the growth rate of training runs. Depending on how much overtraining is assumed, the exhaustion point moves earlier

Here it meets this article's axis. The more total volume becomes the binding constraint, the larger the relative weight of the composition question: which cell do you fill? The remaining headroom shifts from expanding the total to optimising the mixture. That is part of why filtering research in the FineWeb vein and data mixture optimisation have grown so visibly in recent years.

8. The situation for non-English data

Mapping all of this onto a smaller language adds a layer. Absolute volume and internal distribution are unfavourable simultaneously.

Volume has numbers. In the Common Crawl language statistics for the July 2026 snapshot (CC-MAIN-2026-30), document share by detected primary language runs as follows.

  • English 40.58% — two fifths of everything, in one language
  • Japanese 5.32%
  • Chinese 4.43%
  • Korean 0.84%

Even within East Asia, Korean sits at roughly a sixth of Japanese. A measurement caveat attaches: these are CLD2 language-detector estimates of a document's primary language, and detectors wobble on short or multilingual documents. Read the figures for order of magnitude rather than precision. The order of magnitude gap is nonetheless real.

The second layer matters more: where that remaining 0.84% clusters. Text produced actively in Korean on the web is thick in news, community posts, blogs and commercial content. Academic originals are frequently written in English even by domestic researchers, and technical standards, industry specifications and full-text case law are not structured to land on the open web. The principle above, that density and publication probability run in opposite directions, operates more forcefully in a smaller language.

The resulting symptoms are predictable.

  • Fluent casual usage alongside a thin professional layer. Fluency and expertise come apart
  • Answers drift toward the nearest English-language analogue. Ask about an institution that exists only in one jurisdiction and you can get an explanation of a similarly named foreign one
  • Error rates rise on proper nouns, agency names and article numbers. These do not generalise and must be read from source, and the source is thin
  • Asking the same question in English sometimes yields a better answer. Whether that answer fits the local system is a separate matter entirely

Public programmes exist to build language resources, such as the National Institute of Korean Language's Modu Corpus and the open datasets on AI Hub. Volume keeps growing, but it is not of a kind to be compared with web-crawl corpora by order of magnitude. Treating the gap as a problem about to be solved and treating it as a design constraint to carry lead to entirely different engineering choices. The second is the realistic posture.

9. Predicting where answers will be weak

Everything above is background; this section is what practitioners use. Even without knowing a model's data composition, you can predict fairly well which questions land on thin ground. Three axes.

  • Does it exist as public web text? Knowledge behind paywalls, inside organisations, or on paper only is thin in the model too
  • Does the correct answer change over time? Time-varying values are frozen at the training boundary. This is a timing problem, not a composition one, and only lookup resolves it
  • Does it hinge on a jurisdiction-specific institution or standard? If so the answer can slide toward an English-language analogue

The value of this table is not the prediction but deciding where to spend verification effort. Doubting every answer equally destroys the benefit of using a model at all. Attach checks only to questions that trip one of the three axes, and use the rest directly.

10. What could not be confirmed

Collected in one place: what was left out for want of grounds.

  • The actual data composition of major commercial models. Domain proportions, sources and filter criteria are largely undisclosed. Nothing here asserts any company's corpus composition
  • Domain-level breakdowns for smaller languages. No official tally worth citing was available for how, say, Korean documents inside public web corpora split across news, community and academic material. The statements above are directional judgements, not measurements
  • Third-party reproduction of the grade-five experiment. The corpus is not yet released, so external verification is absent. Only the direction of the result is cited, not fine-grained numbers
  • Robustness of the filtering. Whether concepts above a grade level can be excluded from text completely has been questioned. In natural text, concepts do not appear only explicitly. How much that shakes the result is a post-reproduction question
  • The outcome of training-data copyright disputes. Proceedings are live in several jurisdictions and it is not the stage to state conclusions. Only the fact of dispute is noted
  • The cost of changing composition. The real cost of altering a data mixture and the retraining burden it implies were not estimated from public material

Conclusion: the ceiling is drawn in the data, everything else moves beneath it

Scaling the model, adding post-training, refining the prompt. All three work. But what all three do is closer to drawing out what is already there than to creating what is not. That is what the grade-five experiment showed under controlled conditions.

That is not a reason to land on AI has limits after all. The same finding breaks the opposite position just as cleanly. Just add more data does not fit this result either. Growing the total without deciding what to grow mostly thickens what was already thick.

Three things to carry into practice.

  • Model performance is a terrain that varies by region, not a single value. Comparing on one benchmark average hides exactly where your question will land
  • Check first whether your domain is thick or thin on the public web. If thin, that knowledge must be supplied through context or lookup rather than expected from the model
  • Assume professional domains in smaller languages are thin by default. This is a data-side structure rather than a defect of a particular model, so switching models changes little

When you want to know what a model knows, the question worth asking is how much of this knowledge ever existed as text in the world. If you can estimate that, you can gauge the reliability of the next answer far more accurately than by studying a benchmark table.
