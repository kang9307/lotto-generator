It Gets Weaker As You Use It and Comes Back With Rest - Where Myasthenia Gravis Is Suspected and What the Clinic Checks

Not the muscle but the junction where nerve meets it. The signature of fluctuation, what each test can and cannot see, and the moment it becomes an emergency

Sep 1, 2026 Health & Wellness

An eyelid droops. It was fine in the morning. By the afternoon one side has sunk, and after a few minutes with the eyes closed it lifts again. The eye clinic says there is nothing wrong with the eye itself, and someone suggests it is just tiredness. Circling that loop for months is not unusual.

Myasthenia gravis is missed right there. The name makes it sound like a disease that ruins muscle, but the thing that breaks is not the muscle. It is the junction where the nerve tells the muscle to contract. And this condition carries a fingerprint that sets it apart from other muscle problems: it weakens with use and comes back with rest.

This article is general information and does not replace diagnosis or treatment. Whether a test is needed and which treatment is chosen are decisions made by clinicians. Nothing written here should be used to confirm or rule out a condition on your own.

What breaks is the junction, not the muscle

Nerve and muscle are not wired together like a cable. There is a gap between them, and the signal crosses that gap chemically. Acetylcholine is released from the nerve ending, receptors on the muscle side receive it, and the muscle contracts. That place is the neuromuscular junction.

The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency rare disease helpline describes what happens in this condition as follows. Autoantibodies to the acetylcholine receptor bind to the receptors and destroy them, block transmission at the same time, and increase the rate at which receptors are turned over. The outcome is a drop in the number of working receptors.

So the muscle itself is largely normal. Strength is missing not because the muscle is damaged but because the signal does not arrive in sufficient quantity. The direction of treatment follows from that. The usual explanation for why repetition makes things worse is the exhaustion of a reserve: in a healthy junction more signal is delivered than a single contraction requires. When that margin has been cut back, the first few attempts work and the next one does not.

First, the emergency - myasthenic crisis

The first thing worth writing down is not a symptom list but an emergency. The muscles that lose strength include the ones used for breathing and swallowing. If those two weaken rapidly, that alone is life-threatening. It is called myasthenic crisis.

The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency helpline describes it as an emergency in which respiratory muscle weakness appears together with swallowing difficulty severe enough that saliva and other secretions cannot be cleared. A 2023 review in Journal of Neurology reports that 10 to 20 percent of people with generalised disease experience it at least once in their lives, and treats progressive weakness of respiratory and bulbar muscles as the sign of an approaching crisis.

Signs that mean an emergency department without delay

  • Breathlessness, worse when lying flat, needing to breathe mid-sentence
  • Repeated choking on saliva or water, or saliva pooling because it cannot be swallowed
  • Slurred or nasal speech that gets noticeably worse over a few hours
  • The head dropping forward because the neck cannot hold it up
  • Any of the above deteriorating visibly within a single day

Diagnosed or not, this is not the moment to wait for a clinic appointment. Night or weekend, the emergency department is the safer choice.

The 2023 Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine summary defines crisis as respiratory or bulbar weakness severe enough to require intubation or mechanical ventilation, and points to marked weakness of the neck flexors and shoulder external rotators as a sign that one is approaching. Checks like these are not tools for reassurance, though. The judgement is made in the clinic.

Fluctuation is the most telling sign

What separates this condition from other muscle problems is not the kind of symptom but the timetable of the symptom. Seoul National University Hospital describes it as mild in the morning, tending to worsen through the afternoon, and improving after a short rest or a nap. The clinical name for it is fatigable weakness. In practice it looks like this.

  • The first flight of stairs is fine, and by the third the leg will not lift. A few minutes of rest and it climbs again
  • Chewing goes well at the start of a meal, then the jaw grows heavy halfway through and food is left
  • A long phone call turns the voice nasal. Call back and the first few minutes are normal again
  • After a long stretch at a screen the letters split in two, and closing the eyes for a while brings them back together
  • Raising the arms to wash hair works for a few seconds and then stops working

It is also worth noting what does not fit this pattern, because a different shape usually points somewhere else first.

  • Stiffness that is worst on waking and loosens with movement. That timetable runs the other way
  • Muscle pain that hurts on touch. Weakness here is usually painless
  • Numbness or tingling. Sensory nerves are not the stage this condition plays on
  • Changes in bladder or bowel control, or in level of consciousness

The Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine summary likewise notes the absence of sensory symptoms, pain, bowel or bladder dysfunction and altered mental status, with deep tendon reflexes generally preserved. This list is not an exclusion rule, however. Two things can coexist, and it works better as a list of what else to ask about.

The first symptom usually shows in the eyes

The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency helpline states that in most cases, around 85 percent, the illness begins in the ocular form, with ptosis and double vision as the first symptoms. The Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine summary likewise puts ocular symptoms at presentation in up to 85 percent.

Ptosis often starts on one side and can switch sides. Drooping on the right today and on the left next week is a mobility that other causes struggle to explain. Double vision comes with one useful clue. If covering one eye makes the doubling disappear, the problem lies in how the two eyes work together; if the doubling persists with one eye open, the problem is more likely in that eye. The usual bedside checks look like this.

  • Sustained upgaze — looking upward for a few minutes to see whether the muscle that lifts the lid tires out
  • Ice pack test — an ice pack held on the drooping lid for about two minutes, then measuring how much further the eye opens. The threshold for a positive result is an improvement of two millimetres or more
  • Strength of eye closure — in this condition the muscles that close the eye can weaken along with those that open it

What matters is how well those checks perform. The 2022 Journal of Neurology review collects a sensitivity of 28 to 96 percent and a specificity of 31 to 100 percent for the ice pack test. A range that wide means a good result is not a diagnosis and a poor one is not an exclusion.

Ocular and generalised

When symptoms stay in the eye muscles it is called ocular; when they spread to the limbs, the bulbar muscles and the respiratory muscles it is generalised. The classification most widely used internationally comes from the Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America.

ClassDescription
Class IOcular muscle weakness only, all other strength normal
Class IIMild weakness of muscles other than ocular
Class IIIModerate weakness of muscles other than ocular
Class IVSevere weakness of muscles other than ocular
Class VIntubation required, with or without mechanical ventilation

How often ocular disease becomes generalised varies widely between studies. An observational study published in Archives of Neurology in 2003 followed 94 of 147 ocular patients to the two-year mark and reported generalised disease in 4 of 58 treated with prednisone (7 percent) and in 13 of 36 who were untreated (36 percent). An abnormal antibody level carried a higher risk of generalisation (odds ratio 6.33). Because it was observational rather than randomised, the two groups may have differed from the start. The direction still stands.

  • Ocular disease is not a settled state that will stay in the eyes; it is a state under observation
  • Several sources agree that the first few years are the stretch that needs watching most
  • When to begin immune treatment in ocular disease remains a point that international guidance keeps revising

What appears when it spreads

Bulbar refers to the muscles served by the nerves emerging from the brainstem. Chewing, swallowing and speaking hang on them. The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency helpline notes dysarthria, dysphagia, breathing difficulty and trouble chewing when this territory is involved. It leads straight into the crisis described above.

  • Chewing — the jaw grows heavier towards the end of a meal, and tough food gets left
  • Swallowing — choking on water becomes frequent, with a feeling that something stays in the throat
  • Speaking — after talking for a while the voice turns nasal or the articulation blurs
  • Limbs — the shoulders and hips, the parts closer to the trunk, often weaken first
  • Neck — the head drops forward because holding it up becomes hard

Which antibody a person carries can change the distribution. In a study across 18 Korean centres published in PLoS One in 2018, patients with MuSK antibodies were 88.2 percent female, showed predominant bulbar involvement in 70 percent, and had frequent crises in 58.3 percent. That is why antibody testing feeds into the management plan rather than serving as paperwork.

Each test looks at a different layer

There are several tests because no single one is enough. Antibodies look at the cause, electrophysiology at whether transmission actually falls off, and imaging at the thymus.

Antibody testing

The acetylcholine receptor antibody comes first. The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency helpline records positivity of 85 percent in adult generalised disease and 50 percent in prepubertal children. If it is negative, the search moves to the next target. The same source notes that MuSK antibodies are found in 40 to 70 percent of generalised patients who are negative for the receptor antibody.

Electrophysiology

Repetitive nerve stimulation stimulates the same nerve several times and watches whether the response falls. The 2022 Journal of Neurology review gives the traditional cut-off as a 10 percent drop in response amplitude. Single-fibre electromyography measures something finer, the variation in arrival time of individual muscle fibres.

Chest imaging

Computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging is used to check the size of the thymus and look for a mass. The reason gets its own section below.

The ranges in the table are what the 2022 Journal of Neurology review gathered across many studies. They are wide because populations and methods differed, and that width is itself the statement that no single test settles the question. In the same review, single-fibre electromyography has the highest sensitivity and the acetylcholine receptor antibody the highest specificity.

Why a negative result does not rule it out

A negative line on a report reads like the end of the road, but in this condition it often is not. The Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America states that a person can still have the disease if the clinical criteria are met even when no antibody is detected. The numbers show why. Sensitivity of the acetylcholine receptor antibody is 85 to 90 percent in generalised disease but 50 to 70 percent in ocular disease.

Korean data point the same way. The 2018 PLoS One study retested 62 generalised patients who were negative for the receptor antibody on conventional assays and found antibodies in 25 of them (40.3 percent): 7 to clustered AChR, 17 to MuSK and 2 to LRP4, with 3 positive for two targets at once. The same people, and a different assay reversed the result.

  • A negative antibody is closer to a signal to move to the next test than to an exclusion
  • The more ocular the presentation, the harder it is to judge on antibodies alone
  • Cell-based assays pick up antibodies that older methods miss
  • In the other direction, Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine notes that a normal single-fibre EMG essentially rules the condition out

Why the thymus is imaged

The thymus sits in front of the heart, trains immune cells in childhood and shrinks with age. There are two reasons to image it: not to miss a tumour, and because the thymus appears to take part in producing the antibodies.

The 2025 Mediastinum review reports thymic abnormality, either follicular hyperplasia or thymoma, in roughly 80 percent of antibody-positive patients, with about 10 to 30 percent having thymoma, usually men over 50. In early-onset disease, follicular hyperplasia is seen in 50 to 60 percent.

  • A thymoma is a tumour that has to be managed in its own right, separate from the myasthenia
  • The state of the thymus is one of the forks in whether surgery gets discussed
  • A normal scan does not overturn the diagnosis. Confirming the diagnosis is not what the scan is for

This is also where thymectomy enters the conversation. The MGTX international randomised trial appeared in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2016, and according to a commentary in Journal of Thoracic Disease, following 126 antibody-positive generalised patients without thymoma over three years, the group that also had surgery had better clinical scores, needed less steroid and had fewer hospital admissions for exacerbation. That said, it is a result from a group enrolled under specific conditions and does not mean surgery is advised for everyone.

The four branches of treatment

The list of drug names is confusing, but grouped by purpose it is simple. Four branches each do a different job, and none substitutes for another.

BranchPurposeWhat it targetsWhat it does not do
Symptom controlMakes the remaining transmission last longerThe rate at which acetylcholine is broken downDoes not reduce the antibody
ImmunosuppressionReduces what produces the antibodyThe immune system broadly or a specific pathwayDoes not work immediately
Acute treatmentLowers the antibody effect quicklyCirculating antibody and complementDoes not last long
Thymic surgeryRemoves tissue involved in antibody productionThe thymusIs not a treatment that helps straight away

The order the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency helpline sets out overlaps with that structure. A drug that inhibits the enzyme breaking down acetylcholine comes first, immunosuppression including corticosteroids is added if needed, plasma exchange and intravenous immunoglobulin are available, and thymectomy is discussed separately. The 2020 update to the international consensus guidance added recommendations covering rituximab, eculizumab and methotrexate.

  • This article does not say which drug at what dose or how to take it. That varies with antibody type, age and other conditions
  • Symptom control working well does not mean immune treatment is unnecessary. The purposes differ
  • Cutting or stopping immunosuppression on one's own is a common trigger for crisis
  • Language promising a cure is not used here. The aim is to keep symptoms minimal while limiting side effects

What triggers a worsening

A sudden deterioration after a stable stretch usually has a trigger. Knowing them is not about being frightened but about booking an appointment ahead of a situation you can see coming.

TriggerWhy it mattersWhat the clinic does
Infection, respiratory in particularThe margin left in transmission is cut furtherIdentifies and treats it early
Surgery and anaesthesiaMuscle relaxants act directly on the junctionWants the diagnosis known before anaesthesia
A newly started medicineSome drugs can interfere with transmissionWants the diagnosis known before prescribing
HeatAdmissions in the United States were observed to peak in summerAdvises avoiding heavy exposure
Pregnancy and childbirthThe course shifts differently from person to personPlans ahead together with obstetrics
Rapid tapering of immune treatmentWhat was suppressed comes backSets the pace of tapering

The 2023 Journal of Neurology review notes that respiratory infection can precipitate a crisis, and reports the observation that admissions for this condition in the United States were most frequent in summer, offering heat-induced impairment of neuromuscular transmission as a possible reason. It is more accurate not to carry a stated possibility across as a settled fact. The same review points out that weakness of the laryngeal and pharyngeal muscles can lead to aspiration and pneumonia.

  • A cold is not something to wave off. A common infection can lead to an uncommon outcome here
  • Non-urgent surgery is scheduled for a point when the condition is stable
  • Overlapping triggers, such as starting a new medicine during an infection, are the most dangerous

Before starting a new medicine

This is a matter of habit rather than of memorising a list. The Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America publishes a cautionary drug list and writes that certain medications and over-the-counter preparations may worsen symptoms, so it is important to check with a doctor before starting anything new. It then adds, immediately, that appearing on the list does not mean a patient should not be prescribed those medications, and that some may be necessary. It is not a tool for a patient to refuse medicines with, but a tool for the prescriber to decide with.

  • Tell every doctor and dentist about the diagnosis. That includes the local clinic and the dental chair
  • If things worsen after starting something new, tell the prescriber. Do not simply stop it yourself
  • Supplements and over-the-counter products count too. No prescription does not mean no relevance
  • A card or phone note carrying the diagnosis shortens the explaining in an emergency

The same 2023 summary singles out certain antibiotic classes, intravenous magnesium, botulinum toxin, penicillamine and immune checkpoint inhibitors as needing particular caution. Checkpoint inhibitors are cited as the most common iatrogenic route to myasthenia gravis. The reason this article stops at class names is plain. Whether a drug is dangerous for a given person is not settled by the diagnosis alone; it depends on what alternatives exist and on the other conditions in play.

What can be adjusted day to day

The goal here is not to fix the disease but closer to deciding where the day's margin gets spent. Strength falls with use and returns to a degree with rest, so how a day is arranged affects real function.

  • Split the activity — short rests in between rather than everything at once. Resting before exhaustion recovers faster than after
  • Put the important things early — if the evening is worse, place work needing concentration and any driving in the morning
  • Pick mealtimes — avoid the hours when chewing is hard. If swallowing worsens noticeably, that is a clinical signal, not a scheduling one
  • Avoid heat — if a hot bath or midsummer afternoon leaves things worse, record it and mention it at the appointment
  • Protect sleep — in a condition where rest is the recovery mechanism, poor sleep cuts the mechanism

Exercise is where the answer depends on the state. Overdoing repetition drops strength on the spot, while not moving creates a separate problem. That balance point is not set by a general rule, so it belongs in a conversation with the treating team. No evidence that supplements or alternative remedies change the course of this condition appeared in the sources reviewed here.

Chronic fatigue and weakness are not the same

This is the point that stays confusing longest. In everyday speech tired and unable to exert are used interchangeably, but in the clinic they are two different things. The 2023 Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine summary notes that fatigue is common in these patients too, reported by about 80 percent, and adds that the fatigue is not grounds for escalating treatment. Other causes such as deconditioning, disturbed sleep and weight gain are layered into it. The clinical marker is not fatigue but fatigable weakness.

What to look atOrdinary fatigue and exhaustionWeakness in myasthenia gravis
What is missingEnergy, drive, overall staminaThe strength of specific muscles
TimetableSimilar all day, or irregularWorse with use, better with rest
RangeThe whole body feels heavyLocated: eyelids, chewing, swallowing, shoulders
Associated symptomsPain, sleep problems and mood change are commonPain or altered sensation is not typical
On examinationStrength testing is usually normalRepetition drops the strength visibly

One question does a lot of work. After rest, does that muscle actually get its strength back? If it does, there is a timetable, and a timetable is a clue. If the heaviness is even all day and the strength itself holds, other explanations come first. The two can of course overlap, and telling them apart is done by examination and testing.

  • A few days of notes reveal the timetable: when, and after what, things get worse
  • Photographs help. For ptosis, a morning shot and an evening shot say more than words
  • Emphasising only fatigue pushes this condition down the list. Describing what cannot be done, as an action, is more accurate

Registration and cost in South Korea

In South Korea myasthenia gravis is a listed rare disease on the helpline with the code G70.0. Once the diagnosis is confirmed, the national health insurance copayment scheme for rare diseases lowers what the patient pays. As of September 2026 the National Health Insurance Service sets it out as follows.

  • The legal basis is Article 44 of the National Health Insurance Act and Article 19(1) with Annex 2 of its Enforcement Decree
  • Registered rare disease patients pay 10 percent of the total covered cost for outpatient and inpatient care
  • The benefit runs for five years from the registration date for care given for that condition. Rare disease of unspecified type differs at one year
  • A registration form issued by the physician is submitted, and applying within 30 days of confirmation backdates the five years to the confirmation date. Later than that, it applies from the day of application

That 30-day window is missed often. The weeks right after a diagnosis are taken up with tests and treatment planning, and once it passes there is no backdating. Raising the registration form on the day the diagnosis is given avoids the problem. Documentation is confirmed with the treating institution and the insurance service branch.

What to prepare before the appointment

This condition often will not reproduce itself during five minutes in a consulting room. A morning appointment means meeting the doctor when symptoms are at their mildest. So the quality of the information you bring shortens the time to an answer.

  • When in the day it is worst: morning versus afternoon, and after what activity
  • How much comes back with rest, and whether that takes minutes or hours
  • What cannot be done, described as an action. Three flights of stairs, washing hair
  • Two photographs of the eyelid, at its best and at its worst
  • Changes in swallowing and speech, with how often and when choking happens
  • Everything recently started, medicines and supplements alike. Photographing the boxes is accurate

Three things are worth remembering. The fault lies not in the muscle but at the junction. The fingerprint that separates it from other muscle problems is not the kind of symptom but the timetable of worsening with use and recovering with rest. And breathing or swallowing that deteriorates within hours is not something to wait out; it is a reason to go to an emergency department. The rest is not for this article to decide.

Once more, in writing. This article is general information, and diagnosis and treatment rest on clinical judgement. The test performance figures and proportions given here shift with the study population and method; they are not numbers that predict an individual case. If symptoms are present, have them checked in a neurology clinic, and if breathing or swallowing worsens within a short time, go to an emergency department without delay.

What I could not verify

  • The full text of the 2020 international consensus guidance update was refused by the journal server. Its scope was confirmed from the public summary by the European Reference Network for rare neuromuscular diseases, and no individual recommendation is quoted here.
  • The original MGTX paper could not be opened either. The direction of the three-year result was taken from a commentary, and because that commentary carries no p values, no significance figures are given. Steroid dose comparisons were confirmed but not carried across.
  • The helpline records 125 per million as an incidence figure, and since it cannot be determined from that page whether it means incidence or prevalence, the number is not used in the body of this article.
  • The claim that oxygen saturation falls late in muscular respiratory failure is often repeated, but it was not found in the literature opened here, so it is left out of the red-flag section.
  • The original 2000 special report behind the MGFA classification was also refused by its server. The table follows only the Class I to V descriptions given on a public calculator page, and the subclass wording is not carried over.
  • No source confirming the mechanism by which heat worsens symptoms was found. The 2023 review also states it only as a possibility.
  • Reported rates of generalisation from ocular disease varied widely across sources, so only the figures from a single 2003 observational study are given, together with its conditions.
