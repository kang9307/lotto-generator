How to Use Sodium Percarbonate Properly, and What You Need First

What it turns into in water, why cold water wastes it, and what must never go in the same bucket — as of August 2026

2026-08-07 Life & Practical

Why the same powder works for one person and not another

Sodium percarbonate — sold almost everywhere as oxygen bleach, oxygen booster or percarbonate soda — is the same white powder in every tub, yet one household gets the smell out of its towels and another sees no change at all. The product is rarely the variable. What separates the two is water temperature first, soaking time second, and what else went into the water third. Get that order right and the results become repeatable.

Before the order, though, something else has to be said. This is not really a detergent. It is an oxidiser. Safety data sheets classify it with the signal word Danger, warn that it may intensify fire, and list serious eye damage among its hazards. Japan added it to the list of regulated hazardous substances under fire safety law. It gets introduced in home-cleaning circles alongside the word natural, and that word carries no information about safety whatsoever.

This piece runs in four blocks: the one-line chemistry, the safety boundary, the per-task sequence, and the places it must not go. No brands, no product recommendations. Concentrations and exact temperatures vary between products, so read everything below with one assumption in place — the label on your tub is the final authority.

Before you start — the combinations that must never happen

This belongs at the front, not buried near the end. Cleaning accidents that end in an emergency room are almost never about cleaning power. They are about mixing.

Never mix these

  • Chlorine bleach (sodium hypochlorite) + any acid — citric acid, vinegar, acidic toilet cleaners. The reaction releases chlorine gas: coughing, breathing difficulty, streaming eyes, and in severe cases fluid in the lungs. This combination kills people in ordinary homes.
  • Chlorine bleach + ammonia-based cleaner — some glass and multi-surface cleaners contain ammonia. The result is chloramine gas.
  • Sodium percarbonate + chlorine bleach — two oxidisers tearing each other apart. Cleaning and disinfecting power vanish, the reaction turns vigorous, and you gain nothing but risk.
  • Sodium percarbonate + citric acid (or vinegar) — alkali and acid neutralise each other, both go flat, and gas is produced. Do that inside a sealed container and the container bursts.
  • Sequential use is still mixing. Using chlorine bleach in a drain, a drum or a basin and then adding something else without flushing thoroughly is the same as combining them.
  • Never seal a dissolved solution. Oxygen keeps coming off and pressure builds. That covers spray bottles and capped plastic bottles alike.

The rule is one sentence. One product at a time, and flush thoroughly with water before moving to the next.

One more framing point. The line oxygen bleach is safer than chlorine bleach is half true. It sits outside the combinations that generate chlorine gas — that much is real. It does not mean the stuff is safe. It is still a strongly alkaline solution and still a substance whose hazard classification includes serious eye damage.

The moment it hits water it becomes two things

The formal name is sodium percarbonate, an adduct of sodium carbonate and hydrogen peroxide. Put it in water and it separates back into those two components.

sodium percarbonate + water → sodium carbonate (alkali) + hydrogen peroxide (oxidiser)

Everything about how this product behaves is in that line, because each half does a different job.

  • Hydrogen peroxide — releases active oxygen as it breaks down. That oxidises pigment out of stains, oxidises the compounds behind bad smells, and acts on microorganisms. This half is why the category is called oxygen bleach.
  • Sodium carbonate — makes the solution strongly alkaline. It lifts grease the way soap does and breaks down protein soils. The gunk that peels off the outside of a washing drum is mostly this half at work.
  • Bleaching from the peroxide, cleaning from the carbonate. Both arrive in one tub, which is why stain removal and soil removal happen together.
  • Turn that around and you get a limitation: once the active oxygen has gassed off, what remains is just alkaline water. That is why a solution left standing for hours no longer bleaches anything.
  • Because no chlorine is involved, no chlorine smell is left behind. That is the practical advantage of the oxygen category.

One more thing worth naming. The fizzing in a drum or a drain is oxygen, not carbon dioxide. It is a different phenomenon from the foam you get pouring vinegar onto baking soda, and it is exactly why sealing the solution is dangerous.

Why nothing happens when you dissolve it in cold water

This is the single most common failure. The powder dissolving and the oxygen coming out are separate events. In cold water the powder does disperse to a degree, the water goes cloudy, and it is easy to conclude that the job is under way. But the rate at which active oxygen is released depends heavily on temperature, and in cold water very little of it appears before the machine moves on to rinsing.

So a large share of the it did nothing for me reviews are not about the product at all. They are about cold washing. A standard machine cycle usually runs on whatever comes out of the tap, and winter tap water sits well below the range where the bleaching reaction does meaningful work.

Here is the honest part. The exact temperature at which it starts working cannot be pinned to a single number, because purity, additives, concentration and soaking time all move it. The 40 to 60 range above is the band that multiple usage guides converge on, not a threshold. If your product prints a recommended temperature, that one wins.

Two things help in practice. First, do not scatter powder into the drum. Dissolve it fully in hot water separately and then add it. Second, if you have no warm cycle, run a basin of warm water, soak first and then transfer to the machine. Pouring hot water straight into a machine is restricted on some models, so check the manual.

These four are entirely different substances that get bundled together under the heading of natural cleaning. They do different jobs, and the reasons not to mix them differ in each case.

Why people keep being told to combine them

The advice that pouring citric acid onto baking soda makes it fizz and lifts the dirt has circulated for years. What actually happens is neutralisation. Alkali and acid cancel each other out and give off carbon dioxide. The fizz is highly visible; the cleaning power disappears at the same moment. For a drain, where the point is to physically push material along, a little foam helps. For removing soil chemically, you have just made it weaker.

  • Alkaline-side soils — grease, protein, food residue — respond to alkaline cleaners. Percarbonate and baking soda live here.
  • Soils that need acid — limescale, soap scum, the urine scale behind toilet rings — call for citric acid.
  • When both kinds of soil are present, split it into stages. Treat one, rinse thoroughly, then treat the other.
  • Do not pre-mix percarbonate with detergent for storage. Active oxygen gets consumed breaking down the detergent components. Add each separately at the point of use.
  • A natural label says nothing about safety. Citric acid still makes chlorine gas when it meets bleach, and percarbonate in an eye is still an emergency.

What to have ready before you start

Without the right kit you end up doing it bare-handed, and bare-handed means fingertips that feel slippery and then split, because a strongly alkaline solution strips the lipids out of skin.

Do not start without these

  • Rubber or nitrile gloves — thin disposable food gloves are not built for long soaking work. Long cuffs help.
  • Eye protection — goggles are best; at minimum wear your glasses. Powder dropped into hot water spits. Hazard classification for this substance includes serious eye damage.
  • Ventilation — open a window or run an extractor before you begin. This matters most in a bathroom or a small utility room.
  • A stainless steel or plastic basin — not aluminium. Aluminium discolours and the surface is attacked.
  • Something to measure with — dose to the label rather than by eye. More powder does not make it work better.
  • An uncovered vessel — leave soaking containers open, or if you must cover them, rest the lid on loosely rather than sealing it.

Worth having

  • A thermometer — a kitchen one is fine. What people call warm water varies by more than ten degrees from person to person.
  • A timer — soaking time drives the result, and almost everyone forgets to track it.
  • A long-handled brush and an old toothbrush — the drum rim, the folds in a door gasket and the gaps in a drain are all out of finger reach.
  • A strainer or small net — after a drum clean, debris floats up and needs lifting out before it blocks the drain filter.
  • A vented storage container — it must not be fully airtight. The manufacturer's own packaging is the safest option.
  • An apron and sleeve covers — splashes on coloured clothing leave permanent pale spots.

Cleaning the washing machine drum

This is where the effect is most visible. The outer face of the drum accumulates detergent residue, softener, limescale and mould in layers. The alkali loosens the layer and the active oxygen removes the smell and the colour. The key technique is never to scatter powder into the drum. Dissolve it in hot water first so it does not simply settle on the bottom.

  • Take everything out and run it on an empty drum.
  • Empty the lint trap and drain filter before the job and again after it. Loosened debris blocking the filter is a very common outcome.
  • Lift out the black flakes floating on the surface after the soak. Draining them straight down sends them into the pipework.
  • The folds in the door gasket never see the water, so they have to be wiped by hand.
  • Pull the detergent drawer out and wash it separately. That is often where the smell originates.
  • Finish with a plain water rinse cycle. Alkaline residue left behind transfers into the next load.
  • When it is done, leave the door open and let it dry completely. Closing it up wet puts you back where you started.

Soaking white cotton and linen

This is for yellowed white shirts, collar marks and old sweat stains. It damages fibres less than boiling and costs less colour and smell than chlorine bleach. Still, the right framing is that it is safest on whites rather than reserved for them.

  • Dissolve completely first, then add the garment. Sprinkling powder directly onto fabric can bleach that one spot.
  • Soaking longer does not keep improving things. Once the active oxygen is spent, you are just soaking in alkaline water.
  • When the water cools, the reaction stops. An overnight soak mostly does its work in the first few hours.
  • Keep out garments with zips, metal buttons or decorative hardware.
  • Soaking coloured items alongside whites lets released dye transfer onto the whites.
  • Rinse thoroughly when the soak ends. Alkaline residue left in a garment can sting the skin.

Dishcloth and towel odour

The sour smell in towels is mostly bacteria plus oils trapped deep in the fibre. Alkali releases the oils while active oxygen oxidises the odour compounds, which makes this a well-matched task.

  • If you simmer them on the hob, do not use an aluminium pot. Stainless steel or enamel instead.
  • It can boil over, so do not fill the pot to the brim and do not walk away from it.
  • Drying completely after rinsing is half the job. Folded away slightly damp, the smell returns within days.
  • Heavy fabric softener use reduces absorbency and builds up oils in towels. Cutting the cause back is the more durable fix.
  • Coloured cloths can lose colour. Separate them from the whites.

Drains

The method is powder into the drain, then hot water, then leave it. Organic matter breaks down and the smell drops. It is not a tool for clearing a physical blockage, though. Hair masses still have to be pulled out.

  • If chlorine bleach went down recently, flush thoroughly with water first. Otherwise you are mixing on a delay.
  • The same applies when alternating with citric acid: put a generous water rinse between them.
  • If the pipework includes aluminium fittings, it is better to avoid this entirely.
  • On a septic tank or a private treatment system, check with whoever maintains it before making this a routine.
  • Hot water can splash back, so keep your face away from the opening.

Bathroom mould — adjust the expectation

This one needs an honest split. On surface soiling across tile grout and walls, oxygen bleach does work. But mould that has rooted into silicone sealant and stained it black is a different problem. Chlorine bleach is far stronger in that territory, and the oxygen route rarely gives a satisfying result.

  • Do not put it in a spray bottle. Pressure builds. If you need to hold solution against a surface, soak kitchen paper and lay it over instead.
  • Bathrooms are small and enclosed. Do not work in one without ventilation.
  • If you decide to switch to chlorine bleach, do it only after rinsing the percarbonate away completely. Layering products on the same spot counts as mixing.
  • Sealant that has already degraded will not come back from any chemical. Replacing the bead is usually the real answer.
  • Marble and natural stone finishes can be marked by alkali, so test somewhere hidden first.

Your machine type changes the method

The same drum clean plays out differently on a top loader and a front loader because the physical conditions differ. Applying top-loader instructions to a front loader simply does not work.

Top loaders

  • The big advantage is that you can fill it high, so the solution reaches the upper walls of the drum.
  • Fill to the maximum level with hot water and avoid diluting it with cold, which helps hold the temperature.
  • Add the powder, run a few minutes of agitation to dissolve it properly, then stop and let it soak. Undissolved powder settles on the bottom and does nothing.
  • Skim off floating debris during the soak, then drain.
  • After draining, run one more cycle on plain water to clear the residue.

Front loaders

  • Water only reaches the bottom of the door, so the soaking approach does not apply.
  • Use the tub-clean or highest-temperature cycle instead and let the machine supply the heat.
  • Excess foam can trigger errors or stretch the cycle on a front loader. Do not increase the dose.
  • The folds in the door gasket and the pooled water at the bottom are not solved by any cycle. They need wiping by hand.
  • Always check the drain filter, usually behind a panel at the base. Loosened debris collects there.
  • Leave the door ajar afterwards so the interior dries. Front loaders seal tightly and hold moisture.

One point applies to both. Some manufacturers advise against anything but their own drum-cleaning product, and that can bear on warranty terms, so reading the manual first is the safe move. Guidance varies by maker and model, which is why nothing categorical can be stated here.

Where it must not go

A percarbonate solution is strong alkali plus oxidiser. Anything vulnerable to those two properties lands on the banned list.

  • Wool and cashmere — animal protein fibres. Vulnerable to alkali and oxidation, so they harden and shrink irreversibly.
  • Silk — banned for the same reason. The lustre dies and it yellows.
  • Down and feather — also protein. Puffer jackets and duvets belong with specialist detergents.
  • Leather and suede — the surface is damaged and stiffens.
  • Heavily elastane-blended garments — stretch fibres lose their recovery to oxidation.
  • Aluminium — pots, basins, window frames, machine parts, any of them. It discolours and the surface corrodes. Once it has gone chalky white it is hard to reverse.
  • Some coated metals and hardware — zips, buttons and buckles can discolour.
  • Wood and wooden floors — alkali and moisture together cause staining and blanching. Solid wood chopping boards are better kept out too.
  • Marble and natural stone — the surface can be etched, so a hidden test comes first.
  • Deeply coloured clothing — not forbidden, but not safe either. Always spot-test.
  • Waterproof, water-repellent and coated fabrics — the treatment layer can be damaged.
  • Directly on skin — this is not a hand or body wash. Strong alkali damages the skin barrier.

When in doubt, read the garment's care label. Whether oxygen bleach is permitted is marked there as a symbol. If the label is worn away or missing, keeping the item out of the soak is the safer call.

Seal the solution and the container bursts — storage and shelf life

This section maps directly onto accidents. A percarbonate solution keeps releasing oxygen. Seal it and the gas has no exit, internal pressure climbs, and eventually the container swells and bursts. If that happens at face height, it goes into your eyes.

  • Do not put it in a spray bottle. The most common and most dangerous mistake there is.
  • Do not store it in a capped plastic or drinks bottle. The pressure problem is compounded by the risk of someone drinking it.
  • Mix only what you need, when you need it. Leftovers stay uncovered and then go down the drain with plenty of water.
  • Some powder packs ship with a small hole punched in them. That is not a defect, it is a vent. Do not block it.
  • If you decant, choose a container that is not fully airtight. Keeping the manufacturer's packaging is safest.
  • Store it cool, dry and ventilated. Moisture getting in means the reaction has already started inside the pack.
  • Keep it away from direct sunlight and heat sources — beside a hob, in a boiler cupboard, in a car in summer. Rising temperature can drive self-accelerating decomposition.
  • Being an oxidiser, it should not share a cupboard with paper, cloth or oily rags. It will feed a fire hard.
  • If the powder has caked into lumps, treat it as having absorbed moisture and already decomposed to a significant degree. That is usually why it no longer performs.
  • Store it out of reach of children and pets. It is a white powder that can be mistaken for sugar or salt.
  • If you spill it, sweep up as much as possible while it is dry before going anywhere near it with a wet cloth.

How long an opened pack lasts is not something to pin to a number here, because it varies enormously with product and storage. The practical test is simple though. If it is lumpy and barely fizzes in hot water, that pack is finished.

Protective kit, and what to do if something goes wrong

While you work

  • Gloves and eye protection go on before you start. Putting them on after a splash is too late.
  • When adding powder, keep your face away from over the water. Contact with hot water makes it spit.
  • Powder can drift, so do not pour it where a fan or air conditioner is blowing directly across the work.
  • In a small bathroom or utility room, work with the door open and the extractor running.
  • Keep children and pets out of the work area. Never leave a basin of solution on the floor and walk away.
  • Do not line up other cleaning products in the same spot. People reach for whatever is nearest and combine them by accident.
  • Wash your hands afterwards and moisturise. Alkali-exposed skin dries out.

If it contacts you or is swallowed

What follows is the general direction of first aid. It is not a substitute for diagnosis or treatment. If there are symptoms, the rule is to contact a medical professional.

  • In the eyes — flush immediately under running lukewarm water for at least 15 minutes, holding the lids open so the water reaches inside. Remove contact lenses if that is easy to do, and keep rinsing. Then get it checked medically. Do not self-assess an eye.
  • On skin — wash off with plenty of water and remove contaminated clothing. If redness or pain persists, seek medical advice.
  • If swallowed — do not induce vomiting. Rinse the mouth if the person is conscious, and contact a medical facility or your poison control centre immediately. Bring the packaging so the contents can be identified.
  • If gas or dust is inhaled — leave the area at once and get fresh air. If coughing or breathing difficulty continues, call emergency services without waiting.
  • Emergency contacts — use your local emergency number and your national or regional poison control centre. Keep those numbers where you can find them rather than searching mid-incident.
  • If a mixing accident produces a sharp smell — do not go closer to identify it. Get people and pets out first, then open the windows.

Mistakes that come up again and again

  • Dissolving it in cold water and concluding it does not work — the most common by far. Fix the temperature first.
  • Scattering powder straight into the drum — it settles on the bottom undissolved and the job ends there.
  • Assuming more powder means better results — temperature and time drive the reaction. Overdosing only makes rinsing harder and triggers foam errors in front loaders.
  • Soaking overnight — once the water cools the reaction stops. The first few hours do most of the work.
  • Decanting into a spray bottle — pressure builds and it bursts. Worth repeating as often as it takes.
  • Alternating with chlorine bleach without rinsing — that is mixing on a delay.
  • Adding citric acid and believing the fizz means it is working — that is the sound of both of them being neutralised.
  • Letting a wool jumper or silk blouse into the soak — there is no route back from that.
  • Simmering things in an aluminium pot — the pot degrades before anything else does.
  • Skipping the drain filter after a drum clean — the next wash throws a drainage error.
  • Working bare-handed — it seems fine at the time, and then fingertips split.
  • Storing it in the damp cupboard under the sink — moisture decomposes it slowly, so it is already weak next time you reach for it.

  • The exact activation temperature — no single threshold could be confirmed for where meaningful reaction begins. The 40 to 60 degree band is simply where multiple usage guides converge.
  • Grams per litre of water — no standardised recommended concentration could be confirmed. Purity and additives differ, so the product label governs.
  • Optimal soaking time — it shifts with temperature, concentration and soil type, so no universal figure is offered.
  • Shelf life after opening — no standard figure could be confirmed. Caking and the fizz test are the only practical guides.
  • Individual machine manufacturers' official positions — whether percarbonate drum cleaning is sanctioned, and whether it bears on warranty, differs by maker and model and could not be checked exhaustively.
  • Quantified performance on mould embedded in silicone sealant — how far the oxygen route actually gets could not be confirmed numerically.
  • Effects on septic tanks and private treatment systems — the impact of repeated use could not be confirmed.
  • Variation in purity across retail products — active content and additive composition could not be surveyed comprehensively.

Frequently asked questions

Q. Can I use sodium percarbonate in cold water?

Powder dissolving and bleaching happening are different events. The release of active oxygen, which does the bleaching and deodorising, is strongly temperature dependent, so in cold water very little appears before the rinse arrives. Guidance in several countries converges on roughly 40 to 60 degrees Celsius, but no single threshold can be stated as fact. Follow the temperature on your product label, and check what your machine cycle actually heats to alongside the fabric's heat tolerance.

Q. Does mixing it with chlorine bleach make it stronger?

No. Both are oxidisers, so they destroy each other, the cleaning and disinfecting power disappears, and only the violence of the reaction remains. The more dangerous combinations sit elsewhere: chlorine bleach with citric acid or vinegar releases chlorine gas, and chlorine bleach with an ammonia-based cleaner releases chloramine gas. Sequential use in the same drain or drum without a thorough flush in between is exactly as dangerous.

Q. Can I keep the dissolved solution in a spray bottle?

No. The solution keeps releasing oxygen, so sealing it raises internal pressure and the container can burst. That is also why many powder packs carry a vent hole. Mix only what you are about to use, and leave any remainder uncovered before flushing it away with plenty of water. Never decant it into anything that could be mistaken for a drink.

Q. Is it safe on coloured clothes, wool or silk?

Never on protein fibres such as wool, silk, cashmere and down. They are vulnerable to strong alkali and oxidation, so they harden and yellow irreversibly. Coloured cotton and linen tolerate it better than chlorine bleach, but better is not safe, so test on a hidden area such as an inside seam or hem first. Garments with metal trims risk discolouration and are best kept out of the soak.

Q. What is the correct order for cleaning a washing machine drum?

On a top loader, fill to the maximum level with hot water, dissolve the label dose completely, agitate briefly, soak, then drain and rinse. A front loader cannot be filled that high, so use the tub-clean or hottest cycle to get the temperature instead. Either way, empty the lint trap and drain filter afterwards and wipe inside the door seal by hand. Some manufacturers restrict drum cleaning to their own products, so check the manual, and if chlorine bleach was used recently, run a rinse first.

Closing

Using sodium percarbonate well comes down to three sentences. Dissolve it fully in warm water. Use one product at a time. Never seal it. When it does not work, the first sentence is usually where things went wrong. When someone gets hurt, it is usually the second or the third.

And do not relax because a label says natural. This is a substance classified as causing serious eye damage and as capable of intensifying a fire. It is genuinely easier to handle than chlorine bleach, but that is not the same as safe. Gloves, eye protection and one open window prevent almost all of the accidents.

Note: the methods and safety information here are general guidance as of August 2026. Purity, additives and recommended doses vary between products, so the label on what you bought is the final authority. The emergency material describes the general direction of first aid only; it does not replace diagnosis or treatment, and anyone with symptoms should contact a medical professional.
