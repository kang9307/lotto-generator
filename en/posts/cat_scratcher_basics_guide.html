Cat Scratchers - The Criteria and What to Know Before You Start

Understand why scratching is essential and half the product choice is already made

2026-08-18 Life & Practical

You bought a scratcher, and the cat walks straight past it to work on the corner of the sofa. The usual response is to search for a "better scratcher". Yet this problem is rarely solved by swapping products - it is far more often solved by understanding what scratching is and changing where the scratcher stands. The most common mistake in choosing a scratcher is not material or price. It is location.

This article recommends no specific products. Instead it gives you an order of operations: why scratching cannot be trained away, what to look at when picking material and shape, why placement beats product, and how to move a sofa-scratching cat onto a scratcher. The basis is the feline environmental needs guidelines published jointly by the International Society of Feline Medicine (ISFM) and the American Association of Feline Practitioners (AAFP), plus behavioral studies that actually measured scratcher preferences. Sources are collected at the end.

1. Scratching Is Not a Habit - It Is Essential Behavior

First, the premise needs correcting. Scratching is not a bad habit, and it is not defiance. It is behavior built into the cat as an animal, and three distinct functions overlap in the single motion.

  • Claw maintenance - Scratching peels away the worn outer husk of the claw and exposes the new claw underneath. It is grooming that keeps the claws in working condition for hunting and climbing.
  • Scent marking - The paw pads carry scent glands. While the cat scratches, that scent transfers to the surface as a chemical signal saying this territory is taken.
  • Visual marking - The scratch marks themselves are a visible sign to other cats. The mark is complete only when scent and visible traces are left together.
  • Stretching - Reaching out the front legs, loading body weight and pulling stretches the back, shoulders and forelegs. Cats do it especially often right after waking.

The environmental needs guidelines issued jointly by the AAFP and ISFM classify scratching areas as a key environmental resource, ranked alongside food, water, litter boxes and resting places. That means a scratcher is not an optional toy but basic equipment whose absence creates deprivation. An outdoor cat has tree trunks for the job; deny an indoor cat a scratcher and the substitute will be the most scratchable surfaces in the house - the sofa and the wallpaper. A cat scratching the sofa is not misbehaving. It is making the most rational choice its environment offers.

One more thing worth knowing spares you a scare. Near the scratcher or on the floor you may find thin, translucent claw-shaped shells. At first glance it looks like a claw has fallen out, but these are the worn outer husks shed by scratching. They are evidence the scratcher is doing its job, not a warning sign. If an entire claw has come out at the root or there is bleeding, that is a different matter - see a vet first.

2. What a Scratcher Solves - and What It Does Not

Set the goal correctly and you will not be disappointed. Here is what a scratcher does.

  • It becomes the outlet that redirects the scratching urge onto an approved surface. Protecting the furniture is the byproduct.
  • It serves as stretching equipment, especially in vertical form.
  • It becomes a marking point, which gives the cat a sense of security.
  • In multi-cat homes it acts as a per-group marking resource and helps reduce tension.

What a scratcher cannot do is just as clear.

  • It does not eliminate scratching, and there is no reason to. The goal is not "stop scratching" but "scratch here".
  • It does not replace nail trims. Scratching sheds the husk; it does not shorten the claw.
  • It does not remove sources of stress. A sudden large increase in scratching may have a separate cause, such as a change in the environment or tension with another cat.
  • It does not screen for disease. Constant licking around the claws, limping, or split and bleeding claws are not scratcher problems.

When the vet comes first. Abrupt changes in how much a cat scratches, abnormalities of the claws or toes, and overgrown claws in older cats can be health issues rather than behavior issues. This article covers normal scratching behavior; it is not a substitute for veterinary care.

3. Materials - Cardboard, Sisal, Carpet, Wood

Material is the first axis on which feline taste divides. In a study that placed scratchers of several materials in homes with adult cats and measured actual use, scratchers wrapped in rope (sisal-type) and cardboard drew more interaction than those covered in sofa fabric or carpet. That is a group average, though - an individual cat's preference is confirmed only by observation.

The rule for choosing is simple. Start with a texture similar to whatever is being scratched now. If the cat works on the sofa's coarse weave, sisal is the likely fit; if it scratches rugs or mats, a horizontal cardboard pad has the better odds. If you already have a carpet-scratching problem, a carpet-covered scratcher only deepens the confusion and is best avoided. Within sisal, wound rope and tightly stretched fabric feel quite different - some cats ignore the rope version and use the fabric one. Do not assume they are the same product just because both say sisal.

If you have no idea of your cat's taste yet, let cost set the order. Horizontal cardboard pads are cheap, which makes them the lowest-risk taste test. Use cardboard to learn where and in which direction the cat scratches, then invest in a long-lived sisal or wood piece that matches - better than buying an expensive cat tree first and watching it gather dust.

4. Shape - Vertical, Horizontal, Inclined

After material comes orientation. Every cat leans toward standing up to scratch or crouching to scratch, and many use both. The conditions for each shape are as follows.

ShapeFits whenWhat makes it work
Vertical postThe cat scratches vertical faces - sofa sides, door frames, wall cornersHeight to spare at full body extension, and stability that does not budge when the cat loads its weight and pulls. One wobble can be enough to get it abandoned
Horizontal pad or boxThe cat scratches rugs, mattresses or flooring; also kittens and seniors with stiff jointsIt must not slide. Anchor light cardboard against a wall or put anti-slip matting under it
InclinedHalfway between vertical and horizontal. A safe trial when the preference is unknownEnough weight and floor grip that the ramp does not lift

Most vertical-post failures come down to two things: not tall enough for a full stretch, or a base too light to resist the pull. Scratching is a motion of pulling with body weight loaded, so stability comes before texture. Not sure of the preference? Look at what is being scratched today. Vertical faces mean a post; the floor means a pad.

5. Placement - Why Location Beats Product

The same scratcher gets used in the middle of the living room and abandoned in a balcony corner. The reason lies in what scratching is for. Territorial marking is a display behavior. A mark left where no one passes is not a mark. The moment you tidy the scratcher away to somewhere it will not offend the eye, the cat loses its reason to use it. The AAFP and ISFM guidelines say exactly this: put scratching areas near sleeping areas and in prominent locations.

LocationWhy
Next to the sleeping spotCats scratch as part of stretching right after waking. If a scratcher stands within a few steps of the bed, that spot becomes the habit
Main routes and room entrancesWell-traveled paths and territory boundaries are where marks belong. The more the space is shared by cat and human, the better
Right beside the scratched furnitureThe spot already being scratched is the location the cat itself selected. Standing a substitute next to it is where relocation starts
By windows and cat treesVantage points overlooking the territory are where the marking urge concentrates
Places to avoidSpare rooms, balconies, beside the litter box - anywhere off the daily route. However good the product, it will not be used

To sum up: a scratcher is not furniture, it is a notice board. A notice board only gets read where people walk. If the position feels like it spoils the decor, you have probably placed it correctly.

Confirming the spot is also a matter of observation. Leave cheap cardboard scratchers in two or three candidate positions for a few days each, check where scratch marks actually appear, and put the real product there. It fails less often when the human ratifies the spot the cat has already chosen rather than assigning a spot the human prefers.

6. When the Sofa Is the Target - Moving the Cat by Substitution

If the sofa is already being scratched, there is a sequence. The core principle is single: offer the better alternative before you take the scratching spot away. Block without an alternative and the behavior simply migrates to the next piece of furniture.

  1. Observe - Establish what is scratched and in which direction. Sofa side (vertical, fabric) or rug (horizontal) determines the substitute's shape and material.
  2. Install the substitute - Place the scratcher right next to the scratched spot. Not across the room - half a step away.
  3. Lower the old surface's appeal - Temporarily cover the scratched area with a throw or protector to change its texture. This is not punishment; it is creating a difference in attractiveness between the options.
  4. Reward use - When the cat uses the scratcher, reward on the spot with a treat or praise. For adult cats, sprinkling catnip on the scratcher is also valid - in a study of adult cats, catnip increased scratching time.
  5. Move gradually - Once the scratcher is established, if you want it elsewhere, shift it a little at a time over days rather than all at once.
  6. Remove the cover - After scratcher use has become habit, take the furniture cover off. If the sofa relapses, go back one step.

There is one thing not to do: grabbing the cat's front paws and miming a scratch against the post. Forced contact can create avoidance of the object itself. Luring and waiting is faster in the end. Play-based invitation is fine - trail a wand toy over the pad or up the post, the claws catch the surface in the chase, and if the texture feels good the cat comes back on its own.

Set realistic expectations for the timeline too. Some cats switch within days; several weeks is common. The longer a cat has worked on the sofa, the deeper the scent and habit soaked into that spot. Returning to the sofa once or twice midway is not failure but part of the process, and each time the productive response is to re-examine the substitute's appeal rather than to punish.

7. Punishment and Declawing - The Two Things Not to Do

Why spray bottles, clapping and shouting fail is simple: scratching is essential behavior, so punishment does not remove the urge. What disappears is only the sight of scratching in front of you. A cat links the punishment to the person more readily than to the act, and the outcome is a cat wary of its owner plus furniture scratched in secret. The AAFP and ISFM environmental guidelines state plainly that marking behaviors, scratching included, should never be punished. Essential behavior is not to be blocked but redirected.

Declawing is a problem of a different order. Despite the name it is not removal of the claw but amputation of the last bone of each toe (the third phalanx). The AAFP strongly opposed elective declawing in its 2017 position statement, and from 2021 its Cat Friendly Practice program no longer permits elective declawing at accredited clinics at all. The procedure is banned in the European Union, the United Kingdom, Australia, Brazil, Israel and other jurisdictions, as well as in some US states and cities. Beyond acute pain, bleeding and infection, chronic pain, gait changes and behavioral problems have been reported. If claws are causing real problems, the alternatives are these.

  • Regular nail trims - The length control that scratching does not provide. Learning technique and frequency from a vet or groomer is the fast route.
  • Nail caps - Soft covers glued over the claws. The scratching motion is preserved while surface damage drops.
  • A better scratching setup - Re-checking the material, shape and placement covered in this article.
  • Synthetic pheromones - A product category used as an adjunct for scratching problems. Whether to use one is a conversation for the vet.

8. Multi-Cat Homes - The Count and Spread Principle

With several cats, scratchers are not "shared equipment" but per-group resources. Because scratching is territorial marking, asking cats that do not get along to share one marking point is like asking rivals to overwrite each other's claims on a single notice board. The AAFP and ISFM guidelines recommend distributing key resources across multiple separated locations so that each social group of cats in the home gets the chance to leave scent by scratching and facial rubbing.

  • Do not cluster scratchers in one room - spread them across living areas so cats can use them without crossing paths.
  • Secure at least one near each cat's sleeping spot.
  • Mix vertical and horizontal types so individuals with different tastes each have an outlet.
  • The popular "one per cat plus one" is a rule of thumb in circulation, not a guideline figure. The real criterion is not the count but whether every group has access.

If one cat blocks another at a particular scratcher, or furniture scratching jumps after a new cat arrives, suspect count and placement first. That is not product dissatisfaction - it is a resource-competition signal. When introducing a new cat, do not have it share the residents' scratchers; give the newcomer its own new scratcher in its own space from the start to ease early tension. A marking point saturated with another cat's scent can read to the newcomer as a warning sign, not a resource.

9. Replacement Timing and Safety Checks

A scratcher is a consumable - but distinguish "worn" from "unusable". A shredded-looking scratcher may be an eyesore to you while being, to the cat, the most valuable object in the house, saturated with its own scent. Swap a well-used scratcher wholesale for a new one and the scent resets, and the new one may be snubbed for a while. Products with replaceable refills allow partial renewal; if it must be replaced whole, put the new one beside the old and remove the old one later.

  • Cardboard - When the grooves deepen and crumbs surge, or torn chunks come loose, it is time. If the cat shows signs of swallowing pieces, remove it immediately.
  • Sisal rope - Once the rope unravels into long strands there is a chew-and-swallow risk; replace it.
  • Tilt - A vertical post that leans even slightly can topple mid-use. Periodically tighten base screws and the post joint.
  • Floor grip - A horizontal pad that slides with every stroke gets used less. Put anti-slip matting under it.
  • Cat tree combos - Test the wobble under top-perch load with your own hands first. A fall is the most expensive accident a scratcher can cause.
  • Dust control - The area around cardboard needs frequent cleaning. If a family member has sensitive airways, factor that into placement.

10. Kittens and Senior Cats - Not the Same Criteria

Kittens begin scratching before eight weeks of age. In an experiment measuring scratcher preference in kittens, an S-shaped cardboard scratcher was preferred over post types, and given the same material they used the horizontal version lying on the floor more than the standing one. The striking finding concerns attractants: catnip, catnip oil and even the hair of other cats all failed to increase kitten scratching. Cats under about three months are understood not yet to have developed the system that responds to catnip. In other words, for kittens the working lever is shape selection, not catnip sprinkling. A kitten that settles into scratcher use in this period preempts most of the furniture problems of adulthood.

Senior cats need consideration in the opposite direction. As joints stiffen, the tall reach of vertical scratching can become a strain, so horizontal pads or low inclines are easier to access. Adjust placement closer to the sleeping spot to shorten the walk. The following changes belong to the clinic, not the scratcher aisle.

  • Scratching frequency drops noticeably - possibly a signal of pain or joint disease.
  • Claws thicken and overgrow into the paw pads - not rare in senior cats, and it needs treatment.
  • Constant licking around the claws or limping - the first step is a vet check, not a scratcher swap.

What I Could Not Verify

The backbone of this article comes from published veterinary guidelines and behavioral studies, but for the following items I could not confirm figures or evidence in official sources. They are left open rather than asserted.

  • A minimum height figure for vertical posts - The principle of full-body extension is confirmed in the guidelines, but no official centimeter threshold was found. The figures commonly quoted come from retailers or general media.
  • An official count formula for multi-cat homes - What the guidelines state is the principle of per-group access and distributed placement; figures like one per cat plus one do not appear in the guideline text I checked.
  • Lifespan data by material - No public data measuring average service life of cardboard or sisal was found. The replacement signals in this article are safety-oriented generalities.
  • Attractants other than catnip - Catnip's effect in adult cats is study-confirmed, but whether alternatives such as silver vine increase scratcher use was outside the material I reviewed.
  • Safety standards for commercial scratchers - Whether official safety standards exist for materials and adhesives could not be confirmed.

Conclusion - It Is the Sequence, Not the Product

The failure path is usually the same: buy a nice-looking product, put it in a corner where it will not spoil the room, and blame the cat when it goes unused. Reverse the sequence and the result reverses. Observe first (what is scratched, in which direction), pick the matching material and shape, place it somewhere prominent and next to the sleeping spot, and reward use. In this sequence, the product's price is the last variable.

And keeping the premise in mind makes everything easier. A scratching cat is not making trouble - it is being a cat. The owner's job is not to erase the behavior but to lay out the stage so that it plays out somewhere other than the sofa.
