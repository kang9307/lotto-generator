Bulk Data Does Not Only Leave Through Hacking - The Four Routes One Word Hides

What left and how it left decides the legal shape of the event, and what you actually have to do about it

Sep 2, 2026 Tech & IT

Leak is one word, but the events behind it come in at least four kinds. Someone climbing over a wall, a door that was standing open, years of material scraped from the outside and piled up, and material that came not from the original service but from another company that had once received a copy of it - these are entirely different things. The end state looks the same in every case. A large bundle of files is sitting somewhere, and the headline carries a number of terabytes.

And yet the four call for completely different responses. The law attaches at different points, the buttons a user should press are different, and even the question of whether when did they get in is a coherent question at all depends on which one you are looking at. This piece takes a recent story as its occasion but does not try to settle it. It is about what you have to separate out when you read a sentence like that one.

This piece is about the general standards for reading a data exposure story, and it does not judge the fault or liability of any company. As of 2 September 2026 the case discussed below has no official confirmation from the company involved, and the reporting itself is split on how the material got out. For the notification and remedy procedure that applies when personal data really has been exposed, the right place is the data breach response guide rather than this article.

The occasion - one sentence saying 12TB leaked

At the end of August 2026 several outlets reported that an archive of roughly twelve terabytes had begun circulating over torrents. Korean developer communities carried it under a headline saying that more than 12TB of Steam data accumulated over ten years had leaked. The first thing most people picture on reading that sentence is their own account. Did my password go out. Did my payment method. Do I need to change something right now.

Follow the reporting, though, and the character of the material is different. What multiple outlets described was game depots, builds and development assets that sat on Steam's older content system between 2003 and 2013. Unreleased prototypes, beta builds and files from cancelled projects were what people talked about, and coverage noted that content from other publishers was mixed in alongside first party titles. A statement that user accounts or passwords were included appears in none of the reporting published so far.

The same word, leak, is pointing at two different events. One is a decade of a studio's old development assets ending up in the hands of game preservation hobbyists. The other is your account being in danger. The headline does not separate them. The separation has to be done by the reader.

What is settled in this case and what is not

There are points every outlet reports the same way, and points they report differently. For this article the second group matters more.

  • Agreed - the scale is around twelve terabytes, the period runs from 2003 to early 2013, and the contents are game content and development assets. The reason it stops in 2013 is described as the content distribution system moving from the older generation to the current SteamPipe around then
  • Agreed - the researcher credited with first verifying the material stated that no hacking was involved and that everything was obtained through a publicly accessible endpoint, and most outlets quoted that statement as it stood
  • Split - the size was reported as both 12TB and 13TB. The difference is explained as different mirrors measuring the total differently, but which figure is correct has not been settled
  • Split - there is more than one account of the route. Alongside the exposed endpoint tied to old infrastructure, reporting also carried a source saying the incident may not have happened on the original service's side at all, but may be a breach of third party servers that had been hosting previously leaked material
  • Absent - no official statement from the company had appeared as of the coverage I read. Several outlets wrote that they had asked and were waiting for a response
  • Absent - community discussion noted that a large share of the files remain encrypted and need depot keys to open. In other words, the volume downloaded and the content actually opened are not the same number

So the picture is this. What came out is largely agreed on, and how it came out has not been narrowed to one answer. There is no reporting saying user personal data was included, and there is also no confirmation that it was not. In that state, writing a sentence that fixes the cause is not something the evidence supports.

The four routes data takes out the door

There are several axes you could sort incidents along, but if you cut along the line where the response actually diverges, you get four. They can overlap. Large incidents very often contain two or three of them at once.

1. Breach - someone got past access control

The form everyone pictures first. Access control was in place and an access happened that went past it. When personal data is involved, notification and reporting duties attach cleanly, and the time and circumstances can often be pinned down. The response procedure is the most standardised of the four. The trouble is that the expectation that every incident will be this shape is exactly what hides the other three.

2. Accumulated collection - public material gathered over a long time

Individual requests to a public API or to pages viewable without a login all went through normally, and years of them piled into an enormous dataset. Each single request may have broken no rule. And yet the end product is nothing the service ever intended to hand anyone. This route has the distinguishing feature that when did they get in barely makes sense as a question. The practical load and defence side of scraping is covered separately in a year of fighting scrapers and who gets the bill for crawlers.

3. Third party route - it came from someone you never signed with

The original service is untouched, and the material leaves from a partner, a processor, a research copy, or an archive that had been collecting older leaked material. That possibility was raised in this case too. It is the most awkward form from a user's point of view, because the stage of the event is a company whose name they have never heard. Tracing it, and sorting out who is answerable, takes the longest.

4. Open configuration and insiders - it was never closed in the first place

A store reachable without authentication, a directory with indexing left on, an old endpoint that keeps answering long after retirement. Not a breach but a consequence of configuration. Nobody climbed the wall and the goods are outside anyway. The reason someone with legitimate access removing material sits in the same box is that both cases share a property: no anomalous signal appears in the access control logs, which makes them similarly hard to detect.

RouteHow it looks from outsideWhere the character divergesWhat it means for a user
1. BreachA pile of files is sold or distributedAccess went past a control. Timing can usually be pinned downWait for notification and take account measures immediately
2. Accumulated collectionIdenticalIndividual access may have been normal. There is no single momentSeparate from account compromise. Visibility settings are the real lever
3. Third party routeIdenticalThe stage is not the original serviceIt is unclear who to even contact. Notice arrives late or never
4. Open configurationIdenticalNo control was passed, because none was thereExposure may have run long, so scope is hard to estimate

Size is not sensitivity

Headlines lead with volume because it is the only figure that is easy to count. File size is trivially verifiable and a big number catches the eye. But volume has almost no relationship to the size of the risk. Twelve terabytes of game builds may point at not one identifiable person, while a list of username and password pairs threatens hundreds of thousands of accounts at a few dozen kilobytes.

Four properties decide sensitivity. They are independent of one another, so judging from any single one goes wrong.

PropertyThe question it asksLow endHigh end
IdentifiabilityCan a specific person be recognisedGame executables, texture assetsRecords carrying a name and contact details
ReusabilityDoes the value work elsewhere as it standsInternal identifiers used only inside that serviceA username and password used in many places
MutabilityCan it be changed afterwardsPasswords, access tokensDate of birth, fingerprints and other fixed attributes
JoinabilityWhat does it become joined to other materialAggregate figures with a single fieldRecords with several fields sitting on the same row
  • What to look for first in coverage is not the total but the list of fields. Data is not a field
  • Setting your response level before the fields are known usually produces something either excessive or insufficient
  • The only thing a large volume reliably tells you is roughly what the storage cost is for whoever downloaded it
  • Conversely a small volume is no basis for relief. The most dangerous lists are generally small

What ten years of material really means - an event with no moment

Put the phrase accumulated over ten years in front of people and they read it as ten years of continuous leaking. In reality two entirely different structures hide behind it. Material trickling out over ten years and a store holding ten years of material being opened once call for opposite responses. The first is a detection failure. The second is an asset management failure.

This distinction matters in practice because the law asks about timing. Article 34(1) of the Korean Personal Information Protection Act requires a controller who becomes aware that personal data has been lost, stolen or leaked to notify the data subject without delay, and among the matters to be notified it lists the categories of personal data involved and when and how the leak occurred. In accumulation shaped incidents, filling those two boxes is structurally hard.

  • The moment exposure began, the moment somebody actually took a copy, and the moment it was redistributed are all different. Even with logs, joining the first two is difficult
  • This is why company statements come late or vague. That is not by itself evidence of a cover up
  • At the same time vagueness is not absolution either, because an inability to pin the timing is itself information about the state of management
  • As a reader, swapping the question from when were they breached to from when was this reachable sometimes produces an answer where the first one produced none

The doors retired systems leave behind

That the material in this case stops dead in 2013 is itself telling. The explanation carried in the reporting is that the content distribution structure changed generation around then, and nothing from after the move to the new structure is in this archive. What the current official developer documentation confirms is roughly that SteamPipe is the content system and depots are its unit in the present distribution structure; exactly when the transition completed could not be confirmed from that documentation.

The part that generalises has nothing to do with any company name. When a system is replaced, the old one usually does not get deleted. Deleting is risky, irreversible, and wins nobody any praise. So it stays in a state of not being used. And not being used is not the same as being switched off.

  • A system that has fallen off the asset inventory also falls off the review list, because security review works by walking an inventory
  • A retired system authenticates the way its era did. Settings that were reasonable then read as an open door by today's standards
  • The people who ran it have moved on. With nobody left who knows what it is, even the decision to switch it off keeps getting deferred
  • This is not only a web service story. How the same problem shows up on the industrial side is covered in why controllers do not belong on the internet
  • The measure that actually works is not periodic review but putting a shutdown confirmation into the retirement procedure. The job is not done at migrated, it is done at switched off

How to read a story that says data leaked

This is the practical part. Incidents keep happening and there is never time to chase the primary sources each time. Instead, keeping a seven row table in your head while you read makes it quick to see what a piece is claiming and what it is quietly not claiming.

  • Who is the subject. Is it company A leaked, or is it something came out of somewhere that held company A's data. Headlines mix these two constantly
  • Are the fields stated. If there is a total and no field list, this is a stage at which nothing can be judged yet
  • Which of the four routes. Which one, or is it still undetermined
  • What is the first hand statement. A company notice, a regulator's announcement, a researcher's social post, an anonymous forum message. In this case the first hand statement is one researcher's post and the outlets are quoting it
  • Where did the number come from. Both 12TB and 13TB circulated here. If the explanation is that different mirrors measured differently, then every comparison and ranking built on that number wobbles too
  • Is there a company statement. Its absence is information. Reading no response as an admission is a different class of error
  • Has anything been checked against the original. Has anyone opened a sample, or are only file listings and screenshots going around
CheckWhat to look for in the pieceThe misreading when it is missing
SubjectIs the organisation where it happened namedA company that is related but not the stage hardens into the culprit
FieldsIs what came out written out field by fieldVolume gets misread as severity
RouteBreach, collection, third party or open configurationEverything collapses into hacking and the wrong response follows
First hand statementWho said it firstA quote of a quote is mistaken for verification
Source of figuresWho measured the scale, and howTwo different numbers get cited on the same authority
Official positionIs there confirmation, or is a request pendingSilence gets read as an admission

Public information changes character once it is gathered

It was public anyway, so there is no problem sounds intuitive, but the Korean statute does not draw the line that simply. Article 2 subparagraph 1 item (b) of the Personal Information Protection Act counts as personal information anything that cannot identify a specific individual on its own but can identify one when readily combined with other information, and it goes on to direct that whether combination is easy shall be judged by reasonably considering the time, cost and technology required to identify the person, including how obtainable that other information is. This is the text as amended on 14 March 2023 and in force from 15 September of that year.

The phrase worth dwelling on is how obtainable. It means the difficulty of combination sits inside the test, and difficulty is not a constant. Material that was scattered and awkward to join yesterday becomes a different proposition once it exists permanently online as one block. That is why a bulk archive matters. The nature of the individual fields did not change; the cost of joining them did.

  • A nickname alone, a signup date alone, an activity record alone may each be harmless. On the same row they are a different object
  • Using the same nickname across several services makes it easy to become the key to the join. Sometimes that matters more than any visibility setting
  • So the defence that this was public to begin with can be true as a factual statement and still fail to reach a conclusion
  • Equally, the existence of an archive does not by itself establish an offence. The judgment rests on the fields and the joinability

The line between pseudonymised and anonymised

The same statute carries two more concepts that are routinely confused. Article 2 subparagraph 1-2 defines pseudonymisation as processing personal information by deleting part of it or replacing part or all of it, in such a way that a specific individual cannot be identified without additional information. What comes out of that process is the pseudonymised information of Article 2 subparagraph 1 item (c). The important point is that pseudonymised information still sits inside the definition of personal information in subparagraph 1. Pseudonymisation is not a door out of the Act.

The exit is elsewhere. Article 58-2 sets out an exclusion, providing that the Act shall not apply to information that, when the time, cost and technology are reasonably considered, can no longer identify a specific individual even if other information is used. This is what people usually call anonymised information.

Set the two side by side and the point where practice goes wrong becomes visible. The test in Article 58-2 contains time, cost and technology. Anonymity is therefore not a property of the material but a judgment tied to the circumstances of the moment. As more material becomes available to join against, the same dataset can attract a different assessment.

  • Pseudonymised information is personal information. The safeguard obligations travel with it
  • Anonymised information falls outside the Act, but that determination only holds with a date attached
  • So we anonymised it, therefore it is safe is half a sentence until you say as of when
  • Bulk archives circulating permanently are precisely the sort of circumstance that changes the premise of that determination

What a user should actually check

Escalating the fear helps nobody. Even in a case like this one, where no evidence of personal data has surfaced, the items below are housekeeping you will have to do sooner or later. Using an incident as the prompt to finish them in one sitting is the efficient move.

  • Look at which fields on your account are public. Profile, activity history, friends list, purchases and library, wishlist. Defaults differ by service and generally lean toward public
  • Find every other service where you reuse the same password. This belongs at the top of the list, for the reason in the next section
  • Turn on two factor authentication. App based or a passkey where possible; where neither is offered, an SMS code still beats nothing
  • Check your recovery routes. Is the recovery email and phone number on file something you still use. The older the account, the more often that box is empty or stale
  • Clear out connected apps and tokens. An integration you used once years ago and forgot is very commonly still live
  • Turn on sign in alerts. New device notifications are the cheapest after the fact detection you can get
  • Run your email address through a public exposure lookup once. The procedure and the caveats are set out in the dark web exposure check guide
  • If you actually received a notification, follow the formal procedure rather than this list. The full order including reporting and remedy is in the data breach response guide

It is worth writing down what you do not need to do as well. Changing every password on every service right now usually fizzles out within days, and along the way it manufactures a family of near identical passwords. Starting with the ones you actually reuse is the version that gets finished.

Why password reuse is the real danger

OWASP defines credential stuffing as the automated injection of stolen username and password pairs into website login forms in order to fraudulently gain access to user accounts. It notes that while this is a subset of brute force, it is distinguished from guessing many passwords by using known breached pairs against other websites.

The part of this structure users cannot feel is the lag. A pair that escaped service A years ago surfaces today as an account takeover at service B. From the inside it looks as though B was breached, and B was never breached. So the search for a cause finds nothing, and the response is always late.

  • Break the reuse and the whole chain breaks. Cutting the structure is executable in a way that predicting individual incidents is not
  • A password manager makes reuse disappear on its own. If you will not use one, at minimum keep mail, banking and identity verification accounts on values that are all different
  • Multi factor authentication is the measure the OWASP page points to. Even a correct pair fails at the second factor
  • Tokens and API keys have the same property. The specific way credentials leak through a repository's remote URL is covered in where git credentials leak
  • You do not need to understand attack technique in detail to defend. What is needed here is one rule: never use the same value in two places

What a platform can do, and where each measure stops

There is no universal instrument on the operator's side either. Each measure blocks something specific and misses something specific, which makes it hard to claim a problem is handled because one of them is switched on.

MeasureWhat it stopsWhat it does not stop
Request rate limitingFast bulk collection from a single originDistributed origins, slow collection spread over months
Requiring authenticationAnonymous indiscriminate harvestingIf making an account suffices, the bar is low
Default visibility scopeReduces exposure itself. The largest effect of any measureReducing exposure collides head on with service metrics
Asset inventory and retirementForgotten systems being left standingInvisible work, so it keeps slipping down the priority list
Anomaly detection and loggingBulk access with a clear patternAccess that is indistinguishable from normal traffic
  • The lever with the biggest effect is not a technical block but the default. What was never public does not get collected
  • It is also the lever with the most resistance. Narrow the visibility and the service goes quiet and the numbers go down
  • Blocking collection always carries the problem of blocking legitimate use along with it. What that balance looks like in practice is covered in robots.txt and the reality of AI crawlers
  • Controlling connectivity and controlling permission are also frequently conflated. That distinction is set out in what network access control cannot stop

Summing up

Leak is not the name of an event. It is the name of an outcome. At least four roads arrive at that outcome, and where the road differs, so does the question of responsibility, the point at which a notification duty attaches, and the button a user should press. The headline does not separate the four.

What is settled in this case is broad agreement on what came out, and disagreement on how. The researcher's statement points to a publicly reachable endpoint rather than a hack; another source pointed toward third party servers. There is no confirmation from the company. In that state a sentence fixing the cause cannot be written, and should not be.

What is left for a user is short. Read past the terabyte figure in the headline and find the field list, then see whether any of those fields connects to your account. And quite separately from any of it, breaking a reused password and turning on two factor authentication are things you can do today, regardless of which incident prompted the thought.
