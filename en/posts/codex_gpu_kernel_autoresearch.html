How to Read a 232x Speedup - What an Automated Search Loop Actually Requires

The denominator sets the ratio, and the checker sets the loop

Aug 16, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: ask for the denominator before the ratio

Open any performance write-up and the headline carries a multiple. 232x, 40x, 3.5x. But a ratio is made of a numerator and a denominator, and only the numerator survives into the title. Strip out what it was measured against and the number has magnitude but no meaning.

This distinction has become sharper for a specific reason. Performance work is increasingly being reframed from a person having ideas into a loop producing candidates. You hand a coding agent a target metric and a checker, then let it run hundreds or thousands of rounds. Numbers produced this way tend to come out larger than hand-tuned numbers, and a substantial part of that gap comes not from the optimization itself but from the choice of denominator and the shape of the rulebook.

This piece is about that loop. What has to be in place for automated performance search to work at all, what breaks at each stage, and how far the method carries before it stops carrying.

The general procedure for breaking a published performance claim into checkable pieces is covered in AI Fixed More Bugs in a Month Than in Two Years - Taking the Announcement Apart Into Checkable Pieces, and measurement hygiene itself in Why Local LLMs Are Slow on a Mac - How to Check the GPU Is Actually Being Used. Neither is repeated here. The focus is the conditions under which the loop runs.

1. What is 232x measured against? The baseline makes the ratio

The working example is a personal record of optimizing a batched square QR decomposition kernel through automated search. Starting with the conditions the write-up states:

  • Task: implement a batched square compact-Householder QR factorization
  • Hardware: NVIDIA B200 on Modal
  • Inputs: batched square FP32 matrices, n = 32, 176, 352, 512, 1024, 2048, 4096, with 512 as the center of gravity
  • Ranking metric: among submissions that pass the correctness check, the geometric mean of runtime across several shapes and conditioning cases
  • Baseline: the torch.geqrf path at roughly 419,000µs
  • Final: 1,805µs. The 232x figure is the ratio of those two
  • Context: over 1,500 submissions across 14 days, placing 12th out of 183 participants

The last two lines are the interesting ones. The same write-up records a second denominator. An early tracked figure of 108,803µs against the final 1,805µs is described as a 98.34% reduction. Divide by that instead and the same artifact becomes roughly 60x. And placing 12th of 183 means eleven faster submissions existed on the same rulebook.

Three ratios come out of one piece of code. 232x, 60x, and something below 1 relative to the leader. What changed? Not a single line. Only the denominator.

1.1 Five kinds of baseline and what each one is telling you

Kind of baselineWhat a ratio against it actually tells youWhy it inflates easilyWhat to ask when reading

The denominator in this case belongs to the second row. The PyTorch documentation describes torch.geqrf as a low-level function for calling LAPACK geqrf directly. In other words the baseline is not an implementation prepared for the contest conditions of batched square matrices, a specific size set, and FP32 input. It is the general path. How a library call handles the batch dimension in a batched QR makes a large difference to that denominator.

The minimum format for quoting. Never move a ratio alone. Carry four things with it: what the baseline was, what hardware it ran on, what input sizes and distributions were used, and how it was timed. If even one of the four is missing from the source, that ratio is a number under unverified conditions. In this case the write-up covers the first three; measurement procedure such as warmup counts, repeat counts, and clock locking is not stated. Those items are collected in section 10.

2. The three conditions that make automated search work

Whether a problem admits a loop is decided by structure, not by domain. There are three conditions and all three are required.

2.1 Correctness has to be decidable by machine

Whether a candidate is right or wrong must be answerable without a human, by a single exit code. Without that, the loop does not know what to promote. Producing plausible code and producing correct code are entirely different problems, and the thing that separates them is the oracle.

What matters here is how strict the oracle is. A loose checker gets found out. An optimizer does not respect the specification; it optimizes the objective function. The boundary of what a machine can and cannot verify is laid out in What a Machine Can and Cannot Verify in an LLM Answer - Where a Verification Harness Draws the Line.

2.2 Measurement has to be reproducible

If the same code measured twice moves a lot, improvement is indistinguishable from noise. The loop then chases noise. A candidate that happened to measure well gets promoted, and the next generation takes it as a parent and branches in the wrong direction.

So there is one value you must measure before attaching any loop: the noise floor. Run the same benchmark ten times with nothing changed and record the spread. If the spread is 3% and a candidate reports a 2% gain, that is not a gain.

2.3 The search space has to be expressible and the cycle short

Candidates must be writable as code, and one round trip from generation to measurement has to be short enough. A 30-minute cycle gives you forty-eight rounds a day, which is not enough to be called search. Over 1,500 submissions in 14 days in this case implies a cycle measured in minutes or less.

  • The more continuous the space, the better. Axes that move in small steps such as tile size, unroll factor, and stage count let local gains accumulate
  • Candidates must be independently evaluable. One candidate must not contaminate the measurement of the next
  • Failure has to be cheap. A compile failure should resolve in seconds

3. Why GPU kernels satisfy these conditions unusually well

Numeric kernels satisfy all three near-ideally, which is why the success stories of automated search cluster in this field. Going through the reasons one by one also shows what goes missing when the method is moved elsewhere.

  • The answer is defined mathematically. For QR that means checking whether the original reconstructs as QR, whether Q transpose times Q is close to the identity, and whether Q transpose times the original is close to R. The grader described in the write-up rebuilt Q from the Householder representation and verified exactly those three
  • A reference implementation already exists. You compare against the library result, so ground truth does not have to be manufactured
  • The output is a numeric array. Comparison is one norm computation. Nobody has to eyeball whether it looks close
  • Failure is loud. Compile errors, illegal memory accesses, NaN. Most failure modes do not pass silently
  • The interface is narrow. Tensors in, tensors out. No global state and no side effects, so the verification scope closes around a single function
  • The unit of measurement is microseconds. A short cycle is what makes repetition viable
  • There is real headroom. Memory hierarchy, tiling, tensor cores, fusion: many axes to touch, each with a large effect on the result

That last item matters most. Search only means anything where headroom exists. Attach a loop to already well-tuned code and hundreds of rounds will buy a few percent. What actually happens inside a GPU when a kernel launches, and which axes at each stage govern performance, is covered in What Happens Inside a GPU When You Launch a CUDA Kernel - Deep Dive.

4. The shape of the loop: generate, compile, verify, measure, feed back

The skeleton is simple. What matters is which information crosses from each stage into the next.

# Objective:   minimize  geomean_runtime(cand)
# Constraint:  check(cand) == PASS

beam = top 3-5 current candidates       # keep several families, not one champion

while budget remains:
    parent     = pick one from beam
    hypothesis = generator(parent.src, parent.profile, failure_log)
    src        = realize the hypothesis as code

    ok, log = compile(src)
    if not ok:
        record(FAIL_COMPILE, log);   continue      # the cheapest failure

    ok, err = check(src)                           # A≈QR, QᵀQ≈I, QᵀA≈R
    if not ok:
        record(FAIL_CORRECT, err);   continue      # incorrect candidates die here

    t = benchmark(src)                             # warmup, then repeat, geometric mean

    if t < parent.t:
        promote(src, t); beam = update(beam)
    else:
        mark_negative(hypothesis.family)           # mark negative per idea family

    parent.profile = profile(src)                  # becomes input to the next hypothesis

The line most often dropped is the last one. If profiling results do not flow back into the generator, this is not search, it is a lottery. The next candidate has to be a function of the previous one before it earns the name. In this case, profiling access was placed inside the loop so that each candidate could be profiled and the results compared.

The second most important line is beam. Holding a single current best and improving it turns the process into single-point hill climbing, and one locally poor result kills an entire idea family. The author of the write-up looked back and noted that the beam should have been there from the start. It is the mechanism that keeps a locally negative result from prematurely killing useful ingredients.

5. What breaks at each stage

The loop fails in five different ways at five different stages. Because the failure modes differ in kind, the defenses have to differ too.

StageTypical failureHow it shows upDefense
Candidate generationEndless variations on one idea. Stuck in a local optimumGains converge toward zero while parameter values keep jigglingKeep a beam, tag idea families, consult a stronger advisor model, inject domain knowledge
CompilationNonexistent intrinsics, type mismatches, instructions the target generation does not supportFails immediately. The easiest failure to handleFeed the raw failure log into the next prompt. Pin the target architecture in the docs
Correctness checkQuietly shaving precision while staying inside tolerancePasses the check but is wrong only on badly conditioned inputsInclude pathological cases in the check set. Log the error margin as a continuous value, not just pass or fail
BenchmarkWarmup, cache, and clock drift make noise look like improvementRe-measuring flips the rankingSeparate warmup, repeat and take medians, re-measure under identical conditions, compare against the noise floor
FeedbackNext hypotheses built from text guessing with no profileGroundless hypotheses repeat and the same failure recursPut the profiler inside the loop. Accumulate failure logs per family
Objective definitionThe metric drifts from the actual purposeThe leaderboard climbs while the real workload does not get fasterMatch input sizes and distributions to real use. Make the metric a set, not one number

The last row showed up here in an interesting concrete form. The write-up notes that the faster kernels detected and exploited the input distributions. For n = 512 and n = 1024 the inputs arrived in several families such as dense, clustered, rank-deficient and mixed, and the quick kernels added detectors that routed each family down its own path.

Inside the rulebook this is legal and clever. But the reader has to take one thing from it. Move that kernel to your own data and the ratio does not follow. What was optimized is not the kernel; it is the pairing of kernel and grading inputs.

6. Benchmark traps: not counting noise as improvement

Automated search measures thousands of times, which makes measurement error far more expensive than it is for a person tuning by hand. One badly measured value becomes a parent and steers hundreds of descendants.

TrapWhat actually happensSymptomMinimum defense
No warmupKernel setup, allocation, and lazy initialization all land on the first callThe first value alone is large and the rest are stableExclude warmup iterations from the measurement
Fixed cache stateRepeating the same input measures only the hot-cache caseThe benchmark improves but real usage does notRotate inputs, invalidate caches, reproduce the real access pattern
Clock and power driftBoost and throttle make the same code report different numbersThe ranking depends on measurement orderLock clocks, or shuffle the order and repeat
Unsynchronized async callsMany CUDA calls are asynchronous, so the clock stops before the kernel doesImplausibly fast numbers appearTime with CUDA events, or synchronize before stopping a CPU clock
Skewed input distributionThe check inputs do not represent real usageA candidate that only fits that distribution winsUse several distributions and aggregate by geometric mean. Watch the worst case too
Precision loss read as speedDropping precision is counted as optimizationSpeed rises while the error margin quietly shrinksState the tolerance and log the actual error separately from pass or fail

The asynchronous item is one the vendor documentation warns about directly. The NVIDIA CUDA C++ Best Practices Guide points out that many CUDA API functions return control to the calling thread before their work completes, and that timing them accurately with a CPU timer requires synchronizing with cudaDeviceSynchronize(). It also describes the event API as providing timestamped records converted to milliseconds. The same document notes that synchronization points stall the pipeline and should be used sparingly, which means the timed region has to be designed narrowly.

The precision item ties directly to the contest rules here. According to the write-up, using low-bit representations internally was permitted, but the returned factors still had to satisfy FP32-style QR checks. In the end what decides how far precision can be dropped is not the kernel but the tolerance of the checker. A loose checker will always be found out by the search.

7. Where search stalls: local optima and domain knowledge

Even a healthy loop stops somewhere. The author records getting stuck in a local optimum in the range from 3,000µs down to 1,800µs. The symptom was endless hand-tuning of parameters and small variants of the same idea.

This is the most honest part of the methodology. The gaps the author identified between his own result and the top entries all have the same character.

  • Top solutions stripped out library functions more aggressively. Some entries implemented the triangular solve themselves rather than leaving it to the library
  • Keeping the intermediate matrix resident in a lower precision removes the cost of moving between representations, and that idea never entered the space
  • The newest instructions for driving that generation of tensor cores more deeply went unused
  • The beam was introduced too late

Three of the four are not the kind of thing search discovers. They are the kind of thing a person who knows the field supplies. A loop is strong at sweeping a given space and weak at widening it. So when attaching automated search, the practical bottleneck is not iteration count but how well the initial space was defined.

The practical implication. Before putting anything in a loop, write down the optimization axes for that problem. What can change, what range each axis spans, which combinations are physically impossible. That document is the search space, and an axis that is not in the document is invisible to the loop. Adding one more axis is usually cheaper than doubling the iteration budget.

8. Where it transfers and where it does not

Whether the method moves to another problem is decided almost entirely by two axes: is correctness mechanizable and is measurement reproducible.

Problem typeCorrectness decisionMeasurement reproducibilityFit for a search loop
Numeric kernels and math librariesMechanical (mathematical properties)HighVery high
Compression and serialization formatsMechanical (round-trip equality)HighHigh
Build and test pipeline timeMechanical (identical artifact, tests pass)Medium (shared runner noise)Medium
Query plans and index tuningPartial (result set must match)Medium (data and cache dependent)Medium
General application codeOnly as far as test coverage reachesLow to mediumLow
UI and interaction designNot mechanically decidable—Does not work
Architecture and design choicesNot mechanically decidable—Does not work

Everything in the lower half shares one property. The right answer cannot be written as a function. A good screen, a good design, an enjoyable game have no oracle separating pass from fail. Force a score onto them and the score becomes the target while the original meaning drains away. Optimize for click-through and screens that bait clicks win; optimize for line count and merely short code wins. How far automation carries when the goal cannot be written as a spec, and where it stops, is covered in Fun Cannot Be Written as a Spec - Which Game Development Stages AI Changes and Where It Stops.

The middle band has a known handling pattern. Carve out the decidable part for the loop and keep the rest with a human. In query tuning, for instance, result-set equality is checked by machine while the write-side cost of an added index is a human call. Splitting a large change into stages with gates and stop rules at each one is covered in Large-Scale Code Migration With AI Agents - Sharding, Gates, Stop Rules.

9. Attaching a search loop to your own codebase

Most attempts run the order backwards. The agent goes on first and the checker gets built later, at which point the loop optimizes the holes in the checker. The correct order is oracle, then measurement, then loop.

9.1 Checklist before you start

  1. Can you write the quantity to be minimized in one line, units included?
  2. Can the correctness decision run as a single command that splits cleanly into exit code 0 and 1?
  3. If that decision has a tolerance, have you written the number down?
  4. Does the check input set represent real-world distribution, including pathological and edge cases?
  5. Measured ten times, what is the spread on identical code? Without this you cannot judge improvement
  6. How many minutes is one cycle from generation to measurement?
  7. Is failure loud or quiet? If anything fails silently, make it loud before anything else
  8. Can profiling output be produced in a form the loop can read?
  9. Are there promotion criteria and stop criteria? Without a stopping rule you only burn budget
  10. Is there a ceiling on compute and cost?

9.2 The order to build it in

  • Step 1 — build the oracle first. Take the existing implementation as reference and write a script that answers only pass or fail. Then feed it a few deliberately wrong implementations and confirm it catches them. Skip that regression test and you have no basis for trusting the oracle
  • Step 2 — measure the noise floor. Ten measurements with nothing changed, spread recorded. Every improvement judgment afterwards happens above that number
  • Step 3 — shorten the cycle. Reorder so that failures surface early: compile, then correctness, then performance, with the cheap checks first
  • Step 4 — write the axes down. Which parameters can change, their ranges, and forbidden combinations. That is the search space
  • Step 5 — only now attach the loop. Start with a beam and feed profiling results back
  • Step 6 — re-measure promoted candidates under real conditions. Winning the benchmark and winning the workload are separate events

Budget is worth pinning down in advance too. The setup the author describes was two commercial coding model subscriptions plus GPU execution credits, with over 1,500 submissions across 14 days, and the write-up mentions the workspace running out of execution credits at one point. The bottleneck of a loop tends to be execution budget rather than ideas. The broader framing of reviewing machine output through a process rather than by hand is covered in Building a Verification Pipeline for AI Output - What Syntax Checks Never Catch.

10. What could not be confirmed

This kernel was not reproduced here. Every figure in the text is as stated in the source, and the following are not established by it.

  • The numeric tolerance of the correctness check. The three checked properties are stated; the threshold values are not
  • Measurement procedure details. Warmup counts, repeat counts, whether timing was event-based, and whether clocks were locked are not specified
  • Baseline implementation details. How the torch.geqrf path handled batching, and whether the baseline was measured under conditions identical to the final submission, is not established
  • The top entry and the ratio against it. The 12th place standing is given; the leading times are not
  • Per-size and per-conditioning figures. What is published is the geometric mean
  • Total actual cost. The tooling setup is described; the final spend is not

That is why every mention of 232x in this piece carries its conditions: roughly 419,000µs on the torch.geqrf path against 1,805µs, on NVIDIA B200, batched square FP32, geometric mean across several shapes and conditioning cases. A ratio stripped of its conditions is not a quotable number.

Conclusion: the checker comes before the loop

The protagonist of an automated performance optimization story is not the agent. It is the oracle and the timer. When those two are accurate and fast, almost any search layered on top produces meaningful results. When they are weak, the best search engine in the world optimizes noise and checker holes.

GPU kernels became the flagship case not because the field is special but because the answer is defined mathematically, failure is loud, and the cycle is short. If you are asking whether the method transfers to your problem, the answer lives in those three conditions rather than in the domain name.

And when reading numbers, always look at the denominator first. Watching 232x and 60x emerge from identical code makes it clear what the cheapest way to grow a ratio really is. Never assume someone else's multiple transfers to your environment — holding to that one rule removes most of the misunderstanding in performance conversations.
