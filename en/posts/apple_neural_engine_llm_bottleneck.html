LLM Bottlenecks Are Data Movement, Not Compute - What Reverse-Engineering Apple's Neural Engine Revealed

From M5 ANE-GPU Folding to 2,048 MAC Lanes and Serialized DMA Latency - A Deep Architectural Analysis

Sep 13, 2026 Dev & Infra

Across the competitive landscape of modern artificial intelligence hardware accelerators, semiconductor vendors almost universally headline their promotional disclosures with a single marquee specification: raw computational throughput measured in trillions of operations per second (TOPS). From mobile smartphones and ultraportable laptops to high-end engineering workstations, chipmakers routinely claim generational leaps by announcing that their dedicated Neural Processing Unit (NPU) delivers two, three, or even four times the peak arithmetic capability of its predecessor. The prevailing intuition presented to engineers and developers is deceptively simple: packing denser arrays of fixed-point or half-precision multiply-accumulate (MAC) units onto silicon will naturally translate into proportional throughput gains when executing state-of-the-art neural networks.

However, the moment practitioners deploy large language models (LLMs) based on generative transformer architectures onto real-world hardware, this linear intuition collapses entirely. Even on state-of-the-art silicon boasting tens of nominal NPU TOPS, executing multi-billion-parameter models during interactive generation often yields token generation velocities stalled in the single digits or barely scraping into modest double digits. Arithmetic processing units sit largely starved of operands, while overall hardware utilization remains abysmal. Resolving this performance contradiction requires looking past theoretical peak compute numbers and dissecting the physical pathways through which matrices and vectors traverse on-chip and off-chip memory hierarchies.

A rigorous reverse-engineering analysis published by systems software engineer Eileen Yoon illuminates the root causes of this structural disparity within Apple Silicon. By dismantling the internal architecture of the Apple Neural Engine (ANE) down to its register files, execution pipelines, direct memory access (DMA) engines, and custom datapath blocks, this research clarifies why an accelerator armed with 16 parallel compute cores and 2,048 multiply-accumulate lanes finds itself fundamentally constrained not by arithmetic limits, but by severe data movement bottlenecks. Furthermore, it reveals the compelling technical rationale behind Apple's strategic architectural pivot in the M5 generation, where standalone ANE hardware was folded directly into the GPU execution fabric.

This technical analysis is grounded directly in the reverse-engineering disclosures and micro-architectural benchmarks published by Eileen Yoon on August 10, 2026. The objective is not to disparage proprietary silicon engineering, but rather to examine the structural friction that inevitably arises when hardware assumptions committed to silicon during the 2017 Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) era confront the fundamentally distinct execution profiles of modern autoregressive transformers.

Why Apple Folded ANE into the GPU in M5 - The Turning Point for Standalone NPUs

Eileen Yoon begins the retrospective analysis by detailing why she paused development on the open-source Linux kernel driver for the Apple Neural Engine three years prior. The conclusion reached at the time was stark: "the ANE block is just not that useful." The underlying hardware architecture was excessively specialized and opinionated, preventing systems software developers from fashioning a flexible, general-purpose acceleration platform on top of it. Providing exposed hardware driver interfaces could not broaden the narrow domain of tensor operations the silicon could natively execute. Even within Apple's own desktop operating system, macOS rarely engaged the ANE for demanding interactive workloads, reserving the engine primarily for passive background tasks such as generating upsampled preview thumbnails in the Finder.

However, the release of the M5 processor family in 2025 marked a definitive watershed moment for this hardware paradigm. Apple prominently designated "LLM performance" as the headline marketing and engineering objective of the chip. Concurrently, silicon floorplans revealed that the standalone ANE block had been eliminated; instead, Apple "conveniently folded the ANE cores inside the GPU cores." As the author observed, this consolidation signaled "the beginning of the end for the standalone NPU."

Folding dedicated accelerator cores directly into the general-purpose execution units of a unified GPU is an architectural admission of profound consequence. It indicates that while the raw arithmetic density of the MAC units remains valuable for tensor contractions, the rigid, isolated dataflow and proprietary scheduling pipelines of the standalone NPU could no longer keep pace with transformer workloads. Reviving the reverse-engineering effort on the M1 ANE was undertaken not merely to catalog legacy silicon, but to map the internal compute, datapath, scheduling, and execution paradigms that governed Apple's silicon investments over nearly a decade.

  • Ecosystem dead-ends for rigid NPUs: The inability to accommodate generalized memory access patterns prevented standalone NPUs from serving as viable targets for evolving machine learning runtimes.
  • The M5 microarchitectural transition: Integrating tensor-oriented compute blocks into GPU shader clusters allowed Apple to target transformer execution via high-bandwidth unified memory pipelines.
  • Shifting silicon paradigms: The transition signals the obsolescence of fixed-function neural engines in favor of high-throughput parallel compute fabrics backed by flexible memory schedulers.

Silicon Legacy from the A11 Bionic - The 2017 CNN-Optimized Architecture

To evaluate the contemporary limitations of any integrated processor block, one must reconstruct the historical context and operating assumptions under which its microarchitecture was originally committed to silicon. The internal foundation of the Neural Engine found across contemporary M-series processors did not originate as a blank-slate design for desktop workstations. Its lineage traces directly back to the A11 Bionic system-on-chip introduced in 2017 for the iPhone X.

In 2017, consumer on-device machine learning was dominated almost exclusively by dense, vision-oriented Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). Mobile silicon architects were tasked with solving immediate edge inference problems: powering Face ID facial recognition subroutines, executing real-time spatial noise filtering on live camera sensor streams, and performing computational photography segmentation to separate foreground subjects from backgrounds. These convolutional operations exhibit a distinct mathematical pattern: a learned weight filter matrix is loaded once and subsequently applied repeatedly across millions of spatial pixels across sliding activation windows.

Under these operating realities, silicon architects in 2017 operated under the entirely valid assumption that moving model parameters from external DRAM into local core memories carried an acceptable one-time latency cost, provided those weights could be reused hundreds or thousands of times across predictable spatial coordinates. In power-constrained mobile environments with strict thermal budgets, maximizing predictable temporal and spatial weight reuse was the single most effective method for achieving high arithmetic density without draining battery reserves. The entire ANE cache topology and datapath routing were permanently hardwired around this fundamental assumption.

  • Architectural provenance: Originating as a dual-core mobile accelerator in the 2017 A11 Bionic, the core architecture scaled up into the 16-core configuration deployed in the M1 desktop processor.
  • Convolutional optimization targets: Designed specifically around dense tensor reductions characterized by intense, highly predictable spatial parameter reuse.
  • Thermal and power boundaries: Tailored to mobile form factors where minimizing external memory bus transactions was essential for maintaining battery longevity and preventing thermal throttling.

The Internal Fabric of 16 Compute Cores and 2,048 Parallel MAC Lanes

Examining the compute cluster reveals an impressive scale of spatial parallelism. The M1 ANE contains 16 identical parallel compute cores arranged symmetrically. Each individual compute core integrates 128 parallel multiply-accumulate (MAC) lanes for half-precision floating-point (FP16) operands. When operating under lower 8-bit integer precision (INT8), the effective datapath doubles to accommodate 256 INT8 lanes per core. Scaling this across the full die yields the total spatial compute capacity:

16 cores × 128 FP16 lanes/core = 2,048 parallel MAC lanes

Within every single clock cycle, this array executes 2,048 concurrent scalar multiply-accumulate reductions spatially. As Eileen Yoon observes, an isolated MAC lane possesses no semantic understanding of tensor shapes or matrix dimensions; it does not explicitly encode a 4-channel convolutional layer into silicon gates. Operands a and b are streamed into the execution unit, where a 16-bit multiplier routes results into a 32-bit adder, which continuously updates a local 32-bit accumulator register across recurrent execution cycles.

The defining efficiency feature of this datapath is that the running sum feedback loop resides entirely within the local accumulator register of the MAC lane. Partial sums are never written back to external memory or intermediate buffers between reduction cycles, eliminating off-chip bus energy expenditure during long dot-product accumulations.

Regarding precision handling, the ANE employs a fixed-point reduction scheme with FP16 readout. Multiplication operates at 16-bit precision, intermediate accumulations occur inside a 32-bit register formatted as signed Q16.16 fixed-point numbers, and results are converted back to FP16 via sign-extension. Empirical probing using custom CoreML microbenchmarks demonstrated that the accumulator clamps definitively at 2^15 (32,768), perfectly aligning with the mathematical saturation boundaries of a signed 32-bit fixed-point register with 16 fractional bits.

  • Localized accumulation loops: Retaining intermediate partial sums in dedicated 32-bit registers shields the memory bus from high-frequency accumulation traffic.
  • Fixed-point numerical stability: Utilizing Q16.16 fixed-point arithmetic for accumulation minimizes numerical drift before formatting final outputs into standard FP16 representations.

Workload Mismatch - Convolutional Reuse Patterns vs Autoregressive Transformer Decoding

The critical breakdown occurs when this array of 2,048 MAC lanes encounters the computational dynamics of autoregressive transformers. At an elemental mathematical level, computing a convolutional dot product and calculating the attention dot product between a query vector and a key vector involve identical scalar multiply-accumulate operations. The arithmetic execution units themselves are completely agnostic to model topology.

The failure stems from the dataflow surrounding the MAC units: determining when, where, and how operands enter, dwell, and depart. Generative language model execution consists of two distinct phases: prompt ingestion (prefill) and autoregressive token generation (decode). While prefill processes batches of tokens simultaneously, real-world user latency during conversational interaction is dictated almost entirely by the autoregressive decode phase.

During autoregressive decoding, exactly one new token vector is evaluated per generation step. To perform the necessary general matrix-vector multiplications (GEMV), the multi-gigabyte weight tensors representing the entire neural network must be streamed from main system DRAM across physical buses into the compute units. Once that single token is produced, calculating the subsequent token requires streaming the entire multi-gigabyte parameter matrix from DRAM all over again. Consequently, the foundational assumption upon which the 2017 silicon architecture was predicated—intense spatial and temporal weight reuse—evaporates completely during LLM decoding.

  • Collapse of weight reuse: While convolutional filters are reused across tens of thousands of spatial pixels, autoregressive decode features an effective weight reuse factor of exactly one per step.
  • Plummeting arithmetic intensity: With minimal arithmetic operations performed per byte fetched from DRAM, execution shifts from a compute-bound regime into an extreme memory bandwidth-bound regime.
  • Architectural frustration: Compute arrays spend the overwhelming majority of their clock cycles idle, waiting for the memory subsystem to feed model parameters across constrained buses.

Measured DMA Bandwidth - Disparities Across Kernel, Tile, and GPU Read Paths

This architectural mismatch is quantitatively validated by empirical microbenchmarks. Eileen Yoon constructed targeted performance tests measuring the precise throughput of the ANE's direct memory access (DMA) engines against the memory fetch bandwidth of the integrated GPU. CoreML execution durations divided by kernel and tile buffer sizes established ANE transfer rates, while Metal compute shaders executing buffer-read loops measured unified memory access performance on the GPU.

The empirical findings expose stark performance disparities. The ANE Kernel DMA engine—responsible for fetching model weights—achieved an effective bandwidth of just 37.99 GB/s. The Tile DMA engine—tasked with transferring input activation tensors from DRAM into the on-chip L2 cache—reached 59.08 GB/s. Meanwhile, the neighboring GPU achieved a sustained read bandwidth of 77.70 GB/s across the same unified memory architecture.

Comparing these baseline transfer metrics reveals that the ANE's parameter ingestion channel operates at less than half the bandwidth available to the GPU. Regardless of how many parallel MAC lanes are packed into the core, the physical conduit feeding parameters from DRAM into the arithmetic registers is structurally constrained.

  • Constrained parameter ingestion: The 37.99 GB/s ceiling of Kernel DMA places an immediate hardware limit on how rapidly multi-gigabyte transformer weights can reach the compute cores.
  • Sub-optimal tile streaming: Tile DMA operates at 59.08 GB/s, failing to fully saturate the physical memory controller available to the integrated system-on-chip.
  • GPU memory superiority: General-purpose GPU memory pipelines demonstrated vastly superior read performance over the dedicated neural engine across identical silicon dies.

The Core Bottleneck - Serialization of Independent DMA Channels Rather Than Peak Throughput

While the modest individual transfer speeds are problematic, the definitive architectural flaw uncovered by the reverse-engineering analysis lies in how the ANE orchestrates concurrent memory operations. Adding the empirical bandwidth of Kernel DMA (37.99 GB/s) and Tile DMA (59.08 GB/s) yields an aggregate theoretical demand of 97.07 GB/s (approximately 98 GB/s). This aggregate figure approaches, but remains comfortably below, the 100 GB/s system DRAM ceiling of the M3 test system.

Had the ANE memory controller been engineered to support concurrent, asynchronous DMA dispatch, the hardware could have overlapped weight streaming and activation streaming within that 98 GB/s envelope. However, when composite workloads requiring concurrent kernel and tile transfers were executed, the total elapsed execution duration matched the arithmetic sum of the individual channel runtimes exactly.

This empirical result proves that the ANE's DMA channels do not execute concurrently; they dispatch requests serially. Kernel DMA must fully complete its parameter transfer before Tile DMA is permitted to initiate its memory transactions. The author characterizes this structural limitation as leaving ANE DRAM throughput "doubly penalized." Not only are the individual memory channels slower than the GPU's memory pipelines, but their latencies are compounded sequentially rather than hidden through overlapping execution.

  • Sequential transaction dispatch: Independent memory channels execute in strict chronological sequence, preventing latency hiding across weight and activation transfers.
  • Compounded latency penalties: Sub-optimal physical channel bandwidth combines with serialized queuing to severely depress effective memory bus utilization.
  • DRAM starvation: While the SoC unified memory fabric can deliver 100 GB/s, internal ANE scheduling limitations leave available memory bandwidth severely underutilized.

Roofline Limits and Practical Engineering Implications for On-Device LLM Serving

The standard analytical framework for diagnosing computational ceilings in computer architecture is the Roofline Model. By plotting an algorithm's arithmetic intensity (FLOPs per byte transferred) against the intersection of peak processor compute and maximum sustainable memory bandwidth, the model delineates whether a workload is bound by arithmetic capacity or memory streaming limits.

The M1 ANE is specified to deliver 11 TOPS of compute across a system DRAM bandwidth baseline of 68 GB/s. When running autoregressive transformer decode—where billions of operations require streaming entire model parameters with minimal byte reuse—the operating point falls deep into the steep, bandwidth-limited sloped boundary on the far left of the roofline curve.

Under these constraints, the relationship between hardware improvements and token generation latency becomes strictly linear. As demonstrated in Yoon's calculations, attempting to scale autoregressive generation from 10 tokens per second to 25 tokens per second (a 2.5x performance increase) cannot be achieved by adding more MAC lanes or boosting clock frequencies. Because the system is anchored to the memory roofline, achieving that 2.5x throughput acceleration strictly necessitates a 2.5x expansion in sustained memory streaming bandwidth.

For systems engineers and software architects building on-device generative AI applications, these findings carry vital practical implications. Evaluating hardware accelerators solely by nominal "NPU TOPS" is an exercise in futility when deploying large language models. Without verifying the physical width of memory buses, the concurrency of DMA channels, and the sustained streaming bandwidth of the underlying memory fabric, deploying models to standalone NPUs frequently yields disappointing performance compared to optimized GPU backends (such as Metal Performance Shaders), which inherently offer superior streaming bandwidth.

  • The fallacy of peak TOPS: Impressive arithmetic ratings provide zero guarantee of inference responsiveness during memory-bound autoregressive decoding.
  • Linear bandwidth dependency: Generation throughput scales in direct proportion to memory bus bandwidth, rendering surplus compute cores redundant.
  • Pragmatic framework selection: When engineering local inference pipelines, flexible GPU backends that maximize unified memory throughput routinely outperform rigid NPU targets.

Control Pipeline and Datapath - From ControlDMA to Fused Activation Logic

Developing a comprehensive mental model of the ANE requires tracing the end-to-end execution flow from task ingestion down to the nonlinear activation stage. The operational lifecycle of an ANE workload proceeds through a strictly partitioned three-stage DMA pipeline.

First, ControlDMA executes, transferring task descriptors (TD) submitted by host software into internal configuration register files. Second, KernelDMA triggers, reading weight tensor W from main DRAM and storing it into the dedicated kernel memory (KMem) attached locally to each compute core. Third, TileDMA engages, reading input activation tensor X from DRAM and writing it into the shared on-chip L2 cache. The 16 compute cores then execute row-wise dot-product reductions across the MAC array, producing a completed output row vector Y.

A critical architectural detail uncovered during reverse engineering is that no physical data path exists between the shared L2 cache and core-local KMem. Parameters stored in KMem can only arrive via KernelDMA, while activations in L2 cache can only be populated by TileDMA. The silicon enforces a hard partition between model parameters and intermediate activation states, precluding dynamic on-chip cache sharing.

Following matrix reduction, post-processing is handled by dedicated activation units fused directly to the MAC lane outputs, bypassing intermediate memory round-trips. By inspecting compiled hardware register files (hwx) generated by the CoreML compiler for activation layers, Yoon discovered a contiguous array of 33 half-precision floating-point constants beginning at register offset 0x4288:

These 33 constants represent IEEE FP16 quantized evaluations of the tanh function across 32 uniform intervals. Rather than incorporating complex, multi-cycle transcendental arithmetic circuits into every core, the ANE evaluates nonlinear activations using a 33-entry piecewise-linear lookup table (LUT) with dedicated interpolation hardware. While this specialized circuitry delivered remarkable energy efficiency for 2017-era vision tasks, its clever optimizations cannot alleviate the upstream data starvation imposed by serialized DMA transfers.

  • Partitioned three-stage DMA: ControlDMA, KernelDMA, and TileDMA operate within segregated hardware domains with zero inter-buffer crossover paths.
  • Rigid buffer isolation: The absence of a physical interconnect between L2 cache and KMem prevents dynamic reallocation of on-chip memory.
  • 33-entry piecewise-linear LUT: Transcendental activations are accelerated via 33-point linear interpolation tables, reflecting an extreme dedication to fixed-function pipeline efficiency.

What could not be verified

Based on the documented scope of Eileen Yoon's technical retrospective and the inherent boundaries of user-space hardware register inspection, the following details could not be independently confirmed:

  • The precise microarchitectural layout of the M5 ANE-GPU fusion: While the M5 headline features confirm that ANE compute cores were integrated directly into GPU clusters, the exact register maps, shared cache topologies, and unified dispatch schedulers were not detailed in the source analysis.
  • Persistence of DMA serialization across intermediate chip generations (M3 and M4): Detailed register probing and hardware tracing were conducted specifically on the M1 processor; whether standalone ANE revisions in the M3 or M4 families implemented asynchronous DMA overlapping remains unconfirmed.
  • Empirical throughput behaviors under sub-4-bit weight quantization: Because benchmarking focused on native FP16 and INT8 execution, the precise impact of aggressive 2-bit or 3-bit weight quantization schemes on Kernel DMA bottlenecks was not evaluated.
  • Driver-level software queue overhead in alternative operating environments: All DMA measurements were captured using Apple's official CoreML toolchain and runtime drivers, leaving the exact proportion of software scheduling latency introduced by macOS driver stacks unquantified.
