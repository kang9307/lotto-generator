Reissuable and Permanent - Reading a Breach Item List in Layers

Sort the list by whether a value can be reissued and the order of what to do today appears on its own

2026-09-09 Law & Legal

Breach notices almost always present the exposed fields as a single line. Name, phone number, email address, date of birth, and then whatever records that particular service happens to accumulate. Written out in one row, every field carries the same visual weight. In practice they do not.

One test is enough to separate them. Can the value be reissued? A password can be replaced in under a minute, and the moment it is, the old value is worthless to anyone holding it. A phone number or an email address can also be replaced, at the cost of moving every account and every notification that depends on it. A date of birth cannot be replaced at all. And a record of which organisation somebody dealt with, on which date, about what, is not only unreplaceable but also implies things that were never written down in the list.

Once the list is split into layers, the work changes shape. In the upper layers the answer is replacement, and replacement finishes today. In the lower layers replacement is not on the menu, so the answer becomes reducing the number of places where that value is accepted as proof of identity. What follows sets out those layers, explains why the law singles out the lower ones, and pairs each layer with what can and cannot be done about it now.

This piece is general information drawn from a published operator notice and from the text of the Korean Personal Information Protection Act. The incident used as an example occurred in South Korea and the statutes cited are Korean; readers elsewhere should treat the legal sections as an illustration of how a legal system separates one layer of data from the rest, not as advice on their own jurisdiction. Whether a particular field carries a particular legal character in a specific incident, and what follows from that, is settled by the competent authority and the courts. Dates and figures below are as confirmed on 9 September 2026, and matters still under investigation are described only as being under investigation.

The occasion - what the notice states and what it does not

The occasion for this piece is a personal data breach notice published by the operator of a Korean beauty and medical information platform. It is issued over the chief executive signature and carries an apology, a timeline, the list of exposed fields and a lookup window. The table below separates what is legible in the notice text itself from what circulates outside it. The reason for grading sources is simple. The same sentence deserves different handling depending on where it came from.

Check the unit before the number

Numbers describing scale wobble at the level of units more often than at the level of magnitude. Some announcements count accounts and others count people; some deduplicate and others do not, which can leave the same incident described by two figures that differ by nearly a factor of two. This notice labels its figure affected customers and prints the total alongside a per-country breakdown. Adding the country values does not contradict the total. So the unit the notice uses is people, and the country split is consistent with the total; that is the extent of what the text itself establishes. Anything beyond it, such as how one person holding several accounts was counted, is not stated in the notice.

The fields the notice lists

The notice groups the exposed fields into categories. Because each individual sees the fields that apply to them through a separate lookup window, the list below is a union across everyone, not one person list. Missing that distinction leads people to assume a wider personal exposure than they have.

  • Name, contact details, date of birth and gender, country and region of residence
  • Social login identifier and internal identification number
  • Service usage information - device information, application version, connecting address
  • Consultation request fields - procedure title, practitioner name and clinic name, visitor name and contact
  • Consultation usage information - progress status, photographs attached to the consultation, entry path, preferred appointment time, creation and modification timestamps, stated motivation
  • Payment information - price and amount charged, method and timestamp, payer name and contact, order identifiers
  • Procedure information - items of interest, requested and actual entries, visit and procedure dates, practitioner identifier

Passwords are not on that list. That fact becomes the baseline when the layers are drawn below.

Sorting the fields into layers

Rearranging the same list by reissuability makes its character visible. The higher the layer, the easier the replacement; the lower the layer, the more completely replacement disappears as an option.

LayerValues involvedReplaceabilityCharacter once exposed
Layer 1 - immediate replacementPasswords, login sessionsThe holder changes them within minutes at no costThe old value loses its usefulness the instant it is changed. The exposure is cut off at a point in time
Layer 2 - replaceable at a costPhone number, email address, payment instrument numberReplaceable, but every linked account and notification has to move with itThe contact channel stays open until the change is complete. The longer the change takes, the wider the window
Layer 3 - fixed and unreplaceableName, date of birth, genderNo practical procedure for changing them existsRecovery is not a coherent idea here. The problem is less the value itself than the procedures that accept it as proof
Layer 4 - unreplaceable activity recordsWhich organisation was dealt with and when, what was asked about, when a payment happenedThese are events that already happened, so changing the value is not a meaningful phraseThis is the layer that lets facts outside the list be inferred. It is treated separately under combination below
Layer 5 - unreplaceable originalsFiles that are themselves the original, such as photographsThey cannot be changed, and once copied they cannot be recalledAn individual has essentially no means of reversing redistribution. What remains is after-the-fact response and takedown routes

The arrangement earns its keep because the kind of response differs by layer. Layers one and two are solved by changing a value, and that work concludes today. From layer three downward the value stays and the procedure has to be adjusted instead, account by account. Which is why an undifferentiated list of remedial steps reads as though everything is equally urgent, and an attempt to do everything usually ends in doing nothing.

What each layer allows and forbids

Pairing each layer with the step that is actually within reach, and with the place that step does not reach, produces an order of operations.

LayerWhat can be done nowWhat the step does not achieve
Layer 1Find every other service using the same password and give each a distinct value. If the sign-in history shows an unfamiliar device, sign every session outIt does nothing to recall values already exposed in other layers. Even where a notice list contains no password, cleaning up reuse blocks a route that other incidents open
Layer 2If the contact details cannot be changed straight away, enable caller identification and spam filtering, and check that the notification address on each account is the one still in use. For payment instruments, review the transaction history and the list of recurring chargesThe fact that the number and address are known does not change. Filtering blocks individual senders rather than removing the attempts themselves
Layer 3Find the accounts where a fixed value such as a date of birth is enough to complete identity verification and add a second factor. If a support desk verifies identity from a handful of fixed values, start with that accountThe fixed value itself cannot be recovered. A second factor closes the sign-in route and does nothing about a conversation designed to persuade a person directly
Layer 4There is no replacement here, so the response shifts toward recognising contact that pretends to know the record. The identification criteria section below is that part of the workAn individual has no way to erase a record that has already travelled. Requests to the operator for access, correction and deletion are a separate procedure
Layer 5If the same image appears elsewhere, use both the takedown channel of the hosting service and the infringement reporting channelCopies already made cannot all be recovered. A takedown request works only against material that has been located

If an order has to be named, layer one comes first. It costs the least, its effect is immediate, and unlike the others it finishes within the day. A free way to check whether account credentials are already circulating is covered in checking dark web exposure, and the mechanics of hardening sign-in are in two-factor authentication and password managers.

Why the law fences off the lower layers

The instinct to separate layers is written into the statute as well. Korean data protection law does not apply one uniform rule to all personal data; it fences off part of it and attaches narrower conditions.

Article 23 - restriction on processing sensitive information

Article 23(1) of the Personal Information Protection Act names ideology and belief, joining and leaving a trade union or political party, political opinion, health, sex life and similar information, together with any further personal data prescribed by Presidential Decree as likely to seriously intrude on privacy, and calls the resulting set sensitive information, which as a rule may not be processed. There are exactly two exceptions. One is consent obtained separately from consent to the processing of other personal data. The other is a statute that requires or permits the processing.

Article 23(2) provides that where such information is processed, the controller must take the security measures required under Article 29 so that it is not lost, stolen, leaked, forged, altered or damaged. Article 23(3) provides that where information disclosed in the course of providing goods or services would include sensitive information and carry a risk of privacy intrusion, the controller must inform the data subject, in an easily recognisable way and before provision, of the possibility of disclosure and of how to opt out of it.

The categories added by decree sit in Article 18 of the Enforcement Decree: genetic information obtained from genetic testing and the like; information constituting criminal history records; information generated through technical means about physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics for the purpose of identifying a specific individual; and information on race or ethnicity.

The structure of the provision explains why this layer stands apart. Other personal data may be covered by a bundled consent, while this layer demands a separate one. A consent gathered in a bundle is not a basis for processing it, and that requirement encodes a judgement that this layer affects the data subject differently. That said, whether a given field falls inside the category in a specific incident, and whether a lawful basis was in place, is a matter for the competent authority and the courts. The provision sets requirements; it does not settle outcomes.

Notification and reporting requirements

What an operator must do once a breach occurs is set by Article 34 and its Enforcement Decree. Sensitive information appears here as well.

ProvisionWhat it setsHow it reads in practice
Act Article 34(1)On becoming aware of a leak the controller must notify the data subject without delay. The matters to be notified are enumerated in numbered subparagraphsNotification is a statutory duty rather than an operator choice. Where the contact details are unknown and there is a justifiable reason, a substitute measure is permitted
Decree Article 39(1)Notification must be given in writing or an equivalent form within 72 hours. Where urgent measures such as blocking the access route or checking and patching a weakness are required, and where an unavoidable reason exists, notification may follow without delay once the reason is resolvedThe 72 hours is a deadline for the notice going out, not for the investigation finishing. The exceptions live in the same provision
Decree Article 39(2)Where the fields and circumstances are not yet established, the controller must first notify the fact of the leak, what has been established so far and the remaining matters, then notify further findings as soon as they are establishedA thin first notice is anticipated by the design. A second notice is expected to follow
Decree Article 39(3)Where contact details are unknown and a justifiable reason exists, the enumerated matters may be posted on the controller website for at least 30 days in place of individual notificationThe placement and the duration are inside the provision. This returns below under notice design
Decree Article 40(1)Three triggers for reporting: a leak affecting the personal data of 1,000 or more data subjects; a leak of sensitive information or unique identifying information; and a leak caused by unlawful external access to the processing system or to the devices used by those handling the dataThe middle trigger carries no headcount threshold. A small incident still qualifies once that layer is in the set
Decree Article 40(3)Designates the Korea Internet and Security Agency as the specialised institution receiving reportsThis is the basis for the reporting destination named in the operator notice

Subparagraph 2 of Decree Article 40(1) meets the theme of this article head on. It opens the reporting duty by layer rather than by headcount. Even where the scale falls short of the reporting threshold, the presence of that layer satisfies the requirement, and that design rests on the same judgement as the separate-consent rule in Article 23.

What changes on 11 September 2026

Article 34 was amended on 10 March 2026 and the amended provision takes effect on 11 September 2026. Three things change. First, the notification list grows from five items to seven; the new subparagraph 6 requires the notice itself to carry information about legal rights and how to exercise them, including damages claims, statutory damages claims and dispute mediation. Second, a new paragraph 2 requires that where the controller becomes aware of a possibility of a leak as prescribed by decree, it must inform all potentially affected data subjects of harm-minimisation information. Third, forgery, alteration and damage are folded into the defined term for a leak, and the wording of Article 23(2) is aligned on the same date. The reporting duty moves from paragraph 3 to paragraph 4.

What the combination produces

This is where intuition most often goes astray. Read one field at a time and almost everything looks harmless. Names are shared by many people, regions are broad, and plenty of other things happened on any given date. The difficulty arises because these values are not standing apart but sitting on the same row.

Structurally it works like this. Each field is an axis that narrows the pool of candidate people. With one axis a wide pool remains. With two, only the intersection survives. With three, the intersection contracts sharply. The contraction behaves like multiplication rather than addition.

Axis 1  who        name + date of birth + gender       -> many people still share these
Axis 2  where      organisation name + staff name      -> pool narrows to that organisation
Axis 3  when       dates and times of contact, payment -> very few candidates remain

With all three axes on one row, facts never written in the list start to
read as though they were, in the form of having been there.

The layers diverge here. Values in layers one and two do not form axes; a password narrows nobody, it merely opens a door. Values in layer four are axes in themselves. So layer four has a second property beyond being unreplaceable: it manufactures facts that are not on the list.

Knowing that structure changes two practical things. First, when reading a personal exposure list, count axes rather than fields. Second, when judging later contact, look at the kind of information the other party appears to hold. Contact that knows an axis sounds different from contact that does not. The wider question of how bulk data ends up outside an organisation in the first place is covered in four routes by which bulk data leaves.

Where a notice is placed

A notice has a location as well as a content, and the location is part of the notice. The same idea appears in Decree Article 39(3) above, which sets the placement and duration as the controller website for at least 30 days. The provision regulates where the text sits, not only what it says.

Placement matters for three reasons.

  • Domain - it is the only mechanical clue a reader has for authenticity. Anyone can copy the wording, but the operator domain cannot be copied. When the notice lives inside that domain, the reader no longer has to adjudicate the text
  • Certificate - the connection information behind the address bar attests that the address really is a server for that domain. Change the domain and the attestation points at something else
  • Permanence - a notice has to be in the same place thirty days later for anyone to check it. A board the operator keeps running and a page put up temporarily are different in this respect

What the record establishes for this case is the following. The document carrying the apology and the field list is hosted not on the operator official domain but on a domain supplied by an external website-building service. The lookup window for individual fields, by contrast, sits at a path under the operator official domain, and the confirmation button on the notice document reaches that path after passing once through a link-tracking short domain.

None of that is a statement about who did well or badly. It is a point about notice design. When the document and the window sit on different domains, the reader loses the ability to use the domain as the test that separates genuine from fake. Impersonation is built in exactly that gap, because once a genuine notice lives on an unfamiliar domain, an unfamiliar domain stops being a warning sign.

Telling a genuine notice apart

Which is why trying to tell them apart by wording rarely works. An impersonation copies the genuine notice, so the texts converge. The test is not the sentence but the route.

Follow the link you were sent  ->  a person has to judge whether the address is real
Type the official address      ->  there is nothing left to judge
  • Instead of tapping the link in the message, type the operator official address into the address bar or start from an application that is already installed
  • If the lookup window opens only after sign-in, then a screen asking for a name, a date of birth or a card number before sign-in is not that procedure
  • Where the notice document and the lookup window sit on different domains, the one worth memorising is the window domain. It is the window that serves as the starting point next time
  • An instruction to install a file or a remote-support program is not part of a breach lookup procedure
  • Noting the enquiry number and operating hours printed in the notice in advance settles any contact arriving from a different number on the spot

The window when contact attempts increase

The period after a breach becomes public and a lookup window opens has every condition impersonation needs. Many people know about the same incident, many are actively waiting for a message, and the shape of the procedure is published. This is not a reason for alarm; it is a reason to hold a test.

Type of contactThe usual shapeHow to test it
Compensation or discount offerOffers a benefit as an apology and steers toward a link or an application screenThe notice text in this case contains no sentence about a compensation procedure. Contact describing a procedure absent from the notice does not match the notice
Refund or payment error messageOpens with wording that appears to know the timing and the amount of a paymentSpecificity is not authenticity. Payment-related fields are on the list, so that much specificity can be manufactured. Verify inside the official application of whoever took the payment
Impersonation of an organisation or staff memberUses the name of an organisation and a staff member the person actually dealt with and carries on from thereKnowing a name is not a credential. Calling the published main number of that organisation back is the reliable check
Fake lookup windowOffers to check exposure and asks for personal data to be enteredThe window described in the notice sits inside the operator website and carries a 30-day limit. Any other lookup screen is not the published procedure

What to do now - what each step blocks and misses

Two things are deliberately absent. Paid monitoring products are best considered after establishing what the public measures above leave uncovered. And a resident registration number change is a separate scheme with its own requirements and evidentiary burden under Korean residence registration law; it does not follow automatically from the fact of a leak.

What has a deadline and what does not

A last division, this time of time itself. The period after a breach mixes two kinds of clock. One closes on a date and the other does not.

  • Closes - a lookup window opened by an operator carries a period. This notice sets it at 30 days from posting. Where an operator opens a separate claim procedure, that usually carries a period too
  • Does not close - statutory claims and mediation schemes remain independent of any operator window. Their requirements and time limits differ by scheme, and the provision requiring a notice to carry that guidance takes effect on 11 September 2026

Handling the shorter clock first is the sensible order. The lookup window takes a few taps but closes within days; the routes that remain stay open indefinitely but cost documents and time. The procedure and deadlines after receiving a notice, and the rights that survive regardless of any claim, are set out at provision level in when a breach notice arrives - the window that closes and the rights that do not, so they are not repeated here. The procedure for demanding access from an operator is in the right of access to personal data.

Whichever route is taken, the common requirement is a record. Keep the text of the notice received, the screen showing your individual fields in the lookup window, and the date and content of any contact that follows. Screens do not come back once they have gone.

This is a general description of a legal scheme and not legal advice on any specific matter. The current wording and effective dates of the provisions can be reviewed in the original text at the Korean national law information centre, and the exposed fields and the terms of the lookup window are governed by the operator notice. What legal character a given field carries in a specific matter, and what follows from it, is determined by the competent authority and the courts. Readers outside Korea should check the equivalent scheme in their own jurisdiction, which will differ in thresholds, deadlines and terminology.

What could not be confirmed

The following could not be grounded in primary sources at the time of writing. Values known through other routes are listed here when they are absent from the primary text.

  • Whether a notice with the same wording is also posted on the operator official domain. The site routes visitors to different domains depending on where they connect from, so the screen shown inside Korea could not be opened as such. What is established is that the lookup window address is a path under the official domain, taken from the link target of the notice
  • How the figure of 219,665 was derived. The notice labels the figure as affected customers and the country breakdown is consistent with the total, but there is no statement about how a person holding several accounts was counted
  • The wording of the individual notification. The notice states that affected customers were notified individually, but that message is not publicly viewable and its text could not be read
  • How many people each field in the aggregate list applies to. The list is a union, and the fields applying to an individual are visible only through the lookup window
  • The nature and cause of the vulnerability. The notice carries no description and the matter is under investigation, so it is not addressed here
  • The content of decree amendments aligned with the statute taking effect on 11 September 2026. The decree cited here is the version effective 20 August 2026, and decree wording matching the renumbered paragraphs of the amended statute could not be confirmed
  • Whether any compensation or comparable procedure exists. What is established is only that the notice text contains no such sentence; whether one is arranged later is unknown
