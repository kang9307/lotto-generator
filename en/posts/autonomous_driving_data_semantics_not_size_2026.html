This article draws on both the Korean and English versions of Sangmin Yoon’s personal blog post “The Real Problem with AV Data Isn't Size” (the page shows Oct 05, 2026). The post’s own sentences and figures are carried over, every value calculated here is labeled as a calculation, and the RAND report is cited separately under RAND as the source, apart from what the post says.

Trigger — The Original’s Starting Point That Size Is Not the Hardest Part

The first sentence of the post says the hardest part of working with autonomous driving data is not its size. The author writes that before working with the data directly, he used to be overwhelmed by the several terabytes a single car produces in a day. When you collect this much vehicle data, it is easy to get stuck thinking only about how to store and load it efficiently, and to be fair, the data really is huge. The post then moves on to why size is actually the easier problem.

The reason the post gives for size being easy is that the answer is already known. Object storage, compression and distributed processing tools are mature and easy to use, it says. So size is not a question of how do we do this but of how much will we spend. The post then states that the three problems below do not go away just because you spend more money, and takes up time, rarity and reproducibility in turn.

The post follows exactly that structure, taking its three problems in turn, and the figures it gives can be followed through arithmetic. One distinction matters. The post’s last sentence says the core of autonomous driving data engineering is less about managing the amount of data and more about preserving what the data means. Reading that as an independent fourth problem would diverge from the post. Preserving meaning is the author’s conclusion covering the three problems.

  • Author and publication: The blog credits Sangmin Yoon and the post carries the date Oct 05, 2026. GeekNews (hada) listed the same post on its October 5 list.
  • Affiliation: The post does not state the author’s affiliation. At its end it says only that he will share his thoughts without revealing any internal company information.
  • Announced follow-ups: The author writes that in the next posts he will share what has been done and what still needs to be done to deal with these problems. The post covered here is the first of them.
  • Layout of the page: The Korean and English versions sit on a single page together, and their figures and structure are the same.

Data Scale in Numbers — Six nuScenes Cameras and 1.1 TB per Hour

The example the post uses to show size is the nuScenes dataset, released by Motional in 2019. It uses six cameras per car, each at 1600x900 resolution and 12Hz. The post works out one uncompressed image as roughly 1600 × 900 × 3 bytes, which it gives as about 4.3MB, and says the six cameras together come to about 311MB per second and about 1.1TB per hour. The post states directly that these figures are cameras only, without lidar and radar.

The calculated rows in the table do not conflict with the scale the post gives. One convention is needed in the working, however. Decimal units are used here, with 1TB as one million MB. Binary units would change the values slightly, but the conclusion the post draws, that cameras alone come to a little over 1TB per hour, would not change. The post does not state its unit, so the post’s own value of about 1.1TB is the reference either way.

  • A feel for several TB a day: The post says one car produces several TB a day. At about 1.1TB per hour for cameras alone, 3TB is about 2.7 hours of camera output (3 ÷ 1.1, calculated). How many hours a day the sensors run is not in the post.
  • What the frame count means: 259,200 frames per hour counts the images six cameras make in an hour. Where this number touches the time problem comes up in the next section. For a single camera it is 4.32MB × 12 = 51.84MB per second, or about 186.6GB per hour (calculated), and six cameras are six times that.
  • Sensors left out: Per-sensor data volumes for lidar and radar are not in the post. All we can say is that the whole vehicle’s hourly figure may exceed 1.1TB.
  • Uncompressed basis: The calculation uses uncompressed images. Real storage size depends on the compression method, and the post does not discuss compression methods.

Problem 1: Time — Tying the Same Moment Together as One Event

The first problem the post names is time. In most data engineering, it says, there is a key you use to join different pieces of data. In web services it is user_id, and in log analysis it is request_id or trace_id. In autonomous driving, the most important key is time, because what the cameras, lidar and radar saw at the same moment has to be tied together as one event.

The post conveys the weight of this problem by contrast with web services. In a web service, a user’s events being a few seconds apart does not turn them into different users. In autonomous driving, even a few milliseconds can mean two sensors are looking at different worlds. According to the post, the problem is that sensors do not produce data on the same clock or at the same rate, and the more sensors you add, the more clocks you have. The post does not say which periods or synchronization methods or protocols the sensors use.

  • The post’s speed example: A car driving at 100km/h moves about 28m per second. A time gap of just 10ms means an object’s position is off by 28cm.
  • Check: 100km/h is 100,000m divided by 3,600 seconds, about 27.8m per second. Multiplied by 0.01 seconds that gives about 0.278m, or 27.8cm (calculated), matching the post’s 28cm.
  • Scaled down to 1 ms: At the same speed a 1ms gap is about 2.8cm (calculated). Conversely, a gap of about 36ms (1 ÷ 27.8, calculated) is enough to put a position 1m off.
  • Between camera frames: A 12Hz camera has about 83.3ms between frames (1 ÷ 12, calculated), and a car at 100km/h travels about 2.3m in that time (27.8 ÷ 12, calculated). This overlays the post’s speed example on nuScenes’ 12Hz .

The post’s conclusion is that for this reason the approach people usually reach for with databases, just join on the nearest timestamp, does not work. All that can be added is the arithmetic above. Even when records are paired by the nearest time, a gap of several to several tens of milliseconds can remain between the two, and as the calculation shows that corresponds to a position difference of centimeters to meters. How large a gap actually arises for each sensor is not in the post. For scale, 10ms is about 12% of the 83.3ms between two frames of a 12Hz camera (10 ÷ 83.3, calculated).

Problem 2: Rarity — Boring Data and Rare Incidents

The second problem is rarity. The post says most driving data is boring. What you really need are the rare incidents, and it lists sudden cut-ins, construction zones, jaywalkers and strange objects on the road as examples. And this kind of data, it says, is very rare. The post attaches no ratio to this. To give a sense of how rare, it links to a study.

The linked study is introduced as saying that because crashes and deaths are so rare relative to miles driven, proving an autonomous car is safe statistically would take tens to hundreds of years of driving. That is the one sentence the post itself writes. The author goes on to say that collecting more data is not everything, that finding the rare data well matters more, and that the competitive edge comes from there.

  • What the post wrote: It cites a study saying crashes and deaths are so rare relative to miles driven that a statistical proof of safety needs tens to hundreds of years of driving. The post gives no authors or year for the study, only a link.
  • The RAND report (in RAND’s words): The report behind the link, RAND RR-1478, is “Driving to Safety” by Nidhi Kalra and Susan M. Paddock, marked as published April 12, 2016. The RAND page says autonomous vehicles would have to be driven hundreds of millions of miles and sometimes hundreds of billions of miles to demonstrate their reliability in terms of fatalities and injuries.
  • RAND’s conversion to time: The same page says that under even aggressive testing assumptions, existing fleets would take tens and sometimes hundreds of years to drive these miles. The post’s “tens to hundreds of years” has the same thrust as this sentence. Still, the whole of RAND’s conclusion cannot be treated as the post’s own statement.
  • What RAND goes on to say: RAND writes that test-driving alone cannot provide sufficient evidence, so new methods of demonstrating safety are needed. The post does not carry this over, and what the author says is that finding rare data well is what matters.

The post’s statements and RAND’s differ in scope. The post says briefly, from a data engineering viewpoint, why rarity is a problem, while the RAND report works out how large a task it is to demonstrate safety statistically. What can be confirmed from the post is the author’s judgment that the ability to find rare events well is the competitive edge, and the fact that a study was cited as background.

Problem 3: Reproducibility — Driving Logs Used More Than Once

The third problem is reproducibility. The post says most logs have done their job once they are aggregated. Driving logs, by nature, get used again and again, and new metadata keeps getting added on top of the raw data. So at any time you need a clear answer to the question of which data and which label version a model was trained on. The post’s conclusion is that in autonomous driving, managing data versions matters just as much as managing the data itself.

The post’s text in this section is short and offers no concrete method. There is no description of which tools manage versions, how often labels change or where derived data is kept. What the post says about reproducibility has three layers: metadata keeps piling up on the raw data, you must be able to answer later which data and label versions trained a model, and so managing data versions matters as much as the data itself.

  • Contrast with ordinary logs: The post sets logs that end once aggregated against driving logs that get reused again and again. It does not say which kind is more numerous.
  • What accumulates: New metadata keeps being added on top of the raw data. The kinds and volume of that metadata are not in the post.
  • The question to be answered: You must be able to answer at any time which data and which label version a model was trained on. This question is the closest the post comes to a criterion for reproducibility.
  • What the post does not say: The concrete procedure for keeping things reproducible, a version numbering scheme and the pipeline layout are not in the post. The author only announces that he will take them up in the following posts.

The Conclusion That Ties the Three Together — Preserving Meaning, Not Managing Amount

In the post’s last paragraph the author writes that he came to think the core of autonomous driving data engineering is less about managing the amount of data and more about preserving what the data means. The sentence reads as a summary after the three problems above. The time problem connects to preserving the meaning of what was seen at the same moment, the rarity problem to the meaning of finding rare events, and the reproducibility problem to keeping the meaning of data and label versions. That link, though, is an overlay of the post’s sentences and not something the post itemizes.

Following the Original’s Numbers by Hand — Calculations and Limits

The post contains calculations in two places. One is the camera data volume and the other is displacement by speed. Working each out line by line shows what assumptions the post’s values stand on. Every number below is a calculated value.

  • One image: 1600×900 is 1,440,000 pixels. Multiplying by 3 bytes gives 4,320,000 bytes, or 4.32MB, which the post gives as about 4.3MB. The 3-byte assumption is what the post writes in its multiplication, and the post does not say what color representation it means.
  • Six cameras together: 4.32MB times 72 frames per second is 311.04MB per second, the same as the post’s about 311MB. This multiplication rests on the assumption that every camera always shoots at 12Hz.
  • Per hour: 311.04MB per second times 3,600 seconds is about 1.12TB. The post writes about 1.1TB. The two differ only by rounding.
  • Displacement: 100km/h is about 27.8m per second, so about 27.8cm in 10ms. The post writes this as 28cm. Precisely it is 27.78m per second, so the post’s 28m is a rounded figure. The calculation assumes the car moves at constant speed and does not deal with accelerating stretches or with the object itself moving.

What these two calculations show is simple. Size is a problem reached by multiplication, while time is a problem where the same multiplication appears as an error in position. The reason the post separates size as the easy problem and time as the hard one can be read from here. With size the value only grows larger, whereas with a time error the records of two sensors stop matching once the value differs. This interpretation is a paraphrase of the post’s sentences and not a contrast the post draws directly with formulas.

  • Synchronization protocol: The post does not name a specific protocol for aligning sensor time. Per-sensor periods and clock layout also do not appear other than the 12Hz of the nuScenes cameras.
  • Data volume of lidar and radar: The post’s 1.1TB per hour is a cameras-only value. We could not confirm per-sensor data volumes for lidar and radar.
