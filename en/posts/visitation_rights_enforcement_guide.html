Enforcing Child Visitation Rights in Korea

An agreement alone does not compel performance - the separate compliance order procedure and its limits

2026-07-27 Law
Legal disclaimer — This article is written for general information and is not legal advice. It describes the system of the Republic of Korea, based on the Korean Civil Act and the Family Litigation Act; family law in the country where you live is likely to differ substantially, so do not transplant these rules to another jurisdiction. Outcomes in visitation cases shift sharply with the age and condition of the child, the wording of the existing ruling, and the circumstances of both parents. Introduction: when a judgment exists and you still cannot see your child

Most Korean divorces end with a visitation schedule written down. The mediation protocol or the judgment carries a phrase such as the second and fourth Saturday of every month. Then the day arrives, and nobody answers the phone, or the child is said to be ill again, or no one shows up at the agreed place. At this point many people think I have a judgment, so it will work itself out, and they wait. Months pass.

Here is the fact that changes everything. A document about visitation does not execute itself. The approach that works for money, attaching a bank account, does not apply here, and direct enforcement in the sense of physically collecting the child is not permitted either. Instead the law provides a separate procedure called a compliance order. Until you file for it, nothing happens at all.

This article sets out what you can actually do when visitation is not honoured, article by article. It covers the requirements and the procedure for a compliance order, the ceiling on the fine imposed for a breach, the frequently misunderstood reason detention does not apply to visitation, the principle that child support and visitation cannot be traded against each other, and how to build a record that proves non-compliance.

A principle worth stating first — Visitation is not a card parents play to beat each other. The Civil Act defines it not as a right belonging to a parent alone but as a right held mutually by the child and the parent. Every procedure below exists to help the child keep a stable relationship with both parents, and the yardstick the court applies from start to finish is the welfare of the child. Once sanctioning the other parent becomes the goal, you may win the procedure and still lose the relationship with your child.

1. Whose right is visitation

Before discussing remedies, the nature of the right has to be pinned down. Misreading this point sends the whole procedure off course.

1.1 It is not a right of the parent alone

Article 837-2(1) of the Civil Act reads as follows. A parent who does not directly raise the child and the child have the right to visit each other mutually. The pivotal word is mutually. The amendment of 21 December 2007 made the child a holder of the right as well. Before that, the provision framed it purely as the right of the non-custodial parent.

That difference carries real weight in practice. Blocking visitation does not merely infringe the right of the other parent; it infringes the right of the child at the same time. Conversely, a non-custodial parent who fails to appear without justifiable cause is likewise abandoning a right that belongs to the child. The duty of visitation, in other words, does not rest on one side only.

1.2 Grandparents may petition too

Article 837-2(2) of the Civil Act, newly inserted on 2 December 2016 and in force from 3 June 2017, grants a right of petition to the lineal ascendants of the non-custodial parent, that is grandparents and the like. It is not unconditional, however. The petition may be brought before the family court where the parent who does not directly raise the child is unable to exercise visitation because of death, illness, residence abroad, or another unavoidable circumstance. In deciding, the court must take into account the wishes of the child, the relationship between the petitioner and the child, the motive for the petition, and other circumstances.

1.3 The test is always the welfare of the child

Paragraph 3 of the same article provides that the family court may restrict, exclude, or modify visitation on the application of a party or on its own authority where necessary for the welfare of the child. Visitation is therefore not an absolute right but one adjusted against the higher standard of child welfare. Whether you are pursuing enforcement or seeking a restriction, the language that persuades a Korean court ultimately has to be what effect does this have on the child.

2. An agreement alone does not compel performance

This is the most important part of the article, and the point at which most people lose time.

2.1 The document is only a starting point

If a mediation protocol, a judgment, or a ruling specifies a visitation schedule, that means the legal basis is in place. Having a basis and being enforced automatically are two entirely different things. What you can do with that document when the other parent ignores it is apply to the family court for a compliance order. Without an application, the court has no way of knowing the dispute exists.

Where no document exists at all — an oral understanding, say, or a consensual divorce completed without fixing visitation — there is nothing for a compliance order to attach to. In that situation you must first petition for a ruling on visitation and have the schedule fixed. The sequence cannot be skipped.

2.2 Direct enforcement is not permitted

When money is owed, attachment and compulsory execution are available. Visitation admits no such direct enforcement, because a child cannot be made the object of execution like a piece of property. Picture a court officer physically removing a child and the reason becomes obvious: the scene itself leaves a deep wound on the child.

Korean law therefore chose to induce performance through indirect pressure. The court orders performance, and if the order is still ignored it imposes a monetary sanction. That is the relationship between a compliance order and a fine.

4. Filing for a compliance order in practice

4.1 The governing provision and its scope

Article 64(1) of the Family Litigation Act provides that where a person obliged to perform under a judgment, ruling, mediation protocol, decision in lieu of mediation, or a child support obligation record fails to perform without justifiable cause, the family court may, on the application of a party, order performance within a fixed period. Three duties fall within its scope.

  • Subparagraph 1 — property obligations such as the payment of money (child support being the classic case)
  • Subparagraph 2 — the duty to hand over an infant
  • Subparagraph 3 — the duty to allow visitation with a child

Visitation appears expressly in subparagraph 3. It is the formal route the legislature intended, so there is no reason to hesitate about using it.

4.2 How the procedure unfolds

  1. File the application — Submit the application for a compliance order to the family court with jurisdiction over the address of the other parent. Attach a copy of the existing mediation protocol or judgment together with a summary of the failures to comply.
  2. Service — The court serves the application on the other parent.
  3. Examination — Article 64(2) of the Family Litigation Act requires the court, absent special circumstances, to examine the parties in advance and recommend performance before issuing the order. It is not unusual for the matter to settle at this hearing with a promise to comply.
  4. Notice of sanctions — The same paragraph requires the court to give advance notice of the sanctions prescribed in Article 67(1) and Article 68. This step makes clear to the other parent exactly what a breach will trigger.
  5. Issue of the compliance order — The court fixes a period and orders that visitation be allowed.
  6. Apply for a fine on breach — If the order is still ignored, apply for the imposition of a fine.

Costs amount to stamp duty for the case type plus service fees, and are not large. For exact amounts and the correct forms, the family affairs division of the competent court or the electronic civil petition centre of the Supreme Court of Korea is the most reliable source.

4.3 What counts as justifiable cause

The provision keys on failing to perform without justifiable cause, so once the other parent asserts a reason the court assesses it. The usual division runs roughly as follows.

  • Potentially accepted as justifiable — sudden illness or hospitalisation of the child, an unavoidable clash with a school event, or the non-custodial parent being intoxicated or otherwise presenting a danger to the child
  • Unlikely to be accepted — the personal schedule of the custodial parent, awkwardness within a remarried household, dissatisfaction over unpaid child support, resentment toward the other parent, or a repeated vague claim that the child does not want to go

What matters is whether the incident is isolated or repeated. A single change of circumstances is a scheduling problem, but when the same reason recurs the court reads it as avoidance. That is precisely why the record matters.

5. Fines and detention - what is available and what is not

5.1 The fine is capped at 10 million won

Article 67(1) of the Family Litigation Act provides that where a party or a related person breaches an order under Article 64 without justifiable cause, the family court, the conciliation committee, or the judge in charge of conciliation may impose a fine of up to 10 million won by decision, ex officio or on the application of the entitled party.

One caveat deserves attention. Ten million won is a ceiling, not a standard amount. The sum actually imposed is set with regard to the number and gravity of the breaches and the surrounding circumstances, and in ordinary cases it lands far below the cap. The fine is also money paid to the State, so the applicant receives nothing. The practical function of the fine is therefore closer to psychological pressure and an official record than to financial recovery.

5.2 Detention does not apply to visitation

This is the most frequently misunderstood point. Article 68(1) of the Family Litigation Act limits detention to three situations.

  • Subparagraph 1 — a person ordered to pay money periodically who fails to perform the duty for three or more instalments without justifiable cause
  • Subparagraph 2 — a person ordered to hand over an infant who, after being sanctioned under Article 67(1), fails to comply within 30 days without justifiable cause
  • Subparagraph 3 — a person subject to an order to pay child support as a lump sum who fails to comply within 30 days without justifiable cause

None of the three includes the duty to allow visitation. Detention is a powerful sanction that confines a person for up to 30 days until the duty is performed, but confining the custodial parent, who is usually the one raising the child, removes the very person who cares for the child. A system built to protect child welfare would end up inflicting greater harm on the child. The legislature accordingly left visitation out of the detention provisions.

5.3 So what is left

The unavailability of detention does not leave you without options. The choices that follow in practice are these.

  • Repeat applications for a fine — where non-compliance continues, you may apply again each time it happens. The accumulated record itself becomes the basis for later decisions.
  • Petition to modify the visitation terms — Article 837-2(3) of the Civil Act permits modification as well. Moving the handover point to a neutral institution, spelling out the handover mechanics, or adding video calls reshapes the arrangement into something easier to comply with, which often removes the source of the conflict.
  • Petition to change the arrangements for upbringing — Article 837(5) of the Civil Act provides that where the family court finds it necessary for the welfare of the child, it may, on the petition of the father, mother, child, or public prosecutor, or on its own authority, change the arrangements concerning upbringing or make any other appropriate disposition. Persistent and deliberate obstruction of visitation can be a circumstance that casts doubt on fitness as a custodial parent. Bear in mind, though, that changing the custodial parent overturns the entire living environment of the child, so courts weigh it with great caution.

6. Child support and visitation cannot be traded

This is the most common and the most costly misconception on the ground. Responses of the type no child support, no access to the child or no access, no child support have no legal footing whatsoever.

6.1 The two duties are not conditions of each other

Child support is the cost of the child living day to day, and visitation is the right of the child to maintain a relationship with both parents. Both exist for the child, not as terms of a bargain between the parents. Failure to perform one does not create a right to refuse the other; they are not in a relationship of concurrent performance.

6.2 Retaliation hurts both sides in the end

  • If you cut off child support — you become subject to a compliance order, and failing to pay three or more periodic instalments opens the way to detention. Various further sanctions under the child support enforcement scheme follow as well.
  • If you block visitation — you become subject to a compliance order and a fine of up to 10 million won, and repetition can be raised as a ground for changing the arrangements for upbringing.
  • For both parents — because the court decides by reference to the welfare of the child, any indication that a child was used as a bargaining chip counts against whichever parent did it.
  • And for the child — caught between two entrenched parents, the child comes to feel that they are the cause. That is the largest loss of all.

The answer is simple. Each problem is solved through its own procedure. Child support goes through the child support enforcement route, visitation through a visitation compliance order. It looks like more work, but it is the fastest path and the least harmful one for the child.

7. Building a record that proves non-compliance

What decides an application for a compliance order is how calmly you can demonstrate that non-compliance was repeated. A dry record is far more powerful than an emotional appeal.

A rule for record keeping — Do not question the child about the other parent or ask the child to testify. The moment a child is pulled into a dispute between parents, you may gain material to file with the court but the child carries the heavy burden of a loyalty conflict. Korean courts look closely at exactly this kind of conduct. Build the record among the adults only.

8. When the child refuses to meet

This is the hardest situation of all: not the custodial parent blocking contact, but the child saying no.

  • The wishes of the child must be respected — since the Civil Act also frames visitation as a right of the child, forcing a meeting the child does not want runs against the purpose of the system. The older and more mature the child, the more weight the stated wish carries.
  • But the background is examined too — in practice the court tries to distinguish whether the refusal is genuinely the wish of the child, whether negative remarks by the custodial parent shaped it, or whether it stems from a bad experience in the past. Where necessary the court verifies the true intent through an investigation by a family court investigator.
  • What the non-custodial parent should do — rebuild the relationship rather than apply pressure. Starting with short meetings, using a neutral venue or a contact centre, or resuming with low-intensity contact such as phone and video calls is realistic. Petitioning to modify the terms so that the arrangement becomes sustainable is also an option.
  • What the custodial parent should do — simply relaying over and over that the child refuses is unlikely to be accepted as justifiable cause. Keeping a record of cooperation aimed at making the meetings actually happen works in your own favour as well.

9. When visitation is restricted or excluded

Not every visitation arrangement should be enforced. Under Article 837-2(3) of the Civil Act the court may restrict, exclude, or modify it where necessary for the welfare of the child. The following circumstances are the ones typically weighed.

  • a history of abuse or violence against the child
  • the child repeatedly suffering serious emotional harm during the visits
  • a concrete risk that the child will be taken away or that contact will be severed
  • the condition of the non-custodial parent posing a threat to the safety of the child
  • continued conduct that damages relationships, such as instilling hostility toward the other parent in the child

That said, exclusion is a last resort. In practice, rather than shutting contact down completely, courts more often adjust the conditions using supervision by a third party, meetings at a designated place, shortened sessions, or substitute forms such as video calls and letters. Because a further petition to modify may be brought when circumstances change, a restriction is not permanent either.

10. If litigation is under way - provisional dispositions

Where a divorce action or a custody determination case is pending and you cannot see the child at all in the meantime, waiting for the final outcome is untenable. The tool for this situation is the provisional disposition under Article 62 of the Family Litigation Act.

That article provides that where an action, a petition for a ruling, or an application for mediation in a family case has been brought, the family court may, on its own authority or on the application of a party, take dispositions it deems appropriate, including dispositions for the care and upbringing of the persons concerned, when it finds them particularly necessary to resolve the case. In practice this is the basis on which provisional dispositions fixing interim visitation during litigation are made. The longer the trial runs, the longer the gap with the child, so think of it as a device that prevents the relationship from being severed while the dispute is fought out.

11. Frequently asked questions

11.1 Will applying for a compliance order make the relationship worse

That is a fair worry. It is worth setting beside it, however, the point that leaving non-compliance unaddressed does not repair the relationship either. If anything, the longer the gap grows, the harder it becomes to reconnect with the child. It is also worth noting that Article 64(2) makes examination and a recommendation to perform the default. The procedure is designed to draw out performance, not to punish.

11.2 What if the other parent has moved far away

A change in distance is a classic reason the existing schedule becomes hard to keep. Here it is more effective to petition to modify the terms first rather than press for enforcement. Adjusting the frequency and duration to reflect travel time and cost, and increasing longer visits during school holidays, is a workable redesign.

11.3 I have remarried - must visitation continue

The remarriage of the custodial parent is not in itself a justifiable cause for refusing visitation, because the relationship between a child and a biological parent is separate from the formation of a new household. If the child is struggling to adjust, however, the appropriate approach is to use that fact as a basis for discussing adjustments to the schedule and the format.

11.4 What if the non-custodial parent keeps breaking promises

The duty of visitation does not fall on the custodial parent alone. Where the non-custodial parent repeatedly fails to appear at the agreed times so that the child waits and is disappointed, that too infringes the right of the child. The custodial side may petition to modify the visitation terms to make the schedule realistic, or seek a restriction if that is what the situation requires.

Conclusion: follow the procedure without losing sight of the purpose

Two things stand out. First, visitation is not enforced automatically by an agreement or a judgment. The court steps in only once you apply for a compliance order under Article 64(1)3 of the Family Litigation Act. A breach can draw a fine of up to 10 million won under Article 67(1). Second, detention does not apply to visitation. The grounds for detention in Article 68(1) are limited to periodic payments of money, handover of an infant, and lump-sum child support, because confining the custodial parent removes the person who cares for the child. What remains instead is repeat applications for a fine, modification of the visitation terms, and a petition under Article 837(5) of the Civil Act to change the arrangements for upbringing.

One more thing. Child support and visitation are not cards to be swapped. They are independent duties that must be handled through their own procedures, and retaliation merely exposes both parents to sanctions.

Finally, a word about direction. Article 837-2(1) of the Civil Act, the source of every procedure above, defines visitation as a right held mutually by parent and child. The purpose of the procedure is not to defeat the other parent but to help the child keep a relationship with both of them. When you draft the application and when you stand at the examination hearing, setting your test as what effect does this have on the child makes the court easier to persuade and, more importantly, changes what is left standing once the procedure ends.
