Introduction: how much do you believe a report of zero violations?

You ran the checker. It reported zero violations. Before that number reassures you, there is one question worth asking. Does this zero mean the subject was clean, or that the checker looked at nothing? Both cases print the same zero.

Code that inspects other code has a strange property. Graders, tests, linters, static analyzers, and review scripts all belong to this family. When this kind of code breaks, it does not throw an exception or blank a screen. It simply passes more things. And nobody opens a pass.

A neat illustration of the problem was published recently. Someone who had built an automated grader for order-handling answers took the same twenty-nine answers, handed them to a commercial LLM along with the rubric, and had each side score them independently. Twenty-seven verdicts matched. The two that split were the interesting part, and on inspection the wrong party was not the model but the grader its author had written.

What this article is about is not that incident but the question behind it. What happens when the inspecting side is wrong, how do you notice, and when may you believe an automated grade? The same story applies to anyone who treats tests, linters, and review scripts as grounds for confidence.

1. Failures are loud and false passes are silent

A checker can err in two directions: calling a problem where none exists, and missing a problem that does. The two errors arise symmetrically, but the probability of discovering them is not symmetric at all.

A false positive lands in front of a person immediately. The build turns red, the deploy halts, and somebody opens the offending line. Usually it is identified within minutes. A miss, by contrast, leaves no trace. Not one extra line in a log, and the dashboard numbers may even look better. The only route to discovering a miss is tracing backwards after the problem surfaces some other way, and by then months of decisions have accumulated on top of that checker.

This asymmetry produces several practical consequences.

  • All pressure on a checker runs toward reducing false positives. Nobody ever asks you to increase misses, but requests to quiet down the noise arrive weekly
  • Add one more condition to kill a false positive and the real violations that condition excludes disappear along with it. That loss never appears on screen
  • Judging checker quality by how much it catches inverts the incentive. A checker that catches nothing is the quietest and the best liked
  • A single rule with chronic false positives trains people to ignore the whole list and hides real violations for a long time

The last item is especially annoying. On this site's own review tooling, one rule kept flagging the same item on one language edition, and after people got in the habit of skipping that alert they went nearly two months without seeing two genuine defects mixed in beneath it. The checker worked correctly. The defect was that people had stopped trusting it.

2. Four shapes a checker takes when it is wrong

Sorting checker defects into types decides where to look first. In practice you meet roughly four.

TypeSymptomWhy it stays invisibleHow to catch it
The rule is narrowOther valid shapes get counted as violations or missed entirelyOnly the shapes the author knew about made it into the ruleCompare against a second judge of a different kind
The scope is narrowSubjects drop out entirely and you get zeroThe subject count is absent from the outputAlways print how many subjects were inspected
It only looks at shapeA matching name, tag, or extension is enough to passA pass leaves behind no evidenceSeed positive fixtures and confirm detection
The rubric is wrongThe checker runs perfectly but the criterion is the errorEvery judge shares the same rubricHave a human label a small sample directly

The first three you can catch with tooling. Only the fourth resists it. If the rubric is wrong, the rule-based checker and the model judge produce the same answer, and the agreement rate comes out looking beautiful.

3. A narrow rule reports absent for something present

This is the most common type. When people write rules, they write the shapes they have seen. The trouble is that several shapes are valid and only one of them makes it into the rule.

One of the two splits in the grading write-up above had exactly this shape. The answer had not left an item unhandled; it had asked back about the size, requesting confirmation. The grading code only checked mechanically whether that item code appeared in the confirmed list. It was not in the list, so it counted as missing. A human reading it sees an obviously appropriate response, and the model, which read the whole document, judged it that way too.

The structure repeats regardless of domain.

  • A parser that knows one date format starts reporting no data the day the notation changes
  • A regex that assumes a word order floods one language with false positives and misses another language wholesale
  • A rule that demands a negation word misses every sentence carrying the same meaning without one
  • Pass conditions pinned to exact string equality mark synonyms and abbreviations as failures

The second and third are types actually encountered in this site's multilingual review work. Measure the scale of a problem with an improvised regex and you will be wrong in a different direction per language, and running a bulk edit off that number inserts the same content a second time into documents that were already fine.

Below is an illustration of the idea. It is pseudocode whose behavior has not been verified and is not meant to drop into a real grader.

# Narrow rule - only checks membership in the confirmed list
def is_handled_narrow(answer, item_code):
    return item_code in answer.confirmed_codes

# Widened rule - a clarification request counts as handled
def is_handled(answer, item_code):
    if item_code in answer.confirmed_codes:
        return True
    if answer.has_clarification_request(item_code):
        return True          # asking back is not the same as ignoring
    return False

What matters is not the code but the ordering. Before you can widen a rule you have to learn that it needs widening. A narrow rule never announces its own narrowness.

4. Whatever is outside the scope does not exist

Even a correct rule yields the same result if the input it applies to never arrives. The checker's input boundary is the definition of its blind spot.

The second divergence in the same write-up was this type. The person answering had written the clarification in the margin rather than the designated field, and the code grader was written to read only designated fields, so it did not know the note existed. The model read the document whole and saw it. Not a rule problem but a scope problem.

The places this type hides in practice are fairly fixed.

  • The pattern that builds the file list quietly omits hidden files or a particular extension
  • A check that reads only the last N lines of a log cannot see the event that preceded them
  • A read fails on a differently encoded file, the exception is swallowed, and the subject count drops to zero
  • A filter upstream has already removed most items, so only a handful reach the checking stage

The remedy is simple, and as simple things go it is poorly observed. Always print the number of subjects inspected. Zero violations and zero subjects are completely different events, and if the output is identical there is no way to tell them apart. Add a warning when the subject count differs sharply from yesterday and you catch most silent truncation.

5. Checks that pass on the name alone

The third type confuses shape with meaning. If the class name is there, the style must be applied; if the schema key is there, the value must be valid; if the tag is there, the content must be right. This is mistaking an existence check for a content check.

Two types from this site's review tooling fall here. One was a check that only confirmed whether links existed. Every link was alive, so the results stayed clean for a long time, while the actual problem was that English documents were pointing at Korean pages. It only surfaced, in bulk, once a separate rule was added to check that the languages matched.

The other was a rule that located markup by string matching. One attribute got added to a tag, the pattern stopped matching, and rather than erroring the checker quietly passed. Zero violations kept printing. A rule that cannot find what is there is worse than no rule at all. With no rule, at least everyone knows nothing is being looked at; a rule that cannot find things makes you believe something was.

One sentence worth keeping. When a checker prints a pass, it does not mean the subject is correct; it means this rule did not fire. The distance between those two sentences grows as the rule gets narrower.

6. Cross-checking - when two different judges disagree

Every type so far shares one trait. A checker cannot generate a signal that it is wrong. The signal has to be imported from outside, and cross-checking is how you import it.

Not any two will do. The two judges must not share a premise. Two checkers using the same parser, or two rules reusing the same regex, are not a cross-check but a duplicate. They fail at the same point together.

Grading methodWhere it is strongStructural blind spotReproducibility
Rule-based checkFormat, figures, presence. Fast and cheapCounts unanticipated valid shapes as violationsFully reproducible
Reference-answer matchTasks with exactly one correct answerPowerless when several answers are correctFully reproducible
Model judgmentIntent, context, whole document. Cheap second buildSame input may not produce the same verdictPartly reproducible
Human labelingThe only check on the rubric's own validityDoes not scale and humans disagree tooPartly reproducible

What to read out of the table is not each row's strength but the fact that the blind spots do not overlap. Rule-based is strong on shape and weak on intent. Model judgment is the reverse. Put the two together and the verdicts split inside one side's blind region, and the splits become your investigation list.

The operating rule is simple. Agreement carries little information; disagreement is the information. Two matching verdicts do not guarantee both are right, but when two verdicts differ, at least one is definitely wrong. The cost is manageable too, since instead of re-reviewing everything a person opens only the handful that split.

7. What you gain by seating an LLM as second grader

Cross-checking is an old idea that almost nobody actually practiced. The reason is plain. Building the second implementation cost about what the first one cost. A proposal to write the grader twice does not survive any planning meeting.

What model judgment changed is not accuracy but that cost. Write the rubric out in plain language, hand it over, and a second verdict comes back. That is exactly what happened in the write-up above. Without rewriting the grader, simply putting the same rubric into sentences and handing it to a model produced two independent grading results, and their difference pointed at the bug.

There are three places where model judgment behaves distinctly differently from rules.

  • The scope is wider. Instead of reading only the designated boxes it reads the whole document. What is written in the margin enters the input
  • It reads intent instead of shape. Different phrasings with the same meaning get the same verdict, and valid shapes the rule never knew about, such as asking back, get accepted
  • It forces you to restate the rubric in words. Drafting the sentences you will hand to a model exposes assumptions that had been implicit in the code

The third looks like a side effect but is worth a great deal. Write the rubric out completely in plain language and you will find items the team believed were settled that were never actually agreed on.

8. Limits of an LLM grader - nondeterminism and shared misreadings

For the same reasons, the cautions are clear. Do not place model judgment in the same seat as a rule-based check.

First, it may not reproduce. Grade the same answer against the same rubric twice and there is no guarantee of the same result. Wire it into a gate and the outcome can flip on a rerun with no code change at all. If you must gate on it, run it several times and take a majority, or use it strictly for disagreement detection rather than for the verdict itself.

Second, if the rubric is wrong, it is wrong along with you. This is the more fundamental limit. A model handed a flawed rubric produces judgments that are faithfully flawed, and it agrees comfortably with the rule-based checker built from the same rubric. A high agreement rate is not evidence that both judges are right; it can be evidence that both share a premise. Cross-checking catches errors of implementation, not errors of criteria.

Third, agreement rates depend on sample composition. This is a limit the author of that write-up flagged himself. With twenty-six of twenty-nine answers correct, two graders can do nothing at all and still agree at a high rate. To measure discrimination you need enough wrong samples in the mix. A high agreement rate drawn from a pile of good answers proves nothing.

Fourth, the problem of grading the grader returns. Validating model judgment requires another judge, and that judge needs validating too. The regress does not end in tooling. Somewhere a person has to read a small sample and attach labels, and those labels become the floor. Since nobody can read everything, how you select the sample a human sees becomes the real design question.

9. Attach regression tests to the checker itself

Cross-checking is the device for finding shapes you did not anticipate. You separately need a device that guards whether shapes you already know keep getting caught. That is a regression test attached to the checker.

The method is what the name says. Hand-build the defects the checker must catch, seed them into fixtures, run the checker, and assert that all of them fire. At the same time keep clean fixtures that must never fire and confirm that nothing does. The direction is the reverse of an ordinary unit test. Instead of expecting valid output from valid input, you expect an alarm from invalid input.

Fixture you seedExpected verdictWhat a failure means
A defect that actually occurred beforeMust be detectedThe same incident passes again
Boundary values of the rule's wordingMust be detectedNarrowing the rule shaved off detection
A clean case that once drew a false positiveNo detectionThe false positive came back
A clean case worded differently, same meaningNo detectionThe rule overfit to a surface form
Input where the subject list is emptyReports zero subjectsIt cannot tell zero found from zero looked at

Here is a skeleton to convey the idea. It is an example that has not been executed or verified, and the framework and naming should change to fit your environment.

# Regression tests for the checker itself (conceptual, not executed)
POSITIVE = [                      # fixtures that MUST fire
    ("case_missing_field", "MISSING"),
    ("case_wrong_order",   "CRITICAL"),
]
NEGATIVE = [                      # fixtures that must NEVER fire
    "case_clarification_request",  # asking back is valid handling
    "case_note_in_margin",         # margin notes must be read too
]

def test_checker_detects_known_defects():
    for sample, expected in POSITIVE:
        assert run_checker(load(sample)).kind == expected

def test_checker_stays_quiet_on_valid_input():
    for sample in NEGATIVE:
        assert run_checker(load(sample)).violations == []

def test_scope_is_not_silently_empty():
    result = run_checker(load("case_empty_scope"))
    assert result.scanned_count == 0   # zero looked at, not zero found

One rule keeps this alive in practice. Every time you discover a new shape, add that shape as a fixture. The growing fixture set is what stops the same incident from happening twice. Run this suite first whenever you touch a rule and the moment detection power drops shows up on screen. Narrowing a rule without regression tests is turning down the sensitivity with your eyes closed.

10. What happens to old scores when you fix the grader

Fixing a checker bug brings unwelcome consequences too. Nothing about the subject changed, and yet scores you already published move. That is precisely what happened in the write-up above: the model and the answers were unchanged, and the previously published results differed purely because the grader was fixed.

The principle that falls out is short. A score is a function of the subject and the grader version, not of the subject alone. A score without a stated grader is a number without units. The moment you line several runs up as a time series and read a trend, values measured with different rulers share one chart.

  • When recording a score, store the grader version and a fingerprint of the rule contents alongside it
  • On the day you fix the grader, re-grade every historical subject and replace the series
  • If re-grading is impossible, mark grader changes as vertical lines on the chart and state in the writeup which version produced the value
  • Ask the same question when citing external scores. Which grader produced this number?

That last item applies directly when reading benchmark figures. Two evaluations can share a name and still differ in scoring implementation. That is why comparisons that place identically named scores from two organizations side by side so often do not hold.

11. A checklist before you trust automated grading

Compressed into something actionable, the article comes to this. Whether the checker is one you just built or one you inherited, ask in the same order.

  • Is the number of inspected subjects in the output? Without it you cannot separate zero found from zero looked at
  • Do positive fixtures actually fire? Until you seed a known defect and run it, detection power is unknown
  • Do negative fixtures stay quiet? Accumulated false positives make people ignore the entire list
  • Has each rule caught anything recently? A rule sitting at zero for months may be quietly dead, and if it has never been compared against a second judge of a different kind, the miss region is entirely unmeasured
  • Has a person read and labeled a small sample directly? Errors in the rubric itself surface only here
  • Does the sample contain enough wrong cases? A high agreement rate from a pile of correct answers carries no information
  • Is the grader version recorded with the score? If not, comparisons across runs do not hold
  • Is there a procedure for a human to open the splits? Auto-resolving disagreements toward one side destroys the signal

The order matters. The top two can be added in an afternoon and by themselves prevent the most common failures. Cost rises as you go down, so decide how far to go in proportion to the weight of the verdict. A gate that blocks deploys warrants going all the way; an advisory alert is often well served by the first four.

What could not be confirmed

Collecting in one place the items without a source behind them.

  • Reproducibility figures for model judgment. How much a verdict wobbles across repeated grading depends on task, rubric, and model. No generalizable number was confirmed
  • Any claim that one model is a better grader. There is no basis here to rank products. The argument rests on pairing two judges of different character, not on a particular model
  • The share of defects cross-checking catches. The cited write-up had two out of twenty-nine, but the sample is small and skewed toward correct answers, so it cannot be read as a general rate
  • Quantified effect of adding regression tests. Measuring how much misses drop requires knowing the true miss count, and knowing that is the original problem
  • The right sample size for human labeling. How many items a person must read before rubric errors surface depends on task difficulty and the distribution of failure shapes

Conclusion: zero is a claim, not a conclusion

The zero violations a checker prints looks like a statement of fact but is really a claim: nothing fired when these rules were applied to this scope. The grounds compress into three.

First, say what you looked at. The single line that prints the number of inspected subjects is the cheapest defense available. Silent truncation is mostly exposed by that line alone.

Second, show that catching is possible. A regression suite that seeds known defects and confirms the checker catches them turns zero into evidence rather than the result of looking at nothing. That suite also puts the moment detection power drops onto a screen every time a rule is touched.

Third, compare against another set of eyes. Attach a second judge of a different character and have a person open only the splits. Seating a model as the second grader is worth doing right now not because its verdicts are more accurate but because the cost of a second implementation came down.

One last thing. Even with all three in place, something remains unguarded: the case where the rubric itself is wrong. There, the rule-based checker and the model judge produce the same wrong answer together and the agreement rate improves. Errors of criteria surface only when a person reads samples directly. That is precisely the seat automation cannot take.
