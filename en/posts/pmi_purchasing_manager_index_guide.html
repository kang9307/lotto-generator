How to Read the PMI (Purchasing Managers' Index) - Manufacturing, Services & Investing Guide

Reading the Business Cycle From One Number - New Orders to ISM, S&P Global, and RatingDog PMI

Jul 14, 2026 Finance & Economy

Introduction: Why the Market Moves on One Number Every Month

In the first week of nearly every month, financial headlines run some version of "ISM Manufacturing beats expectations" or "China's PMI disappoints." What rarely gets explained is why this single number moves bond yields, stocks, and currencies within minutes of release. The PMI (Purchasing Managers' Index) is a survey indicator that directly asks purchasing executives at factories and service firms one simple question: did things get better or worse than last month? It isn't a complex statistical model - it converts on-the-ground sentiment straight into a number, which is exactly why it arrives weeks or months before official production and trade statistics.

This guide explains, as of July 2026, how the PMI is constructed, why the "50" threshold matters so much, how the ISM, S&P Global, and China's two PMIs differ from one another, and how to read the number without jumping to hasty conclusions. This is educational content about how the indicator works, not investment guidance toward any specific position.

1. What Is the PMI - A Survey-Based Diffusion Index

1.1 Who Is Asked, and What

The PMI starts with a monthly survey that asks purchasing managers at manufacturing or services firms whether items such as new orders, output, employment, inventories, and supplier delivery times improved, stayed the same, or worsened compared with the prior month. Respondents report direction only, not precise sales or output figures, so the survey is quick to complete and the results can be collected and tabulated fast. The aggregated responses become a measure of how an entire sector feels about business conditions, with a very short lag.

1.2 How the Diffusion Index Is Calculated

The PMI is computed as a statistical diffusion index. The formula is simple: multiply the share of "improved" responses by 1, the share of "unchanged" responses by 0.5, and the share of "worsened" responses by 0, then add them together. For example, if 30% of respondents report improvement, 50% report no change, and 20% report deterioration, the index is 30 + (50×0.5) + (20×0) = 55. Because of this formula, the PMI always falls between 0 and 100, and it converges near 50 whenever improvement and deterioration responses split evenly with the rest reporting no change.

2. What the 50 Threshold Really Means

2.1 The Line Between Expansion and Contraction

The first thing to check when reading a PMI print is not the decimal, but the 50 line. A reading above 50 means business conditions expanded (improved) versus the prior month; below 50 means they contracted (worsened). A reading of exactly 50 means essentially no change. The key point is that PMI reflects the direction and pace of change versus last month, not the level of economic activity itself. A move from 48 to 52 doesn't mean conditions suddenly got much better - it signals a turn from contraction into expansion.

2.2 The Farther From 50, the Stronger the Signal

The farther a reading sits from 50, the larger that month's change. A reading of 55 means more respondents reported improvement than a reading of 52 would; a reading of 45 means deterioration was reported more broadly than at 48. Keep in mind, though, that the PMI value itself doesn't directly translate into a physical magnitude like a GDP growth rate. In practice, it's safer to focus on month-over-month and year-over-year directional shifts and multi-month trends rather than a single month's level.

3. Breaking Down the Sub-Components - New Orders, Production, Employment, Inventories, Supplier Deliveries

3.1 What Each Component Tells You

The PMI headline is built by combining several sub-indexes: new orders (a leading signal for future production), production/output (the current level of activity), employment (hiring versus layoffs), inventories (changes in raw material and finished goods stock), and supplier delivery times (how delayed raw material procurement is). Supplier delivery times are a particularly interesting case: slower deliveries sound negative at first glance, but they're interpreted as a sign that demand has surged and supply chains are stretched, so they add to the expansion side of the score. Because each sub-index is read in a different direction, skimming only the headline number leaves a lot of information on the table.

3.2 Weighting Differs by Provider - ISM vs S&P Global

This is where methodologies diverge. The US ISM Manufacturing PMI applies an equal weight of 20% each to new orders, production, employment, supplier deliveries, and inventories. S&P Global's (formerly Markit's) Manufacturing PMI instead uses a weighted average: new orders 30%, output 25%, employment 20%, suppliers' delivery times 15%, and stocks of purchases 10%. Even when two PMIs are calculated for the same month in different countries, the underlying calculation philosophy differs - understanding this reduces the risk of misreading a straight country-to-country comparison.

4. Manufacturing PMI vs. Services PMI vs. Composite PMI

4.1 Manufacturing and Services Move Differently

While the Manufacturing PMI blends the five sub-indexes above, the Services PMI is structured a bit differently. S&P Global's Services PMI headline is the Business Activity Index, a single diffusion index built around one question: did business volume (sales/activity) rise or fall versus last month? Sub-indexes such as new business, employment, and backlogs of work are reported separately. Manufacturing and services often move differently through the cycle - manufacturing is more sensitive to global demand, trade, and inventory cycles, so it tends to turn down and rebound earlier, while services tend to move more gradually, tied to domestic consumption and employment.

4.2 What the Composite PMI Shows

The Composite PMI is a weighted average of the manufacturing and services indexes, weighted by each sector's contribution to output. Because services make up a far larger share of GDP in most advanced economies, the Composite PMI is often driven more by services trends than by manufacturing. Relying on manufacturing PMI alone can therefore miss what's really happening in services-heavy advanced economies, which is why it's safer to track manufacturing, services, and composite together.

5. Comparing the Big Three PMIs - ISM, S&P Global, and China's Two Surveys

5.1 ISM Manufacturing PMI (United States)

The US ISM Manufacturing PMI is published by the Institute for Supply Management, released around the first business day of the month, making it one of the earliest economic releases each month. For instance, the June 2026 ISM Manufacturing PMI registered 53.3%, down 0.7 percentage point from May, but still marking the sixth straight month of manufacturing expansion, with the overall economy in its 20th consecutive month of expansion. ISM also publishes a separate Services PMI (formerly the Non-Manufacturing Index, NMI) each month.

5.2 S&P Global PMI (45 Economies Worldwide)

S&P Global PMI surveys 45 economies and 30 sectors worldwide using the same methodology. Each country panel is built from 400-plus companies designed to mirror the actual structure of that sector, with questionnaires collected in the second half of the month. Notably, a flash (preliminary) PMI, compiled from a substantial share of responses before the final figure is ready, is released around the middle of the month - a signal that reaches markets weeks ahead of official government statistics.

5.3 China's Two PMIs - the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) and RatingDog (formerly Caixin)

China publishes two separate PMIs that frequently tell different stories. The official PMI from the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) surveys about 3,200 firms and skews toward larger, state-owned enterprises. The other survey, drawn from a panel of roughly 650 firms weighted more toward private, export-oriented small and mid-sized businesses, was long known as the "Caixin PMI" - but from August 2025 it was renamed the "RatingDog China PMI" after a sponsorship change (S&P Global still compiles the survey and the methodology is unchanged). When the two diverge, it can signal a temperature gap between the large state-owned sector and the private, export-facing sector.

6. Why the PMI Matters as a "Leading Indicator"

6.1 A Sentiment Signal That Arrives Before Hard Data

The PMI is called a leading indicator because of how it's produced. Hard data like GDP or industrial production must be compiled from actual transaction and production records, so releases lag by weeks or months. The PMI, by contrast, is a sentiment survey of purchasing managers, so responses are collected and tabulated quickly, making it one of the earliest economic data points each month. Purchasing managers sense shifts in demand at the order-planning stage before actual orders land, which is why their sentiment tends to move ahead of hard indicators.

6.2 Its Relationship to Lagging Indicators Like GDP and Employment

That's why market participants and central banks don't wait for GDP to gauge the direction of a quarter's growth - they lean on the PMI instead. ISM even publishes an empirical relationship between the PMI level and past real GDP growth, translating a given PMI reading into a rough annualized GDP growth equivalent. This should be treated as a historical correlation used for reference, not a formula that precisely forecasts future GDP.

7. How PMI Releases Move Markets and Rates

7.1 How Bonds, Stocks, and Currencies React

The PMI is one of the few indicators that moves bond, equity, and currency markets almost immediately on release. A stronger-than-expected PMI often signals expansion and upside price pressure, pushing government bond yields higher; a weaker-than-expected PMI often raises growth concerns and pulls yields lower. When a highly leading sub-component like new orders swings sharply, markets can react even more sensitively than to the headline figure.

7.2 An Input Into Central Bank Policy Decisions

Central banks also use the PMI as reference material when setting monetary policy. When the PMI stays above 50 for several consecutive months while new orders and employment improve together, it reinforces the case that growth is solid; conversely, when the PMI falls below 50 with new orders dropping sharply, expectations for slower growth and easier policy tend to build. That said, no rate decision is made on the PMI alone - it's interpreted together with inflation and employment data.

8. Practical Tips for Reading the PMI Yourself

8.1 A Checklist - Don't Stop at the Headline

  • Check first whether the headline is above or below 50, and whether the direction changed from last month.
  • Confirm whether a highly leading sub-component like new orders points the same direction as the headline (a mismatch can lower the headline's reliability).
  • Look at the trend over the last 3-6 months rather than a single month's reading.
  • Track manufacturing, services, and composite together, and when comparing across countries, confirm whether the survey provider (ISM vs. S&P Global) is the same.

8.2 Limitations - The Inherent Constraints of a Survey

The PMI's inherent limitations are real. Because responses reflect sentiment rather than actual sales or output, a single month's reading can be swayed by psychological factors, seasonal events, or one-off news. Sample sizes are relatively small - a few hundred firms per country - so a single month's number can be noisy. Flash (preliminary) estimates are also often revised modestly in the final release. Remember that the PMI shows direction and breadth of change, not a precise measure of the absolute magnitude of growth.

Conclusion - The Number Worth Checking First Every Month

The PMI isn't a complex model - it starts from the simple question "was it better than last month?" - and that very simplicity is why it signals the direction of the business cycle faster than almost any other indicator. Reading the 50 threshold together with highly leading sub-components like new orders, and understanding the different pictures painted by the ISM, S&P Global, and China's two PMIs, helps you find the real signal behind the PMI headlines that flood the news every month. Pairing this with the guide to reading GDP and growth indicators, plus employment and inflation data, gives a fuller picture of where the cycle stands.
