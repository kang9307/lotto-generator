How to Write a Will - A Complete Guide to Types, Requirements, and Pitfalls

From the five forms of a will to handwritten-will requirements, void traps, forced shares, and probate

2026-06-10 Law

Introduction: A Will Is a Final Act of Care for Those Left Behind

When people hear the word "will," they often picture an elderly tycoon with vast wealth, or a plot device in a soap opera about a feuding family. In reality, however, a will is one of the most efficient tools there is for reducing the confusion and conflict your family may face after you are gone — regardless of how large or small your estate is. If you leave no will, your property is divided entirely by the order and proportions the law prescribes, known as intestate succession. That outcome may differ from what you actually wanted, and the gap can turn into a long-running dispute among those left behind.

A will is the act of fixing your wishes — to whom and how your property should pass — in a form the law recognizes. But it is precisely that "form" that is demanding. Korean civil law recognizes exactly five forms of will, and breaking even one requirement can void an entirely heartfelt will. This article is an informational guide that brings together, in one place, why a will matters, the five forms of a will and their requirements, the must-have elements and void traps of the most common handwritten will, forced heirship shares, probate and execution, and tips for storing and updating a will. It centers on Korean civil law, while repeatedly noting that every requirement and figure may differ by time, jurisdiction, and amendment.

1. Why You Need a Will

A will shines brightest when "what you want" differs from "the default the law sets." Without a will, succession is processed automatically by the rules of intestate succession — and in the situations below, that default may diverge from your true wishes.

1.1 Preventing Inheritance Disputes

A large share of inheritance disputes erupt over "what the deceased actually wanted." One heir argues they cared for the parents and so deserve more; another insists everything be split equally by law. A will reduces the very starting point of such quarrels. When your intentions survive as a legally effective document, the fundamental dispute over "what the deceased wanted" is greatly eased.

1.2 Making Your Intentions Clear - A Distribution Different From the Default

Intestate succession follows a fixed order and proportions. But real families each have their own circumstances. You may want to leave the family shop to the child who built the business with you, give more generously to a child with a disability, or leave property to someone who is not a statutory heir — a common-law spouse, or a person who cared for you all your life. Such a distribution is possible only through a will (a bequest).

1.3 Charitable Giving and Giving Back

If you want to donate part or all of your estate to a school, religious body, or public-interest foundation, you can do so by bequest in a will. Even here, though, the limit known as the forced heirship share may apply, so the plan must account for the minimum portion guaranteed to heirs.

In one line: a will is the only lawful way to turn "the default distribution set by law" into "the distribution I want." But it cannot cross the safety line of forced shares.

2. The Five Forms of a Will - At a Glance

Korean civil law limits wills to five forms: holographic (handwritten), recorded, notarial, secret, and dictated (the principle of strict form). A will that departs from these five forms — for example, words left on a video or a messaging-app text — is hard to recognize as a legal will on its own. The table below compares the five forms.

HolographicHandwritten full text, date, address, name + sealNoneRequired
RecordedSpoken substance, name, date + witness confirmation1 or moreRequired
NotarialDictated before a notary; notary writes and reads it back2Not required
SecretSealed will, submitted to witnesses, fixed date within 5 days2 or moreRequired
DictatedFor urgent cases; dictated, witness writes, reads, confirms2 or moreRequired (probate within 7 days)

Below we walk through the requirements, strengths, and weaknesses of each form. The two used most in practice are the holographic will and the notarial will.

3. The Holographic Will - The Simplest, Yet the Most Demanding

A holographic will is one the testator writes entirely by hand. It needs no witness and no notary, and costs nothing, so it is the most accessible option. But that very simplicity also makes it the form most often voided for breaching a formal requirement.

3.1 Mandatory Requirements - All Five, By Hand

For a holographic will to be valid under Korean civil law, the testator must satisfy all five of the following.

  • Handwriting the full text: the entire content of the will must be written by the testator's own hand. Typing and printing it, or having another person write it, makes it void.
  • The date: the date of writing must be recorded down to the year, month, and day. Omitting the day makes it void. Because the date determines priority when several wills exist, it is critical.
  • The address: the testator must write their address. Courts have voided wills where the address was missing, even when the testator could otherwise be identified without difficulty.
  • The name: the testator writes their own name so that whose will it is becomes clear.
  • The seal: a seal (stamp) must be affixed. A will without a seal is regarded as lacking the assurance that it is a definitive will rather than a mere draft, and the Supreme Court has held holographic wills without a seal to be void.
A holographic will can be entirely void if even one of the five — full text, date, address, name, plus seal — is missing. In particular, omitting the "day" of the date, the address, or the seal has actually voided wills in many cases.

3.2 Practical Drafting Tips

To reduce misunderstanding, write clearly with a pen, and if a correction is needed, do not scribble over it carelessly — rewriting is safer. When you must alter a character, follow the amendment method the law prescribes (indicate the place changed, add a note that it was changed and sign, and seal the altered part). State plainly who receives what, in lean prose, and after writing, keep the will somewhere safe while letting a trusted person know its location so it can be found after death.

4. The Notarial Will - The Safest, But Needs Cost and Witnesses

A notarial will is one in which the testator states the substance of the will by mouth before a notary (dictation), the notary writes it down and reads it back, and then the testator and witnesses approve its accuracy and each sign and seal. Drafting requires two witnesses and incurs a notarization fee.

4.1 Advantages - No Probate, Protection Against Forgery

The greatest advantage of the notarial will is that it need not go through court probate. Because a public expert — the notary — is involved in drafting, the risk of being voided for a formal defect is small, and the original is kept at the notarial office, greatly reducing the risk of loss, forgery, or alteration. It is especially recommended when you want to minimize the chance of post-death disputes or when the estate is large.

4.2 Beware of Disqualified Witnesses

Not just anyone can serve as a witness to a will. Minors, persons under adult or limited guardianship, and those who will benefit from the will along with their spouse and lineal blood relatives cannot be witnesses. Putting an interested person forward as a witness can shake the will itself, so witness selection deserves care.

5. The Recorded Will

A recorded will is one in which the testator states by mouth the substance of the will, their name, and the date, and a participating witness states the accuracy of the will and their own name, all recorded. It can be used when writing by hand is difficult, and one witness is enough.

That said, audio and video files can be damaged depending on the storage and playback environment, or give rise to authenticity disputes, and after the testator's death the recording must go through family-court probate. The witness-disqualification rules noted above apply equally to recorded wills. Convenient as it looks, it demands attention in terms of post-death proof and storage.

6. The Secret Will

A secret will is used when you want to keep the contents secret until death while still securing the will's existence and authenticity. The procedure runs as follows.

  • The testator writes a document setting out the will's content and records the writer's name on it (it need not be handwritten in this case).
  • The document is placed in an envelope and strictly sealed and stamped.
  • It is submitted before two or more witnesses as the testator's own will, the date of submission is written on the surface of the sealed package, and the testator and witnesses each sign or affix a name-and-seal.
  • Within five days from the date on the surface, it must be submitted to a notary or family-court clerk to obtain a fixed date on the seal.

It is also worth knowing that there is a conversion rule: even if a secret will is defective in that form, it can take effect as a holographic will if it meets the requirements of a holographic will.

7. The Dictated Will - An Exception for Urgent Situations

A dictated will is an exceptional, emergency form used when illness or another urgent cause makes the previous four forms impossible. With two or more witnesses present, the testator states the substance of the will by mouth to one of them; that person writes it down and reads it aloud, and the testator and the other witnesses approve its accuracy and each sign or affix a name-and-seal.

Because the dictated will rests on urgency, the after-the-fact controls are strict. A witness or interested party must apply to the family court for probate within seven days from the day the urgent cause ends for it to remain effective. So if another form is feasible, there is no reason to choose the dictated form; it is best understood purely as a last-resort measure.

8. Common Reasons Wills Are Voided

Most reasons a will is voided lie not in its "content" but in its "form." Here are the frequently appearing grounds for invalidity or dispute.

  • Breach of the handwriting requirement: a holographic will that was typed and printed, or written by another person.
  • Missing or unclear date: the "day" of the date was not written, or it is unspecified, such as "on an auspicious day in year ____."
  • Missing address: the address was not written in a holographic will (courts have voided such wills even where identification was not hindered).
  • Missing seal: no seal was affixed. Courts have held that a signature alone is not enough.
  • Disqualified witness: a disqualified person — a minor, the beneficiary, or their spouse or lineal blood relative — was made a witness.
  • Lack of testamentary capacity: it is recognized that the testator lacked mental capacity when writing. A will by someone who has not reached the minimum age (17 in Korea) is also void.
  • Forgery, alteration, or duress: the will was not based on the testator's free will.
Key point: most invalidity stems from "defects of form." Even though it costs money, drafting a notarial will greatly reduces these formal risks.

9. Forced Heirship Shares - The Minimum a Will Cannot Cross

Even if you decide in a will that "all my property goes to one person," certain heirs retain a legally guaranteed minimum. This is the forced heirship share (yuryubun in Korea). It is the minimum proportion of an estate that statutory heirs can secure despite a will or lifetime gifts.

9.1 The Ratios

Korean law has set the forced share by class of holder as follows.

  • The deceased's descendants (children, etc.) and spouse: one-half of the statutory share
  • The deceased's ascendants (parents, etc.): one-third of the statutory share

For reference, siblings were once granted a forced share too (one-third of the statutory share), but as the Constitutional Court ruled the siblings' forced-share provision unconstitutional in 2024, that part lost its effect. Because the forced-share system can change with social debate and legislative amendment, checking the current law is essential.

9.2 The Deadline for a Forced-Share Claim

A holder whose forced share is infringed may claim restitution up to the extent of the infringement. But this right is extinguished by prescription if not exercised within one year of learning of both the commencement of succession and the gift or bequest to be restored, and within ten years of the commencement of succession. Even with the right, missing the deadline makes recovery difficult, so managing the timing matters.

Planning tip: even a will that funnels property to one person will spark a dispute if a forced-share holder seeks restitution. Designing the distribution with forced shares in mind in advance greatly reduces later conflict. The relationship with renunciation and qualified acceptance of inheritance also deserves review.

10. Probate and Execution of a Will

When the testator dies, the will takes effect and moves to the stage of being realized in practice. Here you need to understand two procedures: "probate" and "execution."

10.1 Probate - A Procedure to Confirm and Preserve the Document

Probate is a procedure the family court performs to confirm the form and condition of the will and preserve its current state. The holder or finder of any will except a notarial will (holographic, recorded, secret, dictated) must, without delay after the testator's death, submit the will to court and seek probate. A sealed will is opened at court in the presence of heirs and others. Note an important caveat: probate merely confirms and preserves the fact that the will exists in form; it does not establish that the will's contents are valid. A will with grounds for invalidity can still be contested even after probate.

10.2 The Executor - The Person Who Carries Out the Will

Execution of a will is the procedure that actually realizes its contents — transferring title to real estate, withdrawing deposits, carrying out bequests, and so on. The testator may name an executor in the will; if none is named, an heir becomes the executor or the family court may appoint one. The more dispute-prone the matter, the smoother execution tends to be when a trusted third party (such as a lawyer) is named as executor.

11. Practical Tips for Writing, Storing, and Updating a Will

Just as important as meeting formal requirements is "storing it well and updating it in time."

11.1 Which Form to Choose

If you value simplicity and low cost and the content is straightforward, a holographic will is an option. But because the risk of a formal defect is high, when the estate is large or disputes among heirs are expected, a notarial will is recommended. The notarial will needs no probate and carries little risk of forgery or alteration, making it the most stable after death.

11.2 Storage - A Will No One Finds Is Meaningless

However perfect a will is, it is useless if it is not found after death. Keep a holographic will somewhere safe, but let a trusted person — a spouse, child, or the executor — know its location. A notarial will's original is kept at the notarial office, so there is little worry of loss.

11.3 Updating - When Life Changes, So Does the Will

You may freely revoke or amend a will during your lifetime. It is wise to revisit your will at life's turning points — marriage or divorce, the birth of a child, changes in the makeup of your assets. If a later will conflicts with an earlier one, that part is treated as revoked by the later will, and a lifetime disposition that contradicts the will revokes that part too. So always record the date clearly so that, if several wills surface, their order can be determined.

12. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q. Is there a fixed will template I must use?

There is no legally fixed template, but for a holographic will you must satisfy the requirement of handwriting the full text, date, address, and name and affixing a seal. As long as the formal requirements are met, the wording itself is free. Still, writing clearly who receives what, without ambiguity, helps prevent disputes.

Q. Can a married couple make a will together in one document?

A joint will made by two or more people in the same document is generally not recognized. Even spouses should each draft a separate will to be safe. Consult a professional on the specific method of drafting.

Q. Can a will pass on debts too?

Succession comprehensively transfers not only positive assets but also debts. If you judge that debts exceed assets, heirs can consider renouncing the inheritance or accepting it conditionally (qualified acceptance) — and there are deadlines for these. See the related article for details, and discuss your own situation with a professional.

Conclusion - A Will That Keeps Its Form Is a Real Will

A will is not only for the wealthy. It is a final act of care that reduces the confusion and conflict your family will face and that fixes your wishes in a form the law recognizes. But for that care to take effect, you must draft it in one of the five forms the law prescribes, meeting every requirement without omission. In particular, never forget that the most common holographic will can be void if even one of its five elements — full text, date, address, name, and seal — is missing.

Finally, three points once more. First, form is the lifeblood of a will. If you value safety, choose the notarial will, which needs no probate. Second, a will cannot cross the heirs' minimum forced share, and a restitution claim has a deadline. Third, every requirement and figure varies by time, jurisdiction, and amendment. Once more: this article is for information and education only and is not legal advice. For the actual drafting of a will and estate planning, please consult a licensed professional such as a lawyer, notary, or judicial scrivener.
