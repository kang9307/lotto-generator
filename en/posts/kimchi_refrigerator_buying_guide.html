What a Kimchi Refrigerator Actually Controls - What to Check Before Buying

Not making it cold, but holding it steady. From structure through capacity, efficiency ratings and installation

2026-09-01 Life & Practical

The kimchi refrigerator is a Korean appliance category, and everything below — the temperature modes, the rating scheme, the electricity tariff — is South Korean. It is worth reading anyway, because the design problem is general: a fermented food has to be held at a low temperature that barely moves. In a showroom, buyers look at capacity and price first and the efficiency sticker second. The point where this machine parts company with the ordinary refrigerator beside it is in none of the three. The centre of the design is not how cold it gets, but how little the cold it has made is allowed to move.

Miss that axis and two things go wrong. A unit labelled 300 litres does not hold 300 litres of kimchi, and the one with the larger number sometimes holds less. And a grade 1 sticker is not absolute performance but a position relative to the baseline in force when it was issued — a baseline that moved sharply in 2022.

This article recommends no manufacturer or model and disparages none. The temperatures, power figures and tariffs quoted come from different reference dates and vary by model, so the label and manual for the actual product take precedence. Storage temperature and duration touch on safety, so nothing is asserted beyond what published sources confirm.

The target is not temperature but the swing

Both machines run the same refrigeration cycle. A compressor squeezes the refrigerant, a condenser dumps the heat, an evaporator takes heat back out. On a parts list there is nothing to tell them apart. What differs is the goal the cycle is tuned for.

An ordinary refrigerator is optimised for recovery. It gets opened dozens of times a day and its contents change constantly, so it circulates chilled air with a fan to cool any compartment quickly. Two costs follow. Temperature swings hard on every opening, and the moving air pulls moisture off the surface of food. That is why the crisper has a lid of its own. The kimchi refrigerator faces the opposite conditions. The door is rarely opened, the contents are mostly one thing, and a load sits for months. Here the expensive capability is not cooling fast but not drifting off the set point for months on end.

Why the swing matters so much comes from the fermentation side. Microbial activity does not scale with temperature; it responds steeply. Two profiles with the same average behave differently, because fermentation that ran during the warm excursion does not run backwards during the cold one. Progress accumulates and nothing recovers it. What this machine sells is not a low temperature but a narrow band, which is why LG leads with a ±0.3℃ deviation figure in its own product notes — a line that rarely appears in an ordinary refrigerator brochure.

  • Two different machines out of the same parts. It was not the hardware but the control objective that split them
  • Use a kimchi refrigerator like an ordinary one and the advantage disappears. Open it ten times a day and all that is left is a slightly lower temperature
  • Conversely, kimchi kept for months in an ordinary fridge has roughly the right temperature but the wrong steadiness. The same kimchi turns sour sooner

Sub-zero, and still not frozen

The kimchi compartment runs below zero. Samsung service documentation lists standard kimchi at -0.8℃ on mild, -1.3℃ on standard and -1.8℃ on strong. Sub-zero in something that is not a freezer looks odd at first.

The answer is salt. Kimchi brine is a salt solution, not pure water, and the more dissolved matter it carries the lower its freezing point falls. The same page tells you to store ordinary kimchi, at the usual 2 to 3 percent salinity, on standard, and provides a separate low-salt profile — which is the same statement twice. Turn it around: lower salinity raises the freezing point, and the same setting freezes it.

ModeOrdinary kimchiLow-salt kimchiWhat it is for
Mild-0.8℃0.0℃The default within each profile
Standard-1.3℃-0.5℃Napa cabbage kimchi, cut kimchi, kkakdugi, spring onion and mustard leaf kimchi
Strong-1.8℃-1.0℃Ordinary: aged and heavily salted kimchi. Low-salt: baek kimchi, nabak kimchi, dongchimi, yeolmu water kimchi

What the table is really showing is not the individual numbers but the fact that the modes are cut at half-degree intervals. For that spacing to mean anything, the deviation actually held has to be smaller than it. The varieties that cause trouble in practice are not the cabbage ones but the watery ones. Manufacturers tell you to check within one or two weeks of starting with a new unit and step down one mode if you see ice crystals.

  • One container freezing while another does not is usually not a fault. It is a difference in salinity between the two
  • Guidance notes that ordinary containers and plastic bags freeze more readily. The wall thickness and seal of a purpose-made container act as a buffer
  • Mode names vary by maker. What matters is not the name but the temperature behind it, and it is usually printed in a table at the back of the manual

Direct and indirect cooling, decided by the form

One misconception travels widely: that every kimchi refrigerator uses direct cooling. It is half right. The split LG sets out in its own material is that lid type units cool directly and stand type units cool indirectly. Choosing the form means you have already chosen the cooling method.

Direct cooling runs the cooling pipework against the cabinet wall so the wall itself goes cold. No air is circulated, so there is no draught, so less moisture leaves the food — and moisture that touches the wall freezes where it lands. Thick ice on the wall of a lid type unit is not a fault; it is close to the definition of the method. Samsung service documentation describes it as normal.

Indirect cooling pays on the other side. Air in constant motion carries moisture toward the evaporator, so kimchi under a loosely closed lid dries out. That is why stand type guidance leans harder on sealed containers. What it buys is easy zoning: separate compartments held at separate temperatures. Using the upper compartment for kimchi and the lower one for vegetables or meat took hold on stand type units for structural reasons, not by chance.

  • Direct cooling gives steadiness and moisture retention and costs you frost and freedom in compartment layout
  • Indirect cooling gives zone control and recovery speed and costs you drying, fan noise and cold air lost on opening
  • Neither is the superior technology. They are two different things given up, and which one you can afford to give up is set by how you live

How fermentation tracks temperature

Refrigeration does not stop fermentation, it slows it. The visible marker of how much is the white film of surface yeast that forms on kimchi. Figures published by the World Institute of Kimchi put its appearance at roughly 6 days at 20℃, roughly 8 days at 10℃, and roughly 3 weeks at 4℃.

The intervals are the point. Between 20℃ and 10℃ there are two days in it; between 10℃ and 4℃ there are more than two weeks. What a drop in temperature buys is different in different parts of the range, and the same one degree does more work the lower you go. That is why the modes are cut at half-degree intervals, and why the gap between 4℃ above zero and 1℃ below is far larger than the numbers suggest.

The taste stages are numbered as well. The same source divides kimchi by acidity into an unripe stage below 0.3 percent, a properly ripened stage from 0.3 to 0.8 percent, and an over-ripened stage above 1.0 percent. What the machine does is not erase this curve but stretch the ripened stretch out. Nothing here claims a health benefit from fermentation. How low temperature acts on fermentation rate is confirmed above; what that means inside a human body needs a different kind of evidence.

  • Surface yeast is not mould, but its arrival signals that the surface has been in contact with air for a long time. Pressing the kimchi under its brine is the practical answer
  • Put it in the moment it is made and it takes a long time to ripen. Ripening and storing are different jobs and this machine is good at storing
  • Setting everything to strong on the theory that colder always lasts longer raises the freezing risk. Read it together with the salinity section above

Where lid type and stand type part ways

One thing is physically settled: cold air is heavy and sinks. A lid type opens upward, so the cold stays pooled in the cabinet; a stand type opens toward you, so it pours out from the bottom the moment the door swings. The more often you open it, the more that difference accumulates as compressor runtime. In exchange, a lid type demands something of your body. Reaching a container at the bottom means bending deep and lifting out whatever sits on top of it.

  • You make kimjang and eat it through the winter → rarely opened, storage volume matters. Lid type has the edge
  • You buy kimchi and restock every two or three weeks → access beats volume. Stand type fits
  • You plan to keep vegetables and meat in it too → you need zone temperatures. Stand type
  • Floor area is short and vertical space is spare → stand type. If it has to go somewhere low, lid type
  • Your back or knees complain and you are lifting alone → give up some volume and take the stand type

The label volume and what actually fits

This is where the label and the experience diverge most. The labelled volume measures the internal cabinet, but kimchi sits in the containers you put inside it. Containers have wall thickness, there has to be room between them for a hand, and space where no container can go — such as door shelving — still counts toward the label. Figures compiled by the retail service Nosearch put the sum of container volume at about 40 percent of the label on stand type units and about 67 percent on lid type ones. Then it shrinks once more: kimchi swells as it ferments, so a container is filled to 80 percent.

Stand type, actual kimchi volume (L) ≈ labelled volume × 0.40 × 0.80
Lid type,   actual kimchi volume (L) ≈ labelled volume × 0.67 × 0.80
Cabbage heads                       ≈ actual volume ÷ 2   (1 dressed head ≈ 2L)

e.g. stand type 300L → 300 × 0.40 × 0.80 = 96L  ≈ 48 heads
     lid type   200L → 200 × 0.67 × 0.80 ≈ 107L ≈ 53 heads

The lid type with 100 litres less on the label holds more. That calculation is why comparing the two forms by their litre figures is meaningless. Compare the sum of the supplied container volumes instead. If the product page lists the container set, add it up; if it does not, that is the question to ask.

  • The 2 litres per cabbage head is for a dressed head. Working from the weight of unsalted cabbage will not line up
  • Failing to fill the containers costs you too. Empty space has little thermal mass and swings more. Capacity is not to be sized generously but correctly
  • Purpose-made containers are consumables. Whether the same format stays available once they crack may matter more over time than the litre figure

What the efficiency rating measures

Units first. The power consumption on a Korean kimchi refrigerator label is monthly, not annual, in kWh per month. The Korea Energy Agency product listing is organised by a monthly consumption column too. What is stated annually is not consumption but the estimated energy cost. Mix the two and you are out by a factor of twelve.

The grade comes from measurement under KS C 9321, from which an efficiency index is calculated and mapped to grades 1 through 5. What matters is not the formula but the fact that the index is relative. Move the baseline and the same performance earns a different grade — and it moved. In April 2022 the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy revised the regulation on efficiency-managed equipment and raised the thresholds for kimchi refrigerators. The projection reported at the time was a fall in the grade 1 share from 64.4 percent to 12.1 percent, meaning six units in ten had carried the top grade beforehand.

The same revision moved classification from the number of kimchi compartments to the number of doors, and changed the index from dividing maximum consumption by monthly consumption to dividing monthly consumption by storage volume — closing a structure that had favoured large units. Longer-term targets to tighten by a further 3 to 20 percent after three years and 3 percent again three years later were published alongside. The bar keeps rising.

  • With a carried-over or second-hand unit, establish which baseline the grade belongs to first. An old label's grade and a new one's are not directly comparable
  • Instead of comparing grades, compare the kWh per month on the label. A grade is a rounded summary; the kWh figure is a measurement
  • The standard test runs close to steady state, with no door opening. The label value is a scale for comparing products, not a forecast of your bill
  • Reading appliance ratings in general is covered more broadly in how to choose energy-efficient appliances

Converting a rating into a bill

A grade comparison only becomes a decision in money, and the unit price is where people go wrong. A kimchi refrigerator is a load added on top of what you already use, so what applies is not the average price on your bill but the rate of the progressive tier you are currently sitting in. In the Korea Electric Power residential low-voltage schedule the energy charge outside summer is 120.0 won up to 200kWh, 214.6 won from 201 to 400kWh, and 307.3 won above 400kWh; in summer the tier boundaries move to 300kWh and 450kWh. The figures below use those rates and exclude the standing charge, VAT and the power industry fund.

Everything that is not kimchi

Few households keep only kimchi in it. A little below minus one degree behaves completely differently once you step outside kimchi, so the distinctions matter. The principle is single: the wetter and less salty something is, the more readily it freezes. Smell arises at the same place. Samsung service documentation states that apart from a burning smell, most odours originate in the stored food rather than the appliance, and puts sealed containers first among the remedies.

  • Meat and fish — the sub-zero band suits keeping things cold without freezing them. But no public standard for a safe storage duration could be confirmed, so this article states none. Seal it, and do not share a compartment with kimchi
  • Leafy vegetables — generally a poor fit. Wet and unsalted, they freeze easily, and some vegetables are damaged by the cold itself. If the unit has a vegetable mode, use that compartment
  • Fruit — varies by type. Tropical fruit is often cold-sensitive. Do not store something like an apple, which takes cold storage well, next to something like a banana, which does not
  • Alcohol — freezing expands the volume and breaks the container. The lower the strength, the higher the freezing point. Leaving cans or bottles at kimchi mode temperature is best avoided
  • Rice, nuts and flour — many people use it to hold off insects and rancidity. Unsealed, they take on odours, and condensation forms on the surface when they come out
  • General — do not put strong-smelling things in the same compartment as things that absorb smells. That one rule removes half of all odour complaints

If multi-purpose use is definite, the form decision is settled early. You need compartments that can hold separate temperatures, and that generally means the stand type.

Where you put it sets what it does

Installation is easy to treat as the last step, the one you leave to the delivery crew, but a good part of what you chose earlier gets decided here. A refrigeration cycle works by dumping the heat from inside to the outside. Block the place it dumps to and it has to run longer, and all of that runtime is money.

Clearance and floor

Samsung service guidance sets at least 5cm at the sides and rear and 10cm above. The accompanying note says that tight clearance impedes ventilation, weakens cooling and raises consumption. The floor has to be level; otherwise you get vibration and noise, and the door falls out of alignment.

  • Slotting it neatly between kitchen units looks good and is the worst possible arrangement for heat rejection. If the back is against a wall, at least keep the sides clear
  • Measure the door swing and the route in before delivery. For a stand type, allow for the drawer at full extension; for a lid type, check whether the lid clears the wall cabinets as it opens
  • Avoid the space beside a hob or oven and a window in direct sun. Every degree of ambient warmth is more compressor runtime

The winter balcony

The most common placement and the most frequent source of trouble. Manufacturer guidance sets an ambient range of 5℃ to 43℃ and explicitly says to avoid excessively cold locations. An unheated balcony in a Korean winter drops below that floor easily. What exactly fails first under those conditions is not published by manufacturers and could not be confirmed here. What is certain is that it is use outside the recommended range, and a fault arising in that state is hard to treat as normal use.

Noise, frost and smell

A large share of service calls land on these three, and a large share of those are not faults. This is where dividing the cooling methods earlier pays off. Knowing which structure you bought largely settles what counts as normal.

Noise comes from the compressor and the refrigerant. A sound that suddenly got louder, a regular metallic knock, or vibration travelling through the floor usually means the unit is off level or the back panel is touching the wall. A flowing refrigerant sound, or frost melting, is structural. When in doubt, pull the unit forward 5cm, re-level it and listen again. A lot of noise disappears at that step.

Frost forms in lid type units because the wall is doing the cooling. Samsung service documentation explains that moisture from the food and from air admitted during use settles on the wall and turns into a wall of ice, and describes it as normal. Removal means switching that compartment off, letting it melt, wiping and switching back on. If frost thickens unusually fast, though, suspect a lid that is not closing properly first.

Smell comes mostly from what is stored. Spoiled food, spilled brine and containers left ajar top the list of causes. Units with a deodorising element carry guidance to regenerate it by drying it in sunlight for a day about every two years. A smell usually means something liquid has leaked somewhere, so cleaning comes before deodorising.

When to buy

It is a seasonal product, so price and stock follow the calendar. Retail data compiled by the Korean price comparison site Danawa in 2014 shows new models starting to appear as early as June or July, arriving in force in September, with sales peaking in the second and third weeks of November. Consumer interest, it notes, rises more than 40 percent from October.

That material is more than a decade old and the retail landscape has changed since, so it is safer to take the shape of the cycle than the numbers. The shape is simple. The moment new models arrive and the moment demand peaks are out of phase, and carried-over stock sits in the gap.

  • When new models go on display, the previous year's are pushed aside. In a category where the year-on-year difference is small, that is the best value on the floor
  • Peak season offers the widest choice and the weakest bargaining position. If you are not in a hurry there is no reason to walk into it
  • Count the installation schedule in. Delivery and installation slip in peak season, and if the kimjang date is fixed that outranks the price
  • If you are replacing an old unit, line up the disposal collection with the delivery. With nowhere to move the kimchi, a whole day goes sideways

When you do not need one

This machine earns its keep under specific conditions. Outside them it becomes an expensive second refrigerator that still draws power. If several of the following apply, waiting is the rational call.

  • You do not make kimjang and buy your kimchi — shop kimchi is generally bought a few kilograms at a time and finished within weeks. At that turnover, long cold storage buys almost nothing
  • One or two people, and kimchi is not a staple — containers you cannot fill leave empty space, and empty space swings more. Buying a smaller unit does not remove the problem
  • Your ordinary refrigerator has a spare shelf — the fridge compartment runs warmer, but for a few weeks' worth the difference is consumed before it shows
  • The only spot drops below 5℃ in winter — if there is no indoor space and only the balcony, solve the placement problem first
  • You normally sit in the top progressive tier — the right-hand column of the table above applies in full. Without a real storage need it is a straight loss

The alternative deserves a mention. Small well-sealed containers in the kimchi drawer of an ordinary refrigerator, with ripening finished briefly at room temperature before moving to cold, works perfectly well at small volumes. What you do not get there is the narrow band. If you are keeping it for months rather than weeks, that difference shows up in the result.

What long use brings, and upkeep

This is an appliance with a long replacement cycle, so its decline does not arrive as an incident. It proceeds slowly, in a way the owner does not notice. Half of what we call upkeep is slowing that down and the other half is noticing it.

Cleaning interval

Twice a year is the practical rhythm. Empty and wipe it out just before kimjang so new kimchi does not inherit old smells, and once more before summer to catch dried spills.

  • Take everything out and switch it off. Any frost will melt on its own in that state
  • Wipe brine out of the shelves, the walls and the drawer runners. That is generally where the smell starts
  • Wash containers with a soft sponge and neutral detergent, then dry them fully. Putting them back wet adds to the frost
  • Clean the groove around the door or lid gasket. Debris in that groove is the most common cause of a poor seal
  • Switch it back on and check that the set temperature has not reset. Some units return to a default mode after a power cycle

What gives out first with age

The rubber gasket goes first. Over years it hardens and takes a set, the seal degrades, humid air gets in, frost increases, the compressor runs longer and consumption rises with it. It is not one fault but a chain. Close the door on a sheet of paper: if it pulls out easily, the seal has weakened.

Temperature deviation comes next, and there is no practical way for an owner to measure it — it only shows up in the result. If the same kimchi in the same mode turns sour faster than it used to, that may be the signal that the band has widened. Covering it by dropping a mode only moves the problem toward freezing. The conversion table above serves again in deciding between repair and replacement, but savings alone rarely justify replacing. Those figures run to hundreds of thousands of won, not millions.

What I Could Not Verify

  • Whether the residential tariff in force in 2026 matches the schedule quoted could not be established. If it has since been revised the absolute amounts change, but the calculation structure still holds
  • The measured consumption difference between grade 1 products before and after the revision could not be confirmed. The projected share was reported; how far individual models differ in kWh per month is a separate question
  • The claim that lid type units use less electricity than stand type ones circulates widely and is explicable through cold air loss, but no side-by-side measurement at matched capacity and grade could be found. That is why this comparison is not converted into money here
  • No public standard for a safe storage period for meat or fish in a kimchi refrigerator could be confirmed. Since that temperature band sits between chilling and freezing, ordinary refrigeration guidance does not transfer cleanly
  • Which component fails first when the ambient drops below 5℃ could not be found in published manufacturer material. Only the recommended range and a general description are stated above
  • Whether a common standard exists for kimchi containers could not be confirmed. If you plan to buy containers separately, check that manufacturer's format
  • The material cited for new model timing is 2014 retail data. No newer statistics were found, so only the shape of the cycle was carried over, not the figures
