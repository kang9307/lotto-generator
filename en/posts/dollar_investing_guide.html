Introduction: Why Dollar Investing Is Attracting Attention in Korea

Between 2025 and 2026, the KRW/USD exchange rate repeatedly exceeded 1,400–1,500 KRW per dollar, reaching its highest levels since the global financial crisis. This sharp KRW depreciation has sparked widespread interest in dollar investing among Korean retail investors, who seek both to profit from future exchange rate rises and to hedge their predominantly KRW-denominated portfolios.

The US dollar's status as the world's reserve currency means it often strengthens during periods of global economic uncertainty — making it a potential safe-haven asset. However, when the exchange rate falls, KRW-based investors holding dollars will experience principal losses. This guide covers the four main ways to invest in dollars from Korea, the key drivers of the KRW/USD exchange rate, the costs involved, the tax treatment, and the risks — from the perspective of a Korea-based individual investor.

1. Four Main Ways to Invest in Dollars from Korea

There are four primary methods for Korean residents to invest in US dollars. Each differs in terms of safety, return potential, liquidity, and accessibility.

1.1 Foreign Currency Demand Account (Waewhwa Tongjanf)

A foreign currency demand account is a bank account that holds US dollars (or other foreign currencies) with free deposits and withdrawals. Most major Korean banks offer these accounts. The interest rate is very low — typically around 0–1% per year — but it allows you to hold dollars and benefit if the exchange rate rises.

  • Pros: Freely accessible, low minimum, eligible for deposit protection (up to the legal limit; verify before opening)
  • Cons: Very low interest; principal loss in KRW terms if the exchange rate falls
  • Best for: Holding dollars for overseas spending, remittances, or long-term exchange rate exposure

1.2 USD Time Deposit (Waewhwa Jeonggi Yegeum)

A USD time deposit locks your dollars for a fixed term (e.g., 3 months to 1 year) in exchange for a higher interest rate. Rates are influenced by the US Federal Reserve's policy rate — in the high-rate environment of 2024–2026, some Korean banks offered around 4% per annum on USD time deposits (rates vary significantly by institution and time). Early withdrawal typically results in a penalty reduction in the applied interest rate.

  • Pros: Higher interest than demand accounts; bank product with relative safety
  • Cons: Limited liquidity during the term; early redemption penalty; principal loss if exchange rate falls
  • Best for: Earning interest on dollars you plan to hold for a fixed period

1.3 Dollar RP (Repurchase Agreement)

A dollar RP is a product offered by Korean securities firms where your USD is invested in dollar-denominated government bonds or high-grade corporate bonds, with a commitment to repurchase at a set yield after a short period. According to the KDI Economic Information Center, dollar RPs can offer a slightly higher yield than foreign currency deposits. However, RP products from securities firms are generally not covered by the deposit protection system — this is an important distinction to verify before investing.

  • Pros: Potentially higher return than USD deposits; suitable for short-term parking
  • Cons: Not covered by deposit protection in most cases; issuer credit risk exists
  • Best for: Investors with brokerage accounts looking to earn short-term yield on USD

1.4 Dollar ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund)

Dollar ETFs listed in Korea (e.g., TIGER US Dollar Futures, KODEX US Dollar Futures) are designed to track the KRW/USD exchange rate and trade on the Korea Exchange just like stocks. When the rate rises, the ETF gains; when it falls, it loses. Leveraged ETFs (2x) amplify daily returns and carry significantly higher risk. Inverse ETFs are designed to profit when the exchange rate falls.

  • Pros: Low minimum investment, accessible via standard brokerage account, high intraday liquidity
  • Cons: Management fees; tracking error; leveraged products are high risk; not covered by deposit protection
  • Best for: Experienced equity investors who want market-rate exposure to KRW/USD movements

2. What Drives the KRW/USD Exchange Rate?

The exchange rate is the most critical variable in dollar investing, and it is influenced by multiple macroeconomic forces simultaneously. Understanding the main drivers helps you interpret exchange rate news — but it does not make prediction reliable.

2.1 US–Korea Interest Rate Differential

When the US Federal Reserve's policy rate is significantly higher than the Bank of Korea's, dollar-denominated assets offer relatively higher yields, increasing demand for dollars and pushing up the KRW/USD rate (KRW depreciation). The prolonged interest rate inversion between Korea and the US from 2022–2025 was a major structural driver of KRW weakness.

2.2 Current Account and Trade Balance

When Korea runs a large trade surplus, export earnings bring dollars into the country, supporting KRW. Conversely, weak exports or a tendency for Korean companies to hold dollar earnings rather than convert them (hoarding behavior) increases exchange rate pressure. Global trade tensions and slowdowns in Korean semiconductor or auto exports can weaken KRW significantly.

2.3 Global Risk Sentiment

During periods of geopolitical tension or global financial stress, investors tend to move into the dollar as a safe-haven currency. This strengthens the dollar broadly and weakens emerging-market currencies including KRW. In calmer markets, capital returns to higher-yielding assets and KRW may recover.

2.4 Foreign Capital Flows

Large-scale selling of Korean equities or bonds by foreign investors converts KRW to dollars, weakening KRW. Foreign capital inflows have the opposite effect. These flows can cause sharp short-term exchange rate moves that are difficult to anticipate.

Key takeaway: The exchange rate reflects the interaction of interest rates, trade, capital flows, and market sentiment simultaneously. No single indicator reliably predicts its direction, especially in the short term.

3. Conversion Fees — The Hidden Cost of Dollar Investing

Exchange conversion fees are easy to overlook but can significantly erode returns, especially for smaller investment amounts.

3.1 What Is the FX Spread?

The spread is the difference between the mid-market (standard) rate and the rate at which the bank sells you dollars. At bank branches with cash exchange, this spread is typically 1–2%. If the standard rate is 1,400 KRW per dollar, you might pay 1,420–1,430 KRW per dollar at the counter.

3.2 How to Reduce Conversion Fees

Using internet or mobile exchange services, or taking advantage of relationship discounts at your primary bank, can reduce fees by up to 90%. The Korea Federation of Banks' Foreign Exchange Guide (exchange.kfb.or.kr) publishes a comparison of internet FX discount rates across banks. Some internet-only banks and fintech platforms offer near-zero fees. Note that the fee structures for cash exchange and wire transfers to a foreign currency account differ — confirm before transacting.

4. Taxes on Dollar Investing

Dollar investing in Korea has two main tax components: exchange-rate gains (capital gains from currency appreciation) and interest income.

4.1 Exchange-Rate Gains — Generally Tax-Free for Individuals

Under Korea's current Income Tax Act, exchange-rate gains realized by individual residents through foreign currency accounts or USD time deposits are generally not subject to capital gains tax. This means if you buy dollars at 1,300 KRW and later convert them back at 1,500 KRW, the 200 KRW-per-dollar gain is generally not taxed as income for individuals. This tax-free treatment is one of the more attractive features of direct USD holding.

Important: Tax rules are subject to change. Exchange-rate gains from USD ETFs, investment trusts, or derivatives may be taxed differently. Always confirm the current tax treatment with a licensed tax advisor or your financial institution before investing.

4.2 Interest Income — 15.4% Withholding Tax

Interest earned on USD time deposits and foreign currency accounts is generally subject to a 15.4% withholding tax (14% income tax + 1.4% local income tax) in Korea. The tax is deducted before interest is credited to your account, so compare post-tax returns rather than gross rates when evaluating products.

4.3 Comprehensive Financial Income Tax

If your total annual financial income (interest + dividends combined) exceeds KRW 20 million, you become subject to comprehensive financial income taxation. USD deposit interest would be counted toward this threshold. If your investment scale is significant, consult a tax professional.

5. Risks of Dollar Investing

5.1 Exchange Rate Risk — Principal Loss

The most fundamental risk is that if the KRW/USD rate falls after you buy dollars, you will lose money in KRW terms. Buying 1,000 USD at 1,500 KRW/USD costs 1,500,000 KRW. If the rate drops to 1,300, your holding is worth 1,300,000 KRW — a 200,000 KRW loss, regardless of any interest earned.

5.2 Timing Risk

Buying dollars at what appears to be a high exchange rate does not guarantee further appreciation. Exchange rate normalization — driven by Fed rate cuts, Korean economic recovery, or an improvement in the current account — can cause rapid KRW strengthening and significant losses for dollar holders who bought near the peak.

5.3 Opportunity Cost

Holding dollars means forgoing the returns you could have earned on KRW-denominated assets. If the exchange rate stays flat, you receive only the (relatively modest) deposit interest with no currency gain.

5.4 Leveraged ETF Risk

Leveraged and inverse dollar ETFs are not suitable for most retail investors. Leveraged products track daily returns at 2x, which means they can lose value rapidly during volatile or sideways markets due to compounding decay. These are sophisticated instruments.

6. Practical Strategies for Beginners

6.1 Dollar-Cost Averaging

Rather than converting a large sum at once, consider converting a fixed amount to dollars each month. This averages your acquisition cost across different exchange rate levels, reducing the impact of buying at a single unfavorable rate.

6.2 Define Your Purpose and Allocation

Clarify why you want to hold dollars. If you have practical dollar needs (overseas travel, foreign purchases, children's overseas education), buy dollars aligned with those requirements. For portfolio diversification purposes, a common starting point is allocating 10–20% of investable assets to foreign currency exposure — though the appropriate allocation depends heavily on personal circumstances and should be determined in consultation with a financial advisor.

6.3 Start with Bank Products

For beginners, foreign currency demand accounts and USD time deposits at banks are the most accessible and relatively straightforward entry points. They are covered by deposit protection (within limits) and have transparent cost structures. Dollar ETFs and RP products can be considered once you understand the additional risks involved.

7. Common Misconceptions

7.1 "The rate is already high, so it must go higher"

High exchange rates do not guarantee further appreciation. Markets can reverse sharply. Never invest based on the assumption that a trend must continue.

7.2 "USD deposit rates are always higher than KRW deposits"

USD deposit rates reflect US policy rates. As the Fed cuts rates, USD deposit rates fall in tandem. The relative advantage over KRW deposits narrows or disappears in a declining US rate environment.

7.3 "Tax-free gains make dollar investing risk-free"

Tax-free exchange gains are a benefit, but they cannot protect you from principal losses if the exchange rate moves against you. A favorable tax treatment should never be conflated with safety.

8. Conclusion

Dollar investing offers meaningful benefits for Korea-based investors: exchange-rate gain potential, portfolio diversification, and (for individuals) tax-free treatment of currency gains under current rules. However, exchange rate volatility can cause principal losses, and timing the market is extremely difficult even for professionals.

Approach dollar investing as a long-term diversification strategy rather than a short-term trading opportunity. Start with clear goals, a defined allocation, and bank-level products before considering higher-risk instruments.
