How to Choose a Body Text Typeface - Looking Good Large Is Not Reading Well Small

From x-height to the density of Hangul finals, webfont weight and licensing - the conditions that actually operate at small sizes

Aug 27, 2026 Trends/Culture

Opening: Stuck in Front of the Specimen Screen

Picking a face for body text usually goes like this. You open a list, pull up a few specimens, stare at some very large letters, and choose one without knowing what you were supposed to compare. The specimen shows 48 points. The place that face will actually work is a 15-pixel paragraph where a reader stays for twenty minutes. You are judging while the thing you need to judge is not on screen.

What brought this back up is a sans-serif released recently. The streaming service Nebula published its brand face, Nebula Sans, and the description reads as follows: a humanist sans based on Paul Hunt's open-source Source Sans, adjusted to work as a drop-in alternative to Whitney SSm, offered in six weights with italics, including curved punctuation and monospaced numerals, and distributed under the SIL Open Font License.

What is worth noticing is less the face itself than the character of that list. Number of weights, the shape of the punctuation, the width of the numerals — none of it affects a single headline, and all of it only shows up once long text is flowing. A list like that is close to a declaration that the face was made to work in body copy. Two things stated up front: this article does not recommend or rank any typeface, and it does not decide what any licence permits.

1. The Starting Point - Display and Text Are Different Objects

Without this distinction first, everything after it wobbles. A typeface changes how it is seen with size, and so the definition of a good one splits.

At large sizes the eye appreciates one letter at a time. The personality of the shapes, the swing between thick and thin, the craft in the terminals are all visible, and the more distinctive it is the better it looks. In body text the opposite holds. The reading eye moves in short jumps, and the span in sharp focus at each stop is only a few characters wide. The rest is processed as a blurred outline that tells the eye where to jump next. What a text face has to survive is not being handsome but staying distinguishable once blurred.

AxisDisplayTextWhy they split

The last row is the one most often skipped. Specimen screens show large type because large type sells, not because it is evidence.

2. What Builds Readability in Text - Element by Element

Readability is not one property but the result of several. Knowing why each element matters tells you what to look for in a specimen.

ElementWhat it isWhy it affects body textTaken too far
x-height Height of the lowercase x Decides how large the type appears at a given point size Ascenders and descenders shorten and the silhouette flattens
Letter width Horizontal width of a glyph Changes how many characters fit on a line Narrow squeezes the counters and separation collapses
Letter spacing Sidebearings plus pair kerning Decides whether words clump or scatter Too tight and letters merge into other letters
Stroke contrast Difference between thick and thin Thin strokes disappear at small sizes and low resolution Letters look broken into pieces
Open counters Interior space of a, e, s, c and its opening Once filled, different letters converge on the same dot Opened too far, the construction goes slack
Ascenders and descenders The extenders of b, d, k and g, p, y Give a word its top and bottom outline for whole-word recognition Long ones force wider leading

The table repeats a structure. Every element carries a cost in the opposite direction. So asking which value is correct does not work; asking where this face put its weight and whether that choice matches your conditions does.

Composition variables belong in the same view. The same face reads differently depending on leading, line length and contrast against the background. Success Criterion 1.4.12 Text Spacing in WCAG 2.2 is a useful reference. The document sets line height at least 1.5 times the font size, spacing after paragraphs at least 2 times, letter spacing at least 0.12 times and word spacing at least 0.16 times, with no loss of content. These are not values authors must apply as written but a requirement that content still work when a user changes them. Even so, checking whether your combination survives that much loosening reveals how much slack it has.

3. The Devices That Separate Confusable Letters

This is where differences between text faces surface fastest. The Latin alphabet has structurally similar pairs, and faces handle them differently.

  • Lowercase l, capital I, digit 1 — all three reduce to a single vertical stroke. The usual devices are a tail on the l, serifs top and bottom on the I, or a prominent flag on the 1; with none of them, the three become the same shape.
  • Digit 0 and capital O — separated by width, or by a slash or dot inside the zero. A slashed zero is conspicuous in running prose, which is its own cost.
  • The r-n pair against m — rn merging into m happens most readily when spacing is tight.
  • a against o, e against c — these converge as counters fill. This is where open counters earn their keep.
  • Quotes and prime marks — curly quotes, straight quotation marks and the prime marks used for feet and angles are all different shapes. The curved punctuation listed in the face that prompted this article belongs to the same family of concerns.

Checking is simple. Set Il1 | O0 | rn m | B8 | S5 at the size you will actually use, then squint or step back from the screen. If the pairs stay separate once blurred, it passes.

Numerals add one more item. Tabular figures give every digit from 0 to 9 the same advance width, which is what you need when columns of figures have to line up vertically. In running prose, proportional figures often sit better, so a face carrying both sets is easier to work with.

4. Hangul Uses Different Criteria From Latin

The vocabulary so far mostly assumes the Latin alphabet. Parts of it do not transfer to Hangul, and that gap is what makes Hangul text faces hard.

First, the character count differs. The Hangul Syllables area of Unicode holds 11,172 characters formed from 19 initials, 21 medials and 28 finals. Those 28 finals include the case of having no final consonant. That is an order of magnitude away from a Latin text family, and this number is also the cause of the webfont weight problem discussed later.

Second, glyphs are built by composition. Latin letters line up sideways, while Hangul components are assembled inside a single square area. That is where the density problem starts.

  • Presence of a final consonant — a syllable with one and a syllable without occupy the same area with a different number of components. Without adjustment, one looks sparse and the other packed.
  • Vowel orientation — vertical and horizontal vowels move where the consonant sits, and the same consonant demands different proportions in different positions.
  • Stroke density — syllables loaded with strokes fill in from the inside first at low resolution. The role counters play in Latin is played in Hangul by the gaps between components.
  • Similar components — pairs separated by small differences exist here too. It is the same problem as l, I and 1 in Latin.

Third, there is a choice about the frame that holds a glyph. Fitting every syllable to the same square area keeps rows and columns aligned and is strong for tables and vertical setting, but the density difference then has to be absorbed by adjustment. Letting syllables without a final consonant sit smaller evens out the density but leaves the baseline of a line uneven.

Put together: because syllables with and without final consonants are mixed inside every paragraph, drawing one good glyph and evening out the grey of a whole paragraph become two different jobs. That is why two characters in a specimen tell you nothing. Setting a passage dense with final consonants next to one nearly without, and comparing the greyness of the two, tells you far more.

5. When Several Scripts Share One Screen

Hangul and Latin sharing a screen is the default, and kana or Han characters often join them. The problem is that these scripts come from different design traditions.

  • Mismatched size — apparent size in Latin is governed by x-height, while Hangul and Han fill their square almost completely. Set at the same point size, the Latin often looks smaller.
  • Mismatched weight — mixing faces means the same weight name gives different actual stroke thickness. If one side reads darker, the eye is pulled there.
  • Mismatched vertical metrics — each face reserves different space above and below, so a fallback shifts line spacing.
  • Which face owns the punctuation — periods, commas, brackets and quotes differ in width and position depending on which face supplies them. Mixed full-width and half-width forms are especially visible.

There are two ways to handle it. One is a family that designs several scripts together, so size and weight are matched from the start. The other is assigning a face per script. In CSS the order of the font-family list decides which characters come from which face, unicode-range names a face per range, and size-adjust narrows the size gap.

The easy thing to miss is that fallback happens silently. Characters absent from the declared face come from the next one, and that substitution is not reported as an error. A state where Hangul is fine but a few Han characters or symbols wear a different face can ship exactly as it is.

6. Screen and Print Are Different Conditions

A face can be good on paper and disappointing on screen. The device that finally draws the letter is different.

A screen is a pixel grid. Lay an outline on that grid and some pixels fall fully inside while others are only clipped; whether a clipped pixel is turned on makes the stroke heavier or lighter. When that rounding lands differently letter by letter, weights go uneven. Microsoft's TrueType hinting document tackles this head-on. Hinting is a set of instructions that deliberately distorts the outline to decide which pixels are turned on at small sizes and low resolutions, and in the document's words it is indispensable in every font intended to be legible at small sizes.

The same document lists seven things hinting addresses: color, readability, spacing, weight, alignment, symmetry and local aesthetics. Color is the especially useful term here. In typography color means the balance of black and white on the page, and the document explains that uneven color distracts the reader. The discussion of Hangul density in the previous section is exactly this color problem.

  • Screen — the pixel grid, antialiasing, background brightness, and rendering that differs by operating system and browser all intervene. The same file looking different across devices is normal.
  • Print — the grid problem disappears, but ink spread on paper arrives instead. Thin strokes do not vanish; they can thicken.
  • Dark backgrounds — light type on dark looks heavier, so dropping one weight is a frequent adjustment.

One conclusion. A judgment not made under the conditions of delivery is not a judgment. How a face chosen on a high-resolution laptop behaves on a low-resolution office monitor is a separate question.

7. What a Typeface Costs on the Web

On the web a typeface becomes a file that has to be downloaded, and one more criterion attaches. Not whether the face is good, but what fetching it costs. Hangul is structurally disadvantaged here. With 11,172 syllables alone, calling a whole family repeats that weight for every weight you use.

The standard mitigation is subsetting. Split the font with the CSS unicode-range descriptor and the browser decides per slice by looking at the page. MDN's description puts it plainly: if the page does not use any character in this range, the font is not downloaded; if it uses at least one, the whole font is downloaded. Finer slices waste less and cost more requests.

The flash during loading is a separate item. What the screen does before the face arrives is decided by font-display, and MDN defines each value as a combination of a block period and a swap period.

ValueBlock periodSwap periodWhat the reader gets
block Short Infinite Text is briefly invisible, then appears
swap Extremely small Infinite Fallback reads first, then swaps
fallback Extremely small Short A late arrival does not swap on that visit
optional Extremely small None Late means unused, so nothing shifts
auto The user agent decides Results vary by environment

Whichever value you pick, actually declaring a fallback face is the precondition. If the two differ much in size, text shifts at the swap and the page jumps, so apply size adjustment to the fallback to match the space it occupies.

  • Cut the number of weights — call only what you use. Two, for body and emphasis, is often enough.
  • Match the subset range to the content — dropping scripts and symbols you never set shrinks it.
  • Treat display separately, and keep system faces as candidates — headline glyphs are few enough to pack individually, and the option that downloads nothing is always the fastest.

8. Accessibility - Within What Is Established

The relation between typeface choice and reading difficulty is discussed often, but the range that can be stated flatly is narrow.

There are faces designed specifically for dyslexia. They advertise a design intent of heavier bottoms or asymmetric shapes to reduce mirror-image confusion. Whether that intent translates into reading performance has been tested. The study by Kuster and colleagues in Annals of Dyslexia checked it in two experiments. The first involved 170 children with dyslexia aged seven to twelve; the second involved 102 children with dyslexia and 45 without. Reading speed and error rates showed no difference between the specialised face and ordinary faces, and most participants preferred the familiar ordinary faces. The conclusion was that the face neither benefits nor impedes reading, and a later meta-analysis in the same journal points the same way.

The care needed is about how far to extend that result. What is established is that the claim of specialised faces outperforming ordinary ones was not supported, not that typography has no effect on reading. What accessibility guidance actually asks for lives in composition, contrast and the user's ability to adjust, not in the name of a face.

  • Set body text large enough and do not block user adjustment — nailing size in fixed pixels hurts zooming.
  • Secure contrast against the background — pale grey body text is not rescued by the typeface.
  • Limit line length — long lines make the start of the next line hard to find.
  • Avoid centred and justified setting in long text — line starts wander, or word spacing opens up.
  • Avoid all caps and heavy tracking — both erase the silhouette cues of a word.

9. Why the Licence Has to Be Checked

Holding the file and being allowed to use it the way you intend are separate things. Each way of using it needs its own permission, and one being granted does not bring the others.

  • Installing and setting — installing on a machine to produce documents. Seat limits are common.
  • Serving as a webfont — uploading to a server and sending it to visitors' browsers. Often treated apart from installation rights.
  • Embedding in documents — including glyph data in a PDF or a presentation.
  • Shipping in apps and devices — bundling into an installer or firmware. A classic item with separate terms.
  • Redistributing or modifying — passing the file on or putting it in a repository, adding glyphs or deriving weights.
  • Turning it into a logo — converting letters to outlines and filing them as a trademark. Sometimes requires separate permission.

Widely used open licences are not condition-free either. The SIL Open Font License FAQ alone lists several things to verify. Redistribution must include the copyright statement, the license notice and the license text. Selling the font on its own is not allowed, while selling a software package that includes it is. Format conversion has to be lossless with metadata preserved for the original name to be kept, and the Reserved Font Names provision means a modified derivative must carry a different name.

This article does not decide any individual font's licence. The same name can carry different terms depending on the distributor and version, and licences get revised. What you check is not a summary blurb but the licence text distributed with the font. If it is for work, open that file and keep it.

10. The Order to Choose In

Turning the above into a procedure gives this. Holding on early to something that will fail a later stage is where the time goes.

  • First, write down the conditions of use — which screens and pages, at what size, with which scripts mixed, over how much text. Without that list, judgment drifts into taste.
  • Second, filter by licence before anything else — a candidate that does not permit your intended use is not a candidate, however good. Checking last makes reversal expensive.
  • Third, confirm the character coverage — Hangul and Latin of course, plus figures, symbols and punctuation. Gaps mean silent fallback.
  • Fourth, look at the weight range — enough to build hierarchy, and actually distinguishable from one another.
  • Fifth, set your real sentences at real size — your content, not a pangram. For Hangul, place a dense passage beside a sparse one, and check the confusable pairs here too.
  • Sixth, review under delivery conditions and count the weight — low-resolution screens, dark backgrounds, print. On the web, fix the number of weights, the subset and the swap policy, then read for ten minutes and watch for fatigue.

The benefit of this order is that it postpones the place where taste enters. After filtering by conditions, licence and coverage, the shortlist is usually a handful, and within it nothing you pick is badly wrong. Reverse the order and you have to give up the one you liked, by which point you have mockups built with it.

Back at the face that prompted this, the items in its description now read differently. Weight count was about whether hierarchy is possible; monospaced numerals about whether columns of figures stay steady; punctuation shape about what face those marks wear inside a paragraph. Naming the licence was a procedure that lets the user check whether they may use it. Choosing a typeface comes down to matching those items against your own conditions, one at a time.

What Could Not Be Verified

The parts with no evidence behind them, gathered in one place.

  • How many percent any single element changes reading speed — no quantitative effect for x-height or spacing is offered here. The material checked does not support numbers.
  • Comparative readability of Hangul text faces — no published comparison on the scale available for Latin was found. Section 4 states properties that follow from the composition structure, not the merits of particular faces.
  • The actual setting quality of the face that prompted this — only the published specification is reported; it was not tested under body-text conditions.
  • Concrete webfont file sizes — these vary with subsetting, weight count and compression, so no figures are given and only the structural cause of character count is covered.
