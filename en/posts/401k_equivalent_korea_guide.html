Introduction: the balance grows and nobody invests it

Ask a Korean office worker whether they are enrolled in a retirement pension and almost everyone says yes. Ask whether it is a DB or a DC plan and the answers split. Ask a DC member what the balance is currently invested in and most do not know. That is not strange. The employer pays it in, the statement arrives rarely, and when it does arrive there is a single product name on it.

In a DC plan, though, not knowing becomes a choice. Under Korean law a defined contribution plan is one where the level of the employer contribution is fixed in advance and the benefit varies with investment performance (Employee Retirement Benefit Security Act, Article 2(9)). If performance is poor the benefit shrinks, and the employer does not top it up. Declining to invest is itself a decision, and the consequences of that decision belong entirely to the member.

Here is the judgment up front. The most common mistake in a DC plan is not picking something risky. It is picking nothing at all. And before deciding what to buy, three things have to be settled: the years remaining until retirement, how much loss you can actually absorb, and what share of your total assets this account represents. Once those three are fixed the product list narrows on its own. Without them, no product recommendation has any grounding.

This article stays on two axes: who carries the investment decision, and where American 401(k) advice breaks when copied across. Tax credit limits and account selection are covered in the pension savings and IRP tax guide, and severance calculation and retirement income tax in the severance pay and retirement pension guide, so those are handed off by link rather than repeated here.

Scope and date. Everything below describes the Korean retirement pension system as of August 2026, with the verification date noted in each table. Readers outside Korea should treat the Korean figures as a comparison case and confirm the rules of their own jurisdiction; the 401(k) figures cited are US federal rules and apply only to US plans. Pension rules change through legislative amendment and supervisory regulation. Before acting, check your workplace pension rules and the Financial Supervisory Service Integrated Pension Portal.

1. Who carries the investment decision - the one line that splits DB, DC and IRP

There are many ways to describe the three plan types, but if you want to know what actually changes, one axis is enough. When the investment result is bad, who absorbs it?

The statute draws the definitions this way. A defined benefit plan (DB) is one where the level of benefit the worker will receive is determined in advance (Article 2(8)). A defined contribution plan (DC) is one where the level of the employer's contribution is determined in advance and the benefit varies with investment performance (Article 2(9)). An individual retirement pension (IRP) is a plan where the worker can open an account and pay additional contributions beyond the employer's (Article 2(10) and Article 24(3)).

1.1 In a DB plan there is exactly one thing to watch

A DB member has no reason to check products monthly. The employer invests and the employer carries the result. Something else deserves attention instead: is the employer funding the plan at the level it should be? If the funding ratio is thin and the business runs into trouble, the risk lands back on the workers. The longer the service and the larger the accrued benefit, the more weight this item carries.

What really decides DB advantage is the wage curve. If pay keeps rising until the final year, DB tilts favourable. If a wage peak arrangement is scheduled, the average wage used in the benefit formula can fall, which is why a switch to DC is often reviewed before that phase begins. Note that changing the plan type requires amending the pension rules with worker representative consent, and the period after the switch is hard to reverse.

1.2 A DC plan hands you a job, not an account

Joining a DC plan means receiving a task, not a container. The task does not need daily attention; once or twice a year, on a fixed checklist, is enough. It is because almost nobody does that once or twice that the statistics in the next section look the way they do.

2. What actually happens when nobody decides

The annual statistics published jointly by Korea's Ministry of Employment and Labor and the Financial Supervisory Service answer this. As of end-2025, total retirement pension assets stood at 501.4 trillion won, up roughly 70 trillion won from 431.7 trillion won a year earlier, a 16.8 percent increase. By plan type, DC and IRP together made up 54.3 percent. In other words, more than half of the money now moves only if an individual instructs it.

Where that money sits is the problem. At end-2025, performance-based assets came to 123.3 trillion won, or 24.6 percent of the total, with the remainder in principal-guaranteed products. That share doubled in three years and still leaves three out of four won on the guaranteed side.

There is a standard misreading here: taking the 2023 gap between 4.08 percent and 13.27 percent and multiplying it across the next thirty years. A single year's return reflects that year's market, and performance-based products have losing years too. What the statistics tell you is not future return but where Korean pension assets are currently parked.

2.1 The same drift shows up at payout

It is not only the investing that gets left alone. So does the payout. In the 2023 statistics, 10.4 percent of new recipients took the money as an annuity on an account-count basis, with the other 89.6 percent taking a lump sum. Measured by amount, the annuity share rises to 49.7 percent. Larger balances get annuitised; small accounts are mostly cashed out.

This matters because a lump sum ends tax deferral on the spot. How much to draw and at what pace is a separate question, handled in the retirement withdrawal strategy guide.

3. Did the default option fix the drift?

The government saw the problem and answered with a rule. The pre-designated investment method, commonly called the default option, came from an amendment to the Employee Retirement Benefit Security Act passed on December 9, 2021 and took effect on July 12, 2022, covering DC and IRP accounts.

It works like this. If a member gives no investment instruction for four weeks after an existing product matures, the pension provider issues a notice, and if there is still no instruction within two weeks of that notice, the balance moves into the method designated in advance. Eligible products are principal-guaranteed products such as deposits and GICs, funds permitted by law, and portfolio-type products, and only those approved by the Minister after review by a committee chaired by the Vice Minister of Employment and Labor reach the menu.

So was the drift fixed? Half of it. At end-2025 default option assets stood at 53.3 trillion won, of which 85.4 percent sat in the ultra-low-risk tier, returning 3.7 percent. In the same year target date funds held 20.1 trillion won and returned 13.7 percent.

Read it this way: the system automated the state of neglect, not the asset allocation judgment. Which risk tier to designate is still the member's pick. Concentration in the ultra-low-risk tier means people chose whatever looked safest at the designation step, and that same pick is being applied by a new graduate and by someone two years from retirement alike.

4. Seven points where this diverges from a 401(k)

Conversations about Korean retirement pensions summon the 401(k) quickly. Both are defined contribution, but they start from different places. The 401(k) grew out of employees deferring their own salary; Korea's system grew out of an employer's obligation to pay severance. That single difference generates almost every row below.

4.1 The concept of a match does not exist

The most quoted line in 401(k) advice is always contribute up to the full employer match. Where a company adds a dollar for every dollar you put in, that stretch earns a return unrelated to markets, so the advice is sound. Korea's system has no such structure. The employer pays the statutory contribution regardless of whether the worker contributes, and there is no default mechanism for carving money out of a paycheck into the plan.

The nearest Korean equivalent is not a match but a tax credit. Money you add to an IRP or a pension savings account returns tax, not employer cash. Limits and credit rates vary by income bracket, and those calculations are set out in the pension savings and IRP tax guide and the pension savings fund guide.

4.2 Automation points in opposite directions

The United States moved to automate enrollment itself. Section 101 of SECURE 2.0 requires 401(k) and 403(b) plans established after December 29, 2022 to enroll eligible participants automatically, at an initial rate between 3 and 10 percent, escalating by one percentage point a year to at least 10 percent and no more than 15 percent. Employers with 10 or fewer employees, businesses under three years old, and pre-existing plans are excluded.

In Korea enrollment is already institutional. The gap needing automation was not joining but what happens after joining, which is what the default option addresses. The same word, automatic, points at different problems on each side.

5. The actual Korean rules - risky asset caps and investment regulation

What a DC or IRP member can do inside the account has a ceiling. No more than 70 percent of the balance may go into risky assets. The Ministry of Employment and Labor confirmed both the cap and its exception when announcing the default option, stating that notwithstanding the existing 70 percent risky asset limit, a pre-designated investment method may run up to 100 percent of the balance.

Three consequences follow in practice.

  • A direct instruction cannot build a 100 percent equity position. The remaining 30 percent goes to principal-guaranteed or non-risky assets
  • Some products, such as target date funds, are treated differently in the cap calculation. Product-level treatment has to be confirmed in the prospectus and provider guidance
  • A designated default method opens the full 100 percent. For anyone wanting a more aggressive stance, that exception is the practical route

To draw the money as an annuity you must apply at age 55 or older and five years must have passed since the pension account was opened. Because of that five-year clock, opening an account for the first time close to 55 can block annuity treatment at the moment you want it. That is why opening the account early is itself an act that widens the option set.

6. The size of the exit door - mid-term withdrawal

This is where the two systems part most sharply. The American structure lets you leave but charges for it. Withdrawals before age 59 and a half carry a 10 percent additional tax, and in exchange the exception list is long: separation from service at 55 or older (50 for public safety employees), qualified domestic relations orders, disability, death, substantially equal periodic payments, medical expenses, emergency personal expense, domestic abuse, terminal illness. Note that the first-time homebuyer allowance of 10,000 dollars, education costs and health insurance premiums while unemployed apply to IRAs only, not to 401(k) plans.

Korea keeps the door narrow to begin with. Being willing to pay more tax does not open it; if the ground does not apply, the withdrawal simply cannot happen. Grounds for DC members are set by Article 22 of the Employee Retirement Benefit Security Act and Article 14 of its Enforcement Decree.

A DB plan has no mid-term withdrawal mechanism at all. The grounds above apply to DC members. In practice that difference is one more thing to weigh when a plan type is on the table: if you want a route to liquidity, DC has a door, and if you would rather the door not exist, DB works as forced saving.

One judgment here. Breaking into a pension to buy a home is more expensive than it looks. Tax deferral ends the moment the money leaves, a tax charge arises at that point, and above all the compounding built up to that date disappears. Structurally it belongs as the last card after other liquidity has been exhausted.

7. Where fees leak

Cost attaches in two layers. One is the operating and asset management fee charged by the pension provider; the other is the expense ratio of the fund or ETF bought inside the account. The first varies by provider and plan type, the second by the product chosen.

The figure captured in official statistics is the total cost ratio. For 2023 it was 0.372 percent, down 0.02 percentage points from 0.392 percent the year before. It looks small, but as a charge applied every year for thirty years it outlasts most product decisions.

  • Provider fees are not negotiable, they are selectable. An individual IRP can move between financial institutions, and rates differ
  • Two products of the same type can carry different expense ratios. The total is printed in the prospectus
  • Cost is part of why ETF use grew. At end-2025, ETF holdings in Korean retirement pensions reached 48.7 trillion won, about 40 percent of the 123.3 trillion won in performance-based assets
  • Unlike return, cost is a known quantity. Future performance is unknown; the fee is fixed at contract time

8. The frame of a strategy - an order of decisions, not a product list

Plenty of writing answers the question of what to buy, and most of it skips the three inputs: time remaining, tolerable loss, and share of total assets. Settle those and the choices narrow by themselves. The order runs like this.

8.1 Step 1 - confirm which plan you are in

If it is DB, the steps below are the employer's work and yours is to read the wage curve and decide about switching. If it is DC or IRP, keep going. It is common to hold a mix of DB and DC periods across jobs, so check workplace by workplace.

8.2 Step 2 - write the years remaining as a number

How many years are left largely determines how much risk is bearable. With time to recover, volatility is a cost you can absorb; without it, the same volatility becomes a realised loss.

Years to retirementThe axis to check firstCommon mistake
20 or moreCan this mix outpace inflation?Holding everything in guaranteed products and calling it safe
10 to 20Is there a rebalancing rule?Setting it once and never returning
5 to 10Is there a path that reduces volatility?Chasing recovery and raising risk instead
Under 5Are the payout date and method settled?Discovering the five-year annuity rule too late

8.3 Step 3 - write down the loss you can absorb

Risk tolerance is only useful when written as a number of won, not as a questionnaire result. If the balance is 50 million won, can you watch it fall by 5 million in a year without switching products? If the answer does not come, that allocation will not survive. Selling into a decline damages outcomes more than a mediocre allocation does.

8.4 Step 4 - look at the share of total assets

Judging the pension aggressive or conservative in isolation means little. What role this account plays in the whole picture, alongside the national pension, housing, deposits and personal investments, comes first. If most of your net worth is already tied up in a home, the pension's character changes. The three-layer view is set out in the retirement preparation guide, and the first layer in the national pension guide.

8.5 Step 5 - fix a rebalancing rule and put it on the calendar

The last step is automation. An allocation that gets followed beats a better one that does not.

  • Set the interval. Once or twice a year, decided in advance rather than by market conditions
  • Set the trigger. How many percentage points of drift from target brings you back
  • Fix the checklist. Current allocation, cumulative return, total cost, default option designation
  • Write down what you will not change. Name the items to leave alone until the next check
  • Keep a record. One line on why you chose it makes the next review much faster

9. Where the account breaks when you change jobs

Retirement benefits transfer as a rule to an individual IRP account in the worker's name. Exceptions where transfer is not required include separation at age 55 or older, repayment of a secured loan, benefits of 3 million won or less, death, emigration, and statutory deductions.

The failures happen not at the transfer but after it. Money arriving in the new account usually sits in a holding state, and without a fresh instruction it stays there for months. Items to check around a job change:

  • Is the transferred amount right? Whether the calculation period and average wage were applied correctly
  • What is it invested in now? Whether it is still sitting idle
  • Has a pre-designated investment method been chosen? A new provider needs a fresh designation
  • How did the fee structure change? A different provider means different rates
  • How will you handle the pull toward a lump sum? Taking it ends tax deferral immediately
  • Do you actually need several accounts? Keeping the count manageable reduces missed reviews

How retirement income tax is calculated and how annuity and lump sum payouts differ in tax treatment is covered in the severance pay and retirement pension guide.

11. Why 401(k) advice breaks when copied across

English-language retirement content is plentiful and often good. The trouble is that it was written on top of American defaults. The points that break in transit:

  • Always capture the full match — no counterpart exists. The nearest Korean priority is the tax credit
  • Start at 100 percent equities — direct instruction caps risky assets at 70 percent. The full 100 opens only through the default option route
  • Use a hardship withdrawal — in Korea it is not a question of accepting a penalty but of whether the ground applies
  • Plan withdrawals around RMDs — Korea has no equivalent minimum distribution duty. It has the age 55 and five-year rules instead
  • Convert to Roth and pay tax early — the Korean retirement pension is not structured with that same option
  • Roll it over when you change jobs — the direction matches, but in Korea IRP transfer is the rule and the exceptions are set by statute

Something is worth importing: the philosophy of automation. SECURE 2.0 pushed automatic enrollment and automatic escalation because people postpone decisions, and that diagnosis does not depend on nationality. The Korean equivalent in practice is choosing a pre-designated investment method deliberately and nailing the rebalancing date to a calendar.

The same trap appears when asking generative AI about pensions or tax. English-language material dominates training data, so a question about the Korean system frequently comes back framed in 401(k) terms. How to phrase questions so that error shrinks is covered in the guide to prompting AI about money.

12. What could not be confirmed

The following were not written as figures or assertions because a primary source could not be pinned down.

  • Sub-caps by risky asset type in the supervisory regulation. The 70 percent aggregate cap was confirmed through a ministry release, but per-type limits and the prohibited asset list could not be read from the regulation text, so no numbers were carried over
  • Separate 2025 returns for principal-guaranteed and performance-based products. The overall 6.5 percent and the default option and TDF returns were confirmed; the split by investment method was not. The 4.08 and 13.27 percent figures in this article are 2023 values
  • The 2025 total cost ratio. The 0.372 percent figure is a 2023 value
  • The current minimum funding ratio for DB plans. Only the importance of checking funding level is stated; no ratio is given
  • How TDFs are treated in the risky asset cap calculation. Guidance varies by product, so it was not generalised. It belongs in the individual prospectus
  • Typical employer match levels in 401(k) plans. Matching varies by plan and is not a statutory rate, so no representative figure is offered

Conclusion: not choosing is also a choice

A Korean DC retirement pension is a system where the employer supplies the money and leaves the consequences with the individual. Do nothing inside that structure and the doing-nothing becomes an allocation. In an era of 500 trillion won in assets, the fact that only one won in four sits in performance-based products and that 85 percent of default option money is in the ultra-low-risk tier reads less like risk aversion and more like postponed decisions.

Comparing with the 401(k) is useful not to rank the two systems but because how far the system decides for you, and where your own share begins, differs by country. The United States automated enrollment; Korea automated investment. Either way, the person choosing the tier at the end is the member.

Three things can be done today: confirm whether your plan is DB or DC, open the DC account once to see what it is actually invested in, and put the next review date on a calendar. Changing products comes after that, and those three usually settle most of it.
