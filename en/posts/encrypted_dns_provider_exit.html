Who Answers When You Ask for a Name - What Encrypted DNS Hides and What It Leaves Behind

One public resolver is closing its doors, which is a good moment to redraw what encryption actually covers and which layer the setting lives in

2026-09-06 Tech & IT

Type a domain into the address bar and, before the page changes, a quiet exchange takes place. Something asks what address belongs to this name, and something answers with a number. The exchange never appears on screen, and it usually finishes in a few tens of milliseconds, so nobody notices it. Yet the party with the most accurate list of every site visited in a day is not any of those sites. It is whoever accepted that question.

That makes the choice of who answers weigh a good deal more than a single configuration field normally does. And this particular field has an unusual property: it is set once and then forgotten. Somebody read an article years ago, typed four numbers into a router, and never opened that screen again. Now a free public encrypted DNS service has published a shutdown date, and every place those numbers were typed is about to become visible at once.

Taken on its own the announcement is a small event. What makes it worth stopping for is the picture it lets us redraw. What exactly does encrypting DNS hide, and what remains in plain view? And how many separate layers is that setting scattered across today, and which of them wins when they disagree? There is no recommendation for a replacement service anywhere below. There is only the question of what to read before choosing one.

Every technical statement below stays inside what the relevant RFC or the vendor's own public documentation says. No particular service is recommended, and configurations aimed at circumventing blocking are out of scope. Anything without a documented basis is collected in a single section near the end rather than filled in by guesswork.

The trigger - one public resolver closes

On 3 September 2026, Mullvad announced that it is shutting down its public encrypted DNS servers and redirecting those resources into financial support for Quad9. The service had run since 2022 in the form anyone could use simply by typing an address into a settings field. The deadline in the announcement is 2 November 2026, and after that date any manually entered configuration stops receiving answers.

Headlines usually carry this as encrypted DNS shutting down. It is far more useful to draw the boundary of what is actually ending. Put the announcement and the help documentation side by side and the line falls like this.

  • What closes — the public resolver that anyone could point at by address. According to the help page, it was offered over both DoH and DoT, split across six hostnames covering variations such as ad blocking and family filtering. The notice on that page reads that the public encrypted DNS service will be discontinued on 2 November 2026
  • What does not close — the internal DNS that handles queries while the company's VPN is in use. The announcement states that the public servers are unnecessary with the VPN running, because that traffic is already encrypted and the internal DNS handles all queries
  • What moves automatically — users of the same company's browser who kept the default DoH setting or the bundled ad blocking one are migrated to Quad9 automatically. By contrast, customised configurations are left untouched. In other words, the more deliberately somebody configured this, the more they have to move it themselves

The stated reason is in the announcement too. Running a privacy focused public DNS service is described as a highly specialised undertaking, and rather than duplicating the efforts of an organisation already doing it in order to achieve only part of what they do, the resources go toward supporting that organisation financially. The part worth carrying away is not one company's decision but the underlying fact: operating a public resolver is a continuing cost. Something being free to use never meant it was free to run.

The lookup path - where a query travels during one connection

Laying out what passes through where during a single connection is worth the space, because everything that follows sits on top of this one diagram.

First, the stub resolver inside the device asks a recursive resolver for a name. If the recursive resolver already holds the answer it returns it from cache, and if it does not, it walks down from the root through the top level domain to the authoritative servers, gathering the answer as it goes. That descent is a question of delegation, and what each layer of it hands to whom is laid out in What You Hand Over When You Move Your Nameservers. Where the returned answer is stored and for how long is the subject of What DNS Caching Teaches. This article looks at the segment before all of that: the single hop between the device and the recursive resolver.

Once the answer arrives, the device opens a TCP connection to that IP address and begins a TLS handshake. From there it is no longer DNS territory. From a privacy standpoint, however, the two stages are joined at the hip, because a connection to that very name opens immediately afterwards.

So it is more precise to split observers into three positions. The three see different things, and encryption addresses only one of them.

  • The observer on the path — equipment sharing the same wireless segment, intermediate networks, and the provider of the access itself. This position sees the packets moving between the device and the resolver
  • The resolver operator — the party that receives the query and answers it. Handling the request means handling both what was asked and who asked it
  • Whoever watches the connection that follows — the position that sees traffic heading toward the destination IP. It sees an address rather than a name, and depending on the case it also sees the server name carried in the handshake

What the specifications define - DoH, DoT and DoQ

What gets lumped together as encrypted DNS is in fact three separate specifications. All three target the segment between the device and the recursive resolver, and the difference lies in which transport they sit on.

The phrase that repeats in the last column is the heart of this article. All three specifications eliminate observation within the network. They promise nothing beyond that. The documents themselves draw the boundary that narrowly.

In practice, the place where the three differ is not a grade of safety but distinguishability. A method on a dedicated port is easy for network equipment to separate from everything else. A method on the web port is not. That property counts as an advantage and as a drawback depending on whether you are the administrator or the user, and no ranking between them falls out of the specification text.

What gets hidden and what does not

Drawing the covered range observer by observer is worth doing carefully, because every later decision derives from this table. The left column is the observer, the two middle columns are what that observer sees, and the right column is the basis.

ObserverVisible with plain DNSVisible with encrypted DNSBasis
Equipment on the same wireless segment, intermediate networks Every domain name asked for, the timing, and the repetition pattern The fact that the device talked to a resolver. The names are not visible. The specification does leave open that analysis of message timings and sizes can still reveal certain details RFC 7858
The provider of the network access Every domain name, and the destination IP of the connection that follows Names are covered. However, the destination IP of the following connection remains, and if the Server Name Indication in the handshake is in the clear, that name is visible too RFC 8744
The resolver operator Every query and the address it came from Nothing changes. The specification states that recursive resolvers see all the traffic since there is typically no caching before them, and summarises it as the recursive resolver knowing a great deal about you RFC 9076
The destination server being contacted The fact of the connection and its IP Nothing changes. DNS encryption does not touch this position at all Out of scope
Software running on the device itself Whatever it looked up on its own Nothing changes. An application with its own resolver goes out by its own path regardless of the operating system setting Out of scope

The third row is where this topic gets inverted most often. Turning on encrypted DNS does not mean nobody can see. One category drops off the list of watchers, and in its place the one you newly chose stays there. RFC 9076 adds one more sentence to this: as an increasing proportion of global DNS resolution traffic is served by only a few entities, users' privacy considerations become highly dependent on the privacy policies and practices of those entities.

What remains after the resolver - SNI and ECH

The TLS connection that opens after the name is resolved carries the Server Name Indication. When many sites sit behind one IP address, the server has to know which of them a request is for, so the client writes the intended name into its first message. The problem is that this first message has not yet been encrypted. RFC 8744, which sets out the issues and requirements for SNI encryption, starts from exactly this: the extension travels in the clear inside the client hello.

So with DNS alone encrypted, the sequence looks like this. The exchange asking for the name is covered, and then the first message of the connection that opens moments later writes that same name out in plain text. What was hidden gets shown again a few milliseconds later.

The specification aimed at this gap is RFC 9849, TLS Encrypted Client Hello, published on the standards track in March 2026. It defines a mechanism for encrypting the client hello under a server public key. Once that is widely deployed, the destination name disappears from the first message.

There is a structure here worth turning over, though. RFC 9849 defines the format of the configuration but delegates the details of DNS publication to other documents. How the configuration is advertised in SVCB and HTTPS records is handled by RFC 9848, and the record format itself is defined by RFC 9460. Which is to say the key needed to hide the name arrives over DNS. The specification does note that other delivery mechanisms are possible, giving a preconfigured client as an example. Still, on the ordinary path, the act of asking for a name comes before the act of hiding it.

The upshot is that the question of where to ask for names does not narrow over time. It gets tangled with more things. A single lookup path is now bound up simultaneously with caching, delegation, certificate issuance and handshake encryption.

Trust does not disappear, it relocates

Follow this far and the conclusion becomes simple. Turning on encrypted DNS is not an operation that removes risk but one that changes who is trusted. Previously the access provider handed out the default resolver and saw the queries. Now the resolver operator you picked yourself sees them. The name on the list changed. The list did not empty.

The same structure repeats with other tools. Open a tunnel and the tunnel operator takes the place the access provider held. What relocates and what stays put is split out segment by segment in What a VPN Actually Protects and What It Does Not. DNS follows precisely the same grammar.

Which is why the shape of the question has to change. Not is this safe, but who am I newly deciding to trust and can I undo that decision later. That second question is the one the shutdown announcement really puts on the table.

What to read when choosing - the range a document can answer for

Pull the selection criteria out of the documents and each item arrives with its own limit attached: what it settles and what it cannot settle. Keeping those two columns next to each other is the whole trick. Read only the guarantee column and a document looks like it is promising far more than it is.

Beyond that only aggregate, non-identifying data is allowed Whether the ceiling written in the document matches the running implementation is not something the document can prove The provider's resolver specific privacy notice

One row is deliberately missing from this table: which one is good. What a document can settle is the shape of a promise and the organisational conditions for keeping it. Everything past that is answered by audits and by time, not by text.

What breaks first when a free public service shuts down

What people look at in a shutdown notice is the date. What actually causes trouble is not the date but the inability to remember everywhere the address was typed. If it went into the router, into a laptop's settings, into a browser's settings and into a server's configuration file over the course of several years, those four places break at four different moments.

Where the address is writtenSymptom at shutdownWhere the fix goesTime until noticed
The router's DHCP distribution Every device on that network stops resolving names at the same moment. It looks like the internet went down The DNS field in the router's admin page Immediately, and loudly
Manual entry in the OS, encrypted only Name resolution fails on that device. Microsoft documentation states that with this setting resolution will not occur if the target server cannot support the encrypted queries The operating system's network settings Immediately, on that device
Manual entry in the OS, encrypted preferred It quietly drops to plain text. The same document states that with this setting you will not be provided with any notification when queries switch from encrypted to plain text The operating system's network settings Never noticed
A custom provider in the browser Only that browser fails to resolve. Every other application is fine, which makes the browser a hard place to look The browser's security settings page Immediately, in that browser only
The browser left on its default If the provider treats it as an automatic migration, nothing happens at all. Users on the browser default in this announcement are in that position Nothing to touch Never noticed
A server config file or a container image Only that workload fails, and usually at redeploy or restart, so the outage date drifts away from the shutdown date Config files and image build definitions At the next deployment

The third row is the worst cell in this table, because it is a configuration whose failure is invisible. When name resolution stops, it gets fixed immediately. When it quietly drops to plain text, people go on believing it is enabled for months. Choosing a failure mode is therefore the same act as choosing when you will find out.

The fifth row is worth turning over too. The people who left the default alone experience nothing. The people who configured it carefully have to move it themselves. The familiar trade of taking more control and receiving more maintenance duty holds here as well.

Where the setting lives - layers and precedence

Most of the real confusion in this topic comes from the fact that the setting is not in one place. It can be set once in the router, once in the operating system and once in the browser, and all three can point somewhere different. When they disagree, the result comes out differently from what the user expects. The table below lays out what each layer covers, what happens when it collides with another, and how to see the current state.

Where it is setScopeWhen it collides with another settingHow to check
The router's DHCP distribution The default for every device on that network. It only applies where the device has not decided for itself Weakest of all. If a value is pinned in the device or the browser, the lookup goes out with the pinned value The DHCP section of the router admin page, and the address the device received
The operating system's DNS server entry Every program on that device that uses the operating system resolver Overrides the router value. It cannot override an application that ships its own resolver A PowerShell query on Windows, the system resolver status on Linux and macOS
The operating system's encryption option Adds a plain text fallback policy on top of the same scope. Windows offers encrypted only, encrypted preferred, and unencrypted only A sub setting inside the same layer. It decides how to reach the address after the address has been decided The DNS encryption field on the network adapter
Private DNS on a mobile operating system The whole device. Android's guidance says that by default the device uses it with all networks that can use it, and offers off, automatic and a provider hostname Takes precedence over what the network hands out. The same guidance notes that the feature secures only DNS questions and answers and cannot protect anything else The private DNS entry under network settings
Secure DNS in the browser Only that browser's lookups. Other applications on the same device are unaffected Inside that browser it overrides the operating system value, though there are several conditions under which it steps back The browser's security settings page
Organisational management policy Managed devices and browsers. Windows group policy offers allow, prohibit and require Takes precedence over user settings. Google's guidance states that if the device is managed or parental controls are turned on, the secure DNS feature cannot be used at all Policy result queries, the browser's managed status indicator
The application's own setting Only that application. Programs with a built in resolver belong here Narrowest and strongest. That application's lookups end up following that application's setting The app's network settings screen or configuration file

The rule running through the table condenses to one line. The narrower the layer, the stronger it is. What the network hands out is weakest, and what an application decided inside itself is strongest. The exception is organisational policy, which is broad in scope yet designed to override user settings.

So the sentence saying it is turned on carries no information by itself. It needs the layer attached before it has a scope. Turn it on in the browser and the messenger and the updater on the same laptop still go out by a different path.

The conditions under which a layer steps back on its own

The picture only closes once one more cell is added to the layer table: the case where it is turned on but does not turn on. Both browsers and operating systems have documentation covering this.

Mozilla's developer documentation lists the reasons the trusted recursive resolver path is skipped in favour of the native resolver. These are the items that actually come up.

  • The user has explicitly disabled the relevant preference
  • System level parental controls are enabled
  • Requests marked to skip that path, such as captive portal and connectivity checks
  • Names present on the excluded domains list
  • The browser has no connectivity, or the system is asleep
  • A VPN, a proxy, or an invalid TLS configuration is detected
  • Enterprise policy is detected, or the certificate roots have been modified

Google's guidance for Chrome is shorter. In automatic mode the browser may fall back to unencrypted lookups if something goes wrong, and with a custom provider selected it will not fall back. And it states plainly that if the device is managed or parental controls are turned on, the feature cannot be used at all.

On the operating system side the most practical sentence sits in Microsoft's documentation. The encrypted preferred setting offers the best compatibility, but gives no notification when queries switch from encrypted to plain text. That same document also warns against enabling the require option on domain joined machines, because the directory service leans heavily on DNS while the server DNS service does not support those queries. This is where the incidents of breaking sign in while tightening a corporate configuration come from.

That is why a procedure for seeing which path is currently in use is worth having. Windows can list the per adapter server addresses and the known encrypted server list separately.

# DNS server addresses configured on the adapter
Get-DnsClientServerAddress -AddressFamily IPv4

# Encrypted DNS servers this system knows about
Get-DnsClientDohServerAddress

Linux and macOS expose the system resolver state directly, and which server actually answered a given lookup shows up in the server line of the query result.

# systemd-resolved: per interface DNS servers and encryption status
resolvectl status

# macOS: the resolvers the system recognises
scutil --dns

# The server address that actually answered
dig example.com | grep SERVER

If those values differ from expectation, walking down the layer table from whichever layers outrank the one you set will surface the cause. It is normal for a system query to point at a different server than the one you configured in the browser. The two layers are performing different lookups.

How networks now designate a resolver - DDR and DNR

Typing an address by hand carries an old problem with it. An IP address alone is not enough to use an encrypted resolver. You also need a hostname, a port, a protocol and in some cases a URI template. Yet the traditional provisioning mechanisms hand out nothing but IP addresses.

Two specifications published in November 2023 close that gap. RFC 9462, Discovery of Designated Resolvers, defines mechanisms for a client to discover a resolver's encrypted DNS configuration from DNS records. There are two branches: query a special use domain when only an IP address is known, and query for SVCB records when the resolver name is already available. RFC 9463, Discovery of Network-designated Resolvers, defines options that carry the same information over DHCP and router advertisements. DHCPv6 option 144, DHCPv4 option 162 and the IPv6 router advertisement option 144 carry an authentication domain name, addresses and service parameters.

What these two mean in practice is straightforward. Networks designating an encrypted resolver automatically is going to become more common. Encryption can now switch on without anyone typing anything, which also means it becomes less visible who decided. That is exactly why a layer table becomes more necessary rather than less. The question worth answering shifts from whether it is on to which layer set that value.

RFC 8484's line about a client not using a different address simply because it was discovered outside the client's own configuration reads the same way in this light. Discovery is a convenience, and trust remains a separate decision.

Keeping yourself in a state where you can move

What genuinely survives this episode is not a knack for picking a replacement but a way of staying able to move next time. Services close again, and policies change again. Lowering the cost of that now is the better investment.

  • Build the list of places the address is written. The router, each device's operating system, each browser, server configuration files, container images. Without that list, a shutdown notice arrives and nobody knows where to look
  • Concentrate the point of change in one place. Because the narrow layer wins, values scattered across several layers mean fixing one of them changes nothing. Setting it in one network layer and leaving everything else on defaults is the cheapest configuration to maintain
  • Choose the failure mode deliberately. Fail immediately, or drop quietly to plain text. Windows offers exactly that pair of choices. If privacy is the goal, a silent fallback defeats the goal
  • Read three things in a shutdown notice besides the date. What migrates automatically, what happens to customised settings, and whether the scope is the public service or extends to bundled features. In this announcement all three answers were different
  • Check internal name resolution first. In an environment with internal domains, forcing encryption cuts internal lookups. That is why the Microsoft documentation warns about domain joined machines
  • Keep a procedure that separates a name failure from a connectivity failure. The few query commands in the previous section are enough to split them. Without that separation, a resolver shutdown gets misdiagnosed as a line fault

The most valuable item on that list is the first one. The rest are only executable once the list exists.

Frequently asked questions

Does turning on encrypted DNS mean no record of my browsing is kept?

The record simply moves. RFC 7858 says the encryption TLS provides eliminates opportunities for eavesdropping and on-path tampering in the network. What is covered is the segment between the device and the resolver. The resolver operator still sees the queries in full, and RFC 9076 states that recursive resolvers see all the traffic because there is typically no caching before them. The connection that opens after the answer arrives still carries the destination IP, and the Server Name Indication travels in the clear exactly as RFC 8744 describes.

Does this shutdown affect people who use the VPN?

The announcement targets the public resolver that anyone could use by typing in an address. The same announcement says the public servers are unnecessary while the VPN is running, because that traffic is already encrypted and the internal DNS handles all queries. What breaks is any configuration where the address was typed by hand into a device, a browser or a router. Reading the headline as the whole service disappearing puts the scope in the wrong place.

Which is safer, DoH or DoT?

Both specifications protect the same thing. DoT runs TLS on the dedicated port 853, and DoH maps each query and response pair into a single HTTP exchange carried over HTTPS. DoQ says of itself that it has privacy properties similar to DoT. The difference is not a grade of safety but how visible and how manageable the traffic is. A dedicated port is easy to separate on a network, and traffic blending into ordinary web requests is not.

I turned it on in the browser, so why do some lookups still go out in the clear?

Because the conditions under which a browser steps back are documented. Parental controls, captive portal and connectivity checks, excluded domains, detection of a VPN or a proxy, enterprise policy and modified certificate roots all appear on that list. The operating system behaves the same way. The encrypted preferred setting on Windows gives no notification when it falls back to plain text.

How do I judge a claim that a resolver keeps no logs?

The only workable method is to read what the document guarantees separately from what it cannot. Mozilla's resolver policy requires a 24 hour retention ceiling, prohibits third party sale and transfer, forbids combining query data with other data to identify individuals, and requires a resolver specific privacy notice plus a transparency report published at least yearly. These items define the shape of the promise without proving it is kept, which is why jurisdiction and funding structure belong in the same reading.

What could not be confirmed

The items below were left out of the body because no primary document established them. Nothing here was filled in by inference.

  • Whether DoT is explicitly included in the shutdown — the announcement refers to the public encrypted DNS servers as DoH, while the notice on the help page says the public encrypted DNS service as a whole is being discontinued. Since that page documents DoH and DoT together, reading it as both is the natural interpretation, but the documents carry no separate statement about whether the dates differ by protocol
  • The names and definitions of Firefox's three protection levels — the relevant support page failed to render across two attempts, so the list of skip reasons in the public developer documentation was used instead. The precise definitions of the default, increased and maximum tiers were kept out of the body
  • The value definitions in Chrome's management policy — the policy list page would not open on the relevant entry, so only the scope written in the consumer support documentation is cited. The exact names and behaviour of the administrator policy values were left out
  • Which protocol Android's private DNS uses — the support page describes the scope of the feature and its three values without naming a specification, so the body does not name one either
  • Apple's encrypted DNS configuration documentation — the target document address did not respond, so no row for it was created in the layer table
  • The difference between the Windows client and server editions — the cited document covers the server edition. Whether the screens and defaults are identical on the client edition was not cross checked against a separate document
  • The details of Quad9's funding structure — the privacy policy names the responsible party and the founding organisations but does not describe funding. The table treats the item as existing without filling in a value
