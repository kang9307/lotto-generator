Getting Started with PDF Editing — What Works and What Was Never Meant To

PDF Editing for Beginners: What Works, What Never Will, and Why

Aug 16, 2026 Tech & IT

It will not edit, and that is not because you picked the wrong tool

A contract arrives as a PDF and one date needs changing. You put the cursor where the text is, expecting it to behave like a word processor, and nothing catches. You track down an editor, change one character, and now that single line juts out past the margin while the font looks subtly wrong.

At this point most people go looking for a better tool. They upload the file to whichever site the search results offered, and when that fails they upload it somewhere else. But the reason you are stuck is not the tool. PDF was never built for this job in the first place.

This article does not tell you which menu to click. It maps out what is inherently easy, what is inherently hard, and where the traps are. Knowing that boundary separates a thirty-second job from a job that needs a different approach entirely, and above all it keeps you clear of the accident where you believed something was hidden and it was not. Behaviour described here follows the PDF standard and the official documentation of each tool, current as of August 2026. Menu names and click paths are deliberately absent — they differ across programs and shift between versions, so searching by feature name lasts longer.

PDF was not built to be edited

This is the starting point. Miss it and you will keep circling.

PDF is defined by an international standard, and the current edition is ISO 32000-2, better known as PDF 2.0. The definition alone tells you its character: a single PDF file encapsulates a complete description of a fixed-layout two-dimensional document, carrying the text, fonts, images and vector graphics together in one file.

The problem this format set out to solve was not editing but reproduction. You send someone a word processor file, they lack the font, lines shift and the page count changes — PDF exists to make that stop happening. So it embeds fonts inside the file and places characters at coordinates rather than in paragraphs.

Put as instructions the difference is sharp. A word processor document says place this paragraph in this style and let it flow to fit the page width. Insert a character and everything after it shifts, with lines rewrapping. A PDF says stamp this character at this position in this font. Nothing shifts, because not shifting was the whole point.

So the difficulty of editing is not a defect; it is the other face of the design goal. Think of it as printed matter turned into a file. Erasing and rewriting words already pressed onto paper was always going to be awkward.

Easy work and hard work — the line is short

The boundary is surprisingly simple. Adding on top of the original is easy; rebuilding the original is hard.

Adding on top covers text boxes, highlights and sticky notes, stamps and signature images, and rotating, deleting, reordering, merging or splitting pages. None of it touches the content already on the page, which is why most viewers do it for free. Rebuilding covers editing characters inside an existing paragraph, restoring a table back into a table, and swapping fonts. All of it requires interpreting and reconstructing what was stamped on the page, and interpretation always contains guesswork, and guesswork is sometimes wrong. Sorted by that rule, the requests people actually bring look like this.

Body text, tables and fonts — why these three in particular

Saying they are hard settles nothing. Each is hard for a different reason, and the reason changes what you do about it.

Body text — there is no concept of a paragraph

A PDF does not necessarily record that a paragraph starts here and ends there. What it records is that these characters sit at these positions on this line. So insert a character and the sentence after it does not push down to the next line; that one line runs past the margin instead.

Editors solve this by guessing where the paragraphs are. They read line spacing, indentation and font size, decide that a paragraph probably ends here, and reflow within that range. When the guess is right the file behaves like a word processor. When it is wrong one paragraph splits in two, or a heading fuses into the body. Short documents fare well; multi-column layouts and pages containing tables fare badly.

Font replacement — only part of the typeface is inside the file

PDF embeds fonts, and to keep files small it very commonly embeds only the shapes of the characters actually used, a practice called subsetting. That is what causes trouble in writing systems with large character sets such as Korean, Chinese and Japanese.

If a document only ever used three characters, only those three glyph shapes are in the file. Type a fourth and there is no shape to stamp. The editor quietly substitutes a similar font from your system, and that line ends up subtly different in shape and weight. English documents rarely show this because the alphabet is small. It is why CJK PDFs hit the problem so often.

Table recovery — there are no tables in a PDF

It is a slightly startling fact that an ordinary PDF contains no such thing as a table. It contains some horizontal lines, some vertical lines, and characters positioned between them. The table is something your eye supplies. So a table extractor is really inferring rows and columns from coordinates, and it goes wrong in these situations.

  • Merged cells — when one value spans two rows, which row it belongs to is a judgement call.
  • Tables without rules — if columns are separated only by whitespace, the column boundaries themselves are a guess.
  • Cells wrapped over two lines, figures carrying footnote marks — a wrapped label reads as a separate row, and an asterisk next to a number turns the value into text.
  • Tables that cross a page break — the repeated header row gets mixed in with the data.

The most common accident is a number landing one column across, and the shifted result still looks perfectly tidy. That is why extracting tables is not an act of trusting output but an act of checking it. If there is a totals row, reconciling the totals first is the quick way. A so-called tagged PDF fares better because paragraphs, tables and reading order are declared explicitly, but most PDFs in circulation carry no such tags.

A scanned PDF is a different object entirely

Everything so far assumed a PDF containing real text. A PDF made by scanning paper or photographing it with a phone is another matter. Each page is a single picture and there are no characters at all. You see writing on screen because letter shapes were drawn into the image.

Telling them apart is easy. Try to select the body text with your mouse. If words highlight individually the file contains text; if the whole page turns blue as one block, or nothing selects, it is a scan. Searching for a word you can plainly see is the same test, and a miss means it is a picture.

Mixed files are common. Plenty of documents have real text in the body while only the stamp, the signature or a pasted table is an image, or were printed, signed and rescanned so that only the front section still holds text. Check in several places from front to back.

To treat a scan as text you need OCR, and there is a misconception here. OCR does not turn the picture into characters. It reads the characters out of the picture and then lays an invisible text layer behind the image. So after OCR the page looks exactly as it did; what changes is that search and copy start working.

It follows that OCR does not make a scan editable either. The letters in the picture are untouched and only the text layer is new, so fixing a character in the text layer changes nothing on screen.

Where OCR goes wrong

OCR accuracy depends far more on the state of the original than on the engine. The official documentation of Tesseract, the widely used open-source engine, spells the conditions out, and what it lists are physical constraints that broadly apply elsewhere too.

  • Resolution — the documentation states it works best on images of at least 300 dpi and advises upscaling images that fall below that. Phone photos are handicapped by focus and lighting regardless of megapixels.
  • Skew — it says plainly that if a page is too skewed the quality of line segmentation drops significantly, which severely damages the OCR overall. Thirty seconds spent squaring the paper beats thirty minutes of cleanup.
  • Noise and margins — certain kinds of noise survive the binarisation step and accuracy falls as a result. Highlighter marks, fold lines and show-through from thin paper all qualify. Too little border around the text hurts recognition, while an excessive border can be treated as a blank page.
  • Tables — the coordinate inference problem from the previous section, now with recognition errors stacked on top. Figures are the dangerous part; 0 and O, 1 and l, 6 and 8 flip with only slightly poor print quality.
  • Handwriting — general OCR built around printed type falls off sharply here. Signatures, margin notes and hand-written amounts need a human reader.
  • Composed scripts — Korean builds each syllable from stacked components, so one smudged stroke produces a different character, and close-looking pairs are plentiful.
  • Vertical and multi-column layouts — in vertically set older documents or two-column pages, reading order collapses before recognition does. Every character is right and the sentences come out interleaved.

Which reduces to a single rule. Never use OCR output before comparing it against the original — all the more so when it carries amounts, dates, names or account numbers, where a wrong character becomes an incident. For where the technology now stands, there is a separate piece on an OCR model that parses long documents in one pass.

A few points deserve to be pulled out.

  • Browser editing does more than people expect. Mozilla's help pages walk through adding text, drawing and inserting a signature in the built-in viewer and then saving the result. For filling a form or signing something, there is no reason to install anything new.
  • Reach for print to PDF first. Set a page range in the print dialogue and choose PDF as the destination, and you get a new file holding only those pages. Worth trying before hunting for a tool to pull out pages 3 to 7. Note that files made this way lose form fields, some annotations and the original structural information. Sometimes that is the point and sometimes it is the accident.
  • A signature drawn in a viewer is not an electronic signature. The shared word invites confusion, but that is a picture laid on the page. A cryptographic digital signature uses a hash of the document and a certificate to prove the document has not changed since signing. A drawn squiggle proves nothing. Where legal effect matters, that distinction is the whole story.
  • Some jobs are creation, not editing. If invoices or certificates must be generated on a server the approach is different altogether, which is covered in self-hosted PDF generation servers. And if what you received is a Korean HWP file rather than a PDF, half of this article does not apply — see the options for opening HWP and HWPX instead.

Before you upload to a converter site — where does the file go

Free, no installation, instant results. The appeal of online tools is obvious. There is a price, and it is not visible. Your file goes onto someone else's server.

From the moment it uploads, that file is outside your control. How long it is kept, who can reach it, where backup copies land, which country the server sits in — all of it is that site's policy and none of it can be verified from outside. A line reading deleted one hour after processing is a promise, not proof. So it is better to settle first on what must never be uploaded.

  • Contracts and settlement agreements — the other party's information travels with them. Even if it is your file, that is not yours alone to decide. For the same reason, any document somebody sent you belongs in this group. They did not send it so it could be posted to the internet.
  • Documents carrying national identity or passport numbers, family and residence certificates — these are values that cannot be undone once loose, and they carry other people's details alongside yours.
  • Diagnoses, prescriptions, test results, payslips, transaction histories, card statements — medical and financial records are the categories where a leak cannot be repaired.
  • Company material generally — internal policy often forbids it outright, and a great many real incidents begin with just this once, I am in a hurry.

How to tell local processing from uploading

Some tools state that processing happens in the browser, meaning the file stays on your own device rather than travelling to a server. There are ways to check whether that is true. Memorise these tests rather than tool names; they last longer.

  • Is it stated at all? Look for a clear claim on the page that nothing is uploaded. If nothing is said, assume it uploads. Local processing is a selling point, so those who do it generally say so.
  • Disconnect and try. With the page already open, cut your internet connection and feed in the file. If a result still appears, the work is happening on your device. This is the definitive test and it takes thirty seconds.
  • Watch for an upload indicator. A large file that returns a result instantly with no progress bar is probably local. To be certain, open your browser's developer tools and see whether a request the size of the file goes out.
  • How does the result come back? If you download it from a link built on that site's domain, the output was sitting on the server.
  • Does it demand an account? Requiring sign-in means the server handles the file, and that the file and your account are tied together in a record.

Fix an order and you never have to decide again. First, whatever is already on the device. Second, tools that run locally. Third, and only for files that are not sensitive, online. Keeping that order removes most accidents. Starting at the third step is what creates them.

A black rectangle is not an eraser

This is the most important section here. The earlier mistakes mostly cost time; this one cannot be undone.

Drawing a black rectangle over an identity number or a name and considering it hidden is extremely common. It is not hidden. The rectangle is merely one more shape stacked on the page, and the characters beneath it stay in the file. They are simply not visible on screen.

So any one of four things exposes them. Select and delete the shape in an editor; drag across the region, copy it and paste it anywhere; run a plain text extraction over the document; or type the value that was supposed to be hidden into the search box. With that last one, the search hit is drawn on top of the black mark.

This is not theory but a recurring accident. It is precisely why the US National Security Agency published its document sanitisation guidance Redacting with Confidence in 2005, and that document lists the most frequent mistakes as covering text with black rectangles or black highlighting, shrinking an image so it becomes unreadable, and forgetting the hidden information attached to a document. Twenty years on the same incidents keep surfacing because the screen confirms the work. A black mark looks perfect to the eye. The variants of the same misunderstanding are worth knowing too.

  • Covering with a white rectangle — worse than black. Nobody can even see that something was concealed, so nobody checks.
  • Setting the text colour to match the background — select it and it comes straight out. Only your eyes were fooled.
  • Blacking it out with a highlighter annotation — annotations are a separate layer that can be switched off or deleted at any time.
  • Shrinking an image — only the display size shrinks, and the full-resolution data can remain in the file.
  • Cropping the page or hiding a layer — both adjust what is visible rather than removing content. Undo the setting and it returns.

To actually remove it

  • Use the redaction feature. Its name is not draw a rectangle but something in the redact family. It deletes what sits beneath the area and leaves the black mark behind. Usually a commercial editor's capability.
  • Without one, rasterise the page. Export that page as an image, paint over it in an image editor and rebuild the PDF. The whole text layer disappears so searching stops working, but nothing is left behind. Printing it, blacking it out with a marker and rescanning works on the same principle (avoid thin paper that shows through).
  • Check whether the same information appears elsewhere. Perfectly cleaning one page is pointless if the same name survives in the contents list, a header or an appended table.
  • Always verify before sending. Open the finished file in a different program and extract the plain text. If a string that should be gone appears, it is not gone. Thirty seconds each time, and skipping it cannot be walked back.

Passwords, permissions and metadata — what security actually covers

Two passwords with completely different characters

A PDF has two passwords that work differently, and failing to tell them apart leaves you believing in a lock that was never fastened. Set the password requested on opening (the user password) and the file will not open without it. That is genuine encryption. Set only the password requested when changing permissions (the owner password) and anyone can simply open the file. All it adds is markers such as no printing and no copying.

Permission restrictions are a request, not a lock

The standard document itself, ISO 32000-1, says as much: once a document has been opened and decrypted successfully a conforming reader technically has access to the entire contents, and there is nothing inherent in PDF encryption that enforces the document permissions specified in the encryption dictionary.

Put plainly, no printing is a rule the viewer chooses to honour. A program that follows the specification faithfully greys out the print button; one that does not simply prints. Screenshots and re-photographing were never things that could be blocked at all.

So the judgement is clear. Permission restrictions prevent mistakes; they do not prevent leaks. If content genuinely must not be seen, take that content out of the file, or set an open password and deliver the password by a different channel. Writing the password into the body of the same email that carries the attachment is hanging the key beside the lock. Managing passwords themselves is covered in more depth in the password manager guide.

Metadata — what the file says about itself

Beyond the body text, a PDF carries information about the file: the author's name, the producing program and its version, creation and modification times, title and keywords. This is exactly the hidden information the NSA guidance says people forget.

It mostly follows in from the source document. Author details in a word processor file are copied straight across on export. A document produced on a company machine may carry an account name or a department, and on a submission meant to be anonymous that single field can identify the author. Other things persist in the file without appearing in the body.

  • Annotations and comments — sometimes collapsed on screen rather than actually deleted.
  • Form field values — they survive as field data even when nothing shows.
  • Cropped regions — as noted, cropping is a display setting.
  • Earlier saved states — a file saved by appending only the changes can still hold what it looked like before the edit.
  • Attached files and embedded fonts — a PDF can contain other files whole, and a proprietary corporate typeface is itself a piece of information.

One habit covers it. Open the document properties once on anything you send out. Delete anything that reads as an unfamiliar name. If your tool offers an inspect-for-hidden-information feature, that is quicker.

What this article will not claim

The following are left open. They change with time, environment and product edition, or cannot be verified from outside.

  • Menu names and click paths are absent. They differ across browsers, operating systems and versions, and change often. Searching by feature name lasts longer.
  • Which product puts which feature behind a paywall is left alone. It varies by edition, subscription tier and region, and shifts frequently. That is why the text describes categories rather than products.
  • Any given online service's real retention and deletion practice cannot be confirmed. The wording on the page is readable; what happens on the server is not verifiable from outside. Hence judgement criteria instead of service names.
  • OCR accuracy percentages for a given script cannot be reduced to one number. Variation caused by document condition exceeds variation between engines. The resolution, skew and margin conditions cited come from Tesseract's official documentation and do not transfer as fixed figures to every engine.
  • Legal judgement is outside the scope here. How far a document must be redacted, and what constitutes a valid signature, rests with the relevant authority and with professional advice. This article stops at how the file behaves.

Frequently asked questions

Q1. Can I edit PDF body text as freely as a Word document?

Not freely. A PDF stores fragments of text placed at coordinates rather than paragraphs, and often has no record of paragraph boundaries. Insert a character and instead of the text reflowing, that one line overruns. Short typo fixes usually work, but rewriting a paragraph or reworking a table is faster and safer done in the source document and exported again.

Q2. How do I tell whether a PDF is a scan?

Try selecting the body text. If words highlight individually the file holds text; if the whole page highlights as one block, or nothing selects, it is a scan. Searching for a word you can plainly see is the same test. Mixed files are common, so check in several places front to back.

Q3. I covered it in black, so why is the information still there?

Drawing a rectangle stacks one more shape on the page, so the characters beneath stay in the file. Delete the shape, copy the region or extract the text and they return. NSA guidance lists black rectangles and highlighting among the classic mistakes. To actually remove content you need a redaction feature that deletes what sits underneath, and without one, rasterising the page, painting it out and rebuilding is reliable.

Q4. Is it safe to use online PDF converter sites?

Judge from the premise that the file goes to someone else's server. Retention, access and server location cannot be verified from outside. Contracts, documents with identity numbers, medical and payroll records and company material do not belong there. Check whether in-browser processing is stated, then disconnect from the internet and try again — a result means it runs on your device. Order: built-in features, local tools, then online.

Q5. Does putting a password on a PDF make it safe?

Distinguish the two. The open password is real encryption and the file will not open without it. Set only the permissions password and anyone can open it, while no-print and no-copy are rules a viewer chooses to honour. The standard states that once a document is opened and decrypted a conforming reader technically has access to the entire contents, and encryption does not enforce permissions. Permission flags prevent mistakes, not leaks.

Wrapping up

There is not much new to learn about PDF. The single fact that this format exists to display faithfully rather than to be edited explains most of the rest. The boundary where adding is easy and rebuilding is hard, the fact that scans are pictures needing OCR, the fact that permission flags are a request rather than a lock — all of it flows from the same property.

Before you start, ask yourself two things. Is this file leaving my device, and is the thing I am removing genuinely removed? If the first answer is yes, look at what travels with it. If the second is uncertain, extract the text before you send. Almost every irreversible accident happens in those two places. And the most practical line of all: when there is a lot to change, stop wrestling with the PDF and ask for the source file.
