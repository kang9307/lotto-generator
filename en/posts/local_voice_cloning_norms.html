What Is Left Where the Control Points Used to Be - Norms After Voice Cloning Moved Onto Local Machines

Rebuilding the space four vanished control points left behind out of distribution, weights, marks and law

2026-09-09 Tech & IT

Synthesising a voice was, for a long time, something that happened on somebody else's computer. You opened a browser, created an account, agreed to terms, and were charged for what you used. The general complaint was that it involved too many steps.

Every one of those steps was also a place where a norm could be enforced. The account tied the person acting to a company. The terms turned prohibitions into a contract. The usage record left a trace that could be walked backwards afterwards. Pre-screening refused some requests outright. Move the tool onto a personal machine and all four disappear together. What disappears is not only the inconvenience.

The question in this article is not what such a tool can do. It is where the norms went once those four control points vanished. The answer is scattered across four places: the licence at the point of distribution, the separate conditions on model weights, the mark on the output, and the law. What each blocks, and what each cannot block, is the body of this piece.

This is general information and not legal advice. Every statute discussed here is a statute of the Republic of Korea, and the article numbers and wording come from the national law information service and provision-level reference services. Other jurisdictions treat a person's voice through quite different instruments, so a reader elsewhere should check the rules that actually apply where they are. Which provision a particular set of facts falls under is a matter for investigating authorities and the courts. Licence wording is taken from the text published by the distributor. The repository values are those returned at 23:19 UTC on 8 September 2026, and because the repository is actively changing they may differ now. No product and no maker is being assessed.

The trigger - what it means precisely to say the threshold is gone

In September 2026 a desktop application for working with speech, running entirely on the machine in front of you, was picked up by technical communities in Korea. The repository calls itself a fully local open-source alternative, and its front page states that the local workflow has no account, no API key, no subscription and no usage meter.

That sentence is not marketing copy. It is a description of structure. No account means there is no party in a position to identify who acted. No usage meter means that nothing anywhere records what was produced or how many times. Where the billing goes, the record goes with it.

The technology itself is not new. What changed is the hardware requirement. The repository's requirements table lists a minimum of 8GB of RAM with 10GB of free disk, and a recommendation of 16GB of RAM or more with 20GB or more on an SSD. A graphics card is optional and a CPU mode is supported. With a graphics card, the stated VRAM figures are 4GB minimum and 8GB or more recommended. A laptop bought for ordinary office work generally falls inside that range.

One of the responses under the Korean summary landed on exactly this point. Surprise that those specifications were all it took sat directly beside a question about what happens when the thing is used for a crime. The two reactions are two sides of the same fact. Lower the threshold and the number of users rises; raise the number of users and the absolute number of the small minority among them rises with it.

The four points the cloud used to hold

Put a subscription product and a local tool side by side and what stands out first is not a difference in features but a difference in the number of places where control attaches. In the subscription form, a single request passes at least four gates. In the local form, it passes none of them.

Control pointWhat it does in the subscription formIts state in a local toolWhat stops working once it is gone
AccountTies the person acting to a company. A payment method and contact details come with itAbsent. The local workflow asks for no accountNo starting point from which to work back to who made something
Terms of serviceTurns prohibited conduct into contractual clauses. A breach brings private sanctions: termination or a blockAbsent. A free software licence does not restrict useNo private sanction fires immediately when a norm is broken
Usage recordWhat was generated, when and how often is retained on the company sideAbsent. Projects and voices sit only on the machine by defaultNo third-party record for an investigation or a dispute to request

The two right-hand columns are the whole of the problem this article is about. That the four points are absent does not mean the norms are absent. It means the place where a norm gets enforced has moved from the company to somewhere else. Trace where it went, layer by layer, and what survives separates fairly cleanly from what does not.

What the figure of 「646 languages」 counted

The most conspicuous value is the language count. The front page puts 16 synthesis engines, 11 recognition engines and a catalogue of 646 languages on one line. What that 646 counted is the part that matters.

The card for the model used by the default synthesis engine lists language codes in its metadata, and that list has 646 entries. A descriptive sentence on the same card says more than 600. The application documentation also writes 646, with a qualifier on the same line: actual coverage and quality depend on which engine is selected. So 646 is the size of the language tag list attached by a single default engine. It is not a figure the 27 engines share, and not one whose quality has been verified.

Open the per-engine table and the spread shows immediately. Of the 16 synthesis engines, only three, all from the default model family, carry a figure above 600. The rest carry 31, 30, 24, 20, 14, 9 and 5, and one lists English alone. The recognition side is narrower: of its 11 engines the widest figure is about 100.

That gap bites in practice. Dubbing a video runs from transcription through translation into synthesis, so it is bounded by the recognition side. A catalogue of 646 does not help when the video is in a language transcription cannot handle. 646 is a catalogue size; the number of languages a job actually passes through is an intersection.

Engine counts read the same way

The counts of 16 and 11 also repay a moment of separation. Three of the 16 synthesis entries are the same base model loaded through different execution paths. Five of the 11 recognition entries are different implementations of the same family of recognition model, and one is a channel that sends audio to a server the user specifies. What has been counted is the number of integrated engine entries, not the number of distinct models. Of the 16 synthesis engines, 12 are marked as capable of cloning a voice and three as not capable.

Performance figures deserve even more caution. The repository carries a separate document setting out a measurement procedure, and at the time of retrieval the results table in that document states that no row has yet been verified. The hardware requirements are the conditions the documentation states; how fast the software runs under them has no public measurement behind it until that table is filled in.

Remaining point 1 - distribution, licence and repository policy

The first layer that survives is the point of distribution. What conditions the code goes out under, and where the code is kept, both attach here.

The application licence for this project is AGPL-3.0. The LICENSE file in the repository is byte-for-byte identical to the AGPL-3.0 text distributed by the Free Software Foundation. Both files are 34,523 bytes and their SHA-256 digests match, so no extra condition has been appended and no wording altered. The repository gives its reason: mixing anything other than the original text into a licence file prevents automated identifiers from recognising it, so the plain-language explanation was moved into a separate file.

One thing has to be stated plainly here. A free software licence does not restrict use. AGPL-3.0 governs the conditions under which source code has to flow. It does not govern what a person is permitted to make with the program. The set of things the distribution layer can block is therefore narrow.

  • Blocks — a derivative spreading in closed form with the conditions hidden. Distribute a modified version and the same conditions travel with it
  • Blocks — being folded into a commercial product by someone unaware of the conditions. A separate commercial licence notice marks that route explicitly
  • Does not block — what the person who downloaded it makes on their own machine. The licence contains no use restriction clause
  • Does not block — copies that have already left. There are 2,641 forks. Repository policy attaches only to the original

Repository policy carries the same limit. Whatever a hosting provider takes down, it cannot reach the copies already cloned at that moment. The distribution layer sets the conditions of flow, not the conditions of use.

Remaining point 2 - weights, the layer the app licence does not cover

The second layer is much less widely understood and catches far more often in practice. The licence of the application and the licence of the model weights are separate layers, and the first does not cover the second.

The licence notice document in the repository states this directly. Model weights, tokenisers and other third-party assets retain their own conditions, and the application licence neither grants rights under them nor summarises them. A footnote to the engine table repeats it: the application licence does not replace the conditions attached to a model or a tokeniser.

Open the card for the model used by the default engine and the conditions split. The code is Apache 2.0, and the pretrained weights are marked CC-BY-NC because of constraints in the training data. NC means non-commercial. Running the same application, the conditions attached to the output change entirely depending on which engine was selected.

The quietly powerful item here is a model licence carrying a use restriction clause. It does what a free software licence does not. The size of that force depends on two variables. One is how many models carry such a condition, which the table puts at one out of sixteen. The other is whether a breach is ever contested, which begins only when a rights holder moves.

The weights layer therefore works reasonably well along commercial distribution routes and barely reaches what an individual does on their own machine. The moment a company wants to put the output into a deliverable, this layer is the first gate. Outside that, the clause exists and nobody is positioned to check it.

What Article 13 of AGPL-3.0 actually requires

The reason AGPL differs from GPL comes down to a single article. Article 13 is titled Remote Network Interaction, and its requirement reads as follows.

Notwithstanding any other provision of the licence, if the Program has been modified and that modified version supports remote interaction through a computer network, then every user interacting remotely with that version must be offered prominently an opportunity to receive the Corresponding Source of that version, free of charge, from a network server.

Read literally, the article sets three conditions. There must have been a modification, the modified version must support network interaction, and a user must interact with it remotely. All three must hold before the duty to provide source arises.

  • Running it alone — even with modifications, if it is not offered to anyone else over a network, the Article 13 source duty does not arise. Nor does it meet the definition of distribution other clauses govern
  • Standing it up internally without modification — the wording of Article 13 presupposes a modification. Where there is none, the requirement in this article never fires
  • Modifying it and opening it as a service — the duty arises here. This application binds to loopback by default, but the documentation states that remote access requires a shared PIN or an API key, acknowledging that a remote exposure path exists
  • The model is separate — what Article 13 requires is Corresponding Source. Model weights are not this application's source but a third-party asset with its own conditions, so complying with Article 13 resolves nothing about them

That last item is the hinge between the two layers. Opening the application under AGPL terms and being permitted to use the model it calls are separate questions. It is also why the repository, when it advertises a separate commercial licence, states explicitly that the commercial licence does not sublicense third-party models.

Remaining point 3 - marks, and the asymmetry that stays

The third layer is the mark attached to the output. The documentation states that this application inserts an audio watermark by default, and watermark insertion and detection appear as separate rows in the feature table. The method used is a published audio watermarking implementation whose own repository states that it distributes both code and model weights under MIT terms.

How many layers a mark divides into and what each does was covered in the piece separating the marks on a generated image, and the fact that a signature can be present while the scene itself remains unvouched for was covered in the piece on what a camera signature does not vouch for. What is left here is the part that does not overlap: where the marking step sits.

Which direction a mark actually proves

In the subscription form, the code that applies the mark runs on the provider's server. The user cannot touch that step. In the local form, that code runs on the user's own machine, the source is public, and the terms are a free software licence. This is not a defect. It is the structural consequence of local execution and open source arriving together. The default being switched on is documented as such, and that it sits under the control of whoever is running the software follows from the same structure.

The result is that a mark yields a conclusion in one direction only.

  • Detected — the audio passed through a tool that inserts marks in that way. This direction is useful and can be relied on
  • Not detected — this does not mean a person spoke it. It may have passed through a different tool that inserts no mark, or the mark may not have survived the journey

That asymmetry gets inverted constantly in practice. The moment the absence of a mark is treated as evidence of authenticity, judgement becomes worse than before marks existed at all, because in a world with marks an unmarked recording looks safer than it is.

How much information a mark carries

The other frequently misunderstood point is how much a mark can carry. What this method embeds alongside the audio is a 16-bit message, and the implementation's documentation notes that this corresponds to 65,536 possible choices and gives identifying a model version as an example use. Detection is stated to work independently of that message.

Sixteen bits is not the size of a thing that identifies a person. It is enough to indicate which family of tool something passed through, and not enough to record who made it or when. Expecting a mark to fill the space vacated by accounts and usage records is a category error. A mark is an indication of whether something was synthesised, not a basis for attribution.

Remaining point 4 - law, and consent as a requirement

The fourth layer is the law. First, fix what the repository documentation says. There is a separate section on responsible use, and its first item is consent: clone or synthesise a voice only where the speaker has given explicit permission. The same section states that the watermark is inserted by default and that, in the default workflow, material does not leave the machine. The Korean summary did not include this section.

The position of that sentence matters. It is guidance in a document, not a licence clause. The AGPL-3.0 text contains no use restriction, so the consent requirement stands as a norm in the documentation while the licence attaches no consequence where it is not followed. Enforcement passes to the law.

From here to the end of the legal sections, every provision cited is a provision of Korean law. Other jurisdictions reach the same subject matter through different instruments — some through a dedicated publicity or personality right, some through data protection alone — so a reader outside Korea should treat what follows as a way of framing the question and check the rules that apply where they are.

Where voice sits in Korean law

The definition of personal information in Article 2 subparagraph 1 of the Personal Information Protection Act (in force 2 October 2025, Act No. 20897) does not contain the word voice. Item (a) gives name, resident registration number and image as examples. Voice enters through the 「and so on」 that follows, or through item (b), covering information readily combinable with other information to identify someone. The test is not vocabulary but identifiability.

One inversion is worth separating out. Voice is not automatically sensitive information. The enumeration in the main text of Article 23(1) does not include it either. The scope of sensitive information is set by the Enforcement Decree, and Article 18 subparagraph 3 of that Decree covers information on the physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics of an individual generated through certain technical means for the purpose of identifying a specific individual. The decisive words are the purpose requirement. The Personal Information Protection Commission's guidance gives voice as an example of a behavioural characteristic, separating feature extraction to establish who is speaking from processing that reads emotion or age.

The state the voice is inLegal statusRequirements that follow
A recorded audio file. Listening to it tells you who it isOrdinary personal information (Article 2 subparagraph 1)Collection and use under Article 15, provision to a third party under Article 17
Processed technically in order to read emotion, age and the likeOrdinary personal informationThe same as above

The fork in practice usually appears on the purpose side. Using a recording you already hold outside the purpose for which consent was originally given falls under Article 18 on use beyond the purpose. Recordings collected to keep a record and later turned into material for synthesis are the standard example. If the use was not inside the original consent purpose, a fresh basis has to be established.

Accounts that reach for the Act on Promotion of Information and Communications Network Utilization and Information Protection generally rely on outdated information. The personal information provisions of that Act were deleted en bloc with effect from 4 February 2020 and moved into the Personal Information Protection Act. What remains there touching on voice is Article 44-7(1), on the prohibition of circulating unlawful information. Subparagraph 1 covers obscene codes, words, sounds, images or video, and subparagraph 3 covers information that repeatedly delivers sounds provoking fear or anxiety. The word sound is in the text of both.

The labelling duty is a separate axis

The Framework Act on the Development of Artificial Intelligence and Establishment of a Foundation for Trust has been in force since 22 January 2026. Article 31(3) provides that where an artificial intelligence system is used to supply a result such as a virtual sound, image or video that is difficult to distinguish from the real thing, notice or a label must be given in a way that lets the user clearly recognise that the result was generated by an artificial intelligence system. Sound sits at the head of that enumeration.

Three things have to be separated, though.

  • The subject of the sentence is an artificial intelligence business operator — the definition in Article 2 subparagraph 7 names a person who develops and supplies artificial intelligence and a person who supplies a product or service using it. Someone who neither develops nor supplies, and simply runs the software on their own equipment, falls within neither limb on the face of the text, sitting closer to the user the same article defines
  • The word watermark does not appear in the statutes — the labelling methods the Enforcement Decree gives are two: a method a person can perceive and a method a machine can read. Where the machine-readable method is chosen, the fact of generation must also be provided at least once by way of a notice or a spoken statement
  • A breach of Article 31(2) or (3) does not go straight to an administrative fine — the route runs through a fact-finding investigation and a corrective order, with the fine attaching where that order is not complied with. That is a different path from a breach of Article 31(1), which governs prior notice

The step-by-step procedure for deciding whether this Act applies is set out separately in the piece organising the assessment order for the AI Framework Act. The point to take from here is one. The labelling duty is a layer that attaches to business operators, and an individual running software locally sits outside that layer. This provision does not fill the space the vanished control points left behind.

Voice as a livelihood - what is actually protected

Some occupations are the voice: voice actors and narrators. The question here is what that voice is treated as in law, and where the ground a contract governs parts company with the ground a statute governs.

Article 2 subparagraph 4 of the Copyright Act (in force 11 August 2026, Act No. 21336) defines a performer as a person who expresses a work by acting, dance, musical performance, singing, recitation or reading or by other artistic means, or who expresses something that is not a work by similar means. Recitation and reading are in the text, so dubbing and narration fall inside the definition.

The rights that attach to a performer run in two directions. On the moral side there is the right of attribution in Article 66 and the right to integrity in Article 67, and Article 68 provides that both are exclusive to the person of the performer, which means they are neither transferable nor inheritable. On the economic side the chain begins with the reproduction right in Article 69 and runs through distribution, rental, public performance, broadcasting and transmission rights to the remuneration claim in Article 76-2.

The core of this section follows from asking what the object of those provisions is. Article 69 gives the right to reproduce 「his or her performance」, and Article 70 gives the right to distribute 「copies of his or her performance」. Nowhere from Article 66 to Article 76-2 do the words voice or timbre appear. What the Copyright Act protects is the performance the person gave and the fixation of it, not the voice as a physical characteristic.

That distinction produces the fork immediately. Copying an existing recording and using it as it stands is squarely within Article 69. A voice newly produced after training is not that person's performance. It may sound like them, but it is an utterance they did not give, so it does not walk into the text of the performer's rights provisions. That is the gap.

Part of the gap is filled by another statute. Article 2(1)(t) of the Unfair Competition Prevention and Trade Secret Protection Act (in force 28 May 2026, Act No. 21065) lists as an act of unfair competition the unauthorised use, for one's own business and by a method contrary to fair commercial practice or competition order, of another person's name, portrait, voice, signature or other indication capable of identifying that person, where the indication is widely recognised domestically and has economic value, thereby infringing that person's economic interest. It is one of the rare provisions in which voice appears expressly; it was inserted by the amendment of 7 December 2021 and took effect on 20 April 2022.

Two requirements are attached, however: that the indication be widely recognised domestically, and that the use be for one's own business. The first keeps out the voice of someone not publicly known, the second keeps out uses unconnected to a business. The remedies are the injunction in Article 4 and damages in Article 5, and item (t) is also within Article 8, on corrective recommendations and corrective orders. The penal provisions list item (t) as excluded, so a breach does not itself carry a criminal penalty.

The third row is the layer most widely used in practice. Neither portrait right nor voice right is a name that appears in a statute. They have been handled by deriving a personality right from Article 10 of the Constitution and framing an infringement as a tort under Article 750 of the Civil Act. The Supreme Court set this out in a judgment of 16 October 2025: everyone has the right not to have their voice recorded, played back, transcribed, reproduced, broadcast or distributed at will against their will, and that right belongs to the personality right guaranteed by the first sentence of Article 10 of the Constitution. The facts of that case concerned recording without consent. It does not transfer unchanged to synthesised voice; what carries across is the reasoning recognising a right in one's voice as a personality right.

The fourth row is still empty. The proposal to insert into the Civil Act a right to the commercial exploitation of indications of personality — name, portrait, voice and so on — did not advance beyond the legislative notice stage, and in the current Civil Act there is no provision between Article 3 and Article 4. As of September 2026 it is not in force.

On top of that structure sits the place where contracts operate. The moral rights fixed by Article 68 are exclusive to the person and cannot be assigned by contract. Economic rights and the scope of use are what a contract settles. The sentences actually argued over in a recording contract are generally three: how far the deliverable may be used, whether the recording may be used for training, and on what conditions a voice produced that way may be used. For the latter two, the law supplies no default. If they are not written down, the recording becomes material later.

Imitating a voice does not by itself land on any particular provision. Requirements differ from provision to provision, and among them purpose and result fork the analysis. Three things are worth reading off the table. First, only two provisions name voice expressly, and both carry an additional requirement. Article 14-2 of the Act on Special Cases Concerning the Punishment of Sexual Crimes requires a sexual context, and Article 82-8 of the Public Official Election Act requires an election campaign purpose. Impersonation meeting neither requirement does not travel to either provision.

Second, the provisions that are indifferent to means catch more widely in practice. Fraud and defamation do not ask whether a voice or a text message was used. Synthesised voice is a problem not because it creates a new offence but because it raises the success rate of deception, a requirement those offences already contain.

Third, the table settles one widespread misunderstanding. Because people say a voice was forged, the forgery-of-documents family comes to mind. So far as can be established, the object of those provisions is a document or a drawing, and the extending provision reaches as far as a reproduced copy. No wording brings an audio recording within them.

The definition in the Special Act on telecommunications-based financial fraud is worth pinning down. On top of deception or extortion it requires that funds actually moved. Simple impersonation with no movement of funds falls outside it. That does not mean there is no harm; it means the counter you go to changes.

The listening side - confirming a person by voice alone

The direction changes here. If the control points on the producing side have thinned, what is left is the procedure on the receiving side — a layer individuals and organisations can change today.

One premise first. In Korea there is no express legal basis providing for identity to be confirmed by voice alone. Article 2 subparagraph 10 of the Electronic Financial Transactions Act, in enumerating access media, includes a user's biometric information as one item, but does not separately name voice or a voiceprint. The financial authorities' non-face-to-face identity verification framework lists a copy of an identity document, a video call, verification through an entrusted institution, a transaction with an existing account and equivalent methods, and does not include voice. It also requires two or more of them to be combined in the first place.

In other words, the system never trusted single-factor verification. Everyday procedure, however, departs from that principle constantly. The habit of treating a familiar voice on the other end of a phone call as the end of the verification is exactly such a departure.

The grounds on which a voice was used as a verification factor were three: that it was hard to imitate, that it responded in real time, and that it shared context. The first has thinned. The second and third remain. Rebuilding the procedure on the two that remain is the realistic response.

One more structure is worth holding on to. An impersonation call generally carries three features together: it manufactures urgency, it discourages any other channel, and it asks for money or information to move. Watching for the overlap of those three works far better than judging a voice by ear, because a voice has become a forgeable element while a procedure is not a thing that gets forged. The types of impersonation call, and where to report them, are collected separately in the piece organising voice phishing and emerging fraud.

What families and organisations can settle in advance

A verification procedure cannot be created after the event, because a new rule cannot be negotiated in the middle of an urgent call. The substance of a procedure is the state of having settled it once beforehand, with everybody knowing it.

What to settle at the family level

  • A verification question agreed in advance — it has to be something that cannot be answered from public information. A birthday or an address can be found anywhere and performs no verification function
  • A call-back rule — you dial the number you stored, not the number that called. This one rule resolves most cases on its own
  • Any request for money changes the channel, without exception — settle in advance the rule that nothing is decided during the call itself
  • An agreement that following the rule is not rude — procedures collapse at a point that is social rather than technical. Agree beforehand that a family member asking a verification question is not awkward

What to settle at the organisation level

  • Make a list of the procedures that execute on a voice instruction alone — start with the ones that are hard to reverse: payment approval, account reset, granting access rights
  • Attach a second factor to every item on that list — an approval request in an internal system or a call back to a registered number, but always a route other than the call
  • Do not make an exception for an urgent instruction from an executive — this shape is the most common route by which a procedure gets skipped. Create the exception and the exception becomes the target
  • Write down that no staff member suffers for asking for confirmation — without this, the rule exists on paper and does not operate

In practice, sentences of about the following length are enough. The longer they get, the less they are followed.

[Call verification rules - household]
1. If money, accounts or verification codes come up, the call ends here.
2. I call back on the number I have stored. The number they gave is not used.
3. If the call back does not connect, check with another family member first.
4. If the agreed verification question is not answered, nothing is decided.

[Call verification rules - organisation]
1. Requests that are hard to reverse are not handled on a call alone.
   (payment approval / account reset / rights granting / bank detail change)
2. The second confirmation arrives through a route other than the call.
3. Urgency does not waive the procedure. No exceptions.
4. Nobody is penalised for having asked for confirmation.

Putting the four points back on the table

Back to the table at the start. Lay out on one page what has taken the place of each of the four vanished points, and the conclusion falls out of it.

Remaining pointWhat it blocksWhat it does not blockWho has to move for it to work
Distribution - licence and repository policyDerivatives circulating with the conditions hidden; commercial absorption by someone unaware of the conditionsUse after the download. Copies that have already leftRights holders and the hosting provider
Weight licencesCommercial circulation of output from models carrying a non-commercial condition; particular uses in families carrying use restriction clausesWhat an individual does on their own machine. Switching to a model with no conditionsThe model's rights holder
Marks on the outputThe state in which there is no basis at all for later establishing whether something was synthesisedAttribution. Judging unmarked audio. Other tools that attach no markThe tool's authors and whoever is doing the checking
LawConduct that satisfies the requirements. Sanctions after the fact and remedies for harmThe act of generation itself. Impersonation outside the requirementsRights holders, investigating authorities and the courts

None of the four blocks the moment of generation. There is no remaining layer doing the work that pre-screening did in the subscription form. What is left is entirely after the fact or at the distribution stage. That running something locally does not shift responsibility was the same conclusion in the piece on model variants with the safety layer removed. The place of execution changes; the requirements of the law stay where they were.

So the weight shifts in practice. On the producing side what remains is the work of transcribing the weight conditions into the contract; on the listening side, the work of settling the procedure in advance. Neither is glamorous and both can be done today. In a world where the control points have thinned, what remains is generally of this kind.

Once more - this article organises the requirements of provisions and who decides them; it is not legal advice. All statutes referred to are those of the Republic of Korea; readers elsewhere should check the rules that apply to them. Which provision a particular set of facts falls under, and what contractual clauses are needed, turns on those facts, and that judgement belongs to lawyers, investigating authorities and the courts. Licence interpretation is likewise settled by rights holders and the courts. The conditions attached to repositories and models change frequently, so checking the text as it stands is a precondition of any actual use.

What could not be confirmed

The items below were kept out of the main text because their wording could not be separated out from a primary source. The reason is given alongside each.

  • Where 「business」 begins in the AI Framework Act — the duty-bearer under Article 31 is an artificial intelligence business operator, and that definition requires a person who supplies. Yet Article 2 contains no definition of business. Whether free distribution counts, whether adjusting a model and publishing it makes someone a person who develops and supplies, and whether uploading generated audio publicly amounts to supplying a service are not settled by the wording alone, so no line was drawn
  • The commencement date of Article 82-8 of the Public Official Election Act — the insertion note at the end of the provision reads 28 December 2023, which the provision text establishes. The commencement date was shown differently across materials, so the main text gives only the date of insertion
  • The 2024 amending act number for Article 14-2 of the Act on Special Cases Concerning the Punishment of Sexual Crimes — the amendment note inside the provision reads 16 October 2024, but the act number differed between sources, so no number is given
  • The actual quality of the synthesised voice — the repository has a document setting out a measurement procedure, but the results table contains no verified row. Which engine runs how fast on which hardware can only be used as a value once public measurements fill that table in
  • The quality of each of the 646 languages — the size of the list could be established from the model card metadata, but per-language evaluation results are not there. Being on a list and being usable are different matters, and no public material supports the second
  • How well the watermark survives real distribution conditions — the implementation's repository states that it withstands editing such as compression, re-encoding and added noise. Under what conditions that was measured sits outside the repository description, so it was not carried across without its conditions
  • The gap between the Korean summary and the original document — the repository documentation has a section on responsible use, and the consent requirement is written inside it. The summary that carried the story in Korea did not include that section. Why it was left out is not established, so only the fact is recorded
