How to Read an LLM Technical Report - MoE and Hybrid Attention via Solar Open 2

Not an article that lists architecture facts, but one about the order in which you read and judge them

Jul 28, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: A Technical Report Is a Spec Sheet, Not a Brochure

On 22 July 2026 Upstage published the technical report for Solar Open 2 on arXiv (arXiv:2607.20062). The headline figures are 250 billion total parameters, 15 billion active per token, and a one-million-token context window. Within days, summary articles and social posts appeared everywhere, and most of them simply repeated those three numbers.

Yet from the perspective of someone evaluating adoption, those three numbers decide nothing. How many GPUs do we need to buy? How many tokens per second can we expect? Are we allowed to use this commercially? How much should we trust the scores in the table? None of these questions is answered. A technical report does contain the answers, but the trouble is that the answers live in table footnotes and appendices, not in the abstract.

This article does not recommend or discourage any particular model. It is about what order to read a technical report in, which lines to make a judgement on, and which sentences to hold in reserve. Solar Open 2 serves as the worked example because it packs several current architectural ideas into one document, not because it comes from Korea. If anything, the habit of reading a domestic model generously is one of the things this article warns against.

The adoption decision procedure for a single model — licence terms, VRAM arithmetic, total cost of ownership — is covered separately in the Kimi K3 open-weight adoption analysis. This article sits one step earlier: how you extract that decision material out of a report in the first place. For where each generation of model sits relative to the others, see the AI model comparison hub; for the small and on-device end of the spectrum, see the on-device model analysis; and for the research-model approach, see the Thinking Machines model analysis.

1. The Order You Open a Report In - Five Lines Before the Abstract

The abstract of a technical report is the impression the authors want to leave. It is not false, but it is selected. The values that actually matter for engineering decisions usually sit three to five pages later, in the configuration table and in the evaluation protocol footnote.

1.1 What to Look For First

Whatever report you open, find these five lines first. If a report is missing them, it is closer to a press release than to a spec sheet.

  • Total and active parameters — both must appear. One alone is half the information
  • Layer composition and attention type — how many layers, and how many of them are global attention
  • Context length and whether that length was trained — window size and actual quality at that length are different questions
  • Weight licence — the licence on the weights, not the licence on the paper
  • Who measured the benchmarks — vendor self-measurement or third-party evaluation

Extracting those five lines from the Solar Open 2 report and the Hugging Face model card gives the following. Every value below comes from primary material published by Upstage, not from press summaries.

  • 250B total / 15B active (the model card states 250,287,794,944)
  • 48 layers, arranged as one softmax layer plus three linear-attention layers, repeated twelve times
  • 1,048,576 tokens, with roughly 0.9T tokens spent on a dedicated length-expansion stage per the report
  • Upstage Solar License (the model card licence field reads other)
  • Per the report, all figures measured with the authors' own evaluation harness

1.2 What Those Five Lines Cannot Answer

Those five lines pin down hardware budget, throughput expectations, legal review items and benchmark credibility. One question remains unpinned: will this model actually be useful on our own workload?

That is not a question a report can answer, because no report has access to the reader's domain data. So however well you read a report, the conclusion always lands in the same place. Use the report to narrow the candidate list, and make the final call with your own evaluation set. Thirty to a hundred examples is enough to see the direction.

2. Total and Active Parameters - One Buys Memory, the Other Buys Speed

The most common misreading of a MoE report happens right here: treating the two numbers as a big value and a small value on the same axis. They are not on the same axis. They are two different axes.

The 2021 Switch Transformer paper (arXiv:2101.03961) made that separation explicit. Before it, model capacity and per-token compute were effectively the same number. Sparse routing made it possible to grow capacity while holding per-token compute constant, and Mixtral (arXiv:2401.04088) later popularised the pattern with 46.7B total against 12.9B active.

2.1 Memory Is Decided by Total Parameters

In a MoE model the router only decides which experts to use once the token arrives. Since that cannot be known ahead of time, all 320 experts must be resident in memory. The fact that only 15 billion parameters are active does not save a single byte of memory.

The arithmetic runs as follows. Multiply the model card's total of 250,287,794,944 by two bytes per parameter at bfloat16 and you get roughly 500 GB. A single H200 carries 141 GB, so four of them provide 564 GB — barely enough to hold the weights. That arithmetic is exactly why the model card states a minimum of four H200s and recommends eight. The recommendation is double the minimum because KV cache and activations need room too.

2.2 Speed Is Decided by Active Parameters

Conversely, the weights actually read to produce one token are only the active parameters. Decoding is a memory-bandwidth-bound phase rather than a compute-bound one, so bytes read per token sets the theoretical ceiling on speed.

  • 15 billion active × 2 bytes means roughly 30 GB read per token
  • A dense model of the same total size would need roughly 500 GB per token
  • On identical hardware, that is more than an order of magnitude difference in the theoretical ceiling
  • Real throughput lands well below the ceiling once parallelism efficiency, routing overhead and batch composition are accounted for

In other words this model consumes memory like a 250B model while computing like a 15B one. That asymmetry dominates every infrastructure discussion that follows.

2.3 What Goes Wrong When the Two Numbers Are Confused

Failures in practice almost always trace back to only one of the two numbers reaching a document.

  • Write 250B model in a procurement document and the GPU budget is right but throughput expectations are understated
  • Write 15B-class speed and the speed expectation is right but the hardware quote collapses
  • This is why recent reports use joined notation such as 250B-A15B
  • Carrying that notation verbatim into internal documents is the safer habit

3. When MoE Helps and When It Hurts

MoE is not universally better; it is a structure that pays off only under a specific combination of constraints. Reports normally describe the favourable side only, so the reader has to supply the unfavourable side.

3.1 Where It Helps

  • Diverse request types — a shared internal model taking code, document summarisation, translation and table extraction through one endpoint lets expert specialisation actually do work
  • Memory already provisioned, bandwidth the bottleneck — the cluster exists and you need more throughput out of it
  • Batch queues that stay full — overnight document processing or large-scale index generation, where batches can be made large
  • Broad knowledge needed on a tight latency budget — you buy breadth with total parameters and latency with active parameters
  • API providers cutting cost per token — the same hardware yields more tokens

3.2 Where It Hurts

  • When GPU memory is the budget constraint — for the same VRAM, fitting a larger dense model may serve quality better
  • When concurrency is low — thin batches expose the cost of fetching each expert's weights. Research analyses suggest batch sizes in the thousands are needed before a sparse model enters the compute-bound regime
  • When batches are simply grown without limit — different tokens pick different experts, so the number of distinct activated experts rises with batch size and decode latency rises with it. Returns from batching bend downwards past a point
  • When fine-tuning is required — routing collapse and expert imbalance make the recipe fussier than for a dense model
  • On-device and edge — total parameters are storage and memory. That tier is served by an entirely different weight class
  • When expert offloading is unavoidable — pushing experts out to CPU or disk adds round trips and visibly degrades perceived speed

3.3 A Judgement Table

SituationMoE suitabilityReason

For reference, the design of splitting experts finely and adding an always-on shared expert was formalised in the DeepSeekMoE paper (arXiv:2401.06066). Solar Open 2's configuration of 320 routed experts plus one shared expert, with eight selected per token, reads as the same lineage. A large expert count means finer-grained combinations are possible, not that the model is smarter.

4. Hybrid Attention - Long-Context Cost Is Set by the KV Cache

A one-million-token context is now a common claim. What is almost never stated is how much memory it costs to actually fill that window. That value may be absent from the report, but it is computable from the published configuration.

4.1 How the KV Cache Grows

Softmax attention has to hold the keys and values of every token seen so far. The KV cache for a single sequence grows like this.

  • Bytes per token = 2 (K and V) × layer count × KV head count × head dimension × bytes per element
  • Then multiply by sequence length. The crucial property is that it grows in direct proportion to length
  • GQA (arXiv:2305.13245) reduces the KV head count and thus lowers that constant. The constant shrinks, but the proportionality to length does not change
  • So as context grows there comes a region where the cache rivals the weights in size

Solar Open 2's published configuration lists 8 KV heads, a head dimension of 128, and bfloat16. That works out to 4 KiB per token per softmax layer. Multiply by the number of softmax layers and you are done.

4.2 What Hybrid Changes

Of the 48 layers, only 12 are softmax and the remaining 36 are linear attention. The published configuration file places the softmax attention layers at indices 0, 4, 8 and so on up to 44, confirming one every four layers.

The table above was calculated directly by this author from the published configuration (8 KV heads, head dimension 128, bfloat16) and covers a single sequence. The fixed state held by the linear layers and the activations are excluded. The report itself describes the hybrid stack as using roughly one quarter of the memory and computation compared with an all-softmax design — the same direction as the arithmetic above.

Why is that saving possible? Linear attention variants update a fixed-size state instead of stacking every past token. The 2020 paper Transformers are RNNs (arXiv:2006.16236) formalised that reformulation, and Jamba (arXiv:2403.19887) later demonstrated hybrid placement at a one-to-seven ratio of attention to state-space layers. Solar Open 2's one-to-three is a more generous allocation of global attention than that.

4.3 It Is Not Free - What to Hold in Reserve

Here is where judgement splits. Cutting the KV cache to a quarter is a fact of memory accounting; it is not the same statement as this model reads long documents well.

  • Linear attention compresses information into a fixed-size state, so exact recall at distant positions behaves differently from softmax
  • Work in the Jamba line also notes that pushing the attention ratio too low can damage capability
  • Solar Open 2 states that it extends a gated delta rule into the negative eigenvalue range so the state can correct itself, and the report identifies this as an extension of the KDA family
  • However, no third-party verification of how much that extension contributes to real long-document quality could be found
  • It also omits positional encoding entirely (NoPE). Research showing that decoders without positional encoding generalise better to length (arXiv:2305.19466) is the usual justification, but those experiments were not at million-token scale

So the practical conclusion is simple. Treat context window size and quality at that length as two separate line items. If long documents are genuinely part of the plan, there is no substitute for running a recall test on your own corpus. Two checks are enough to form a view: whether a fact planted mid-document is retrieved, and how response latency behaves near the top of the window.

5. How Not to Be Fooled by a Benchmark Table

The benchmark table is the most quoted and most misread part of any report. The correct reading order is footnotes before scores.

5.1 Who Measured It

The Solar Open 2 report states its evaluation method explicitly. It carries a sentence to the effect that every figure in the tables was measured with the authors' internal evaluation harness under identical per-benchmark settings. That single sentence has to be read in two directions.

  • Upside — internal consistency is higher than in a table that mixes figures measured under different conditions per model
  • Limitation — competitor scores are therefore values reproduced by Upstage, and may differ from those vendors' own published numbers
  • The comparison set includes DeepSeek-V4-Flash (284B-A13B), MiMo-V2.5 (310B-A15B), Command A+ (218B-A25B), Mistral Medium 3.5 (128B dense) and Upstage's own Solar Open 1 (102B-A12B)
  • The Korean table additionally includes closed APIs such as Claude Haiku 4.5 and GPT-5.4 mini
  • As of publication, no third-party evaluator score for Solar Open 2 could be confirmed. Once one appears, it is the better baseline

5.2 The In-House Benchmark Line Item

Ko-GDPval 86.8 stands out in the table. Yet the report itself describes that benchmark as an in-house Korean officework agent benchmark.

Two things have to be conceded simultaneously. First, a strong score by a model on a benchmark its own makers designed carries little information, because it is hard to fully avoid encoding your model's strengths into the design. Second, the report disclosing that fact is honest handling, and should be distinguished from quietly mixing an undisclosed in-house set into a table. The right posture for the reader is reserved judgement rather than acceptance or dismissal.

5.3 Contamination and Scaffolding - Two Variables Absent From the Table

  • Data contamination — if benchmark test sets leak into training data, scores inflate. Studies across many benchmarks and models (arXiv:2411.03923, arXiv:2410.16186) report that contamination is not rare
  • Scaffolding — agent benchmarks swing widely with tool configuration, retry counts and prompt skeleton. The same model scores differently under different scaffolds
  • Read the low scores too — the same self-measured table also carries HLE 28.8, Terminal Bench Hard 28.3 and APEX-Agents 16.6
  • Quoting only the high entries means you read half the report. The low entries are precisely the list of things the model cannot yet do
  • Also ask each time what the named benchmark has to do with your work. A competition mathematics score does not map directly onto internal document summarisation quality

6. Licensing - The Paper Licence Is Not the Weight Licence

This is where mistakes genuinely happen. The arXiv page displays a document licence at the bottom, and for the Solar Open 2 technical report that is CC BY 4.0. But that is the licence on the paper document. It has nothing to do with the weights.

6.1 Where the Weight Licence Lives

The weight licence lives on the distribution page. The Hugging Face model card metadata sets the licence field to other and names it upstage-solar-license. In other words it is a custom licence, not a standard open source one.

  • Naming obligation — derivative models produced by fine-tuning, distillation and so on must carry a Solar prefix in their name
  • Attribution obligation — related public-facing material must prominently display Built with Solar
  • Notice inclusion — a copy of the licence must ship with any derivative model
  • Commercial use — per Upstage official material, commercial use and derivative development through fine-tuning or distillation are permitted
  • The accurate wording — documents should therefore say open weight under a custom licence, not open source

For a real adoption review, legal counsel should read the licence text itself rather than a summary. The fact that a custom licence has no accumulated precedent, unlike a standard one, is itself a review cost.

7. Running the Solar Open 2 Report Through This Method

Applying the previous six sections in order produces the following. Every value comes from the report and model card published by Upstage, and is read on the premise that it is a vendor self-declaration.

7.1 Structure and Figures

  • 250B total / 15B active, 48 layers
  • 320 routed experts plus one shared expert, eight selected per token
  • Attention as twelve repetitions of one softmax plus three linear layers, with no positional encoding
  • Context of 1,048,576 tokens, vocabulary size 196,608
  • Pre-training on the order of 12 trillion tokens, with a separate length-expansion stage
  • Supported languages per the model card are English, Korean and Japanese
  • Minimum four H200s, eight recommended; vLLM and SGLang serving documented
  • Headline self-measured figures: MMLU-Pro 86.2, GPQA-Diamond 86.3, LiveCodeBench v6 92.4, SWE-Bench Verified 70.4, KMMLU-Pro 78.4, Ko-AIME 25 97.7 (all vendor self-measured)

7.2 Limitations the Report Discloses Itself

In its conclusion the report names the remaining gaps directly: repository- and terminal-level software engineering, and fine-grained numerical precision in officework deliverables, both of which it frames as calling for stronger verification and self-checking behaviour in agent training.

From the reader's side this is the most useful paragraph in the document. The self-measured table is consistent with it — Terminal Bench Hard 28.3 and APEX-Agents 16.6 are indeed low in exactly those areas. A report whose stated limitations match its own numbers is more trustworthy than one that states none. That consistency is usable as a criterion for evaluating reports in general.

7.3 What the Data Composition Suggests

The report gives its pre-training recipe as a four-to-six ratio of real to synthetic data, at least 15% each for mathematics and code, and an English share above 80%. One observation follows.

  • The English share exceeds 80%, yet the Korean benchmark average comes out high
  • Which means the simple explanation that Korean performance comes from Korean data volume is not supported by this report
  • Post-training, alignment data design and evaluation set construction are plausible contributors, but causation cannot be settled from the report alone
  • And that Korean advantage claim is self-measured, partly on an in-house benchmark
  • So the defensible conclusion right now is promising, pending third-party verification — not best in class for Korean

This is the point at which reading a domestic model demands extra care. Wanting a model to succeed and verifying that it does are separate activities. If a generous reading leads to an adoption that underdelivers, the organisation absorbs the loss. Equally, the prejudice that a domestic model will be inadequate anyway is the same species of laziness. Either way the answer is the same — measure it on your own data.

The table below is written so it transfers to any model's report. Work down it in order.

11Serving stack supportIs our runtime on the supported list
12Own evaluation set resultsHave you measured real task quality on 30 to 100 items

Eleven of those twelve rows can be filled from the report alone. Only the last one requires your own work. And yet the decision usually turns on that last row. The eleven above are better understood as a way of narrowing candidates so that the last row is not wasted.

Conclusion - Three Questions Before You Close the Report

First, what does this number decide? Total parameters decide the hardware quote; active parameters decide throughput expectations; attention layer composition decides memory at long context. A number that decides nothing is a headline number, not a decision number.

Second, who measured this value? Vendor self-measurement and third-party evaluation carry different weight even when they sit in the same table, and an in-house benchmark is different again. A table that does not identify who measured is itself a piece of information.

Third, does this condition apply to us? One sentence saying commercial use is permitted does not conclude a legal review. Derivative model naming, public attribution and notice inclusion create real work depending on the shape of the service.

Solar Open 2 made a good exercise for these three questions. MoE, hybrid attention and a custom licence all appear in one document, the report discloses its own limitations, and it does not hide its in-house benchmark. That makes it good material for practising the reading. Whether the model should be adopted is an entirely separate matter, and this article makes no claim about it.

One last repetition. A report is a tool for narrowing candidates, not a tool for making decisions. The better the report, the more openly it says so. The reader's job is not to copy the table across, but to hand the questions the table cannot answer over to their own data.
