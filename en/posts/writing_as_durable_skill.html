Taking Apart the Claim That Writing Is Safe - Four Different Jobs Under One Word

Draft production, subject choice, fact checking, accountability. How far it went and where it stopped

Sep 4, 2026 Trends & Culture

Lists of which jobs will survive have been circulating for years now. This time writing came up. The reasoning starts from an observation anybody can make, that code generation has become visibly good while prose generation has not, and ends at a conclusion, that writing will therefore be among the last things to go.

This piece is not here to rule that conclusion right or wrong. It steps back one square instead. The single word writing is covering four different jobs, and those four do not move at the same speed. Split them and the layers where the claim holds separate cleanly from the layers where it fails. That split is the whole of what this piece does.

This piece does not recommend or discourage any career, and it does not rank the worth of any occupation. The employment projections quoted come from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics and do not transfer as-is to other labour markets. All figures and projection windows are as of September 2026.

The trigger - what the source post actually said

A post that went up on an English-language blog on 31 August 2026 was passed around a Korean developer community a few days later. The title says the safest job from AI may be writing, and the body runs in four sections. The plateau on prose, writing as a wicked problem, writing as an AI-complete problem, and comparative advantage with costly signals.

The author sets the gap between code generation and prose generation in strong terms, then argues that the gap is not an accident but a property of the problem. The final section carries the conclusion. Human cognitive capacity is better deployed on wicked problems, and as AI-generated text saturates the space, the mark of a human having written something acquires value on its own. The post does not include a section for objections. It closes instead by asking readers to say where the logic does not hold up.

This piece answers that request. Arguing against claims the post never made would be pointless, so the first job is separating what is in it from what is not. Employment figures, rates per piece, and any distinction between kinds of writing are not in it. None of those absences are treated here as errors to be scored.

One more piece of housekeeping. This site already carries several pieces on AI and work. The broad outlook, erosion inside engineering roles, redesigning your own expertise, the spread of agents — all of that is covered elsewhere and is not repeated here. If you want them, they are future jobs and skills, what a decade of backend experience is now worth, the stages of redesigning expertise, jobs in the agent era, and what goes before the job does.

The four supports the claim stands on

Reduced to its load-bearing parts, the argument has four supports. In the order the post presents them.

  • The plateau on prose — image, voice and video models improved visibly as compute scaled, while text models plateaued on expression, depth and authenticity
  • A wicked problem — writing has no specification, no stopping rule and no objective success metric. There is nowhere to hook an automatic improvement loop, the way binary verification works for mathematics or a compiler works for code
  • Theory of mind — persuasive prose requires tracking what a reader knows, sentence by sentence. A model optimises statistical probability without a mental model of any particular reader
  • Comparative advantage and costly signals — even where the machine holds absolute advantage, human attention is better spent on wicked problems, and as AI text becomes cheap the trace of a human becomes a costly signal

What is worth noticing is that these four are not the same kind of claim. The first three are claims about what a model cannot do. Only the fourth is a claim about how a market responds. And the conclusion, the safe job, hangs entirely on the fourth.

The reason that distinction matters is simple. All three of the first supports can be true and the conclusion still fails if the fourth is off. Between a model being unable to write well and a person who writes well continuing to earn a living from it, there is an empty square marked market. What goes in that square is the rest of this piece.

Writing is not one job

The word writing covers at least four distinct jobs. They get one name because one person often does all four, but from an automation standpoint they are unrelated objects. Splitting them is the centre of this piece.

Making sentences - draft production

The layer that turns an argument already settled into readable sentences. Input is text and output is text, and the passing bar is readable. It was always the closest surface to automation, and it is the one automation reached first.

Deciding what to write - subject and structure

The layer that decides why this subject now, what goes in and what comes out, and in what order. The grounds for that judgement are not in the text. Who the reader is, what interests are at stake, what happened last week — none of it arrives in the input on its own.

Checking the facts - verification

The layer that puts a claim next to the primary source. The tools multiplied but the difficulty went up, because a wrong sentence and a right sentence now read equally smooth. The old instinct that treated awkward phrasing as an error signal is worth much less here than it used to be.

Carrying the blame - whose name goes on it

The layer that decides whose name is attached when it turns out wrong. This is not a question of ability but of standing. Ability can transfer; standing does not. The reason this layer keeps dropping out of automation debates is that it is not a task at all but an institution.

LayerWhat it doesWhere it stands in 2026Why it lands there
Draft productionTurns a settled argument into sentencesLargely goneText in, text out, and a low passing bar
Subject and structureDecides what to write and what to cutOnly partlyThe grounds for judgement sit outside the text
VerificationPuts claims next to primary sourcesMore tools, more burdenWrong and right sentences share a surface
AccountabilityAttaches a name when it is wrongDoes not transferA matter of standing, not ability

The source post's argument holds strongly on rows two and three of that table, and is already history on row one. The catch is that a large share of the money that has ever been paid for writing was attached to row one. The claim and the income sit on different rows. That mismatch is what this piece is pointing at.

Why draft production fell first

Draft production went first not because it is easy but because it satisfies three conditions for automation at once. Input and output share a format, so nothing has to be converted in between. The passing bar for the output is low, because most commercial copy does not need to be excellent, only read. And review costs less than production, because if fixing a bad draft is cheaper than writing one from scratch, the work gets automated.

Where those three overlap, the cost of supply converges on zero. What happens when the cost of supply converges on zero has already been observed at close range. It is the structure covered in making got cheap, selling did not. The bottleneck on the making side disappears while the bottleneck on the selling side stays exactly where it was, and the gap between them pushes the price down.

One common misreading is worth clearing here. Saying draft production went does not mean the model writes better than people. It means the bulk of the market never needed writing that was better than adequate. The robotic cadence and the plateaued expression the source post objects to are real defects, but they are not defects that the buyer at this layer generally pays to avoid.

  • Copy whose bar is excellent and copy whose bar is readable are not the same market, and the second one is overwhelmingly larger
  • The model having a real weakness and that weakness reaching the purchase decision are separate propositions
  • So an argument conducted purely about quality neither explains this layer's collapse nor prevents it
  • Equally, this layer collapsing does not bring down the other three. That is exactly why the layers have to be separated

What the numbers say

From here the piece works from published projections rather than impressions. The Occupational Outlook Handbook, produced by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, carries a ten-year employment projection for each occupation. As published in September 2026, the 2025 to 2035 window reads as follows for the writing occupations.

OccupationEmployment, 2025Median pay, 2025Change, 2025–35Annual openings
Writers and authors140,300$76,9100% (−500)about 11,900
Editors105,000$77,920−1% (−1,100)about 7,900
Technical writers46,400$90,390+1% (+400)about 3,200

The same handbook states, in the writers and authors entry, that increasing use of artificial intelligence for writing is projected to dampen demand for these workers. The technical writers entry carries a matching note that employment growth may be slowed by AI tools that make these workers more productive. A statistical agency naming AI as a stated reason for a projection is itself a piece of evidence.

That table should not be read as the job disappearing. All three occupations show net changes in the hundreds while annual openings run in the thousands to the tens of thousands. Seats keep opening, and most of them come from replacing people who transfer out or retire. The accurate word is not extinction but stagnation.

A result pointing the other way belongs beside it. Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard linked Danish administrative labour records to large-scale adoption surveys and found precise null effects on earnings and recorded hours two years after chatbot adoption, ruling out effects larger than 2 percent. The nulls held for intensive users, early adopters, workplaces that had invested heavily, and early-career jobs. The authors wrote that the findings challenge narratives of imminent disruption.

  • A projection is a forecast, not an observation. The Bureau revises these every year and the values move when it does
  • The Danish study looks at a two-year window in one country. Early-phase observations should not be stretched into long-run conclusions
  • The two look contradictory because they measure different layers. The projection counts occupations; the administrative records count earnings and hours
  • Neither proves that writing is safe or that writing is finished. Neither is the kind of evidence that can carry a claim that size

Where being good stopped being a shield

There is an assumption buried in the source post's conclusion, that skill acts as a shield. If the model can only produce mediocre prose, then excellent writers are untouched. Studies that looked directly at online labour markets did not find that assumption holding.

The 2023 working paper by Xiang Hui, Oren Reshef and Luofeng Zhou examined freelancer outcomes on a large online platform. Employment and earnings both fell in heavily affected occupations, which is the expected part. The next part is not. The authors found no evidence that high service quality, measured by past performance and employment history, moderated the adverse effects, and reported suggestive evidence that top freelancers were disproportionately affected.

The 2024 working paper by Ozge Demirci, Jonas Hannane and Xinrong Zhu looked at the same kind of market from another angle. Within eight months of the release, job posts for automation-prone work such as writing and coding fell 21 percent relative to manual-intensive work. Posts related to image creation fell 17 percent after image-generating models arrived. The more interesting observation sits alongside those figures. The automation-prone jobs that remained were of greater complexity and offered higher pay.

Put the two together and the picture sharpens. What is left got better and got fewer. The door improved and narrowed in the same motion. Skill raises the odds of passing through that door, but it does not stop the door from narrowing. Skill as a shield only works while the number of seats holds constant.

  • Both studies look at online freelance platforms. Neither transfers directly to salaried roles or to trade publishing
  • Both cover short windows immediately after release. The long-run equilibrium may look nothing like this
  • The finding about top freelancers was described by its own authors as suggestive evidence, and it is not promoted here into settled fact
  • What these results do is nonetheless clear. They make the shield assumption something you can no longer use for free

The layer that does not transfer

None of which means all four layers fell. The second one, deciding what to write, behaves differently. It resists transfer not because the model's language ability falls short but because the information the judgement needs lives outside the text.

Whether a subject deserves attention now depends on who worried about what in last week's meeting. How far you can go depends on the contract and on how much risk the organisation tolerates. What has to be cut depends on what the person reading it already knows. None of those exist until somebody puts them in the prompt, and deciding whether to put them in is itself the judgement in question.

This overlaps with the theory-of-mind support but sits one square away from it. The source post is concerned with the ability to track a reader's mental state in real time. What is described here is the condition prior to ability. Where there is no access to the thing being tracked, the question of ability has nowhere to land. That distinction returns later.

  • Context — why this subject matters right now. Rarely written down anywhere
  • Interests — who stands to lose and gain. Writable in principle, awkward in practice
  • Reader — what they already know and what they are already tired of. Analytics shows half of it
  • Limits — how far you are allowed to go. Partly judgement, partly authority

Where the blame attaches

The fourth layer is harder still. In medicine, law and finance, where being wrong hurts somebody, what goes at the end of a document is not a sentence but a name. And that name currently has to belong to a person as a matter of rule. The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors states that chatbots cannot be listed as authors, because they cannot be responsible for the accuracy, integrity and originality of the work, and those responsibilities are what authorship requires.

How far that principle stretches into a conference hall, and where the line between a tool and a stand-in actually falls, was covered separately yesterday. Only one thing is needed here. The accountability layer is not an automation target but an institutional one. It moves at a speed unrelated to how good the technology gets.

Before treating that survival as grounds for safety, though, one thing is worth counting: how many people this layer needs. A structure that required ten authors for ten drafts and now requires two reviewers for ten drafts has kept the layer and lost the seats. The layer surviving and the headcount surviving are different numbers.

Safe and fine are not the same word

This is the second axis of the piece. A job not disappearing and the money it paid holding up are two different questions. As the table above showed, the occupational category survives. The question is the rate. When supply rises the price of identical output falls, and when the price falls the occupation code stays exactly where it is.

So the sentence it is not going away cannot deliver the meaning it will be fine. Split out which question the word safe is answering and it comes apart like this.

Read only the first row and the post's conclusion is correct. Read rows four and five alongside it and the conclusion wobbles. Because one word, safe, is covering five questions at once, a claim that never says which one it answered can be defended as true and attacked as false with equal ease.

The reading side changed too

The fourth support, the costly-signal argument, presupposes a change on the reading side. That presupposition is real enough. Once a piece carries the impression of having been generated, readers adjust their trust on the spot. Which signals they use, and what a misjudgement costs them, is something I set out separately, and the concrete markers people go by are in the piece on AI smells.

A paradox follows. The cheaper model-written text becomes, the more the mere signal of human authorship is worth. A costly signal, in the source post's terms. Using that paradox as a foundation for safety runs into three holes.

  • Signals get forged. Making something look human-written is cheaper than writing it. A costly signal that becomes cheap stops being a signal
  • Signals wear out. A feature that reads as human today becomes a common style tomorrow. The discrimination threshold does not stay put
  • The cost of reading the signal is paid by the reader. A reader without the time to check every time falls back on the source and the name, which are cheaper signals

The third hole matters most. Once readers judge by source rather than by prose, what accrues value is not the writing but the seat the writing is published from. That is a different class of asset from writing ability.

What actually gets more valuable - access, not prose

So what kind of writing does become more valuable? The common feature in what holds its price is not polish. It is whether the piece contains material the model could not reach.

  • Work built on primary sources — opened the document, counted the rows, and stated what the source does not contain. A summary of a summary never lands here
  • Field records — output that was actually produced, an incident that actually happened, an assumption that actually broke. Reproduction conditions raise the value further
  • Numbers you can check — where the value came from, what date it holds for, and what sits in the denominator. A number missing all three is decoration
  • A correction history — the record of having been wrong and having fixed it. Almost the only cheap way to build trust, and a class of asset a model cannot imitate
  • What was deliberately left out — what you did not write and why. Judgement usually shows up here rather than in what made it in

One thing runs through all five. What the model lacks is not prose ability but access. Were you in the room, can you open that file, can you put your name on it when it is wrong. The distinction deferred earlier returns here. Framed as a capability gap it looks like something that narrows with time; framed as an access gap it is not the kind of gap that narrows at all.

This is not purely in conflict with the source post. It is closer to rewriting, in terms of input availability, the same place the post explained through wickedness and theory of mind. The conclusion differs, though. A capability gap can be closed by training yourself to write better; access is not a thing training addresses.

How to read a forecast

Predictions in the form of which job is safe go wrong often, and they go wrong in patterned ways.

  • They take the occupation as the unit — what actually gets automated is a task. Inside one occupation some tasks go and others stay, and counting by occupation erases that split entirely
  • They look only at absolute capability — asking only whether the machine beats the human. Price is set by relative supply, and that term is missing from the equation
  • They omit the horizon — with no statement of how much by when, no outcome can ever falsify the prediction
  • They let the most visible layer stand for the whole — seeing draft production go and declaring writing finished, or seeing accountability survive and declaring everything safe

Better, then, to fix on things to observe rather than things to predict. The five below can mostly be checked in public data or in a contract, and they are noticeable when they change direction.

  • The direction of posting volume — not the absolute count but the ratio against occupations with low automation exposure
  • The direction of pay per piece — a phase where headcount is flat and only the rate falls genuinely exists. Both values have to be watched together
  • The composition of annual openings — new seats or replacement seats. When only replacement demand is left, the entry path has closed
  • Contract clauses — whether a human-authored confirmation appears, and whether disclosure of tool use becomes an obligation
  • Who the liability clause names — whose name goes down when something is wrong. When that line changes, the fourth layer has actually moved

This piece is not career advice

After all that, the question that remains is whether one should therefore learn to write. There is no answer to it here. Deliberately so.

The reason is plain. Everything cited above is an average over a population. A projection that an occupation's employment will stagnate says nothing about whether a particular person can make a living in it. Entry timing, the access already held, whether there is other income, the size of the market in the country where one lives — those change the answer completely. Recommending a direction without knowing any of them is not advice, it is noise.

What this piece tries to leave behind is a discriminating tool instead. The next time you meet a sentence saying some job is safe, being able to ask back which layer and which question it answered is enough.

Summary

The claim that writing may be the safest job from AI stands on four supports, three of them about the limits of models and only one about the market. The conclusion hangs on that one. Checked against evidence, that one turns out weaker than the conclusion it carries.

Split by layer it comes out like this. Draft production largely went; deciding what to write and carrying the blame did not. The layers that remain need fewer people than the layer that went. And the survival of an occupational category and the survival of the money it paid are different questions, with the evidence pointing in different directions on each.

What is left is a set of questions rather than a forecast. Which of the four layers does your work sit across, which parts of it lean on material from outside the text, and whose name goes on it when it is wrong. Answer those three and you can decide for yourself how to read the next forecast that comes along.

What I could not verify

  • AI-related employment projections for writers and editors in Korea could not be confirmed in a primary source. Every employment figure in this piece therefore comes from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics and does not transfer to the Korean market
  • The specific magnitudes of decline in the Hui, Reshef and Zhou paper are not carried in the public abstract, so they are not used here. Only the qualitative results the abstract states have been reproduced
  • The plateau in prose ability the source post describes is the author's own observation, and no material stating it in a form checkable against a public benchmark was found. This piece therefore treats it as the post's premise rather than as established fact
  • No pricing data showing that a human-authored mark is reflected in pay was located. The statement that the signal carries value runs only as far as logical consequence and is not an observed market price
  • The ruled-out bound in the Danish study is cited at different values between revisions and published versions, so only the figure in the abstract released by the research institute is used here
