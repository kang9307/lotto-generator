Even Realities G2 Review - What a 36g Smart Glasses HUD Says About a Different Path for AR

Instead of an immersive AR headset, this pair of smart glasses bets on light everyday information display - is $599 worth it?

Jul 15, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: A Different Path Called "Information-Display Glasses"

The AR and smart glasses market in 2026 has split into two camps. One prioritizes cameras and video capture, embodied by audio-and-camera glasses like Ray-Ban Meta. The other puts short bursts of information right in front of your eyes through a display. Even Realities is the standard-bearer for that second camp, and its new G2 puts two numbers front and center - $599 and 36 grams - to make the case for "a weight you can actually wear all day." Where bulky AR headsets chase an immersive experience that fills your entire field of view, the G2 goes the opposite direction: it looks and feels like ordinary glasses, and it only surfaces short, practical information such as notifications, directions, translation, and a teleprompter.

This piece is my own analysis, cross-checked against a hands-on review shared on GeekNews (hada.io), Even Realities' official specifications, and multiple international reviews. I look at where this product sits in the smart glasses market and whether $599 is actually worth paying.

1. What Is the Even Realities G2

1.1 Price and Core Specs

The G2 starts at $599 and uses aerospace-grade titanium temples with a magnesium frame. It weighs 36 grams, not far from a regular pair of glasses. It comes in panto and rectangular shapes, in gray, brown, or green. An optional R1 smart ring (about $249 extra) doubles as a finger-only controller you can use without raising your arm, and as a health sensor tracking sleep, calories, and steps.

1.2 Display Technology - Waveguide Plus Micro-LED Monochrome HUD

The core of the G2 is its display approach. Rather than a full-color screen, it uses a waveguide optics plus micro-LED combination that projects information in green monochrome to both eyes. The published specs are 640x350 resolution, a 60Hz refresh rate, and 1,200-nit brightness that auto-adjusts to ambient light. This combination isn't suited to photos or complex graphics - it's optimized for text and simple icons instead. What's worth noting is the choice to go binocular. Unlike Meta's latest display glasses, discussed below, which put a screen in only one eye, the G2 renders to both eyes, creating a sense of depth that makes information feel naturally anchored within your field of view. Hands-on reviews consistently note that this depth difference is quite noticeable while walking or cycling.

1.3 Battery and Durability

The 192mAh battery isn't large, but it reportedly lasts about two days in real use (up from about a day and a half on the G1). The charging case supports up to seven full recharges, so the glasses can go nearly a week without a wall charger. IP67 dust and water resistance was added for the first time, making the glasses usable in rain or during sweaty workouts.

2. What Changed From the G1

The improvements over the previous generation are concrete. Temples are 54% slimmer, the display area is 75% larger, and detail is 50% sharper (thanks to what the company calls Even HAO 2.0, a multi-layer waveguide optic). Battery life grew from a day and a half to two days, and IP67 water and dust resistance arrived for the first time. On the feature side, a new Conversate function surfaces reference information in real time during conversations, and on-device AI responses are reportedly faster. Taken together, the G2 isn't opening an entirely new category - it's a generational hardware refinement.

3. Core Features - Notifications, Navigation, Teleprompter, Translation, AI

The feature used most often day to day is notification customization: texts, calendar events, and stock prices can flash briefly in your field of view and disappear. The teleprompter lets you follow a script during a talk or presentation without breaking eye contact with your audience. Translation is advertised for up to 35 languages, and hands-on reviews say it works well in quiet settings but that speech recognition accuracy drops noticeably in noisy environments or when multiple people talk at once. The "Hey Even" AI voice assistant and the new Conversate feature aim to fact-check or surface reference information mid-conversation, but many reviewers say the real-world accuracy still feels demo-level. Navigation is supported too, but because there's no camera to read the surrounding environment, don't expect the precision of a dedicated mapping app.

4. Strengths - What Lightness and a Normal Look Actually Buy You

What the G2 does well is clear: 36 grams and a look indistinguishable from ordinary glasses. That's not a minor spec - it's the entire reason this category exists. Most smart glasses have failed because they were too heavy or too strange-looking to wear all day. In my view, the fact that the G2 gives you no reason to take it off is itself an achievement. The depth perception from a binocular display is a real, practical benefit too - information sits naturally within your field of view rather than floating awkwardly, so you can glance at short bits of information while walking or cycling without eye strain. For quick, glanceable information like a teleprompter cue or a notification, the G2 has already reached a genuinely practical level of polish.

5. Limits - Monochrome, Narrow Field of View, No Camera, Thin App Ecosystem

The limitations are just as clear. First, a monochrome display is fundamentally unsuited to photos, maps, or complex graphics - it can't carry much beyond text and icons. Second, the field of view itself is narrow, far from an immersive AR experience. This is more an intentional trade-off than a flaw, but it's easy to feel let down if you approach the G2 expecting what the name "AR glasses" implies. Third, there is no camera. That helps battery, weight, and privacy, but it also rules out any context-aware feature that requires recognizing what's around you. Fourth, the app ecosystem is still thin. Plugins can be built with web-based HTML and JS, which keeps the barrier to entry low, but no widely used "killer app" has emerged yet. A common thread across hands-on reviews is that the hardware is excellent, but the software and AI needed to fully use it haven't caught up.

6. Two Paths in the Smart Glasses Market - Camera-First vs. Display-First

The 2026 smart glasses market splits cleanly into two paths. One is the audio-and-camera approach of Ray-Ban Meta (Gen 2, $379), which focuses on capturing photos and video and social sharing with no display at all. The other is the display-first approach of Meta Ray-Ban Display ($799) or the Even G2 ($599), which puts information right in front of your eyes. Interestingly, even within the display-first camp, the approaches diverge. Meta Ray-Ban Display puts a 5,000-nit full-color HUD in a single eye and adds a camera (3K video at up to 60fps, 3x zoom), but requires a gesture-sensing Neural Band wristband for full control. The Even G2, by contrast, skips the camera entirely, renders monochrome to both eyes, and handles input through touch, voice, and an optional smart ring - all for nearly $200 less.

7. Is $599 Worth It - Who Should Buy This

On price alone, $599 is not cheap, and there's an added barrier for buyers outside the US, since the G2 isn't officially sold in every market and often requires an import purchase. Still, the price makes sense for specific people: those who present or speak often and genuinely need a teleprompter, those who want to glance at notifications during meetings or calls without pulling out a phone, and developers or early adopters who want to get hands-on with a new hardware form factor early. For consumers expecting an immersive AR experience with photos, video, or games, or a mature software ecosystem and a reliable AI assistant, it's premature. The hardware is ready; the software and AI needed to fully exploit it are still catching up.

8. Conclusion - Still a Product for Early Adopters

The Even Realities G2 makes the most convincing case yet for the "light information display" path in smart glasses. At 36 grams, with a look indistinguishable from ordinary glasses and two days of battery life, this category has finally reached hardware you can genuinely wear all day. But a monochrome display, a narrow field of view, no camera, a thin app ecosystem, and AI that still feels demo-level keep this from being a product for everyone.

In my assessment, the G2 isn't a finished consumer gadget so much as a well-built signpost pointing toward where smart glasses are headed. Developers and early adopters with a concrete use case - presenting, interpreting, glancing at notifications - will likely have few regrets buying it now. Consumers expecting a fully polished experience are better off waiting for a generation where the software and AI have matured to match the hardware. As of July 2026, the Even G2 proves that smart glasses don't have to chase immersion to be useful - "light and practical" is a viable path of its own.
