The Complete Guide to Preventing QR Code Scams (Quishing)

Tactics, red flags, and response steps for scanning QR codes safely in everyday life

Jul 2, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: Why QR Codes Became a Scam Vector

Menus, parking payment terminals, event check-ins, delivery confirmations - QR codes are everywhere now. Point your camera, and an app install, a payment, or a form fills itself in seconds. That convenience is exactly why QR codes became a fixture of daily life after the pandemic. But behind that convenience, quishing - phishing carried out through QR codes - has been rising in step. The FBI and the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) have both issued consumer warnings that criminals are tampering with QR codes to steal financial and personal information, and law enforcement agencies in multiple countries have echoed similar alerts.

This guide, current as of July 2026, breaks down the confirmed tactics behind quishing, the structural reason this attack is unusually hard to spot, practical steps to identify and prevent it before you scan, and what to do if you become a victim. It avoids naming any specific brand or service and focuses on general principles anyone can apply starting today.

1. Common QR Code Scam Tactics

Quishing is not one fixed technique - it shows up wherever QR codes are used. Here are the tactics most consistently reported.

1.1 Fake Sticker Overlays

The most widely reported tactic is pasting a fraudulent sticker directly over a legitimate QR code. In multiple U.S. cities, fake stickers were found glued over the payment QR codes on parking meters, redirecting drivers to a scammer's payment page instead of the actual parking operator. These stickers can be nearly indistinguishable from the real thing, and some are placed right next to legitimate service logos to boost their apparent credibility.

1.2 Exploiting QR Codes in Parking Lots, Restaurants, and Public Spaces

QR codes in openly accessible places - a restaurant's table menu, a tourist information board, a transit stop schedule - are prime targets. These locations are rarely inspected regularly by their operators and see high foot traffic, meaning a tampered sticker can go unnoticed for a long time.

1.3 Redirecting to Phishing Sites

After scanning, the victim may land on a convincing fake replica of a bank, card issuer, delivery company, or government login page. The moment a username, password, or card number is entered, it goes straight to the attacker. QR codes are also commonly attached to text messages or emails with urgent-sounding messages like "confirm your delivery" or "verify your identity." Because QR codes appear as images rather than clickable text links, embedding them in emails is an effective way to slip past security filters that scan for suspicious URLs - a tactic increasingly used in attacks against businesses as well as individuals.

1.4 Luring Users Into Installing Malicious Apps

Some QR codes point not to an official app store but to a direct file download page, tricking users into installing an application (such as an APK file) from an unverified source. Once installed, such apps can be used to intercept text messages, exfiltrate contacts, or grant attackers remote access to the device.

1.5 Payment Fraud

Scammers exploit situations where a small payment feels routine - parking, a meal, a donation, an event fee - and redirect the victim to a fake payment page to pocket the charge. Because the amounts are often small, victims may not notice anything wrong immediately, even as their card details are being harvested in the background.

2. Why QR Codes Are Especially Risky

Compared with a typical phishing link, QR code-based attacks work through a few structural weaknesses that make them harder to catch.

2.1 The URL Is Invisible Until You Scan

A hyperlink in an email or text can often be previewed by hovering or long-pressing, giving you a chance to spot an odd domain in plain text. A QR code, by contrast, encodes its destination inside a black-and-white grid that is unreadable by eye. You have no way to know where it leads until your camera decodes it - and in that moment, many people default to "let's scan it and see," which is precisely the opening attackers count on.

2.2 Physical Tampering Is Cheap and Easy

Unlike a digital phishing link, a QR code posted in the physical world can be overwritten with nothing more than a printed sticker. Because most operators don't visually inspect every QR code every day, a tampered code can remain undetected for a long time.

2.3 They Appear in Contexts People Already Trust

QR codes typically show up in settings people already trust - a parking garage, a restaurant, a government office, a package label - so people naturally lower their guard when scanning. Add a manufactured sense of urgency, such as "pay now or face a penalty," and critical judgment tends to slip even further.

3. How to Spot and Prevent Scams Before You Scan

The most effective defense against quishing is catching the warning signs before you ever scan.

3.1 Inspect the QR Code's Surface

For any QR code that leads to a payment or login, physically inspect it before scanning. Look and feel for a raised edge, a double-layered sticker, or a print texture that doesn't match its surroundings - all signs that a fake code may have been pasted over a real one.

3.2 Preview the URL and Verify the Domain

Most default camera apps show a preview of the destination URL once they recognize a QR code. Don't tap through immediately - check whether the displayed domain matches the official address of the organization or store you expect. If the spelling looks slightly off, the domain is unfamiliar, or it's a shortened URL that hides the real destination, stop before proceeding.

3.3 Install Apps by Searching the Official Store Directly

If a QR code prompts an app install, don't install through that link. Open Google Play or the App Store yourself and search by developer name and app title instead. Treat any install path that bypasses the official store as a warning sign in itself.

3.4 Prefer Official Apps and Bookmarked Sites

For banks, card issuers, government services, or any service you use regularly, it's safest to go directly through the official app already on your phone or a bookmarked website rather than a QR code. For anything requiring login or payment information, avoid the QR code path entirely as a rule.

3.5 Question the Source Before You Scan

As a baseline, don't scan QR codes that arrive in unexpected texts or emails, or that come with an unclear sender. Be especially wary of codes paired with urgency- or curiosity-driven language like "track your package," "verify your identity," or "claim your prize."

4. What to Do If You've Been Scammed

If you've already scanned a malicious code or entered information, acting quickly along the following steps matters most for limiting the damage.

4.1 Lock Down Accounts and Payments Immediately

If you entered a username and password on a suspicious page, change that password immediately - and change it on any other account where you reused the same password. If you entered card or account details, contact your card issuer or bank right away to request a transaction cancellation, card freeze, or account hold.

4.2 Remove Unfamiliar Apps and Check Your Device

If a QR code led to the installation of an unfamiliar app, delete it immediately. Temporarily turn off Wi-Fi and mobile data to cut off further communication, then run a security scan. Also check for signs of unauthorized text messages being sent or verification codes being intercepted.

4.3 Report the Incident

In the United States, report the incident to the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov or to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov. Many other countries have equivalent consumer protection or cybercrime reporting agencies - reporting not only helps your own recovery process but also helps authorities track and disrupt the same scam pattern before it reaches more victims.

5. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Is it dangerous just to scan a QR code with my camera app?

Simply scanning a code to preview its URL is generally not immediately dangerous by itself. The real risk begins once you tap through to the link, enter information on the resulting page, or install a prompted app. That said, some sophisticated attacks can exploit vulnerabilities merely by loading a page, so it's safest not to scan codes from an unclear or untrusted source in the first place.

Q2. Does that mean I can't trust any QR code in a public place?

Not at all - the vast majority of QR codes in public places work exactly as intended. But locations with looser oversight carry a relatively higher risk of a tampered sticker being placed and left unnoticed, so it's worth making a habit of checking the surface and verifying the URL for any code tied to payment or personal information.

Q3. I scanned a code and landed on a webpage but didn't enter anything - am I safe?

If you didn't enter any information, the risk to your accounts or finances is low. However, if the page triggered an automatic file download or an unusual permission prompt, it's best to close it immediately and run a security check on your device.

Q4. Should I be cautious about QR codes in work emails too?

Yes. Recent quishing campaigns have increasingly targeted employees, embedding QR codes in emails disguised as internal system logins, HR notices, or security update prompts to slip past filters that scan for suspicious text-based links. Verify that the sender and domain match your organization's policy, and when in doubt, check with your IT or security team first.

Q5. How can I use payment QR codes more safely?

Whenever possible, use the official app of the store or organization, or a payment terminal where you enter details directly, instead of scanning a QR code. If you must use a QR code payment, make it a habit to double-check the URL, the amount, and the recipient information that appears right after scanning before you confirm.

Conclusion: Three Seconds of Checking Before You Scan Prevents Most Damage

QR codes themselves are not a dangerous technology. The problem is a structural blind spot - people cannot read the destination with the naked eye - that criminals exploit. As of July 2026, quishing continues to be reported across both offline settings like parking lots and restaurants and online channels like email and text messages, and the tactics keep getting more refined. A handful of habits - inspecting the code's surface before scanning, previewing the URL, installing apps only through the official store, and handling anything sensitive through an official app or website rather than a QR code - can prevent a large share of quishing incidents. And if you've already been victimized, acting without delay to lock down accounts, freeze payments, and file a report matters more than anything else.
