The trigger - what the phrase without backpropagation covers

The page titled Dust: Pretraining Transformers Without Backpropagation was published on the Q Labs Research site under the names of four authors, Samip Dahal, Bishwas Mandal, Serdar Gülbahar and Akshay Vegesna, with the date line October 2026. It was introduced on GeekNews on October 6, 2026. The citation information on the page is a @misc entry that gives the address of the lab page, and a code repository is linked next to it. On its home page Q Labs describes itself as a research lab working on moonshots in deep learning, and it lists Dust under the heading Replacing backprop/gradient descent in its list of research.

The summary at the top of the page makes five claims.

  • That it is the first zeroth-order method that is competitive with backprop at pretraining transformer language models.
  • That with a large population, which is to say with substantially more compute, it approximates backprop closely and in multiple settings even exceeds it.
  • That it is orders of magnitude more efficient than weight-space evolution strategies (ES). The statement that from 1M tokens up it is on the order of 103 to 104 times more efficient than a transformer implementation of EGGROLL carries the qualifier that it is based on the extrapolations of the authors.
  • That larger models are more population-efficient, not less. A 243M-parameter model outperforms a model 120 times smaller at most population sizes.
  • That the gradient estimates align better with the backprop gradient as the population grows, and that the alignment holds at every scale tested, up to 1B tokens.

The phrase without backpropagation in the title has a defined scope. The source limits its purpose in these terms: the goal is to lay the foundations of a search-based credit assignment algorithm that is competitive with backprop at pretraining transformers, and no attempt is made to make it compute-efficient enough to replace backprop today. Three questions therefore have to be put to this announcement. What is perturbed, how is the learning signal obtained, and at what scale was it compared against what. The sections below follow that order.

The motivation the source gives - the assumption that search wins as compute grows

The introduction of the source starts from The Bitter Lesson by Richard Sutton. The lesson is that general methods that scale with compute eventually win, and the source uses AlphaGo Zero as its example: bootstrapping the network on human game records helped it learn faster at first, but with a lot of computation the network trained purely by self-play overtook it. The source then builds an analogy on this. Differentiability and backprop might be good inductive biases in the low-compute regime, where they make learning efficient, but in the high-compute regime they limit the space of architectures that work. The first half of that sentence carries the hedge might. The argument Sutton makes is laid out in the article on Richard Sutton and the bitter lesson.

The motivation the source gives falls into three strands.

  • Freedom of architecture. Backprop requires differentiability, and the source writes that architectures, optimizers and hardware have co-evolved around this constraint. Dust does not need the network to be end-to-end differentiable. The examples the source gives are neural nets with an external program in the loop, and transformers looped over many steps that backpropagation through time (BPTT) struggles to train.
  • Exploring the loss landscape. The source cites prior work (Liu et al., 2020) for the point that gradient-based methods fail to explore the loss landscape optimally even within one architecture, and it writes that a more flexible credit assignment algorithm based on search is likely an important step towards much better generalization. The word likely is part of the claim.
  • Activations as the search space. Pointing to mechanistic interpretability work that has shown reasoning to live in the activations, the source regards activations as a more interesting space to search over than weights. It adds that this approach could turn training into a search over latent reasoning, and the word could is part of that statement too.

The first strand is not something the present experiments test directly. The source writes that it did not train the new kinds of neural nets that Dust makes accessible and that this is left to future work. The second and third strands carry the qualifiers likely and could. What a looped structure does is covered in the article on looped transformers and the monitoring surface.

What is perturbed - activations at every token instead of weights

The bottleneck of evolution strategies is population size. As the source explains it, every member of the population needs its own perturbed copy of the weights and its own forward pass. EGGROLL makes the copies cheap with low-rank perturbations, but each member is still one sequence element of the batch, so the population is bounded by the number of forward passes one can afford.

Dust does not go through weight space at all. It adds Gaussian noise to the output of each linear layer, that is, to the activations, independently at every token. The source explains that at that token the network behaves as if a low-rank perturbation had been applied to the weights of the layer that produced the activations, without the perturbation ever being materialized in the weights. This is what it calls a virtual population. A sequence in a transformer has a few thousand tokens, so one forward pass evaluates a few thousand members per sequence instead of one. The source writes that on a modern transformer a single forward pass evaluates a population at least three orders of magnitude larger than weight-space ES does.

The idea of putting noise into activations is not new in itself. The source states that this is node perturbation and traces it to Widrow and Lehr (1990) and Werfel et al. (2003). The usual argument for node perturbation is dimensionality. The output of a layer has d_out entries and its weights have d_out × d_in, so activation noise lives in a much smaller space. The source itself points out that this argument does not hold naively for transformers. The noise on one sequence is a T × d_out tensor, and once T is at least d_in it has at least as many entries as the weight matrix. The advantage the source puts forward is therefore not a reduction in dimension but a new population that arises along the token axis, and this axis is orthogonal to the batch axis that EGGROLL already relies on.

There is one exception. Every weight in the model is trained this way except the 2L residual mixing scalars (L being the number of blocks), which are trained by ordinary weight-space ES. According to the appendix, these scalars make the residual stream entering each block a learned mix of the output of the previous block and the token embedding. Backprop is not used anywhere in the update, but not every parameter is trained by activation perturbation.

How the signal is obtained - an average of reward-weighted noise

The output of a linear layer y_t = W x_t is jittered at all tokens. y_t becomes y_t + σ a_t, where a_t is drawn from a standard normal distribution and σ is the noise scale. After the forward pass, a centered loss reduction c_s is calculated at each token s. It is the mean perturbed loss at that token across the draws evaluated together in the same batched forward, minus the perturbed loss of the draw in question. The block below is a plain-text transcription of equations (1) to (3) of the source.

r_t = sum_{s >= t} gamma^(s - t) * c_s                      (1)
                    g_t = -(1 / (K * sigma)) * sum_{i = 1..K} r_t(i) * a_t(i)   (2)
                    G_W = sum_t g_t * x_t^T                                     (3)
  • Draw. One independent jitter of all tokens is one draw.
  • Population K. The number of draws used for one update.
  • Reward r_t. The score that the noise at token t receives. It is the loss reduction at that token plus the loss reductions at the tokens after it, each multiplied by a power of the decay γ, because the noise also reaches later tokens through attention. With γ = 0 a jitter is rewarded by its own token alone.
  • Estimated error g_t. The reward-weighted noise averaged over the draws, which serves as the estimate of the error at the output of the layer.
  • Weight gradient G_W. The outer product of the estimated error with the layer input x_t, summed over tokens. The layer input is something the forward pass has already computed.

The source sums up the difference from backprop in one sentence. Backprop forms the same outer product with the same input, and the only difference is whether the output error comes from the chain rule or from the population. For an embedding layer the input is one-hot, so the outer product becomes a scatter-add of the estimated error into the row of that token.

The reason the estimate improves with a larger population is also given in the source. The reward-weighted noise of a single draw is the gradient plus an error with no preferred direction. Over draws the gradient adds up linearly while the errors add up as the square root, so the share of the error falls as the population grows and the interference vanishes in the limit. The source explains that with an unlimited population this estimator is the whole method and every perturbation could go into one forward pass. The rest of the method deals with the cost that arises at a finite population.

Reducing interference, and tuning - the other half of the method

At a population that can be afforded, the main cost is interference. When many layers are jittered at many tokens in the same forward pass, the loss change that rewards the noise of one token also picks up the effect of every other perturbation in that pass. The source reduces this in three ways.

  • Forward passes are split by layer type and by block. Each layer type has its own noise scale, and each block gets its own passes. Because the clean forward is cached, a draw that jitters block l reruns only the blocks from l onward. This is why a draw is slightly cheaper than a forward pass.
  • The attention internals are jittered separately. These are the query, key, value, gate and value embedding. Token losses barely register their jitters, so they are rewarded through the attention output instead.
  • The language modeling head is jittered directly on the cached logits. Only the cross-entropy is re-evaluated, and only for one slab of the vocabulary per draw, so the head runs a much larger population at a small fraction of the cost of a forward pass. According to the appendix, the slab that one head draw jitters is 256 logits.

The scoring of the attention internals is equation (4) of the source. Only the attention output of the block in question is recomputed with the jitter, and the score is how well the resulting change aligns with the error at the attention output that has already been estimated.

c_s = -< g_s, delta_o_s >                                   (4)

Here delta_o_s is the change the jitter makes in the attention output at token s, and g_s is the error at that output estimated with equation (2). The score is computed per head and put in place of the loss reductions in equation (1). In its related work section the source describes this arrangement as combining rewards for each token with local targets for attention outputs.

The allocation table in the appendix shows the detail of this structure. At K = 16,384 in the SGD setting (1M tokens), the draws rewarded with token losses fall into four streams, labelled in the source as Writer pair 7,040, MLP hidden layer 7,040, Attention output 1,664 and Token embedding 640, and they add up to K (7,040 + 7,040 + 1,664 + 640 = 16,384, calculated). There are further draws that are not counted in K. Per block they are 128 for the query, 1,280 for the value, 384 for the gate, 768 for the key and 512 for the value embedding, and the head gets 65,536. The source writes that the draws of the head and the attention internals are a small fraction of the FLOPs of an update. The noise scale also differs by site. The four streams rewarded with token losses use 0.2, except that the attention outputs of the deeper half use 0.4, and the attention internals and the head use 0.05. The decay γ is 0 for direct loss estimates and for queries, and for keys, values, gates and value embeddings it is 0.98 in most runs and 0.99 in the 16k runs at 10M and 20M tokens of the main comparison. Only the layers that later tokens read from receive credit from future tokens.

There are two ways to set these constants. One is a grid search that trains with each setting on a small token budget and keeps the setting that lowers the loss most, which the source calls reliable but expensive. The other is a grid search that maximizes the cosine between the Dust estimate and the backprop gradient on a single batch, which needs no training at all. A larger cosine on one batch does not always lower the loss after training, so the cosine picks candidates and training decides. The appendix writes that the constants specific to Dust (the noise scales, the credit decay and the draw allocation) are chosen with this cosine diagnostic, confirmed in training, and then held fixed across cells. In other words, no backward pass is used in the weight update, while the backprop gradient is used as the reference in the diagnostic that selects the constants (this is a reading that places the two statements side by side).

The source regards the tuning as mostly a one-time cost, since the settings it finds largely generalize across token budgets and population sizes. The exception is the largest population at 10M and 20M tokens, where a slower credit decay and a shift of draws toward the attention side still pay.

Two calculations help to gauge the scale. First, the batch is 16,384 tokens (8 × 2,048, calculated) and training is one epoch, so the number of updates is about 6 at 100k tokens, about 61 at 1M, about 610 at 10M and about 1,221 at 20M (calculated by dividing the token budget by the batch size). Second, the appendix writes the loss of a uniform prediction as ln V, and with a vocabulary of 4,096 this value is about 8.32 (calculated). The backprop test loss of 7.216 at 100k tokens sits about 1.1 below that. The result tables below are to be read on this scale, that is, in a range between a handful of updates and a little over a thousand.

The source also states the measures it took to keep the comparison even. Every method gets the same protocol and is tuned separately at every token budget and population. Dust and backprop share one momentum and learning-rate grid, while EGGROLL is tuned over its own grid of step size, momentum, noise scale and fitness shaping. The units in which the population is counted differ: draws for Dust, forward passes of the batch for EGGROLL. The source explains that from a population of 256 up Dust uses less compute than EGGROLL at the same population, so the comparison is lenient to EGGROLL. The architectural components named in the table are covered in the article comparing LLM architecture components in code, and the character of web corpora such as FineWeb in the article on the composition of training data.

The passage saying that coevolution of optimizers with Dust could lead to further gains is written as a suspicion (the wording is We suspect) and is left to future work.

The EGGROLL comparison - the denominator of the efficiency multiple

EGGROLL at a population of 16k does not reach Dust at 64 draws, even though its population is 256 times larger. It comes within 0.02 at 100k tokens (7.263 against 7.240) and stays 0.4 to 0.6 above at 1M, 10M and 20M tokens (6.708 against 6.290, 6.033 against 5.397, 5.865 against 5.265). The source writes that the same gap is visible in the curves during training: Dust at 1k and 16k draws stays close to the backprop curve throughout, while every EGGROLL curve falls behind early and flattens well above Dust at 64 draws.

This is where the efficiency multiple of 103 to 104 in the summary comes from. The EGGROLL ladder is still falling steeply at 16k, so continuing the curve puts the population it needs to reach the same loss as the smallest Dust population at several thousand to about ten thousand times that population. Appendix D shows in a table that this value depends on the fit. A power law fitted to all five points gives 23,000 times, 16,000 times and 8,100 times at 1M, 10M and 20M tokens. A straight line in log population gives 8,200, 3,700 and 3,300 times with the top three points, and 6,000, 4,100 and 3,100 times with the top two. A power law refitted with one point left out at a time ranges at 1M tokens from 11,000 times to infinity, where infinity marks a refit whose floor lies above the loss of Dust.

  • The denominator of the multiple is EGGROLL. It is not a statement about efficiency relative to backprop.
  • The baseline is an implementation by the authors. The source implements EGGROLL with the same transformer architecture and calls it EGGROLL-Transformer. The experiments that the abstract of the EGGROLL paper lists as its own are the pretraining of nonlinear recurrent language models that operate purely in integer datatypes, post-training of LLMs on reasoning tasks, and reinforcement learning settings.
  • The multiple is a continuation of a fitted curve. The three fits together give about 3,000 to 23,000 times, and the range is wider still once the leave-one-out refits are included.
  • The units of population differ. Draws and forward passes of the batch are compared as the same number, and the source writes that from 256 up this difference works in favor of EGGROLL.

The model-size experiment - the scope of larger models being more population-efficient

The conventional view is that zeroth-order methods cannot train large networks. A forward pass returns a single scalar, so the variance of the gradient estimate grows with the number of perturbed dimensions, and with it the population needed for a useful update. The source tests this by fixing the token budget at 10M and training four model sizes.

The source reads two things from this. First, at every population from 256 up the loss falls from 2M to 7M to 38M parameters, and the 243M model is only slightly worse. Even at the smallest population the model 120 times larger reaches about the same loss, and it is better at every other population. Second, small models saturate sooner while big models keep improving with large populations. The source writes that past 1k draws the 38M and 243M models gain about 30% more than the 2M and 7M ones. In the table the improvement from 1k to 16k is 0.094, 0.096, 0.122 and 0.128 in that order (calculated).

The table carries qualifications that the summary sentence does not.

  • The statement about outperforming a model 120 times smaller compares 243M with 2M. The size with the lowest loss at each population is 7.3M at 64 and 38M everywhere else (the bold entries in the table of the source).
  • The 243M model has a higher loss than the 7.3M model at all five populations. In the backprop row, 243M (5.048) is lower than 7.3M (5.066).
  • At the same population and the same size, the gap to backprop increases with model size. The source writes that the increase is barely noticeable, especially at large population sizes. The differences at 16k are those in the last row of the table.
  • This experiment was run before the protocol of the main comparison. The appendix writes that it uses the settings from before the token-embedding learning rate was improved and that this explains the difference from the main results. For the same 38M model, 10M tokens and 16k, the value in the main comparison is 5.049 and the value in this table is 5.036.
  • There is a single token budget, 10M. Whether the ordering by size holds at other amounts of training is outside the range of this table.

The interpretation the source offers is to think of model size as the size and the geometry of the search space. A larger model has a larger space in which to put a large population to use, and it may have a better-conditioned loss landscape, which could be why the search is more effective even at small populations. The last two clauses carry the words potentially and could in the source.

Gradient alignment - what the cosine measurement supports

The source measures the cosine between the Dust estimate and the backprop gradient on the same batch, per layer type. The measurement is made on checkpoints trained with backprop (10M, 100M and 1B tokens), with the population running from 64 to 128k. This measurement is what the phrase up to 1B tokens in the summary refers to. The largest amount of training in the experiments that pretrain with Dust is 20M tokens, and 1B tokens is a point at which direction was measured on a checkpoint produced by backprop.

The cosine rises with population for every layer type at every stage of training, and it follows the two-parameter law that is equation (5) of the source. c_max is the ceiling and c is the population at which the layer reaches c_max / √2. The source writes that the fit for each layer type has an RMSE below 0.06.

cos(K) = c_max / sqrt(1 + c / K)                             (5)

The fitted values for the 100M-token checkpoint in Appendix G show that the population needed differs widely from layer to layer.

  • Language modeling head: c_max 1.0000, c 759.
  • Attention output: c_max 0.8729, c 21,627. MLP output: 0.8779, 31,623.
  • Token embedding: c_max 0.7318, c 19,498. Value gate: 0.6657, 47,863.
  • Attention query: c_max 1.0000, c 76,736. Attention value: 0.8776, 173,780.
  • Value embedding: c_max 1.0000, c 495,450. Attention key: 1.0000, 616,595.

Putting K = 16,384 into equation (5) gives about 0.98 for the head, about 0.57 for the attention output, about 0.51 for the MLP output and about 0.16 for the attention key (these are calculations from the fitted law, not measured values). The largest population in the main comparison (the SGD ladder), 16k, is about 21.6 times c for the head and about one 37.6th of c for the key (calculated). The source likewise writes that the gradients of Dust approach those of backprop in cosine but that how close they get varies by layer type and by layer. The EGGROLL estimate, measured at the same number of forward passes, is still below 0.05 in cosine at 128k for every type but the head.

The source draws two things from this measurement. One concerns scale. Because the cosine stays flat across two orders of magnitude in tokens at large populations, it is possible, in the wording of the source, that at a large enough population the required population no longer grows with more tokens. The other is that the source regards the fact that the estimate does not converge exactly to the backprop gradient as a good property. The estimate points in a similar direction without being the same, which leads to a different optimization trajectory, and the source writes that in its experiments that trajectory can even be better than that of backprop.

It is worth separating out what kind of evidence this section provides. The cosine is a diagnostic showing that the directions are similar; it is not an experiment that shows training loss directly. The source itself wrote in Section 2.3 that a larger cosine on one batch does not always lower the loss after training.

Relation to prior work - the lines the source itself draws

The label the source gives itself is a zeroth-order optimization algorithm, and it states that its activation perturbations build on node perturbation. The related work section divides the surrounding research into four groups.

  • Zeroth-order pretraining of language models. Allaire et al. (2025) examined the difficulty of training transformers from scratch with weight perturbations, and their later method KronZO (2026) uses compact perturbations with Kronecker structure and selective directional updates to improve pretraining while reducing memory use. EGGROLL (Sarkar et al., 2025) makes large populations of weight perturbations efficient on GPUs through low-rank structure. The source distinguishes Dust by saying that it instead searches over activations and uses rewards for each token to extract more credit from each forward pass.
  • Search on the fine-tuning side. MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023) showed that language models can be adapted with forward passes alone and with memory use close to inference. Evolution Strategies at Scale (Qiu et al., 2026) fine-tunes all parameters with ES in language models with billions of parameters. Neural Thickets (Gan and Isola, 2026) finds useful task experts by randomly perturbing pretrained weights. The source writes that these results show how much search can achieve around a pretrained model, and that its own experiments address learning the representations themselves through pretraining from scratch.
  • Node perturbation and forward gradients. GEMINI (Le Cun et al., 1988) injected noise into the first hidden layer and recovered layerwise gradient estimates through iterative matrix inversion. Zoop (Hu et al., 2025) uses output perturbations for language model fine-tuning. Scaling Forward Gradient with Local Losses (Ren et al., 2023) combines activation perturbations and local losses with forward mode automatic differentiation to reduce estimator variance. Forward gradients with multiple tangents (Flügel et al., 2025) also use forward mode differentiation.
  • Local learning. PC-ALM from Sakana AI (Seely and Gould, 2026) propagates credit through local predictive coding dynamics and Lagrange multipliers. The source writes that this method uses local derivatives and is evaluated on image classification tasks.

Reduced memory use appears in this list in the sentences that introduce KronZO and MeZO. It does not appear as a motivation of Dust itself or as something Dust measures.

The conclusion section names three open questions. They are whether, and how, Dust can find better directions than the first-order gradient of backprop by implicitly exploring the loss landscape; the search space over architectures that opens up because end-to-end differentiability is not needed; and compute efficiency. Of the third, the source writes that it was not the focus of this paper.

What the public repository lets a reader run

The repository qlabs-eng/dust is released under the MIT License. The README introduces it as a minimal implementation of the research and writes that it preserves the estimator and tuned defaults of the paper while omitting the execution optimizations used in the full experiments. The model is in model.py, the algorithm and training loop are in dust.py, and a backpropagation SGD baseline that uses the same model and data is in baselines/backprop.py.

  • Requirements. Linux, Python 3.10 or later, and an NVIDIA GPU with bfloat16 support.
  • Data. prepare_data.py streams FineWeb from Hugging Face and produces the 1M-token and 10M-token training sets together with shared validation and test files. Each held-out split contains 544 sequences of 2,048 prediction tokens. The fixed BPE-4096 tokenizer is included.
  • Supported populations. 256, 1,024, 4,096 and 16,384, which use 1, 1, 4 and 8 GPUs respectively in the paper configurations. The default is 16,384 on eight GPUs. A population of 64 is not in the supported list.
  • What population means. It counts direct-loss draws across all GPUs, and head and local attention draws are additional. Every GPU processes the same batch and contributes different perturbations.
  • Model configuration. Both token budgets use an 8-layer, 512-wide transformer with sequence length 2,048 and batch size 8.
  • Outputs. Each run saves its configuration, metrics, a validation-selected best.pt, and a result.json containing the test loss of the selected checkpoint.

Among the commands in the README, those for data preparation, a single-GPU Dust run and the backprop baseline are as follows.

python prepare_data.py
                    python dust.py --tokens 1M --population 256 --output runs/1m-p256
                    python baselines/backprop.py --tokens 1M --output runs/bp-1m

The values in the source that correspond to this configuration are in the 1M-token column of Table 1 in the appendix. Dust at a population of 256 is 6.067 and backprop is 5.959, both means over three seeds. The 256 cell is still above backprop, and the first cell in that column in which Dust goes below backprop is 1k (5.934). According to the README, a population of 1,024 is also a single-GPU configuration.

Nine questions for reading claims of this type

Turning the way the Dust announcement answers for itself into questions gives a list that can be applied as it is to other announcements that put forward training without backpropagation. The answer for Dust follows each item.

  • What is perturbed. Weights or activations, and are there parameters for which a different method is used as an exception. For Dust it is per-token activations, and only the 2L residual mixing scalars are trained by weight-space ES.
  • In what unit is the population counted. Draws, forward passes or sequences, and is there computation that is not counted. The K of Dust does not include the draws of the head and the attention internals.
  • Where does the learning signal come from. Is the loss used directly, or does the signal pass through a local target. Dust uses the reduction in token loss, and for the attention internals it uses a local target on the attention output.
  • Where was the backprop gradient used. The update and the tuning diagnostic are looked at separately. Dust does not use it in the update and uses it as the reference in the cosine diagnostic that selects the constants.
  • Pretraining or fine-tuning. The source places MeZO and similar work as search around a pretrained model and distinguishes itself as pretraining from scratch.
  • How large are the model, the data and the amount of training. These are read as parameter count, token count and number of updates. The main comparison uses 37.7M parameters and 100k to 20M tokens.
  • Who tuned the baselines, and how. Backprop was tuned on the same grid, and EGGROLL is a transformer implementation by the authors.
  • Measurement or extrapolation. Limit values and efficiency multiples are continuations of fitted curves. The 4.431 at 20M tokens, the 5.248 of the Adam ladder and the multiple relative to EGGROLL belong here.
  • What is reported as cost. Which of compute, time and memory is given. Dust gives the number of draws and the number of GPUs.

An announcement for which these questions have answers lets the reader set the scope of the claim directly. In the case of Dust, that scope can be summed up as follows: in the early phase of training a transformer with tens of millions of parameters, and with substantially more compute, a loss close to that of backprop is reached without a backward pass (this is a summary that puts the figures of the source together).

What could not be confirmed

  • The multiple of compute, training time and memory use relative to backprop. The main text and appendix contain no such figures, and the source says only that compute efficiency was not the focus.
  • The values of the individual points in Figures 1 and 5. They are presented only as figures and were not transcribed. From Figure 3 only the 64k point of the Adam ladder (5.368) was transcribed, and the point values at the other populations were not.
  • Which layers the label Writer pair in the allocation table of the appendix refers to. No defining sentence was found in the main text or the appendix.
  • Whether running the minimal implementation reproduces the values of Table 1 in the appendix. The repository code was not executed, and the README gives no expected figures.
  • The body of the prior papers that are cited. For EGGROLL only the arXiv abstract page was opened, and the other prior work is described in the words with which the source introduces it.
  • Whether the work has been published at a venue or peer reviewed. The citation information on the page is a @misc entry giving the address of the lab, and the page carries no other publication information.
  • Matters concerning Q Labs as an organization. Nothing beyond the introductory text on its home page was opened.
