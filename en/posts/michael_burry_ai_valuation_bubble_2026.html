Michael Burry: SpaceX and Anthropic Aren't Worth a Trillion - The AI Valuation Bubble Debate 2026

The Big Short's Contrarian and the Trillion-Dollar Question

Jun 4, 2026 Finance

Introduction: When The Big Short Says 'No' Again

Just before the 2008 global financial crisis, when almost everyone believed home prices would rise forever, one man stood on the opposite side and placed an enormous bet. That man, etched into public memory by the film The Big Short, is Michael Burry. In 2026, Burry has once again poured cold water on the market's heat. This time the target is not real estate but AI. He has publicly questioned whether SpaceX or Anthropic is really worth a trillion dollars.

This article is my own analysis, written to help readers think about that statement calmly. It neither blindly follows Burry nor blindly mocks him. The goal is to lay out, in a balanced way, what his argument actually is, how the AI valuation bubble debate is structured, what is the same and different versus the dot-com bubble, and what counterarguments stand on the other side - then to set out what an individual investor should discern amid the noise. My conclusion up front: the point is not 'is Burry right or wrong' but the ability to ask, for yourself, 'what does this price assume?'

1. Who Is Michael Burry - A Myth Built on One Correct Call

To understand Burry, start with his temperament. A physician turned investor, he is known - along with a glass eye - for reading social cues less and digging into data with obsessive intensity. In the mid-2000s, he tore through the structure of U.S. subprime mortgage bonds one by one, became convinced the system would collapse, and demanded that banks create credit-default-swap products that did not yet exist so he could build a massive short position.

In the end, he was right. But a fact often forgotten lurks here: he was right too early. For years before the market broke, his positions ran enormous paper losses, investors raged, and he had to forcibly block redemptions from his fund. The market nearly stayed irrational longer than his patience could hold. The episode showcases his insight and, at the same time, shows that 'pointing out a bubble' and 'timing it' are entirely different problems. That distinction is decisive for reading this AI debate.

2. Burry's Argument - The Weight of the Number 'Trillion'

Compressed into one sentence, Burry's critique is this: the price was built on a story, not on results. SpaceX and Anthropic are powerful companies in their respective domains, but a trillion-dollar valuation, he argues, bakes into the price - almost as settled fact - a future expectation of 'this is what it will become,' rather than today's revenue and earnings.

2.1 The Gap Between Valuation and Revenue

The basics of valuation are simple. A company's value is ultimately the present value of the cash flows it will earn in the future. The question is how large and how certain you assume those future cash flows to be. For a trillion-dollar figure to hold, revenue must grow explosively for years, that growth must translate into high margins, and that state must persist - all at once. Burry's skepticism puts a question mark on all three assumptions.

2.2 Expectation as Fuel

The risk Burry sees is the volatility of the fuel called 'expectation.' Results change slowly, but expectations can flip overnight. When a valuation is held up by narrative rather than earnings, the moment that narrative cracks, the price collapses far faster than the fundamentals. This is the mechanism he has warned about repeatedly. To emphasize again, this is not a put-down of SpaceX or Anthropic as companies but skepticism about how the price was formed.

The excellence of a company and the fairness of its share (or stake) price are separate questions. A great company is not always a great investment, and the difference is always made by the price you pay.

3. The Structure of the AI Valuation Bubble Debate

Looking beyond one man's remark to how the AI valuation debate itself is built widens the view. The debate organizes roughly around three axes.

3.1 First Axis - The Revenue Multiple

The most common yardstick is enterprise value as a multiple of revenue. A mature company is valued at a few times revenue, but an AI firm with high-growth expectations can carry multiples of dozens of times. Bulls say 'growth justifies the multiple'; bears say 'the multiple already prices in a flawless future.' The same number yields opposite conclusions.

3.2 Second Axis - The Durability of Growth Expectations

The second issue is how long the growth lasts. Early explosive growth appears in almost every new technology, but the timing and pace at which that growth rate decelerates govern the valuation. For AI, the key variables are the marginal cost of model-performance gains, price declines from intensifying competition, and the burden of compute costs - and how these bend the growth curve.

3.3 Third Axis - The Concentration of Capital

The third is the flow of money. When capital piles into one sector, valuations rise not on fundamentals but on the expectation of 'the next capital to arrive.' That is a classic sign of overheating. If capital stops or reverses, prices correct regardless of fundamentals. What Burry guards against is precisely the possibility that this capital concentration reverses.

4. The Dot-Com Comparison - Same and Different

The analogy most often summoned when discussing an AI bubble is the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s. This comparison is useful, but used lazily it clouds discernment instead. Let us separate the same from the different.

4.1 What's Similar

  • Expectation outruns revenue: The structure of converting a technology's potential into a value far larger than current results is the same.
  • The 'this time is different' narrative: The sentence that appears in every bubble repeats in the AI era too.
  • Infrastructure investment runs ahead: Like the fiber-optic overbuild of the dot-com era, there is concern that today's data-center and GPU investment runs ahead of demand.

4.2 What's Different

  • Real revenue and cash flow: In the dot-com era many firms had little revenue. Today, by contrast, many leading AI and infrastructure companies carry real, large-scale revenue and cash flow.
  • Immediate utility: Many services back then promised usefulness 'someday,' whereas today's generative AI is already used broadly in daily life and work.
  • The character of the capital: Unlike the indiscriminate retail money of the dot-com era, today's AI investment is weighted more toward big tech and professional investors.
The conclusion is neither 'the same' nor 'different.' The point is discernment. The only proper use of the dot-com analogy is to separate which firms have real revenue and a moat from which are inflated by expectation alone.

5. The Counterargument - AI's Real Value and Moat

For balance, the bullish case standing opposite Burry deserves an honest hearing. Its argument rests not on emotional optimism but on structural grounds.

5.1 Real Moats Exist

The heart of the bull case is that, unlike the dot-com era, leading AI firms possess moats that are hard to imitate. The research capability to build frontier models, vast compute infrastructure, scarce top-tier talent, accumulated data, and lock-in effects embedded deep in enterprise customers' workflows are the grounds. Such moats are hard for latecomers to close instantly with capital alone.

5.2 The Market Can Stay Irrational for a Long Time

Another powerful counterpoint is timing. To borrow the economist Keynes, the market can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent. Burry himself suffered under this truth for years before 2008. Even if you pinpoint a bubble precisely, failing to time when it bursts costs even the pessimist dearly. So 'Burry is right' and 'selling now is right' are entirely different propositions.

5.3 AI's Utility Is Already Becoming Revenue

Finally, AI has entered a stage of generating cash, not just promising it. Measurable cost savings and revenue gains are appearing in coding assistance, customer support, content generation, and data analysis. That utility is real is the biggest difference from the dot-com era and the firmest foundation of the bull case.

6. Who's Right - The Wrong Question

Many people ask 'will Burry be right, or the bulls?' I see this as the wrong question. The real lesson of this debate is not about picking a winner.

Burry's value lies not in the accuracy of his prophecy but in the direction of his question. He makes us ask again, when we take a price for granted, 'what does this price assume?' The bull case's value is in presenting counter-evidence that 'there is substance nonetheless.' The two voices are not enemies but the two wings an investor needs to reach a balanced judgment. The moment you listen to only one, discernment collapses.

Following a celebrity's prediction is delegation, not analysis. Whether Burry is right or wrong, the responsibility for the outcome falls back on you, who followed his words.

7. A Korean-Investor Lens - Importing the Frenzy, and the Lag

This debate matters especially for Korean investors, because Korea sits closer to 'importing' the AI valuation frenzy. As the U.S.-originated narrative spreads to local proxies, AI-theme funds, and the semiconductor value chain, lag and amplification often arise.

I want to stress two things. First, distinguish theme from substance. Not every stock labeled 'AI-related' actually earns AI revenue. Second, beware the lag of the narrative. The pattern of expectations already digested once in global markets arriving late at home - causing people to buy at the peak - has repeated. Burry's warning is more useful read as a mirror reflecting the danger of this 'late chasing' than as a verdict on any specific company.

8. Five Lenses for Individual Investors to Discern

Instead of recommending a specific action, here are five questions to check yourself against whenever you encounter AI valuation news. All rest on discernment and caution.

  1. The price's premise: What future does this valuation assume as 'already settled'? How fragile is the price if that assumption misses?
  2. The ratio of substance to expectation: Is this valuation held up by current results or by future expectation? Is that ratio something you can bear?
  3. The timing trap: Judging something a bubble is not a signal to act now. Can the market stay irrational longer than your patience?
  4. Diversification and limits: Are your assets concentrated in one theme, one narrative? Is it within an amount that, if lost, will not shake your life?
  5. Verifying the source: Am I mistaking a celebrity's one-liner for analysis? Have I checked the basis of that claim with my own hands?

9. Outlook and a Critical Eye

Where will this debate go? My forecast splits three ways. First, in the short term, the chance that Burry's warning lands immediately is low. Strong capital and real revenue are likely to hold up valuations for a while. Second, yet not every AI company will survive equally. 'Expectation-only firms' without real moats will be re-rated quickly the moment capital flows cool. Third, therefore, the future battleground will not be the binary of 'bubble or not' but separating the wheat from the chaff.

Critically, I should add that Burry-style pessimism has its own weakness. It is often too early and often expressed in the language of absolute catastrophe, risking pushing the listener out of the market entirely. It is possible to acknowledge a bubble's existence while still discerning and holding assets with real substance within it. The extreme of buying everything or selling everything is closer to emotion than to analysis.

10. Three Signals That Will Decide the Debate

Rather than betting on who wins, it is more useful to watch the signals that will actually settle this argument over the next two years. Three of them matter most, and each cuts both ways.

The first is the revenue trajectory of the leaders. The single cleanest test of the bull case is whether the largest AI firms keep converting capability into accelerating, durable revenue - not one-off contracts, but recurring, expanding usage. If revenue compounds and margins hold, a high valuation becomes a steep-but-defensible bet rather than a fantasy. If growth visibly decelerates while spending stays heavy, Burry's skepticism gains weight. Watch the second derivative, not just the headline number: the rate at which growth itself changes is what valuations are really pricing.

The second is the cost curve of compute. Much of the bear case rests on the idea that the cost of training and serving frontier models stays punishing, squeezing the very margins the bull case assumes. If hardware efficiency, better architectures, and competition drive inference costs down faster than prices, profitability improves and the trillion-dollar math gets easier. If costs prove sticky and a price war erupts, even fast-growing firms can grow into thinner and thinner margins. This is the least-discussed variable in most retail commentary, and arguably the most decisive.

The third is the behavior of capital itself. Valuations at this altitude depend on a steady inflow of new money willing to underwrite the future. The moment that flow hesitates - a rate shock, a high-profile disappointment, a regulatory jolt - prices can reprice violently regardless of fundamentals, exactly as Burry warns. For an individual, the practical reading is not to predict the turn but to size positions so that being wrong about the timing is survivable. The lesson of every prior bubble is that the people who got hurt most were not those who were wrong about the technology, but those who were right about it and still over-committed at the wrong price.

Conclusion - The Answer Is Discernment, Not Prophecy

The trillion-dollar question Burry threw at SpaceX and Anthropic is not itself a matter of right or wrong to be settled. It is a tool that makes us ask better questions. What does the price assume? What is the ratio of substance to expectation? Am I analyzing, or borrowing someone's conviction?

I close with three attitudes of discernment. First, listen to both bulls and bears. The moment you hear only one, balance collapses. Second, do not confuse spotting a bubble with timing it. They are different skills. Third, do not delegate the prediction - check the premise. To say it once more: this article is for information and education only and is not investment advice.
