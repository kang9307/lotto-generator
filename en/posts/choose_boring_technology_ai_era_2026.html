Choose Boring Technology Revisited 2026 - Why Proven Tech Stacks Matter More in the AI Coding Era

Innovation Tokens in the AI Era - Why Choosing Boring Technology Is the 2026 Power Move

Jun 1, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: Why an 11-Year-Old Essay Is Trending Again in the AI Era

On May 31, 2026, "Choose Boring Technology, Revisited" climbed to the front page of news.hada.io as topic #7 with 20 points. The original is Dan McKinley's 2015 essay of the same name. The fact that the same title is back at the top of developer conversations eleven years later is itself worth noticing. Usually an eleven-year-old tech essay belongs in a museum. Instead, this one fits the AI coding era even more sharply. In this piece I want to work through why, in the context of real-world engineering teams.

The core intuition is this. As AI starts writing our code for us, we have paradoxically started treating technology choice more casually. Judgments like "AI writes it anyway, so let's try this new framework" or "we can just ask the AI about this brand-new database, so let's adopt it" have multiplied. The friction of adoption dropped. But the fact that adoption got easier is a completely different thing from running that technology reliably six months later. The way I see it, AI lowered the entry cost of adopting technology while doing nothing for the exit cost of maintaining it.

This article does three things. First, it restates McKinley's original concepts - boring technology and innovation tokens - in my own words (my interpretation, not a translation). Second, it argues why this eleven-year-old idea becomes more important in the AI coding era. Third, it offers a decision framework you can use immediately, along with a balanced look at the risks. The bottom line up front: the real competitive edge in the AI era is not the ability to adopt new tech quickly, but the discipline to decide where novelty is worth spending.

1. What 'Choose Boring Technology' Actually Means - The Core of McKinley's 2015 Essay

First, clear up a misconception. 'Boring technology' does not mean old or behind-the-times technology. What McKinley calls 'boring' means the failure modes are well known, the operational characteristics are predictable, and there are few surprises. It is not that it lacks excitement; it is closer to lacking risk. If an outage hits at 3 a.m. and tens of thousands of people have already hit that exact wall, with the fix one search away, that is boring technology.

Let me restate McKinley's insight my own way. Every technology carries two kinds of unknowns. One is known unknowns, the territory we know we do not know. The other is unknown unknowns, the territory we do not even know we do not know. Proven boring technology has almost no unknown unknowns, because someone has already fallen into every pit. New technology, by contrast, is full of unknown unknowns, and when they detonate in production we are defenseless.

So McKinley's conclusion is simple. A company's core mission is not to use new technology but to deliver value to users, and technology choice should be the means of achieving that mission with the least possible risk. The moment novelty becomes the goal itself, we lose the plot.

2. Innovation Tokens - Innovation Is a Limited Resource, Not an Unlimited One

The most-quoted metaphor in McKinley's essay is the 'innovation token.' In my interpretation it works like this. A company or team has a budget of new risk it can absorb, and McKinley pictures that budget as roughly three tokens. Every time you adopt an unproven new technology, you spend one. Adopt a new language and that is one; a new database, one; a new message queue, another.

Why only three? Because novelty does not add - it multiplies. Stack an unproven deployment model in an unproven language on top of an unproven database, and when an outage hits, locating which of the three is at fault becomes a combinatorial explosion. A team that has spent all its tokens pours every ounce of energy into debugging, and the time to actually build user value evaporates. A large share of the failed projects I have watched followed exactly this pattern.

The core message of innovation tokens: innovation is good, but it is not an unlimited resource. Spend your tokens on the one or two points that matter most to your differentiation, and deliberately make the most boring choice everywhere else.

This metaphor is powerful because it reframes technology choice from a 'good/bad' question into a 'budget allocation' question. No new technology is bad in itself. The real question is whether it is worth spending some of our limited innovation budget. That single shift in perspective filters out most over-eager adoptions.

3. Why It Matters More in the AI Coding Era - The Core of This Article

Now the main argument. Why does a 2015 idea become more important in 2026? I explain it through three mechanisms.

3.1 AI Lowers the Learning Curve and Inflates the Temptation to Adopt New Tech

Adopting a new framework used to mean reading docs, following tutorials, and burning days when you got stuck. That learning friction effectively acted as a natural line of defense. Because adoption was a hassle, you only did it when you truly needed to. AI coding tools have driven that friction to nearly zero. Ask "build a login feature with this brand-new framework" and working code appears in minutes. With the barrier gone, the frequency of "let's just try it" explodes. AI has become the single most powerful accelerator pedal for adopting new technology.

3.2 But Validation and Maintenance Are Still a Human Job

Here is the trap. What AI lowered is only the cost of writing code the first time. Whether that code is secure, handles edge cases, survives a 10x traffic increase six months later, and can be migrated when its library gets abandoned - all of that validation and maintenance is still a human responsibility. And validation cost is low for boring technology and high for new technology. Boring tech has Stack Overflow and countless production case studies to help you validate; with new tech, you might be the first casualty.

3.3 AI-Generated Code + New Technology = Debugging Hell

This is the most dangerous combination. AI is astonishingly accurate on boring technology with abundant training data, but on data-poor new technology it produces plausible hallucinations. It calls APIs that do not exist, mixes patterns from old versions, and confidently proposes subtly wrong configurations. The problem is that because AI wrote the code, the developer does not deeply understand its internals either. When code you do not understand, new technology with thin validation data, and the possibility of hallucination all overlap, it is hard even to find a starting point for debugging when something breaks. I call this 'the new debugging hell of the AI era.'

In short, AI made it easier to spend innovation tokens but more expensive to have spent them all. Adoption got cheaper and recovery got harder. That is why in the AI era my core claim is that you should spend tokens even more sparingly.

4. The Five Strengths of Boring Technology

Here are five concrete strengths boring technology brings in the AI era.

  1. Stability: failure modes are already known, so there are few unknown unknowns. Even during an outage, the candidate causes are narrow and recovery is predictable.
  2. Documentation and training data: years of docs, blogs, and case studies have accumulated. That same corpus is AI's training data, so AI is far more accurate on boring technology. The paradox: boringness raises AI accuracy.
  3. Community: tens of thousands have already hit the same problem. One search yields a fix, and the warnings about dangerous patterns are plentiful.
  4. Hiring: plenty of people on the market can handle PostgreSQL, standard Linux, and proven frameworks. Few can handle brand-new tech, which raises hiring and handover costs.
  5. Debugging: search, tooling, and instrumentation are all mature. With new tech, even the observability tools are immature, so you fight outages blindfolded.

All five share one thing: they are things time has validated. And the validation of time is something AI cannot replace. AI can produce code quickly, but it cannot compress five years of production operating experience.

5. When New Technology Is Justified - Five Conditions

Of course, not all new technology is bad. I consider adopting new tech justified when you satisfy all or most of these five conditions.

  1. Directly tied to core differentiation: the technology creates a decisive competitive edge for your product. For an AI inference company, GPU inference optimization is differentiation.
  2. Structurally impossible with boring tech: the problem genuinely cannot be solved with your proven stack. It must be "now it works" rather than merely "this is cooler."
  3. Understood failure modes + rollback plan: the team understands how this tech breaks and has a path back when it does.
  4. Expected benefit > cost of adoption: even after summing learning, operations, hiring, and migration costs, the benefit is clearly larger.
  5. Within the remaining token budget: if you have already spent your innovation tokens elsewhere, no matter how attractive, you pass this time.

If a new technology cannot pass these five gates, it is probably risk rather than differentiation. "Because it's cool" is not a passing reason.

6. Applying It to AI Tools Themselves - Which AI Stack Is Reliably 'Boring'?

The interesting paradox is that you must apply this principle to your AI tool choices too. As of 2026, the AI ecosystem floods us weekly with new models, new agent frameworks, new vector databases, and new orchestration tools. Pour all your innovation tokens here and the AI infrastructure itself becomes debugging hell.

The 'boring choice for your AI stack' I recommend is this. Make the model layer a proven major model (a stable Claude or GPT-class model) your default, and use the most standard, mature tools you can for orchestration, retrieval, and storage above it. Do not bind your core pipeline to a brand-new agent framework that changes every week; put an abstraction layer in place so you can swap things out. In other words, the faster-moving the area like AI is, the more firmly you should prop it up with boring technology around it, so you can evolve stably while swapping only the model.

7. A Practical Decision Framework - Budgeting Your Innovation Tokens

Let me bring the abstraction down to practice. The innovation-token budgeting procedure I use has four steps.

  1. Define differentiation in one sentence: write "our product's real differentiation is ___" in a single sentence. Tokens go here only.
  2. List your boring defaults: predefine the 'most boring default' for non-differentiating areas - database, auth, deployment, logging, queue - so you do not deliberate every time.
  3. Keep a token ledger: explicitly count how many unproven technologies you currently run. Past three, the warning light comes on.
  4. Add the AI-verifiability question: for every new tech, ask "when AI-generated code on this is wrong, can we catch it?" If you cannot, hold the adoption.

Turning these four steps into a one-page checklist for your tech-decision meetings filters out, in my experience, more than half of impulsive adoptions.

8. Cases - Success with Boring Tech vs Failure with New Tech

Rather than name specific companies, I will generalize two typical patterns I have observed.

The success pattern (concentrating on boring tech): one fintech startup defined its differentiation as a single thing - its risk-scoring algorithm - and went boring on everything else. PostgreSQL, monolithic deployment, a proven web framework. It spent its new-tech tokens only on the algorithm. As a result the team spent almost no time on infrastructure outages and focused on improving the core algorithm, advancing key features twice as fast as competitors with the same headcount.

The failure pattern (scattering across new tech): conversely, one team simultaneously adopted a brand-new graph DB, an experimental language, a just-released serverless platform, and a beta message queue. Each was cool, but when an outage hit, just tracing where the cause lived took days. Having spent four or five innovation tokens at once, the team fought the complexity it had created instead of building user value, and repeatedly missed its launch dates. The difference between the two was not the quality of the tech but whether tokens were concentrated or scattered.

What I want to stress is that the failed team was not less talented. In fact, the engineers who pile on the newest tools are often the most curious and capable people on the team - they read every release note and want to try everything. That is precisely the trap. Curiosity is a strength when it is aimed at the differentiation, and a liability when it scatters innovation tokens across non-differentiating plumbing. The job of an engineering leader in the AI era is not to suppress that curiosity but to channel it, by making the innovation-token budget explicit so that "I really want to try this" must compete openly against "is this where our one remaining token should go." When the conversation is framed that way, even the most excited engineer usually agrees to keep the database boring.

9. Applying It to Startup and Enterprise Environments

This principle is especially useful, but carries specific traps, depending on your environment.

Enterprise / SI: large enterprise and systems-integration shops tend to be conservative and favor proven technology, so they align well with the boring-tech philosophy. The opposite trap, though, is letting 'boring' harden into 'obsolete' and freezing on decade-old tech. The key is to choose technology that is proven yet still actively maintained, not simply old. As AI coding enters these shops, the temptation toward new tech grows, and I argue these are exactly the organizations that should manage an explicit innovation-token ledger.

Startups: conversely, startups face cultural pressure that equates 'using the newest tech' with 'engineering strength.' Listing the latest stack in an investor pitch looks impressive, and a job post's roster of shiny tech attracts talent. But as I see it, an early-stage startup scattering new tech is the most expensive form of self-display. A small team fights infrastructure complexity and loses the time it needs to validate product-market fit. The smaller the startup, the more its survival hinges on the discipline of concentrating tokens on one point of differentiation and going boring on the rest.

10. The Risk - The Trap of 'Always Old Tech' (Balance)

It would be a problem if this article read as 'new tech is always bad.' That is the opposite trap. Let me note the risks of the boring-tech principle in a balanced way.

  • Fossilization risk: using 'boring' as an excuse to reject even genuinely proven new tech lets technical debt pile up and erodes competitiveness. Containers, the cloud, and modern frameworks were once new tech and are now boring standards.
  • Confusing 'proven' with 'abandoned': technology that is old but no longer maintained is not boring - it is dangerous. A library whose security patches have stopped is the worst possible choice.
  • Over-conservatism in the differentiation area: go boring even in your core differentiation and competitors overtake you. Conserve tokens, but spend them boldly where they must be spent.

In the end the core is two axes: not 'new vs old' but 'proven vs unproven,' and 'differentiating vs non-differentiating.' Seen through these two axes, the boring-tech principle is not conservatism but sophisticated risk management.

11. Five Things Developers Can Apply Immediately

  1. Write down your differentiation in one sentence. It becomes the reference point for every technology decision.
  2. Predefine boring defaults for non-differentiating areas. Document the standard choice for DB, auth, deployment, and logging.
  3. Create an innovation-token ledger. Count the unproven technologies you run and stop past three.
  4. Attach the 'AI-verifiability' question to every new tech. Confirm you can catch AI-generated code when it is wrong.
  5. Delete 'because it's cool' from your list of adoption reasons. Cool is not differentiation.

12. Conclusion - In the AI Era, Restraint Is the Edge

The reason Dan McKinley's eleven-year-old message gets sharper in 2026 is clear. To the same degree that AI erased the friction of adopting technology, the human judgment to restrain novelty became scarcer and more valuable. In an era when anyone can try a new technology in minutes with AI, the real edge comes not from "can you try it" but from "can you decide not to try it."

There is also a deeper reason this matters now. In the pre-AI world, a poor technology choice mostly cost you time - weeks of learning, some painful migrations. In the AI world, a poor choice can cost you understanding itself. When AI writes code you do not fully grasp, on a technology the broader community has not yet validated, you accumulate a kind of debt that compound interest does not begin to describe: you own a system whose behavior nobody on the team, and no search engine, can fully explain. Boring technology is the one reliable hedge against that, because boring technology is exactly the technology whose behavior is already explained. Choosing it is less about nostalgia and more about preserving your team's ability to reason about its own system.

I will close with three things you can apply right now. First, explicitly cap your innovation-token budget at three and keep a ledger. Second, concentrate tokens on a single point of differentiation and use the most boring, proven defaults for the rest. Third, before adopting any new tech, ask 'if AI-generated code is wrong here, can we catch it?' Holding to just these three will pre-empt most of the AI-era debugging hell. Choosing boring technology is not a lack of ambition - it is the most mature way to concentrate ambition where it matters most.
