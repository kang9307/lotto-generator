How to Actually Use an AI Assistant at Work

Not a feature tour but a boundary - sorting what fits and what does not by task type

2026-07-29 Tech & IT

Introduction: Can AI do this is a question with no answer

The question has no answer because for nearly every task the answer is it will produce something. What matters is not whether it can, but what it costs to turn the output into something usable. Skip that calculation and there will be a range of work where the tool makes you slower, with no mechanism to notice.

So this article does not list features. It answers one question: is the task in front of me the kind of task I should delegate? Answering it requires two things that no marketing page provides. One is how these systems are built and therefore how they fail. The other is how cheaply I can check the result.

One ground rule first. Every product fact here was confirmed directly in official documentation on 29 July 2026, and anything that could not be confirmed was simply left out. Model lineups, pricing and usage limits change within months, so where a number would go this article puts a checking routine instead. It also does not judge one service better than another. Ranking belongs elsewhere; what is covered here is the boundary that applies whichever tool you use.

No hype and no dismissal. AI does everything is wrong and it is useless is wrong. Every true statement here comes with a condition attached.

1. The boundary comes from the mechanism - why some work fits and some does not

You do not need to memorize the strengths and the weaknesses separately. They come from the same property, so understanding the property produces both lists on its own.

Here is the core. These models are not programs whose rules a person wrote line by line. Anthropic interpretability research is explicit about this: models are trained on large amounts of data and develop their own strategies for solving problems. Those strategies live in computations formed during training rather than in a procedure someone designed. The same research adds one decisive observation: what the model says it did and what actually happened can diverge.

Addition is the sharpest example. Researchers observed the model running parallel paths, one approximating the rough value and another settling the final digit. Yet asked how it computed the answer, it describes the standard carry-the-one procedure taught in school. The account it gives differs from the process that ran. The same work describes motivated reasoning, where the model works backwards from a desired answer, and notes that it can produce plausible-sounding steps that do not reflect its actual computation.

Strengths and weaknesses fall out of that single property together. In table form:

PropertyStrength it producesWeakness from the same property
Strategies form from data, not from rulesFlexible on open-ended language workDrifts on procedural work with one right answer
Very good at producing plausible continuationsFast drafting, summarizing and rewritingProduces plausible text when it does not know
Stated method can diverge from actual processAttaches readable reasoning for humansThat reasoning cannot serve as a check
Knowledge is fixed at a point in timeBroad accumulated general knowledgeAnything later is structurally absent
Answers within the context it is givenAccurate on material you paste inBlind to your organization without your data

The right two columns are not two lists but the same cell seen from either side. That is why hunting for a setting that fixes the weaknesses points the wrong way. What needs fixing is not the tool but the assignment of work.

Current information follows the same logic. Official documentation lists, per model, the date through which its knowledge is most extensive and reliable. As listed on 29 July 2026: Claude Opus 5 is May 2026, Claude Fable 5 and Claude Sonnet 5 are January 2026, and Claude Haiku 4.5 is February 2025. Facts after that date are not in the model. With a search tool attached it can go look; without one, the property above means it may invent rather than decline.

2. Six kinds of work that fit - all share several valid answers with a human choosing

The six below look unrelated but share one condition: more than one output is acceptable, the person keeps the final choice, and errors are visible immediately.

  • Drafting — removes the cost of starting from a blank page. A draft is by definition something you rewrite, so errors in it are cheap. What you gain is not a finished product but something to edit.
  • Format conversion — prose into a table, a table into JSON, a log into a clean list. No information is created, only rearranged, so setting the original beside the result makes verification nearly free.
  • Summarizing — pulls out what was in a long document. Because the source is still there, any suspicious line can be traced back. The advantage disappears the moment you keep the summary and discard the source.
  • First pass on a code review — filters the obvious before a human looks. Whether a finding is real is settled the instant you open the line, which is exactly what cheap verification means.
  • Brainstorming — the job is widening the option set, so wrong ideas cost nothing. A person picks anyway, and weak candidates mark the edges of the strong ones.
  • Mapping an unfamiliar field — used to find out what you should be searching for, not to get an answer. Since the goal is vocabulary and branches rather than conclusions, the follow-up research corrects it naturally.

Compressed into one line: work where the human stays the judge. Conversely, the moment a person passes the output straight into the next step untouched, the same task moves into the next section.

3. Five kinds of work that do not fit - all share one right answer and a high cost of error

These five do not change character as the tools improve, because they follow directly from the property in section 1.

  • Unverified fact-checking — the most common and most expensive misuse. Producing a plausible sentence is more natural for the model than saying it does not know. Tellingly, the first mitigation in Anthropic official documentation is to explicitly give the model permission to admit uncertainty. Having to grant that permission is itself evidence that it is not the default.
  • Current information — nothing after the knowledge date exists. Ask without a search tool and the risk of not being told that something is missing remains. Prices, versions, personnel and amended regulations are the danger zone precisely because changing is their nature.
  • Exact arithmetic — the addition case applies directly. The answer is sometimes right, but the explanation does not describe the process, so it cannot serve as a check. Push amounts, rates and deadlines to a calculator or spreadsheet, and let the model go only as far as composing the formula.
  • Final legal, medical and tax judgments — the cost of a wrong answer is asymmetric, and the right answer shifts with jurisdiction, date and individual circumstance. Useful for understanding background or organizing what to ask a professional, never as the conclusion itself.
  • Decisions without internal data — advice produced without your real numbers, contract terms, staffing and political constraints is a generality. Generalities are dangerous because they are hard to argue with in a meeting. Paste the material in and this changes, but then the input rules in the next section come first.

The shared trait is that one answer is correct and being wrong is hard to undo. The line between sections 2 and 3 is drawn by the structure of the task, not by how clever the tool is.

4. The core rule - do not use it when verification costs more than writing

You do not have to keep both lists in your head. They compress into one rule.

Output from an assistant is not something you ship, it is something you check. So the time actually saved is writing time minus verification time, not writing time. There is a clear range where that subtraction goes negative, and inside it the tool makes you slower or more exposed. Slower is the better outcome; skipping verification and drifting into exposure is more common.

Official documentation supports the rule directly. The page on reducing hallucinations closes by stating that these techniques do not eliminate hallucinations entirely and that critical information should always be validated. Verification is not optional, it is a line item. Line items get counted.

TaskHow you verifyVerification costVerdict
Turn a transcript into decisions, owners, deadlinesCompare against the source side by sideLowDelegate
Convert a table to JSON or CSVRow counts, a few sampled rows, run a parserLowDelegate
Draft a report or announcementA human edits it end to end anywayLowDelegate
First pass over a code diffOpen the flagged lines, run the testsLowDelegate
Collect terms and branches in a new fieldCorrected by the research that followsLowDelegate but never as a conclusion
Summarize an internal document, source keptTrace only suspicious sentences backMediumDelegate with conditions

Every low-cost row shares one trait: everything needed to check is already in your hands — the transcript, the table, the code, the tests. Every high-cost row shares the opposite: checking means going outside. That trip is what eats the saving.

Restated as something you can apply on the spot:

  • If the material needed to check is already in the conversation, delegate — summarizing, converting and extracting from what you pasted all qualify.
  • If checking means going outside, go outside first — if you will open the official page anyway, there is no reason to do it second.
  • Wanting to skip verification is the signal — you want to skip it because it is expensive, and expensive already answered the question.
  • Settle who is accountable if it is wrong — if that is you, verification is not negotiable, it is a cost.

5. Managing inputs - what should never go in

Manage the output but not the input and the problem you create behind you outweighs the time you saved. Two layers need checking: how the service handles data and what your organization permits. Neither substitutes for the other.

Confirmed in Anthropic official privacy guidance on 29 July 2026: on consumer plans, chats and coding sessions are used to improve the models when you have chosen to allow it, with separate conditions such as conversations flagged for safety review. Incognito chats are not used to improve Claude even when that setting is enabled. Commercial products and the API are covered by separate terms, linked from a different page. In other words, the same company's products can be handled differently depending on which product and which setting you are on. That is why opening the settings screen yourself is not a step you can skip.

And permitted by the service is not the same as permitted by your employer. Company devices, company accounts and company material carry their own rules, and those are often stricter.

Input typeDefault callWhyWhat to do instead
Passwords, API keys, tokensNeverA leak causes damage immediatelyUse placeholders, keep real values in a secret manager
National ID, bank or card numbersNeverLegally protected and irreplaceable identifiersKeep the shape, remove the value
Customer names, phone numbers, addressesDo notRaises purpose limitation and third-party issuesPseudonymize as Customer A, 000-0000-0000
Unpublished financials, contract termsNot before checking policyTrade secrets and disclosure rules applyAsk using ratios or relative values
Internal source codeDepends on policy and productConsumer and business terms differApproved tools and accounts only, minimum files
Public documents, standards, papersFineAlready public materialNo limit, but verify quotes against the original
Your own drafts and notesGenerally fineYour own work with low sensitivityOnly check whether third-party data crept in

In practice, pseudonymization solves most of this. Turning analyze why customer Kim's March revenue of 42 million dropped into Customer A's March revenue fell 18 percent month over month leaves answer quality essentially unchanged, because what the model needs is structure rather than identity. The instinct that removing names and real figures will hurt the result is usually unfounded.

6. Workflows, part one - meeting notes, document drafts, format conversion

From here on, this is written as you would actually use it. Each workflow pairs a prompt structure with a verification method. A workflow without a verification method is not a workflow, it is a gamble.

The principles behind the prompts come from the official prompting guide. It advises writing clearly and directly, as if explaining to a bright new colleague who lacks your context, and offers a test: show the prompt to a colleague and if they would be confused, so will the model. It also notes that explaining the reason behind an instruction improves results, and that separating instructions, context and input with XML tags reduces misreading. The templates below simply follow that advice.

Workflow A - decisions and action items from a transcript

<role>
You are the person who writes up meeting records.
</role>

<purpose>
This write-up is read by teammates who missed the meeting.
Decisions and owners matter more than background.
</purpose>

<rules>
1. Use only what is in the transcript. Do not infer to fill gaps.
2. If an owner or deadline was not set, write TBD.
3. Statements that are unclear as decisions go under Parked instead.
4. End each item with one verbatim line from the transcript as evidence.
</rules>

<output_format>
## Decisions
- decision | owner | deadline | supporting line
## Action items
- item | owner | deadline | supporting line
## Parked
- topic | reason
</output_format>

<transcript>
(paste here)
</transcript>
  • Verification — take each supporting line and run a text search against the transcript. If it is not found, that item was invented.
  • Why demand quotes — official documentation advises that for long documents, having the model extract word-for-word quotes first grounds its answer in the actual text and reduces hallucination. Here that technique is repurposed as a verification device.
  • Why allow TBD — it removes the pressure to fill blanks. Creating a place to say I do not know is the first mitigation on the list.

Workflow B - drafting a document

<role>
You write internal announcements.
</role>

<audience_and_goal>
Audience: all staff, including non-technical roles
Goal: warn about disruption from next week's maintenance
</audience_and_goal>

<must_include>
- date, time and expected duration
- which functions are unavailable during the window
- the workaround
- who to contact
</must_include>

<constraints>
- under 250 words, at most 4 paragraphs
- any technical term gets a one-line plain explanation in brackets
- apologize once, at the end
- do not invent facts I did not supply, such as cause or prevention plans
</constraints>

<material>
(list only confirmed facts here)
</material>

Write the draft first, then below it list Needs confirmation
for every item you left blank because I did not supply it.
  • Verification — read the Needs confirmation list first. If it is empty while the draft contains specific figures, those figures were manufactured.
  • Why write long constraints — the official guide advises being specific about the desired output format and constraints. Pinning length and paragraph count to numbers works far better than asking for something concise.
  • What the list buys you — it makes the model report blanks instead of filling them, shrinking the verification surface from a whole document to a few lines.

Workflow C - converting data formats

<task>
Convert the table below into a JSON array.
</task>

<schema>
[
  {
    "id": string,
    "name": string,
    "qty": integer,
    "note": string or null
  }
]
</schema>

<rules>
1. Never guess a value that is missing from the source. Blank becomes null.
2. Strip thousands separators from numbers and emit integers.
3. Do not drop rows you cannot convert. Copy them verbatim into <problem_rows>.
4. Output exactly one JSON code block and one <problem_rows> block. No prose.
</rules>

<verification>
On the last line, print: input rows = N, output elements = M
</verification>

<table>
(paste here)
</table>
  • Verification — count the rows yourself and compare, then run the JSON through a parser. Format conversion is one of the few tasks a machine can verify for you.
  • What the problem-rows block does — it forces failures to be surfaced rather than silently deleted. Shrinking row counts get caught right here.
  • The no-prose clause — mixed prose breaks automated handling. The official guide notes that telling the model what to do beats telling it what not to do, so output exactly two blocks reads better than do not explain.

7. Workflows, part two - code review, unfamiliar territory, learning

Workflow D - first pass on a code review

<role>
You are the first reviewer, running before a human reads this diff.
A person makes the final call.
</role>

<review_order>
1. existing behavior this change could break
2. missing input validation
3. paths with no error handling
4. behavior that needs a test and has none
5. hard-to-read sections (last, briefly)
</review_order>

<rules>
- Never speculate about a file you have not opened.
  If the code is not here, write only: needs review: filename
- Give a file and line number with every finding.
- Mark confidence as high, medium or low.
- Report low-confidence findings too. Filtering is my job, not yours.
</rules>

<diff>
(paste the diff)
</diff>
  • Verification — open the flagged lines. A review finding is adjudicated on the spot, which makes this one of the cheapest tasks to verify.
  • Where the no-speculation clause comes from — the official prompting guide gives, as its example for reducing hallucination in coding work, an instruction to never speculate about code you have not opened and to read the file first. The rule above is that shape transplanted.
  • Why ask for confidence — telling it to report only what matters filters out real bugs along with the noise. Moving the filter to the human side recovers more of them.

Workflow E - mapping unfamiliar territory

I am new to <field>. I do not want an answer,
I want to know what I should be researching.

Organize it in this order.

1. The 5 to 8 core concepts needed to understand this field
   - one line each
2. The main choices practitioners face, and what decides them
3. Three false assumptions beginners commonly hold
4. The kinds of primary sources I should read myself
   - standards, official manuals, or regulations
5. Items in this topic most likely to have changed recently
   - flag anything that may fall after your knowledge date

Mark confidence on every item. If confidence is low, say so.
If you do not know, say you do not know.
  • Verification — this workflow exists to produce search terms, not conclusions. Opening the primary sources named in item 4 is what corrects the errors.
  • What item 5 is for — it makes the model flag what may fall after its own knowledge date. Since documentation lists a different date per model, that item becomes your must-check-externally list.
  • Never treat it as the answer — the output is a map, not a destination. Mistaking the map for the destination is the only way this workflow fails.

Workflow F - learning support

Below is my own understanding of <topic>.
Respond as a conversation partner, not as a grader.

<my_understanding>
(explain it here in your own words)
</my_understanding>

Order:
1. Point out what is factually wrong in my explanation, with reasons.
2. Point out what is right but imprecisely stated.
3. Tell me which important conditions or exceptions I left out.
4. Ask me three questions that test whether I really understand.
   - Do not give the answers.
5. List what I must confirm against primary sources.

Do not summarize the correct answer. Make me explain it again.
  • Verification — answering item 4 yourself is the verification. If you stall, you did not understand it, and this structure surfaces that immediately.
  • Why ban the summary — receiving a summary feels like understanding but does not build the ability to reproduce it. Keeping the explanation on the human side is the whole point of this prompt.
  • The role of item 5 — it separates out what needs checking against primary sources, splitting the study scope from the verification scope.

8. Write prompts as structure, not as sentences

The six templates look long, but they rest on only a handful of principles, and those principles are written down in the official guide. Six things are worth memorizing.

  • Be clear and direct — the documentation advises writing as if to a bright new colleague who lacks your context, and offers the test that if a colleague reading your prompt would be confused, the model will be too. Do your best is not an instruction.
  • Include the reason — the same page states that explaining the motivation behind an instruction improves results. Do not use ellipses works less well than this text will be read aloud by a speech engine, so do not use ellipses.
  • Add three to five examples — the documentation calls examples one of the most reliable ways to steer output format and tone, and recommends three to five that mirror the real case and differ enough from each other.
  • Partition with XML tags — when instructions, context, examples and input blur together, interpretation wobbles. The guide advises wrapping each kind of content in its own tag.
  • Long material at the top, the question at the bottom — for inputs above roughly 20k tokens the guide advises putting long documents near the top, above your query and instructions, and notes tests where queries placed at the end improved response quality by up to 30 percent on complex multi-document inputs.
  • Leave room to say I do not know — the first item in the hallucination guidance is to explicitly allow the model to admit uncertainty. A single line saying if you are not sure, say so genuinely works.

One more thing belongs here. Official documentation states plainly that not every problem is best solved by prompt engineering; latency and cost, for instance, can sometimes be improved more easily by choosing a different model. If you have rewritten a prompt five times and hit the same failure, that is probably not a prompt problem but an assignment problem — a signal to go back to the list in section 3.

If the techniques themselves are what interest you, that axis lives elsewhere: prompt engineering basics for the fundamentals, and advanced prompting techniques for chain of thought and chaining. This article covers only the decision of whether to delegate at all.

9. Models and plans - keep a checking routine instead of numbers

There are deliberately few numbers in this section, because model lineups and pricing are the fastest-aging part of this article. What follows is where to look and what to check each time.

Confirmed in the official model documentation on 29 July 2026: the current-generation table lists Claude Fable 5, Claude Opus 5, Claude Sonnet 5 and Claude Haiku 4.5, while earlier generations such as Claude Opus 4.8, 4.7 and 4.6 and Claude Sonnet 4.6 and 4.5 sit in a separate section. The documentation advises starting with Claude Opus 5 if you are unsure, and points to Claude Fable 5 for workloads needing the highest available capability. Context windows are listed as 1M tokens for Fable 5, Opus 5 and Sonnet 5, and 200k tokens for Haiku 4.5. This lineup can change.

Plans divide into Free, Pro and Max for individuals and Team and Enterprise for organizations. Prices and usage limits are not repeated here, because they move often and a wrong number is worse than no number. Checking the official pricing page before you pay is the only correct procedure.

The choice is not a matter of taste; three checks settle it.

  • Does your material fit the context window — handling a stack of hundred-page contracts and polishing one email need very different lengths. Read the listed context length and decide from that.
  • Is the information you need from after the knowledge date — if so, the fix is not a different model but a search tool or pasting the material in yourself. That is a task-design question, not a plan question.
  • Does it satisfy the data-handling terms your organization requires — as section 5 showed, consumer and business products fall under different terms. This check comes first.
  • If speed or cost is the problem, try a different model — as the documentation itself notes, latency and cost are sometimes solved by model choice rather than prompt work.

If comparing models against each other is what you actually want, that axis is covered separately in the AI model comparison hub. This article covers only the boundary that applies to every model alike.

10. Five common failures and how to correct each

Failures usually come from assignment rather than from the tool. Here are five frequent ones with their real causes.

SymptomActual causeCorrection
Reads well but the facts are wrongAn expensive-to-verify task was delegated unverifiedGo back to the table in section 4 and reassign
Answers differ every run and the format wandersOutput format and constraints were never specifiedPin length, sections and prohibitions to numbers
Ignores the pasted material and speaks in generalitiesThe material was buried below the instructionsMove long material to the top, question at the end
Invents instead of saying it does not knowNo room was left to admit uncertaintyState explicitly that uncertainty should be declared
The arithmetic is wrong but the explanation is flawlessThe explanation does not describe the actual computationCompute elsewhere, let the model write only the formula

The last row is the most dangerous, because the better the explanation reads, the more likely you are to skip the check. As section 1 showed, the explanation is not a record of the computation. A written rationale is readable prose, not a proof.

The third row also gets missed often. When pasted material is apparently ignored, it is tempting to conclude the model disregarded it, when in fact the placement is usually the problem. That is exactly why the official guide tells you to move long material up.

Turning fact-checking into a repeatable process is beyond this article's scope. The staged procedure of extracting claims and tracing them to primary sources is covered in the hallucination fact-check process. This article only decides when that process needs to run.

Conclusion: classify the task before choosing the tool

Should I use AI is a question with no answer. Split into three, it answers every time.

  • Does this task have one right answer or many — many, with you choosing, fits well. Exactly one, hard to undo when wrong, does not.
  • What does checking the result require — if what you already hold is enough, delegate. If you must go outside to find it, go outside from the start.
  • Who is accountable if it is wrong — if that is you, verification is not negotiable, it is a cost. Put it in the calculation up front.

This is a useful tool. But the reasons it is useful differ somewhat from the reasons the advertising gives. Filling a blank page, moving between formats, pulling branches out of a long document, taking one pass before a human looks — there is little that substitutes here. For establishing facts, exact arithmetic, current information and final judgment, other tools and other people do the job far more accurately.

One last word on posture. There is no reason to alarm and no reason to inflate. What is needed is knowing precisely what the tool is good at, and that range comes from official documentation and a verification-cost calculation, not from a marketing line.

```
Write the draft first, then below it list Needs confirmation
for every item you left blank because I did not supply it.
```

**Verify**: an empty Needs confirmation list next to specific figures in the body means those figures were manufactured.

### T3. Data format conversion

```
<task>
Convert the table below into a JSON array.
</task>

<schema>
[
  {
    "id": string,
    "name": string,
    "qty": integer,
    "note": string or null
  }
]
</schema>

<rules>
1. Never guess a value that is missing from the source. Blank becomes null.
2. Strip thousands separators from numbers and emit integers.
3. Do not drop rows you cannot convert. Copy them verbatim into <problem_rows>.
4. Output exactly one JSON code block and one <problem_rows> block. No prose.
</rules>

<verification>
On the last line, print: input rows = N, output elements = M
</verification>

<table>
(paste here)
</table>
```

**Verify**: count the rows yourself and compare, then run the JSON through a parser.

### T4. First pass on a code review

```
<role>
You are the first reviewer, running before a human reads this diff.
A person makes the final call.
</role>

<review_order>
1. existing behavior this change could break
2. missing input validation
3. paths with no error handling
4. behavior that needs a test and has none
5. hard-to-read sections (last, briefly)
</review_order>

<rules>
- Never speculate about a file you have not opened.
  If the code is not here, write only: needs review: filename
- Give a file and line number with every finding.
- Mark confidence as high, medium or low.
- Report low-confidence findings too. Filtering is my job, not yours.
</rules>

<diff>
(paste the diff)
</diff>
```

**Verify**: open the flagged lines and run the tests.

### T5. Mapping unfamiliar territory

```
I am new to <field>. I do not want an answer,
I want to know what I should be researching.

Organize it in this order.

1. The 5 to 8 core concepts needed to understand this field
   - one line each
2. The main choices practitioners face, and what decides them
3. Three false assumptions beginners commonly hold
4. The kinds of primary sources I should read myself
   - standards, official manuals, or regulations
5. Items in this topic most likely to have changed recently
   - flag anything that may fall after your knowledge date

Mark confidence on every item. If confidence is low, say so.
If you do not know, say you do not know.
```

**Verify**: open the primary sources named in item 4. Never treat the output as a conclusion.

### T6. Learning support

```
Below is my own understanding of <topic>.
Respond as a conversation partner, not as a grader.

<my_understanding>
(explain it here in your own words)
</my_understanding>

Order:
1. Point out what is factually wrong in my explanation, with reasons.
2. Point out what is right but imprecisely stated.
3. Tell me which important conditions or exceptions I left out.
4. Ask me three questions that test whether I really understand.
   - Do not give the answers.
5. List what I must confirm against primary sources.

Do not summarize the correct answer. Make me explain it again.
```

**Verify**: answer item 4 yourself.

## 3. Verification checklist

Before handing work over:

- [ ] Does this task have one right answer or many
- [ ] Is the material needed to check the result in my hands right now
- [ ] If checking means going outside, would going outside first be faster
- [ ] Am I the one accountable if it is wrong
- [ ] Does the material I am about to paste contain anything that should not go in

After receiving the result:

- [ ] For any figure, did I ask for a source and open that source
- [ ] For any quotation, did I text-search it against the original
- [ ] For any computation, did I recompute it in a calculator
- [ ] I left room to say I do not know, so why is that section empty
- [ ] Did I confirm every current-information item on an official page
- [ ] If I felt like skipping verification, is that a signal not to delegate this task

## 4. What should never go in

| Input type | Call | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| Passwords, API keys, tokens | Never | Use placeholders, keep real values in a secret manager |
| National ID, bank or card numbers | Never | Keep the shape, remove the value |
| Customer names, phone numbers, addresses | Do not | Pseudonymize as Customer A, 000-0000-0000 |
| Unpublished financials, contract terms | Not before checking policy | Ask using ratios or relative values |
| Internal source code | Depends on policy and product | Approved tools and accounts only, minimum files |
| Notes containing other people's personal data | Only after cleaning | Strip third-party data first |
| Public documents, standards, papers | Fine | Verify quotations against the original |
| Your own drafts and notes | Generally fine | Only check whether third-party data crept in |

**Two layers of input control**

1. How the service handles data — consumer products and business products or the API fall under different terms. Open the settings screen and check for yourself.
2. Your organization's policy — a service may permit what your employer forbids, and the employer side is usually stricter.

**Pseudonymization principle**: what the model needs is structure, not identity. Removing names and real figures leaves answer quality essentially unchanged.

## 5. Six prompt principles

1. Write clearly and directly, as if explaining to a bright new colleague who lacks your context.
2. Include the reason behind the instruction.
3. Add three to five examples that mirror the real case and differ enough from each other.
4. Partition instructions, context, examples and input with XML tags.
5. Put long material near the top of the prompt and the question and instructions below it.
6. Add a line such as if you are not sure, say so, to create room for admitting uncertainty.

If you have rewritten a prompt five times and hit the same failure, it is not a prompt problem but an assignment problem. Go back to table 1.
