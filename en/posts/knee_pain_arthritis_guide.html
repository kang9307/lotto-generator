Knee Pain & Arthritis - A Complete Guide to Causes, Relief & Prevention

How to protect the joint that carries your body weight for a lifetime

Jun 13, 2026 Health & Wellness

Introduction: when a single step down the stairs feels daunting

If your knee aches going down the stairs, feels stiff when you stand up after sitting for a while, or throbs the day after a long walk or a hike, you are far from alone. The knee is the largest joint in the body and a workhorse that bears several times your body weight with every step. That exposes it to a lifetime of wear and load, and more people report knee pain as they age.

The reassuring part is that a great deal of knee pain improves meaningfully with care you can manage yourself. In fact, multiple clinical guidelines on knee osteoarthritis place exercise, weight management, and education - not drugs or surgery - at the foundation of care for all patients. Medications and procedures are adjuncts added when conservative care does not bring symptoms under control.

This guide lays out, on an evidence basis, the common causes of knee pain, the types of arthritis (osteoarthritis vs rheumatoid), self-care with reasonably solid evidence (weight loss, low-impact exercise, quadriceps strengthening, stretching, heat and cold), what to avoid, the evidence on supplements, everyday knee protection, and the warning signs that mean you should see a doctor. It does not decide for you - it is a map for a smarter conversation with your doctor about your own knees.

⚠️ Medical disclaimer. This article is for education and information only and is not medical advice. Diagnosing knee pain, identifying the type and severity of arthritis, decisions about medication, injections, or surgery, and exercise prescriptions must be decided with a physician (such as an orthopedic surgeon or rheumatologist) and a physical therapist. Appropriate management varies greatly by age, weight, cause of pain, and coexisting conditions. The recommendations here are general references based on published guidelines and are not an instruction to self-prescribe any exercise, supplement, or drug. If you have warning signs such as pain after an injury, sudden swelling, fever, or deformity, do not delay - see a medical professional.

1. How the knee is built - the basics of understanding pain

The knee is not a simple hinge. Where the thigh bone (femur), shin bone (tibia), and the kneecap (patella) meet, you find articular cartilage covering the bone ends smoothly, shock-absorbing menisci, stabilizing ligaments, and the muscles and tendons that move it all - intricately interlocked.

When any part of this structure wears, is injured, or is overloaded, pain follows. So behind the single complaint "my knee hurts" there can be several causes, and the right approach depends on which one it is.

2. Common causes of knee pain

2.1 Osteoarthritis (knee OA)

The most common cause is osteoarthritis. As cartilage gradually wears under years and load, bone-on-bone friction increases, producing pain, stiffness, swelling, and grinding. It usually worsens after activity or toward the end of the day, feels stiff in the morning or after sitting a while, but that stiffness typically eases within 30 minutes.

2.2 Cartilage and meniscus injury

Twisting or jumping movements, or the aging process, can tear a meniscus, causing the knee to catch (locking) or to hurt at certain angles.

2.3 Ligament injury

Sports trauma and similar events can injure a ligament (for example, the anterior cruciate ligament), leaving the knee unstable with a "giving way" sensation and swelling.

2.4 Overuse and tendinitis

A sudden jump in activity, repetitive movements, or poor mechanics can overload the tendons and the area around the kneecap (such as patellar tendinitis or runner's knee) and cause pain. It often improves when you reduce the load and correct posture and strength.

2.5 Body weight

The force on the knee while walking is several times your body weight. That makes excess weight an important risk factor for knee load, osteoarthritis progression, and pain - and at the same time, one of the most changeable factors.

2.6 Other causes

  • Inflammatory and autoimmune arthritis: rheumatoid arthritis, gout, and others cause pain through inflammation rather than wear, and are managed differently.
  • Infection: rare but a joint infection causes acute, severe swelling and fever and is an emergency.
  • Referred pain: hip or back problems can feel like knee pain.

3. The two faces of arthritis - osteoarthritis vs rheumatoid

"Arthritis" is not one disease but an umbrella for many joint conditions. Distinguishing the two that matter most in the knee makes the direction of care easier to understand.

3.1 Osteoarthritis - the wear-and-tear type

Osteoarthritis centers on mechanical wear of cartilage. It usually starts in one knee or a specific joint, progresses slowly, worsens after activity, and features short-lived morning stiffness.

3.2 Rheumatoid arthritis - the autoimmune type

Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the joints. It tends to appear symmetrically on both sides (especially the small joints of the hands and feet), with morning stiffness lasting 30 minutes to an hour or more, and a course that can include whole-body symptoms such as fatigue, low-grade fever, and appetite loss with periods of flare and remission.

Symptoms alone cannot settle which is which, and they overlap with other common problems. Accurate distinction comes from medical evaluation and tests, so do not delay care if whole-body symptoms are present or you suspect it.

4. Self-care 1 - movement is medicine

When the knee hurts, the instinct is to avoid moving - but the core of osteoarthritis care is the opposite. Guidelines recommend exercise (supervised, unsupervised, or aquatic) over no exercise and place exercise and weight management at the foundation of care for every patient.

4.1 Low-impact aerobic activity

In an analysis of more than 200 trials, low-impact aerobic activity like walking, cycling, and swimming (water exercise) was most effective for easing knee osteoarthritis pain and improving function. It supports the heart, weight, and mood while sparing the knee, and you can split it into short bouts through the day rather than one long session.

4.2 Strengthening - especially the quadriceps

Leg strength, led by the quadriceps at the front of the thigh, stabilizes the knee and distributes shock. Quadriceps-strengthening exercise is one of the best-supported non-drug interventions for improving pain, function, and quality of life, and some studies suggest that strong thigh muscles may reduce the need for total knee replacement. Build up gradually through pain-free ranges with knee-friendly movements such as squats, lunges, and step-ups.

4.3 Stretching and mobility

Flexibility around the knee (hamstrings, calves, hips) eases the load on the joint. Gentle stretching, and low-impact practices like tai chi and yoga, can also help relieve knee osteoarthritis symptoms.

Key intuition: complete rest is rarely the answer for knee pain. Doing knee-friendly movement consistently - lowering the intensity on bad days but not stopping - is usually the better strategy.

Aim for roughly 150 minutes a week of somewhat hard aerobic activity, but split it up rather than overdoing it at once. Avoid movements that cause sharp pain or swelling, and decide the type and intensity with your doctor or physical therapist based on your own condition.

5. Self-care 2 - weight, the biggest lever

For people who are overweight or obese, sustained weight loss is recommended in guidelines to improve knee pain and function. Because the force on the knee while walking is several times your body weight, even modest loss meaningfully reduces the cumulative load.

The method matters. Rather than trying to lose weight through punishing exercise alone while the knee hurts, gradual loss through low-impact exercise plus a balanced diet is more sustainable and safer. Build the diet around overall balance rather than relying on a single "joint superfood," and discuss specific goals and methods with a clinician based on your own health.

6. Self-care 3 - heat, cold, and pain relief

Among the easy, drug-free ways to manage pain are heat and cold. In general, heat helps relax muscles and ease stiffness and osteoarthritis pain, and is often applied two to three times a day for about 15 minutes at a time. Cold is useful for muscle aches after exercise and for calming swelling and acute irritation.

Wrap the source in a towel to prevent burns or frostbite, and take care over areas with reduced sensation or poor circulation. Heat and cold are supportive relief only and do not replace managing the cause of the pain (exercise, weight, mechanics).

7. What to avoid - habits that make the knee work harder

  • Total inactivity: stopping all movement out of fear of pain leads to lost strength and worse stiffness, which tends to make things worse.
  • Sudden overload: abruptly starting hikes, running, or jumping at an intensity you are not used to invites overuse pain. Increase intensity gradually.
  • Pushing through pain: sharp pain or swelling during exercise is a signal to stop.
  • Stiff, unstable shoes: some studies found that flat, flexible shoes reduced the force on the knee compared with stiff or clog-like shoes. Choose shoes with appropriate cushioning and support.
  • Overusing self-prescribed drugs: taking painkillers or anti-inflammatories long-term on your own carries side-effect risks. Discuss medications with your doctor or pharmacist.

8. Drugs, injections, surgery - always with a doctor

When conservative care is not enough, medications, injections, and surgery are considered as adjuncts. The key is that all of these are individualized through clinical assessment and shared decision-making.

  • Medications: painkillers and anti-inflammatories may be used, but the doctor sets type and duration with your stomach, kidney, and heart risks in mind. Avoid long-term self-medication.
  • Injections: intra-articular injections may be considered in some patients, but the indications and evidence differ by type.
  • Surgery: in advanced osteoarthritis that still disrupts daily life despite conservative and drug treatment, options like joint replacement are considered. Timing and method are decided in full discussion with a specialist.
⚠️ To emphasize again. The descriptions of drugs, injections, and surgery above are general information and are not a recommendation of any specific drug, dose, or procedure. Self-overuse of painkillers and anti-inflammatories is risky, and injections and surgery have indications and contraindications. Treatment choice and monitoring must always be done with a medical professional.

9. The evidence on supplements - glucosamine and chondroitin

The supplements most often discussed for knee joints are glucosamine and chondroitin. But the evidence is mixed. Some studies report a small effect on pain (for example, improvement in some measures when taken alone), while large analyses found no consistent effect for the combined formulation.

Guidelines also diverge. The American College of Rheumatology (ACR, 2019) recommends against chondroitin (alone or combined with glucosamine) for knee osteoarthritis due to insufficient evidence, and OARSI is also negative on chondroitin. By contrast, some guidelines (such as AAOS 2021) note they may be helpful in mild-to-moderate disease while also stating the evidence is inconsistent.

In short, supplements do not replace standard care (exercise, weight, heat/cold) and do not guarantee benefit. If you are considering them - especially with other medications, allergies, or diabetes - talk to your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

10. Protecting your knees in daily life

10.1 Posture and movement

Repeated deep squatting or sharply bending the knee places a heavy load on it. When lifting something heavy, use your back and legs together, and avoid putting all your weight on one side.

10.2 Footwear

Shoes with appropriate cushioning and support help reduce shock to the knee. As a reference, some studies found flat, flexible shoes reduced the force on the knee compared with stiff shoes.

10.3 Stairs and assistive devices

The load on the knee is especially high going down stairs, so hold the handrail and descend slowly; stepping down one foot at a time helps when pain is bad. During periods of severe pain or instability, assistive devices such as a cane or knee brace can ease the load and reduce falls. Discuss the right use for you with a physical therapist.

Knee care also connects with managing other lifestyle markers like cholesterol, blood pressure, and blood sugar. Weight and exercise act on whole-body health together, so it helps to read related guides alongside this one (see the guide to lowering blood pressure, the guide to lowering cholesterol, and the prediabetes and blood sugar guide).

11. When to see a doctor

Consider a knee evaluation in the following situations. Do not delay for the emergency signs in particular.

  • The knee suddenly becomes very swollen, red, hot, and severely painful - possible infection or acute inflammation (emergency).
  • After an injury you cannot bear weight, or there is deformity, marked instability, or sudden loss of movement.
  • Joint swelling with a fever.
  • Pain that does not settle with rest or at night, or that keeps worsening.
  • Knee symptoms that last several days or recur several times a month.
  • Whole-body fatigue, weight loss, or low-grade fever, which warrant evaluation for inflammatory arthritis.

12. Conclusion - knees can be protected

The knee works harder than any other joint, carrying your body weight for a lifetime. Pain is common, but the good news is that most of it improves meaningfully with care you can manage yourself. On the foundation of exercise, weight management, and education, low-impact activity and quadriceps strengthening, a healthy weight, heat and cold, and everyday protective habits are the most dependable ways to protect your knees for the long run.

If this guide leaves you with one message, let it be this: do not stop because it hurts; keep doing knee-friendly movement consistently, and do not delay care when warning signs appear. Whether it is supplements, drugs, or surgery, the right answer lies not in internet generalities but in the state of your own knees and your clinician's judgment. Understood and managed well, your knees can keep carrying your steps for a long time.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. Should I rest and avoid exercise if I have knee arthritis?

Usually the opposite. Guidelines make exercise and weight management the cornerstone of osteoarthritis care and recommend low-impact exercise over no exercise. Walking, cycling, and swimming, plus quadriceps strengthening, have reasonably solid evidence for improving pain and function. Lower the intensity on flare days rather than stopping, and decide the type and intensity with your doctor or physical therapist.

Q2. How is osteoarthritis different from rheumatoid arthritis?

Osteoarthritis is a wear-and-tear condition that starts in a specific joint, worsens after activity, and has short morning stiffness. Rheumatoid arthritis is autoimmune, tends to appear symmetrically on both sides, causes morning stiffness lasting 30 minutes to an hour or more, and can come with whole-body symptoms like fatigue and low-grade fever. Distinguishing them requires medical evaluation, so see a doctor if you suspect it.

Q3. Does losing weight really ease knee pain?

For people who are overweight or obese, sustained weight loss is recommended in guidelines to improve pain and function. Because the force on the knee while walking is several times your body weight, even modest loss reduces the load. Gradual loss combining low-impact exercise with a balanced diet is recommended; discuss specific goals and methods with a clinician.

Q4. Do glucosamine and chondroitin supplements work?

The evidence is mixed. Some studies report a small effect, but major guidelines such as the ACR recommend against chondroitin (alone or combined) for knee osteoarthritis due to insufficient evidence. Some guidelines view them as possibly helpful. They do not replace standard care, so talk to your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

Q5. When should I see a doctor for knee pain?

See a doctor if the knee suddenly becomes very swollen, red, hot, and severely painful (possible infection or acute inflammation), if you cannot bear weight after an injury or have deformity or marked instability, if swelling comes with a fever, if pain does not settle with rest or at night, or if symptoms last several days or recur often. If whole-body fatigue, weight loss, or low-grade fever are present too, an evaluation for inflammatory arthritis may be needed.
