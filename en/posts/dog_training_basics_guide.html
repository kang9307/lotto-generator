Dog Training Basics - What to Teach and What to Change

Not about making a dog obey, but about letting a dog live safely in a predictable environment

Aug 1, 2026 Life & Practical

Introduction: training is not about making a dog obey

The sentence that opens most conversations about dogs is some version of my dog will not listen to me. Unpack it a little and what is actually happening is usually something else. Cases where the dog understood the cue and chose to refuse are rarer than people assume. Far more often the dog has never learned that cue in that situation, or has learned it but never in that place, or is physically uncomfortable, or has a competing need so large that there is no room for the cue to land at all.

So this article is not about getting a dog to obey. It sets the purpose of training differently. The purpose is to let a dog live safely in an environment it can predict, and cues are tools in service of that purpose. You teach a sit not to confirm obedience but so the dog does not shoot out of an opening front door. You teach a recall not to own a dog that comes when called but so you can reverse a situation when the dog has picked up something dangerous.

That framing changes the order of operations. When you see a problem behaviour, you do not start with training. You start by sorting the cause. Most problem behaviour comes not from insufficient training but from needs, health and environment. Teaching more cues to a dog in pain resolves nothing, and neither does teaching a dog to be quiet when it lives in a home where nothing happens all day.

How to read this article. What follows is a set of general principles and an order of approach. Dogs differ enormously between individuals depending on breed, age, temperament and history, and the same method that fits one dog will not fit another. In addition, rules can be amended, so the legal section here must always be read together with its reference date and checked against the current text. The legal statements in this article are current as of 1 August 2026 and describe South Korean rules only. If you live elsewhere, treat that section as an example of the kind of duties to look up locally rather than as rules that apply to you.

What this article does not do. It does not recommend any particular training school, trainer, product or food. It also treats aggression and severe anxiety as outside its scope. Those are veterinary subjects rather than training subjects, and section 1 deals with that boundary first.

1. What to check before training - signs that call for a vet first

The first question to separate is this. Did this behaviour appear because something was never learned, or because a physical or emotional state produced it? Mixing the two means that even an excellent training method produces nothing, and worse, the dog spends that time unwell.

The single most reliable clue in practice is the speed of the change. A habit that settled in over several months and a behaviour that was fine last week and different today are entirely different things. When suddenly and used to be fine are both true, a change in body or environment is more likely than a learning problem.

1.1 Aggression and severe anxiety are not training subjects

This is the sentence this article most wants to make unambiguous. Biting people or other animals, and anxiety severe enough to disrupt daily life, are problems to approach through veterinary care rather than through training. There are three reasons.

  • Medical causes genuinely hide underneath. Pain, reduced sensory function, endocrine problems and age related cognitive change surface as behaviour more often than people expect. Working on behaviour without ruling those out points the whole effort in the wrong direction
  • The wrong approach makes things worse. Punishing a bite tends to remove the warning first. A growl is a signal that gives a person time to back away, and suppressing that signal produces a dog that bites without warning. That is the most dangerous failure mode in all of dog training
  • The cost of an incident is not recoverable. Once a person is injured, the dog own future is on the line. This is not a domain where you learn by trial and error

So when these signs are present the order is simple. First, change the environment immediately so the situation cannot repeat. Second, see the attending veterinarian to identify or rule out medical causes. Third, ask for a referral to a clinic that handles behaviour cases or to a professional qualified in veterinary behaviour. This is not the stage for self treatment based on internet videos or advice from acquaintances.

2. What actually creates learning - consequence and timing

There are countless training methods, but the principle underneath them is simple. The consequence that lands immediately after a behaviour changes how often that behaviour happens next time. A good consequence increases it, no consequence lets it fade, an unpleasant consequence suppresses it. Training is the deliberate design of that relationship.

The thing people most often miss here is timing. A dog does not reason backwards and conclude that the current event relates to something it did a while ago. The consequence attaches to whatever the dog was doing at the moment the consequence appeared. A gap of a few seconds flips the direction of learning entirely.

  • If you hand over the treat after the dog has stood back up rather than at the moment of sitting, what gets reinforced is standing, not sitting
  • If you call a dog, then grab it and do something it dislikes, what gets reinforced is not responding to the call
  • If you find an accident later and scold, what the dog connects it to is not eliminating but the situation at that moment, which is usually the owner approaching
  • If you open the door while the dog is barking rather than after it stops, barking gets reinforced

2.1 A device for marking the moment

The practical solution to the timing problem is to mark the moment first. Choose one short, consistent sound and make it at the exact instant the wanted behaviour appears. The reward can then arrive slightly later and the learning still fixes on that instant, because the sound has stood in for the marker.

  • The marker sound has to be short and consistent. Drawn out praise blurs the moment
  • Keep the gap between marker and reward under a second at first, and stretch it only once the marker is well established
  • A reward must always follow the marker. Marking without paying out makes the signal meaningless
  • The dog decides what counts as a reward. For many dogs a ball, freedom to sniff, or simply being allowed to move forward is far stronger than food

2.2 Why punishment is a difficult tool

Consequences are not only pleasant ones. But using unpleasant consequences as a tool is technically much harder and carries far more risk. Timing is structurally difficult, intensity is hard to calibrate, and you cannot control what the dog associates it with. Scold a dog that has just approached and approaching decreases. Scold a dog in front of a stranger and strangers become a bad signal.

Published guidance from professional bodies is clear on the direction. The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior (AVSAB) states in its public position statement that only reward based training methods should be used for all dog training, and explicitly opposes aversive techniques including electronic collars, prong collars and choke chains (verified 2026-08-01).

This article does not explain how to use any of those tools. The passage above simply reports what the professional body position says. No procedure involving physical punishment or aversive stimulation appears anywhere in this article. If you have already tried such methods and the situation has grown worse, that in itself is a situation that calls for professional consultation.

3. Four basic cues - safety rather than control

The purpose of teaching basic cues is not to move the dog around at will. It is to build, in advance, a channel through which you can intervene at a dangerous moment. So it helps more to decide which situation each cue is for than to decide what to teach.

CueSituation it is actually forCore principleCommon failure
Name responseThe first step in redirecting attention when you spot a hazardOnly good things ever follow the nameThe name is also used when scolding, contaminating the cue
SitCreating a stationary state at doors, lifts and crossingsHand signal first, the word added laterRepeating the word before the dog sits, blurring the cue
WaitWhen a door opens, when getting out of a car, in front of foodExtend duration, distance and distraction separatelyRaising all three at once so failure repeats
RecallWhen the dog has picked up something dangerous or slipped a leadNever attach anything unpleasant after this cueCalling then bathing or ending the walk, which erodes reliability

3.1 Raise one axis of difficulty at a time

When practice has been going well and suddenly collapses, the usual reason is that several axes were made harder at once. For a dog, place is part of the cue. A sit that was perfect in the living room failing on a walking path is not forgetting. It is that the dog has never learned it in that place.

  • Treat a change of place as starting over. From the second location onward it comes back much faster
  • Keep sessions short. One to three minutes is enough, and several short sessions beat one thirty minute block
  • Success rate is the criterion. Eight or more out of ten means move up, five or fewer means step back down
  • End on a success. Finishing on a failure lowers the starting point of the next session
  • Subtract training treats from the daily food allowance. Without managing the total, weight problems follow
[Basic cue practice - 3 sessions a day, 2 minutes each]

Week 1  Name response
  Call the name in a quiet room
  Marker sound the instant eyes meet → reward
  Move up when 8 or more out of 10 succeed

Week 2  Sit / Wait (starting at 1 second)
  Fix the hand signal first, add the word after the behaviour appears
  For Wait, raise duration → distance → distraction, one at a time

[Record] Write only successes over attempts at the end of each session, e.g. 8/10

4. House training - failures are usually about frequency, place and cleaning

House training is the first thing new owners hit and the thing they most often handle wrongly. The core point is this. Most house training failures are not the dog breaking a rule. They are a shortage of opportunities to succeed, or an unclear signal about where the correct place is.

Failure patternActual causeApproach
Several times a day, anywhereFrequency - opportunities are rarer than bladder capacity allowsIncrease the number of opportunities first. After sleeping, after eating and after play are the highest probability moments
Accidents only near the designated spotPlace - the boundary of the correct area is ambiguousStart with a generously wide correct area, raise the success rate, then narrow gradually
Repeatedly the same spotCleaning - residual scent invites a returnUse a cleaning method that breaks down the odour compounds. Ordinary detergent only removes it for the human nose
Accidents only when nobody is aroundPast scolding produced avoidance of human presenceStop punishment completely and respond only to successes
Holds it on walks, goes only indoorsOutdoors is not yet comfortableReduce the socialisation load. See section 5
Fine for a long time, then collapsesMedical cause or environmental changeVet first. See the table in section 1

4.1 Why scolding backfires

This part is worth understanding as a mechanism. Split what actually happens when you scold an accident into two layers.

  • The timing is almost always late. People discover the accident after elimination has finished. As section 2 showed, the consequence attaches to the behaviour at that moment, so what the dog connects it to is not eliminating but the owner approaching
  • The content learned is skewed. What the dog learns is not do not go here but do not go where people can see. The result is hiding to eliminate, or holding it during a walk when the owner is present
  • The most important information disappears. Once the dog starts hiding, the owner can no longer tell when and where accidents occur. When the record breaks, the means of narrowing down the cause goes with it
  • Avoidance spreads to other areas. Learning to avoid an approaching person does not stay confined to house training. Recall reliability and general handling trust drop along with it

What to do instead is straightforward. Create opportunities at moments with a high probability of success, respond immediately at the spot where success happened, and clean up failures quietly. When failure produces no reaction and success does, the direction sets itself.

5. Socialisation - not more, but manageable

The trouble starts the moment socialisation gets translated as meeting as many things as possible. The goal of socialisation is not exposure count but the accumulation of experiences the dog found manageable. That difference turns the outcome around completely.

When a dog is repeatedly exposed to stimuli it cannot handle in a state where it cannot move away, the direction can be increased sensitivity rather than habituation. Taking a puppy to a crowded place where several people take turns picking it up and handling it is commonly described as good socialisation, but from the dog point of view it can be intense exposure with no escape available.

5.1 Adjust three axes separately

  • Distance. How far from the trigger. The easiest axis to control and the first one to adjust
  • Intensity. Volume for sounds, abruptness of movement for people, arousal level for other dogs
  • Duration. How long the dog stays in the situation. Finishing short and finishing well is better
  • Make only one harder at a time and leave the others easy. Raise all three together and you cannot even tell which axis was the problem

5.2 Signs the load is too high

The way a dog says this is not fine is usually quiet. When the signs below appear, increasing distance or leaving is the correct answer, and forcing the dog to stay is not socialisation.

  • Lowering the body or tucking the tail
  • Looking away and holding the head turned
  • Yawning or lip licking out of context
  • Shaking off when nothing has made the coat wet
  • Suddenly sniffing the ground with unusual persistence
  • Moving behind the owner or turning towards the exit
  • Unable to take treats the way it normally would

5.3 Assumptions that often turn out wrong

  • Not every dog has to like other dogs. Being able to walk past another dog without engaging is already a success
  • Greeting is an option, not a target. A head on greeting on a tight lead removes the dog escape option by design
  • For many dogs, a stranger walking past without interacting is more useful than a stranger reaching to pet them. Asking visitors to ignore the dog is legitimate socialisation
  • Start sounds at low intensity. If household noise is part of the problem, it can help to check how loud the actual environment is
  • It is not too late for adult dogs. The pace differs and the process includes unwinding existing experience, so it goes more slowly

On timing. It is widely discussed that there is a particularly important window for socialisation early in life, but the specific age range in weeks and how it should be matched with a vaccination schedule vary by individual and by local circumstances, so that decision belongs with the attending veterinarian. The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior maintains a separate public statement on this topic (Puppy Socialization Position Statement), and the list is linked in the references at the end of this article. The body text of that document could not be opened and verified for this article, so no specific week figures are given here.

6. Walking - pulling is not a bad habit

A dog that pulls on the lead is often described as badly behaved, but structurally pulling is closer to a mismatch of speed and purpose than a habit. A dog walking pace is faster than a human one, and what a dog wants from a walk is usually not travel but scent and information gathering.

  • There is a structure that reinforces pulling. Repeated experience of pulling and then moving forward teaches pulling as an effective behaviour
  • So changing the consequence is the core of the approach. When the rule that forward motion happens on a loose lead and stops on a tight one is applied consistently, the direction shifts
  • Redefining the purpose of the walk also has a large effect. Seen as exploration time rather than distance to cover, you naturally allow enough time to sniff, and that itself is a substantial reward for the dog
  • The start is the hardest part. Arousal is highest right after leaving home, so it is realistic to lower the goal for that stretch only
  • Once arousal is already high, cues do not get through. Increase distance to bring arousal down first, then use the cue

About equipment. This article does not recommend any particular lead, harness or product. Suitability varies greatly with the dog body shape, the condition of the neck and airway, age and the strength of the pulling, and some equipment can place physical strain on particular individuals. Equipment choice belongs in a conversation with a veterinarian or professional who has seen the dog in person. Note separately that safety measures such as fitting a lead outdoors are also a legal duty in South Korea (see section 10).

7. Separation anxiety - a state, not a habit

When a dog barks, destroys things or eliminates while alone, the most frequently heard response is just correct the habit. It is worth spelling out why that sentence is dangerous.

  • Barking, destruction and elimination are outcomes, not causes. Suppress the outcome and the state the dog is in remains, with only its expression reduced
  • Leaving the dog alone longer so it gets used to it can go the other way. Continued exposure at an unmanageable intensity is the same structure as the over exposure described in section 5
  • Punishment fits especially badly here. Adding an unpleasant consequence to an anxious state makes being left alone an even worse signal
  • Long forced confinement is not a solution either. A smaller space settles some dogs, while others injure themselves trying to escape. Individual variation is large, so this is not something to decide arbitrarily

7.1 Observations that help with the distinction

Not every problem that appears when a dog is alone is separation related anxiety. It could be boredom, something never taught, or a reaction to an outside trigger. The following observations help.

  • Does it happen only when the owner is absent, or does similar behaviour appear when someone is home
  • Does it already begin during departure preparations. Whether a reaction appears at the sound of keys or the act of putting on shoes
  • At which point in the alone period does it intensify. Right after departure, or several hours in
  • Is it linked to outside triggers. Whether it starts in time with corridor noise or a doorbell
  • Video recording is decisive. Behaviour when nobody is present cannot be known without a record, and that record becomes the single most valuable material in a professional consultation

Why this area belongs to professionals is equally clear. Exposure intensity has to be calibrated precisely to the individual, exposure at the wrong intensity worsens the state, and judging whether medical factors or medication are involved is a veterinary decision. Attempting it as self directed training and letting the timeline stretch means the dog condition and the family daily life deteriorate together in the meantime.

8. Getting the household aligned on rules

When people in the same home treat the dog differently, the rule becomes, from the dog point of view, something that works sometimes. And a behaviour that works sometimes is far more persistent than one that always works. That is why household agreement matters more than training technique.

The clearest example is the dinner table. If three out of four people never feed from it and one occasionally does, the table becomes a place that mostly does not work but is worth trying. That produces much stronger learning than nobody feeding from it at all.

  • Rules hold better when there are fewer of them. Keeping three beats setting ten
  • When changing something, everyone changes at once. A staggered rollout produces the same effect as intermittent reinforcement

9. Structuring a home shared with children or older adults

The purpose of this section is not to alarm anyone. The point is that most incidents occur in predictable situations, and predictable situations can be blocked structurally. Approaches that rely on human attentiveness do not last, so solving it with space and procedure is more stable.

  • Designate a place where the dog can be alone. It only means anything if the accompanying rule is that nobody approaches or touches the dog while it is there
  • Build in the ability to separate. One door or one gate is the most reliable tool when something happens quickly
  • Redefine supervision. Being in the same room is not supervision. Being positioned so that you can get between them when needed is
  • Set separate access rules around food, sleep and favourite objects. Those are the three most common sources of conflict
  • With older adults, look at fall risk first. The more common real hazard is not biting but being pulled or jumped on and losing balance. Adjusting who handles walks and how the lead is managed is the practical measure
  • Teach children what not to do rather than how to do it. Do not disturb a sleeping dog, do not go near a dog that is eating, do not hug, do not put your face close, do not approach from behind
  • Separate immediately when the load signs from section 5 appear. Leaving a dog in a state of tolerating it is the most dangerous choice available

11. Checklists

11.1 Before you start

  • A recent health check has been done, and the possibility of pain or reduced sensory function has been discussed with a veterinarian
  • You have distinguished whether the behaviour in question appeared gradually or suddenly
  • You have confirmed there are no bite attempts and no anxiety at a level that disrupts daily life. If there are, this goes to veterinary care rather than training
  • You have identified what counts as a reward by the dog standard (food, ball, scent, moving forward)
  • You have subtracted the training treat share from the daily food allowance
  • Everyone in the household has agreed the cue words and rules and written them up
  • You have checked registration, identification tag and other legal duties against the current rules in your jurisdiction

11.3 Signs to see a professional

  • There has been an attempt to bite a person or an animal - immediately, no exceptions
  • Growling or warning behaviour has newly appeared or increased
  • Something previously reliable has suddenly stopped working (house training, stairs, walks, name response)
  • Problems when alone have not improved for two weeks or more
  • Reactions to a particular trigger are getting worse over time
  • The dog avoids being touched or guards a particular area
  • Appetite, water intake and sleep patterns have changed together
  • Someone in the household feels uneasy being with the dog
  • You have tried several approaches and none of them is finding a direction

Conclusion: change the order and most of it changes

Reduced to one sentence, the point of this article is this. Training is the last step, not the first. When you see a problem behaviour, keep the order instead of hunting for a method. Check health first, then look at needs and environment. Only once those two are settled does learning accumulate meaningfully.

And the principle behind learning is simpler than expected. The consequence that lands immediately after a behaviour changes the next one, and a gap of a few seconds in when that consequence lands changes the direction. Good training is therefore usually undramatic. Short, frequent, starting somewhere easy, making only one thing harder at a time.

One last emphasis. Aggression and severe anxiety are problems to approach through veterinary care rather than training. Recognising that boundary precisely is the most practical part of this article, and the shortest route to keeping both the dog and the people safe. Because individual variation is large and rules change, treat what is written here as a starting point and confirm it with your attending veterinarian and the current statutory text.

Other parts of preparing for life with a dog follow the same logic. Estimating veterinary costs in advance is covered in pet insurance, seasonal risk management such as heat in summer pet care, and the procedures around the goodbye that eventually comes in pet funerals. Preparing for it in a single person household is closer to pets in a one person home.

## 2. Basic cue practice sequence

Principles: short (1 to 3 minutes) · frequent (3 times a day) · move up at 8/10 or better · step down at 5/10 or worse · always end on a success.
Raise only one axis of difficulty at a time.

### Week 1 — Name response

Purpose: the first step in redirecting attention when you spot a hazard

1. Say the name once in a quiet room
2. Make the marker sound at the instant eyes meet
3. Reward within one second of the marker
4. Repeat 10 times, record: ___/10

Caution: never use the name when scolding. Only good things ever follow the name.

### Week 2 — Sit / Wait

1. Fix the hand signal first. Add the word only after the behaviour appears
2. Mark at the moment of sitting, not after the dog stands back up
3. Start Wait at one second. Raise duration, then distance, then distraction, one at a time
4. Record: Sit ___/10 · Wait up to ___ seconds

### Week 3 — Recall

1. On lead, starting from two or three steps away
2. Mark while the dog is coming, not after it arrives
3. If you must take hold, grab the lead, reward, then move
4. Record: ___/10

Caution: do not attach a bath, the end of a walk or medication after a recall. The moment you do, reliability drops.

### Week 4 — Changing places

Repeat the same cues from week 1 at the door, then the yard, then the walking path.
Assume difficulty resets whenever the place changes.

## 6. Signs to see a professional

If any one of these applies, stop training and go to consultation.

- [ ] There has been an attempt to bite a person or an animal — immediately, no exceptions
- [ ] Growling or warning behaviour has newly appeared or increased
- [ ] Something previously reliable has suddenly stopped working (house training, stairs, walks, name response)
- [ ] Problems when alone have not improved for two weeks or more
- [ ] Reactions to a particular trigger are getting worse over time
- [ ] The dog avoids being touched or guards a particular area
- [ ] Appetite, water intake and sleep patterns have changed together
- [ ] Someone in the household feels uneasy being with the dog
- [ ] Several approaches have been tried and none is finding a direction

## 7. What not to do

- Hunting for a training method before sorting the cause
- Punishment that arrives late — it attaches to whatever else was happening
- Scolding house training accidents — it makes the dog hide and it breaks your record
- Attaching anything unpleasant after a recall
- Calling strong exposure with no escape available socialisation
- Treating separation related problems as a habit
- One family member breaking the agreed rule
- Leaving a dog in a state of tolerating it

The use of physical punishment or aversive equipment is not covered in this document.
