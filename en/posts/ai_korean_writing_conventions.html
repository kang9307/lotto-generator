Where LLM-Written Korean Goes Wrong

Translationese, hedging and style drift measured across 10.58 million characters, and the layer a linter can never reach

Aug 11, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: the spell checker passes and you can still tell

You open the draft and something snags. No typos. Particles are correct. Three paragraphs in, you know a person did not write this. Ask what is wrong and the answer does not come easily.

That feeling is almost always syntax. The vocabulary is Korean while the order in which a clause is assembled and the way the predicate is built come from English documents. A spell checker passes these sentences because none of them break the orthography rules. So the complaint arrives without a fix list attached.

Building the fix list requires naming the places where it goes wrong. What follows is the distribution obtained by running nineteen checker rules over 1,260 Korean documents, roughly 10.58 million characters. Every document was written by a large language model and then edited by a person, and code blocks were excluded from the count.

Scope — the list of places that break, the shape of a written convention that covers them, and how to enforce that convention at two points: generation time and after output. Adopting a morphological analyzer or training a style transfer model is out of scope.

1. Translationese is syntax, not vocabulary

Treating translationese as a word list does not work. Ban one by-phrase marker and the same structure migrates to a different postposition. What actually causes the problem is an English sentence skeleton wearing Korean vocabulary. The skeleton shows up in five places.

1.1 Inanimate subjects take the slot a person should hold

English puts objects in subject position and predicates actively with almost no restriction. Korean has a much narrower opening there. Rendering This tool allows you to deploy automatically word by word produces a sentence whose subject is the tool and whose predicate means makes possible. The natural Korean arrangement puts the person or the action back in subject position, and the result is one clause shorter.

1.2 The agent moves into an adverbial and the predicate turns passive

This is where the English by-phrase lands. Korean defaults to active voice when the agent is known, and reserves the passive for cases where hiding the agent is deliberate. 사용자에 의해 호출된다 becomes 사용자가 호출한다, and the sentence loses a postposition without losing anything else.

1.3 Verbs get flattened into nouns and joined with particles

Nominalization is the most widespread of the five. review of performance becomes 성능에 대한 검토, and a sentence that would have ended with a verb meaning review instead ends with a verb meaning carry out a review. The sentence gets longer while the information stays the same.

1.4 The have family hardens into one verb

The Korean verb that translators reach for when they see have means something closer to hold in the hand. Describing a property calls for the existential verb or a plain adjectival predicate. 캐시를 가지고 있다 becomes 캐시가 있다, and a phrase meaning has high accuracy becomes a two-word predicate meaning accuracy is high.

1.5 A suffix gets attached to manufacture adverbs

basically, generally and effectively convert automatically into a Sino-Korean adverbial form and then attach to the front of every sentence. Most of them can be deleted without changing the meaning. Keep only the ones that resist deletion.

3. The textbook warnings and the actual failures are different

Every translationese correction guide leads with double passives and the by-agent construction. In this sample the double passive appears six times in 10.58 million characters. The by-agent family appears 141 times, or 0.1 per 10,000 characters. The most cited spelling confusion in Korean appears zero times, and numeral-plus-unit spacing never drifts at all.

Three categories take the top instead.

  • Hedging — the modal construction meaning it can be done runs 14.1 per 10,000 characters, which is roughly 140 occurrences in a 100,000-character manuscript.
  • Typographic convention — quote shape, colons and dashes carried over from English typesetting run 31.0, 13.0 and 8.3 per 10,000 characters.
  • Nominalization — the regarding-type link runs 3.1 per 10,000 characters, the only translationese item in the upper half.

Put simply, rule violations are nearly absent and the choices the rules do not govern keep going wrong. That is also good news: the layer a spell checker covers is already clean. What remains sits above the checker, and the tool for that layer is a written style convention.

4. Hedging - fourteen modal constructions per 10,000 characters

When every sentence carries a possibility, an estimate or an obligation marker, the text stops asserting anything. A sentence meaning you can change the setting was usually going to end as change the setting or as an imperative. A sentence meaning it may be an error attaches itself to a slot where the condition is missing.

The point is not to eliminate hedges. When the judgement really is conditional, the hedge is honest. Three lines are enough for a convention.

  • Assert confirmed facts. If there is evidence, cite the evidence and drop the hedge.
  • When the outcome depends on conditions, write the condition instead of the hedge. Not it may slow down, but it slows down above 200 concurrent connections.
  • Keep the modal only where possibility is genuinely the point, and cap it at one per paragraph.

The third line is the one that ports cleanly into a checker, because it does not judge whether an individual sentence is right. It measures density. The prescriptive must-form, at 3.4 per 10,000 characters, takes the same treatment.

5. Style drift - one document in five mixes speech levels

Korean encodes formality in the sentence ending. English adjusts register through word choice, but Korean re-declares it in the last two or three syllables of every single sentence. Mixing endings is therefore immediately visible.

sentence endings  deferential 47,096 · plain written 105,496 · polite 4,095
documents where the minority ending exceeds 10%: 237 / 1260 = 18.8%
top 5 documents, minority share: 49%, 46%, 42%, 40%, 38%

In the top five, two registers are essentially split down the middle. A text whose first paragraph ends deferentially and whose next paragraph ends in the plain written form forces the reader to recompute the distance to the narrator. This is the single largest reason correct content still reads like a draft.

RegisterTypical endingSample frequencyWhere it belongs
Deferential합니다 · 입니다 · 하십시오47,096User-facing guides, notices, official correspondence
Plain written한다 · 이다 · 아니다105,496Reports, analyses, technical explanation
Polite conversational해요 · 이에요4,095Conversational UI, marketing copy
Noun form확인 · 미측정 · 필요-Table cells, list items, summary lines

Four lines cover it in a convention document.

  • Assign one ending per document type. Reports take the plain written form, user guides take the deferential form, and that is nailed down.
  • End table cells and list items in a noun form. This is the cheapest way to keep them from colliding with body endings.
  • Endings inside quoted material follow the source and are excluded from the check.
  • Headings carry no sentence ending at all.

For public sector documents this is compliance rather than preference. The National Institute of Korean Language publishes a public language style guide (2022 revision) covering drafts, press releases and reports, with appendices listing 100 administrative terms to replace and 50 Japanese-derived terms to avoid.

6. Particles and spacing - the layer surface strings cannot decide

This is where checker authors get burned hardest. Korean orthography article 42 requires dependent nouns to be spaced and article 43 requires unit nouns to be spaced. The rules are unambiguous. The problem is that the same syllable is both a dependent noun and a fragment of a connective ending.

Build the rule from the preceding final consonant plus the following syllable and this happens.

before refinement 339 hits → after refinement 38 hits (0.04 per 10k)
· 소득이 높을수록 유리                    ← comparative connective, correct
· 다른 조건이 같다면 추출수 온도가 높을수록  ← comparative connective, correct
· 이 숫자를 어떻게 읽을지가 중요하다        ← interrogative connective, correct
· 승인을 누구에게 물을지가 정해지고         ← interrogative connective, correct
· 어떤 공정에 예산을 넣을지가 저절로 정해진다 ← interrogative connective, correct

All 339 hits were the comparative connective rather than a dependent noun. Excluding that connective cut the count to 38, and every one of those was the interrogative connective. Not a single real error surfaced. The true positive rate across 10.58 million characters is zero.

This rule does not work without morphological analysis. And if an analyzer is not going in, the correct move is to delete the rule. One rule that emits nothing but false positives costs more than the detections it would have made, because people stop trusting the entire report.

6.1 Two places the rule author slips

Hangul stores initial, medial and final components as one precomposed character. A rule written from the verb stem therefore misses inflected forms. Searching for the double passive stem 보여지 lets 보여진다 pass, because the final consonant fuses into the syllable and produces a different character entirely.

The quieter failure is the adnominal ending. Writing /[을ㄹ]\s*수/ to catch the modal construction matches nothing at all, because the final consonant in those syllables is not a separate character. Reading it requires decomposing the code point.

// syllable = 0xAC00 + (initial*21 + medial)*28 + final. Final index for the liquid is 8.
function jong(ch) {
  const c = ch.codePointAt(0);
  if (c < 0xAC00 || c > 0xD7A3) return -1;
  return (c - 0xAC00) % 28;
}

// actual output
final-consonant trap - /[을ㄹ]\s*수/ matched 0, code-point check matched 2
syllable trap      - /아니/ matched 0, /아[니닙]/ matched 1

The same trap, extended to safe patterns per ending and to normalization and shell boundaries, is covered in The Korean Regex Trap. If you are writing your first Korean checker, start there.

7. Lists, tables, headings - where English document habits carry over

These numbers are the most immediately actionable. They are matters of convention rather than rule, and a convention only has to be internally consistent, which is exactly what breaks.

The third row is the one that matters. Whether list items take a period is the document's choice. But four documents in ten mix both inside the same file. That defect does not come from not knowing the rule. It comes from the earlier choice not being retained, and a reader sees it as sloppiness instantly.

7.1 Quotation marks are already settled by regulation

The punctuation revision promulgated in December 2014 and effective from January 2015 moved the corner brackets from vertical-writing-only into horizontal use. Book and newspaper titles take double corner brackets or double angle brackets, with double quotation marks permitted as a substitute, while subtitles, artwork titles, business names and statute names take single angle brackets. One line in the convention settles it. Whichever you pick, the whole document uses it.

8. Putting the convention in the prompt, and where that stops working

There are two enforcement points: generation time and after output. The prompt owns the first.

8.1 What the prompt version should look like

  • Write substitution pairs, not a ban list. Do not use the by-agent form works far worse than A by B becomes B does A. Without a replacement form the structure migrates to a different marker.
  • Pin the ending in a checkable sentence. Every sentence ends in the plain written form; table cells and list items end in a noun form.
  • Attach three to five examples. A wrong sentence paired with its corrected form outperforms a paragraph explaining the rule.
  • Give numbers. At most one hedge per paragraph is countable; use sparingly is not.
  • Keep the rule count under twenty. The more rules pile up, the more the later ones dilute.

8.2 What the prompt cannot do

  • Document-level consistency. The 40.9 percent mixed list punctuation is the evidence. The rule was known; the earlier choice was not retained.
  • The back half of a long output. The ending held at the start drifts as the text grows.
  • Conflicts between rules. Be concise and cite your evidence collide constantly, and the prompt does not say which one wins.
  • Verification. A prompt never reports whether it was followed. Measuring the output is the only way to know.

Where the strategy of adding more rules hits its ceiling is covered separately in Long policy documents do not control agents.

9. Enforcing after output - block, warn, report

The most common failure when building a checker is giving every rule the same severity. Attach one noisy rule and people soon ignore the whole report. Splitting severity three ways prevents that.

SeverityRulesRationale
Block
fails until fixed		Double passive · mixed sentence endings · mixed list punctuation · period in headingEither no legitimate usage exists, or it is a consistency violation that needs no interpretation
Warn
above a density limit		Through-phrase · nominalized link · have-verb · hedges · must-form · Sino-Korean adverbsIndividual sentences may be correct. Measure per 10,000 characters, not per sentence
Report
number only, always passes		Quote shape · colons · dashes · English glossesDepends on taste and document type. Watch the trend
Drop
removed from the rule set		Dependent noun spacing · inanimate subjectNot decidable from surface strings. Without an analyzer they emit only false positives

Warning thresholds need a baseline. Using this sample's own numbers makes the current state the standard, which is meaningless. Take ten documents you consider well written, measure them with the same rules, and use those values as the ceiling.

The general case of splitting checks into syntactic, runtime and semantic layers, and managing false positives across them, is in Building a verification workflow for AI output. One thing to add here: a Korean checker has to measure its false positive rate per rule. As shown above, one rule can sit at zero precision while the rest of the set is sound.

10. The shape of the convention document - a substitution table, not a ban list

One table carries most of it. Five columns.

  • Target — the form that trips. Write it in the same shape the checker will search for.
  • Replacement — what to write instead. Leave it empty and the rule will not be followed.
  • Exception — cases that pass despite matching. A rule with an empty exception column will produce false positives.
  • Severity — block, warn or report.
  • Threshold — for warn-level rules, hits per 10,000 characters.

Then add the handful of items that apply to the document as a whole.

  • The sentence-ending assignment and the noun-form rule for tables and lists.
  • Terminology — treat the Korean form as canonical and gloss the English only on first appearance. Those glosses run 8.0 per 10,000 characters in the sample, and repeating the gloss for the same term is padding from the second occurrence onward.
  • Number formatting — digit grouping, whether to use myriad units, decimal places.
  • A status marker on every figure — measured, estimated or unmeasured. Adding this to the convention visibly reduces unsourced numbers.
  • A sentence length ceiling. One mechanical rule such as split anything over 60 characters is enough.

For public sector documents, attach the regulatory citation. The institute also publishes a plain-language guide for official documents alongside the orthography text, and runs a public language support portal.

11. What this method misses and what was not verified

  • Particle and spacing rules do not hold without morphological analysis. As shown, precision drops to zero. Reaching that layer genuinely requires an analyzer, which is outside this scope.
  • The density thresholds have no published basis. No authority states how many hits per 10,000 characters is normal. The figures above describe one sample; they are not recommendations.
  • The sample is skewed. All 1,260 documents are Korean technical and practical-information articles. Fiction, legal text and contracts would likely distribute differently, and that was not checked.
  • No controlled comparison exists between prompt conventions and post-output checking. Contrasting the two under identical conditions requires a controlled generation experiment, which was not run.
  • The cause is unverified. Explanations invoking training corpora or alignment for why double passives are rare while hedges are dense are common, but no source was confirmed, so only the distribution is stated here.
  • The institute's materials were confirmed only at the landing page level. The full PDFs were not compared clause by clause, so no individual example sentence from them is quoted.
  • The D-series rules contain false positives. English glosses at 8.0 per 10,000 characters are mostly legitimate in technical writing, and colons in headings at 6.8 percent are widespread in Korean. That is why those items sit at report severity rather than block.

Conclusion: a style convention is an interface, not a preference

What needs fixing in machine-written Korean is not spelling. That layer is already clean. What remains is the English sentence skeleton and document-level consistency, and both belong to convention rather than regulation.

In order:

  • Pin the ending first. One sentence ending per document type, noun forms in tables and lists. The style drift that shows up in one document out of five disappears with that single line.
  • Put the consistency items into the checker next. Mixed list punctuation needs no interpretation to judge and appears in four documents out of ten. It has the highest return per unit of effort.
  • Handle translationese with substitution pairs in the prompt and measure it by density. A ban list will not catch it.
  • Leave spacing rules out until an analyzer is in place. Adding them costs the credibility of the whole checker first.

The reason to write the convention down is not to make sentences pretty. It is so that a person and a machine can judge the same document by the same standard. If the standard is not written as sentences, the complaint that it reads like AI stays an impression, and the next draft comes back identical.
