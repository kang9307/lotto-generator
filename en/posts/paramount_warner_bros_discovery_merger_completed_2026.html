The Trigger - A Completion Announcement and the Name Skydance

On October 6, 2026, Paramount Skydance (hereafter Paramount) announced that it had completed its acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery (hereafter WBD). Ars Technica reporter Jon Brodkin covered it the same day as a 111 billion dollar merger, and the article was introduced on GeekNews on October 7, 2026. The combined company is called Skydance. Ars Technica writes that the name was taken from a firm that Paramount bought in a separate deal last year.

Three different kinds of fact are packed into that single headline. The first is the amount: from the headline alone it is impossible to tell whether 111 billion dollars is an enterprise value, an equity value, or a figure that includes debt. The second is the path: who tried to stop the merger, on what grounds, and through what procedure those attempts ended. The third is the structure: which assets now sit inside one company, who holds the voting shares, and how large the debt is.

The sections below keep the three apart, state how each number was defined in the document that gave it, and cite each fact to its source. Where documents differ on the same point, both statements are given, and the statements of the company side and of opponents are attributed with equal weight.

What Does 111 Billion Dollars Measure - Different Numbers in Different Documents

Ars Technica writes 111 billion dollars in its headline and body. The article does not define whether that is an enterprise value, the sum of the purchase price, or a figure that includes debt. Other documents describe the amount of the same transaction, and each gives a different number.

  • Ars Technica (2026-10-06): a 111 billion dollar merger. The article contains no definition of how the figure is composed.
  • WBD press release filed with the SEC (2026-02-27, announcing the merger agreement): the sentence reads, “Transaction values WBD at enterprise value of $110 billion, representing a multiple of 7.5x on fully synergized 2026 EBITDA.” In plain terms, the deal values WBD at an enterprise value of 110 billion dollars, which equals 7.5 times 2026 EBITDA with all synergies counted.
  • The per-share price in the same press release: “Paramount will pay $31.00 per share in cash for all outstanding shares of WBD.” This is the cash price at signing for every outstanding WBD share.
  • Deal summary in the same press release: “Paramount will acquire 100% of WBD for $31 per share in cash, plus the "ticking fee", valuing WBD at $81 billion in equity value and $110 billion in enterprise value.” In plain terms, a cash price of 31 dollars per share plus the ticking fee, an equity value of 81 billion dollars and an enterprise value of 110 billion dollars.
  • Closing press release (2026-10-06): “WBD shareholders received an amount in cash equal to $31.01666668 per share.”

The enterprise value of 110 billion dollars (at signing, in the SEC filing) and the 111 billion dollars (at closing, in the article) are numbers from different documents written on different dates. The gap is one billion dollars, and none of the cited documents states how it was derived. The two values are therefore not treated as one definition, and each is written here the way its document wrote it.

Part of the change in the per-share price can be explained by calculation. The closing price minus the signing price is 31.01666668 - 31.00 = 0.01666668 dollars. The signing-date press release said that if the transaction had not closed by September 30, WBD shareholders would receive a ticking fee of 0.25 dollars per share for each quarter, measured daily, until closing, and the closing date was October 6. Treating a quarter as 90 days, 0.25 x 6 / 90 = 0.016666..., which equals the difference. A deal-summary sentence in the same press release gives 31 dollars per share in cash “plus the ticking fee”, so it describes the fee as an addition to the price. Still, the match is a result of arithmetic only; none of the documents contains a sentence that links the two numbers.

What the 12-State Settlement Requires - As Reported

The settlement terms are listed only to the extent that they were quoted in the Ars Technica coverage, in the opposing groups brief and in explanations from the California Attorney General office. The full text of the settlement and of the judge approval order is not the basis of the items below. The terms the coverage reports are as follows.

  • Film investment and release: The judge wrote that the settlement addresses the lawsuit complaints about film distribution by “requiring certain minimum thresholds of investment and release of domestic films.”
  • A pledge of 30 releases a year: Ars Technica reports that Paramount pledged to release 30 movies a year in theaters. The closing press release likewise states output commitments of a minimum of 30 theatrical films and 180 or more television shows and series.
  • Independent films: The opposing groups brief describes a condition requiring four independent films each year. The same brief argued that the settlement definition of an independent film includes any movie based on an original screenplay, even one wholly financed, developed and owned by the studio.
  • Consequences of falling short: The article says that if Paramount fails to release enough films it would be required to divest Miramax Studios and to pay 30 million dollars per missed film toward the health and retirement trust funds of various unions and to the National Association of Attorneys General (NAAG).
  • Basic cable channels: After the merger the company must negotiate licensing of the basic cable channels owned by Paramount and by WBD separately. The Attorney General office described the condition as lasting five years, and the article says it does not apply to premium cable channels, streaming services or broadcast content.
  • Editorial independence board: An Editorial Independence Board is set up for CBS News and CNN. The company chooses its members and it reports to the company board of directors, which according to Ars Technica is chaired by CEO David Ellison.

Sentences quoted from the approval order describe the nature of the settlement. The judge wrote that it “represents a reasonable factual and legal resolution of the dispute.” The judge also wrote that a typical settlement “does not fully remediate an alleged violation or even necessarily resolve the ultimate factual and legal issues of a case.”

The Writers Guild of America filed its own lawsuit and then settled. According to the guild description reported in the article, the terms are as follows.

  • Limit on layoffs: Layoffs of writers in the CBS News broadcast division are prohibited for five years.
  • Payment: 17.5 million dollars is paid to the guild health fund. The guild stated, “We continue to believe the merger will cause damage to writers and the industry at large.”

Same Matter, Different Statements - Company, Opponents and Court

Statements about this merger fall into three groups. Each is summarized below as quoted in the coverage, and no judgment is made on which side is right.

The company and the settling parties

  • Paramount (right after the July 24 stipulation): said, “the result is exactly what we have sought from the outset: a direct path to a trial based on the evidence,” and argued that the transaction is “good for competition, good for consumers, and good for creators.”
  • Closing press release: described the unanimous approval of competition authorities covering nearly 70 jurisdictions as “reflecting recognition of the deal's pro-competitive nature and the benefits it brings to consumers and creatives alike.”
  • California Attorney General Rob Bonta (September): said the settlement “is not a vote of support for this merger,” but that it “resolves our antitrust concerns in every market alleged in our case” and is “the best course of action.”
  • California Attorney General office (September, on the basic cable condition): said the condition would help preserve the existing competitive dynamic between the companies and keep prices down for consumers.
  • The states that sued (before the settlement, July 24 article): argued that the merger would eliminate competition by combining two of the five major Hollywood movie studios and two of the five major owners of basic cable TV channels.

Groups and officials who opposed the settlement

  • John Bergmayer, legal director of Public Knowledge: said the settlement “does not address the central problem with this merger: the loss of competition.” In the same statement he argued that the merger leaves fewer studios competing for scripts and talent, reduces streaming choices, and that consumers will face higher prices. On the editorial boards he said they “may be better than nothing” but “are far short of actual independence.”
  • Joint brief of five groups: the Committee for the First Amendment, Free Press, Freedom of the Press Foundation, Future Film Coalition and International Documentary Association argued that the settlement gives residents of the states that sued “virtually nothing.” The brief also argued that the 30-film commitment is lower than the 2027 forecasts the companies gave investors (19 films for Warner Bros., 15 or more for Paramount).
  • League of United Latin American Citizens: argued in its filing that the settlement requires no divestiture as a condition of closing and relies on conduct commitments.
  • Lina Khan, former chair of the Federal Trade Commission: wrote, “Behavioral remedies routinely fail.”
  • Free Press (FCC filing): argued that about 80 billion dollars of debt “will require deep cuts to Paramount's pre-merger holdings and existing operations.”

The court and the hearing

  • The judge approval order (September 30): wrote that objections based on “hopes and desires for the proposed consent decree to reach farther—to achieve more—do not rise to the level of legal violations upon which the Court can reject the parties' negotiated resolution.”
  • The September 24 hearing (derived): the article, citing a Variety report, says the judge told the lawyers, “the court isn't a rubber stamp of your agreement… I have some questions.” The reported aim was to ensure that the outcome was not the result of collusion but of an arm's-length process.

The Last Attempt to Stop It - Five Consumers at the Supreme Court

What Ars Technica calls a last-ditch effort to block the deal is not the lawsuit of the 12 states. The states lawsuit was resolved by a settlement in September, and the article opening sentence refers to a single application by consumers who had filed a separate lawsuit.

  • Applicants: five consumers who buy streaming subscriptions or other video products. They sued separately in the same federal district court (Northern District of California), and the article says that suit failed to stop the merger.
  • Path: the consumers asked the US Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit and were rejected. They then filed an emergency application to block the deal with Justice Elena Kagan, who handles emergency requests for that circuit.
  • Filing and result: the Supreme Court docket (26A455) lists the filing date as October 5, 2026, the first entry as Application (26A455) for an injunction, submitted to Justice Kagan, and the result as Application (26A455) denied by Justice Kagan. The article says Justice Kagan denied it without comment.
  • How to read it: because no reasons were given, the grounds for the denial cannot be read from the documents. The established fact is that the application was denied.

Closing was announced the day after the application was denied. The phrase last-ditch in the article describes a final attempt just before closing; it is not a ruling that settles the legality of the merger. A reader comparing the two lawsuits should keep them apart: the states suit ended by settlement with a judge approval, while the consumers suit reached the Supreme Court only as an emergency application that was denied without comment.

Funding and Control - Non-Voting Equity, Foreign Ownership and Debt

On funding and control, the article and the press release sometimes describe things in different words. Both descriptions are given together, and the documents do not explain how the two fit with each other.

  • New equity: the closing press release states that 47 billion dollars of new equity investment went into Class B Common Stock, priced at 12.00 dollars per share. It names the Ellison Family, RedBird, the Public Investment Fund (PIF), L'IMAD, the Qatar Investment Authority (QIA) and LionTree as leading the investment. The SEC-filed press release at signing said 47 billion dollars of new Class B shares would be issued at 16.02 dollars per share, and the cited sentences do not explain why the per-share price differs between the two documents.
  • The sovereign funds share: citing the Los Angeles Times, Ars Technica says the funds plan to invest 24 billion dollars in the deal, with PIF contributing 10 billion dollars and QIA and Abu Dhabi L'imad Holding adding 7 billion dollars each. 10 + 7 + 7 = 24, a calculation that matches the wording of the article.
  • Voting rights: the October 6 article says the sovereign wealth funds hold non-voting shares and the Ellison family and RedBird Capital Partners own all of the voting shares. The September article says the Ellison family and RedBird own all of the Class A voting shares while foreign investors hold Class B non-voting shares. The closing press release, however, also names the Ellison Family and RedBird among those who led the Class B investment. The documents do not explain how the two descriptions fit together, and the cited sentences alone do not show whether Class B holders are limited to foreign funds.
  • FCC approval: a company that holds broadcast licenses needs FCC approval for direct or indirect foreign ownership above 25 percent. Paramount said its indirect foreign ownership would reach 49.5 percent and petitioned for a waiver, and the article says the Media Bureau approved it by declaratory ruling without a vote of the commissioners. The ruling permits up to 100 percent indirect foreign equity in the aggregate.
  • Conditions the FCC attached: Paramount must monitor foreign ownership, and foreign investors may not influence content decisions or management or access non-public US person data. Any change to their voting, governance or information rights needs further FCC approval.
  • Statements for and against: Paramount told the FCC that the foreign investors receive only non-voting equity and so cannot influence editorial decisions. The FCC Media Bureau order rejected the concern that the investment buys “practical influence” even without voting control, writing “We find this argument unconvincing,” and said it was “persuaded by Paramount's argument that the Foreign Investors therefore will not be able to wield any influence, let alone control, over decisions involving the Licensees.” FCC Commissioner Anna Gomez said, “An investment this large in one of America's biggest media companies doesn't just buy equity, it secures influence over what gets said and what gets made,” and she asked for a vote of the full commission.
  • Debt: the article quotes Reuters as reporting that Skydance is “expected to carry about $80 billion in debt, putting pressure on Ellison to grow streaming, preserve cash flow from cable networks and improve theatrical film performance.” The company sets a target of 3.0x net leverage by the end of 2029.

What Is Settled and What Is Not Yet Decided

The closing settles some points and leaves others open. The division below uses only what the cited documents say directly.

What the documents settle

  • Closing and price: closing on October 6, 2026, with cash of 31.01666668 dollars per share (company announcement).
  • Name and listing: the combined company is Skydance, and its Class B shares trade on the NYSE as SKYD.
  • State lawsuit: it was resolved by a settlement that the judge approved on September 30.
  • Emergency application: the five consumers application was denied on October 5.
  • Settlement terms: film investment and release thresholds, separate basic cable negotiations and an editorial independence board (as reported).
  • Broadcast license requirements: the FCC approved the foreign ownership waiver and attached conditions.

What is not decided or not set by the documents

  • Streaming integration and prices: the closing press release says two global streaming services. The September Ars Technica articles write that the deal will let Paramount merge Paramount+ with HBO Max, but how and when, and any pricing plan, are not in the cited documents. The SEC-filed press release at signing lists “consolidating streaming technology stacks” among the sources of synergies.
  • Workforce: the five-year layoff prohibition covers only writers in the CBS News broadcast division under the guild settlement. The quoted sentences contain no commitment for other divisions.
  • Debt and targets: the 3.0x net leverage ratio (end of 2029) and free cash flow above 10 billion dollars (2030) are company targets, and later results will decide whether they are met.
  • Settlement compliance: whether the 30-film pledge and the five-year cable condition are kept is tied to the remedies for falling short (the Miramax divestiture and 30 million dollars per film). The article gives little detail on how compliance is monitored.
  • Editorial independence board: that the company selects the members and that the board reports to the company board is known. The opposing groups argued that this structure falls short of independence, and Bloomberg described the board as a condition won by the states that held out longest. How it will operate is a later matter.
  • Changes in foreign ownership: Paramount said it expects 49.5 percent but told the FCC the figure could change with routine fluctuations or future investments, and the ruling permits up to 100 percent indirect foreign equity in the aggregate.

For a subscriber, the first thing that stands out is that these documents contain no pricing plan from the company. What Could Not Be Confirmed

  • The full text of the closing press release. The sentences containing amounts, approvals and listing were used as the basis, and whether a sentence describing the total transaction value exists could not be checked against the full text.
  • The basis on which the one billion dollar gap between 111 billion and 110 billion dollars was calculated.
  • The reason the per-share price of the new Class B shares is 16.02 dollars in the signing-date document and 12.00 dollars in the closing document.
  • The full text of the settlement and of the judge approval order, and whether an appeal followed the approval. The settlement terms are given within the range of Ars Technica reporting and quotations from the opposing brief.
  • The original Reuters and Wall Street Journal articles. The roughly 80 billion dollar debt rests only on the sentences Ars Technica quoted.
  • The arguments in the consumers emergency application and the date on which the Ninth Circuit rejected their request.
  • The date and reasons of the Justice Department approval, and the list and conditions of the competition authorities covering nearly 70 jurisdictions.
  • Subscription pricing, how and when the services would be integrated, and the scale of restructuring after the merger. None of the cited documents states them.
  • The listing status of the former Nasdaq ticker PSKY.
