The sources used here are the Citizen Lab item of 6 October 2026, the LEGISinfo page of the Parliament of Canada and the bill text as passed by the House on 18 June 2026, the Department of Justice Canada explanatory material on Part 1, and the Citizen Lab submission of 2 June 2026. Every assessment of the bill is attributed to the party that made it, and the merits of the bill are not argued.

What was reported - Psiphon’s plan to leave and its condition

On 6 October 2026 Citizen Lab posted an “In the Media” item introducing a report by The Globe and Mail. The gist is that Psiphon plans to leave Canada if the federal government’s lawful-access bill passes in its current form. Citizen Lab describes Psiphon as a software company spun out of the Citizen Lab that helps internet users living under oppressive regimes evade online censorship. GeekNews introduced the item on 7 October 2026.

The item conveys five things.

  • The condition. The lead says the bill passes “in its current form”, and the later text says “if Bill C-22 is passed”. The item does not say whether the two phrases describe the same condition.
  • The action. The item reports that the company says it is prepared to move its operations out of Canada.
  • The reason. It reports that the company would be “forced to compromise crucial safety features put in place to protect the privacy of its users”.
  • The scale. The item gives Psiphon’s monthly users as 20 million worldwide and says they rely on the software to bypass online censorship.
  • The description of the bill. The item summarizes the bill as requiring electronic service providers, if it passes, to make secret changes to their systems to give police services and the Canadian Security Intelligence Service (CSIS) surveillance and monitoring capabilities.

The last point is the starting place. The sentence about secretly changing systems is a media summary, not a provision of the bill, so what the provisions actually require has to be checked against the bill text separately. The item goes no further than this, and it names no destination country and no date for a move.

Where the source stands - the party that relayed the report is a party that commented on the bill

The item says Psiphon was originally created as a Citizen Lab project in 2006 and became a private company soon afterward. It also carries a statement from Ron Deibert, founder and director of Citizen Lab. The original reads: Bill C-22 “will make it impossible for a large number of invaluable privacy-preserving tools and applications from operating in Canada.”

Citizen Lab’s position on the bill is already on the same site. Its submission of 2 June 2026, “(Un)forced Errors”, is summarized as analysing Bill C-22 and recommending that several elements of the bill be withdrawn entirely. The body of the submission puts its central recommendation as “our core recommendation is to entirely withdraw Part 2 of the bill”. On 14 September 2026 the site also lists a Civic Tech Toronto meetup titled “Bill C-22 and Lawful Access”.

That position sets the way to read the item. The sentences in it that assess the bill come from an institution that has publicly recommended withdrawing Part 2 of the bill and from a company that spun out of that institution. This does not mean the assessments are wrong. It means the speaker has to be attached to each one. What follows keeps three layers apart.

  • The bill layer. What the bill text and LEGISinfo say. Section numbers and figures are quoted from this layer only.
  • The claims layer. How Citizen Lab and Psiphon read the bill, and the concerns that follow from that reading.
  • The government layer. The government’s explanation as given in the purpose clause, the bill summary and the Department of Justice Canada material.

The three parts of the bill - Parts 1 and 2 do different work

The bill summary divides the bill into three parts.

  • Part 1. It amends the Criminal Code, the CSIS Act and other Acts to modernize provisions on the timely gathering of data and information during an investigation. The summary lists, among other things, a confirmation of service demand directed at telecommunications service providers, a production order for subscriber information, evidence gathering in exigent circumstances, and authority to request transmission data or subscriber information from a foreign entity that provides telecommunications services.
  • Part 2. It enacts the Supporting Authorized Access to Information Act. The summary says that Act establishes a framework for ensuring that electronic service providers can facilitate the exercise, by authorized persons, of authorities to access information conferred under the Criminal Code or the CSIS Act.
  • Part 3. It provides for parliamentary review of Parts 1 and 2. During the third year after the first day on which all provisions are in force, the standing committee of each House that normally considers national security matters is to undertake a comprehensive review (section 48 of the bill).

The “duty to change systems” in the media summary lines up with the regulation and order provisions of Part 2. That is an interpretation, and the item itself does not name Part 2. Under section 40 of the bill, Part 1 other than section 39 of the bill comes into force on the 180th day after the day the Act receives royal assent, and under section 47 of the bill Part 2 comes into force on a day to be fixed by order of the Governor in Council. In what follows, section numbers for Part 2 are those inside the Supporting Authorized Access to Information Act that Part 2 enacts, and sections of the bill itself are written as “section N of the bill”.

Who is covered - the definitions come in three layers

Who bears the duties of Part 2 is decided in the definitions. Under subsection 2(1), an electronic service provider is a person that, individually or as part of a group, provides an electronic service, and that provides the service to persons in Canada or carries on all or part of its business activities in Canada.

  • Electronic service provider (ESP). Meeting either prong above is enough. The definition does not use the location of servers or of a head office as its test.
  • Core provider. An ESP belonging to a class of ESPs set out in the schedule. The Governor in Council may add, amend or delete classes by regulation (subsection 5(1)).
  • An ESP subject to an order. A ministerial order under subsection 7(1) can be made with respect to any ESP, whether or not it is a core provider.
  • The basis for classes. Section 48 says regulations may establish classes of ESPs and distinguish among them, including by the electronic service provided and by the number of persons in Canada to whom the provider provides services.

In the House-passed text, the schedule carries only the heading “Core Providers” and lists no class. Whether a service like Psiphon would be a core provider therefore cannot be settled from the documents, and an order under section 7 is possible regardless of classes.

The relation between the plan to move and the definition comes down to one sentence. If, after a move, the service is still provided to persons in Canada or part of the business is still carried on in Canada, one prong of the definition can remain. Which of these applies depends on the facts of the service and cannot be stated from the provisions alone.

What can be required - the regulations (section 5) and orders (section 7)

Subsection 5(2) lists four matters on which the Governor in Council may make regulations about the obligations of core providers.

  • (a) The development, implementation, assessment, testing and maintenance of operational and technical capabilities. The provision says this includes capabilities related to extracting and organizing information that is authorized to be accessed and to providing access to that information to authorized persons.
  • (b) The installation, use, operation, management, assessment, testing and maintenance of any device, equipment or other thing that may enable an authorized person to access information.
  • (c) Notices to be given to the Minister or other persons, including about those capabilities and devices.
  • (d) The retention of categories of metadata, including transmission data, for reasonable periods not exceeding six months.

An “authorized person” is a person having authority under the Criminal Code or the CSIS Act to access information. The definition of “access” includes, for information related to a communication, intercepting the communication within the meaning of section 183 of the Criminal Code. The media phrase “police and CSIS” can be read as a summary of that category. The Part 2 text itself, however, uses the word “access” and does not use the words surveillance or monitoring.

A ministerial order under section 7 can contain the same kind of provision as a regulation. What differs is the counterparty and the procedure.

  • Counterparty. Any ESP, whether or not it is a core provider.
  • Prior step. The ESP is given an opportunity to make representations before the order is made (section 8).
  • Condition of validity. The order is not valid unless the Intelligence Commissioner approves it in a written decision (subsection 7(2)).
  • Duration. The order must specify a period of validity not exceeding two years (subsection 7(1)).
  • Factors. The benefits to the administration of justice, feasibility of compliance, cost, the impact on the persons the ESP serves, the impact on privacy protection and cybersecurity, and any other factor (subsection 7(3)). Compensation for cost is at the Minister’s discretion (subsection 7(4)).

The text also sets limits. Subsection 5(4) does not authorize regulations requiring retention of information that would reveal the content of transmitted information, a person’s web browsing history or a person’s social media activities. Subsection 5(4.1) allows a retention regulation only if the Governor in Council is satisfied that the category and all its elements are essential for effective and timely investigations or for the effective and timely performance of CSIS functions. Subsection 5(6) says the regulations do not confer an authority to access personal information about the persons to whom the core provider provides services.

Where “secret” comes from - the section 14 disclosure ban and what lies outside it

The provision that corresponds to “secret” in the media summary is section 14. It says an ESP and any person acting on its behalf must not disclose the following information except as permitted under the Act or the Canada Evidence Act.

  • Information contained in an order made under subsection 6(1) (exemption) or 7(1) (order), and information on which the Minister relied in making the order.
  • The fact that the ESP is or was subject to the order.
  • The content of representations made under section 8 and of any response given by the Minister, and the fact that the Minister invited the representations.
  • Information in an exemption application and in the decision on it, and any prescribed information.

There are release valves too. At an ESP’s request, the Minister may issue a written authorization permitting disclosure on reasonable terms (subsection 15(1)), and where the information is relevant to a regulatory authority’s powers or duties, the Minister must authorize disclosure to that authority (subsection 15(2)). Subsections 6(1.1) and 7(1.1) say an order may contain provisions on disclosure of the information in it and of the fact that the ESP is subject to it. Section 14 sits in the list of provisions whose contravention is a violation (paragraph 29(1)(a)) and an offence (paragraph 41(1)(a)).

Some things are not in the section 14 list. The regulations under subsection 5(2) themselves are not in its enumeration. The layer that “secret” can point to is therefore the existence and content of individual orders and exemptions. Whether the summary also means the regulations is not stated in the item, and section 14 does not show by what route regulations are made public.

Three features offset the confidentiality.

  • Annual report (section 49). The Minister must prepare a report within 90 days after the end of each calendar year. It includes the number of orders under subsection 7(1) and the capability obligations they imposed, the number of orders the Intelligence Commissioner did not approve, and information on compliance orders and enforcement actions. It also covers the number of requests for assistance under subsection 13(1) and the classes of persons assisted. The Minister must make the report public within 60 days after it is prepared, with any redactions the Minister considers necessary (subsection 49(3)); if there are redactions, the unredacted report goes to the National Security and Intelligence Committee of Parliamentarians and the National Security and Intelligence Review Agency (subsection 49(4)).
  • Notice to a review body (subsection 9(3)). Within 30 days after receiving the Intelligence Commissioner’s decision, the Minister provides a copy of the order and the information given to the Commissioner to the National Security and Intelligence Review Agency.
  • Notice of compliance orders (subsection 27(3)). Within 30 days after the time allowed in the order for requesting a review ends, the Minister notifies the same agency of the fact that a compliance order was made, the identity of the ESP and the provisions said to be contravened.

Encryption and systemic vulnerability - where the “safety features” statement meets the text

The item does not say what the “safety features” are that Psiphon would have to compromise. Where the text meets that statement is the encryption-related definition and a few carve-outs. The bill defines “electronic protection” as authentication, encryption and any other prescribed type of data protection.

  • Subsection 2(4). No obligation under the Act is to be construed as compelling an ESP to decrypt, or to ensure that an authorized person is able to decrypt, information encrypted by a person to whom the ESP provides services. The exception is where the ESP provided the encryption and possesses the information necessary to decrypt.
  • Subsection 5(5). A core provider need not comply with a provision of a regulation if compliance would have the effect of requiring it to introduce a systemic vulnerability or of preventing it from rectifying one.
  • Subsection 7(5). The same carve-out applies to a ministerial order.
  • Subsection 24(2.1). The same carve-out applies to a compliance order.
  • The definition of systemic vulnerability (passed text). A vulnerability in the electronic protections of an electronic service that creates a “credible risk, based on recognized international technical standards” that secure information could be accessed by a person without any right or authority. A risk relating only to information about persons to whom a warrant, order or other access authority applies is excluded.

Which functions of Psiphon these carve-outs would reach cannot be settled from the wording. The text states the condition “would have the effect of”, but the sentences of those provisions do not say who decides. Read structurally (an interpretation), a dispute can run through a compliance order under section 24, the Minister’s review under section 26, the decision under section 27 (if none is made within 90 days the order is deemed confirmed) and judicial review in the Federal Court under section 17 (written notice 15 days beforehand).

Setting the first-reading text against the House-passed text shows the following differences.

  • The metadata retention cap. The ceiling changed from one year to six months. The passed text adds the requirement that the category be essential for investigations and CSIS functions (subsection 5(4.1)). The exclusion of content, browsing history and social media activity (subsection 5(4)) is already in the first-reading text.
  • The definition of systemic vulnerability. “Substantial risk that secure information could be accessed by a person who does not have any right or authority to do so” became a definition with “credible risk, based on recognized international technical standards” and an exclusion clause.
  • Subsection 2(4). The sentence on compelled decryption does not appear in the Part 2 text of the first reading.
  • The duration of orders. The wording that an order must specify the period during which it has effect became wording with a ceiling of two years.
  • Section numbers. The two versions are numbered one apart in places. The disclosure ban is section 15 in the first-reading text and section 14 in the passed text, and the duty to assist is section 14 in the first-reading text and section 13 in the passed text. Numbers should not be carried across when material on the two versions is read together.

Citizen Lab’s submission of 2 June gives metadata retention as “up to one year”. That matches the “one year” in paragraph 5(2)(d) of the first-reading text and differs from the six-month cap in the passed text. The submission does not say which version it addresses, and because its retention figure matches the first-reading text it appears to have been written about that text (an interpretation). A footnote says the original version was sent to the members of SECU on 28 May 2026. The difference between the submission’s figure and the passed text’s figure can come from this difference in versions.

Procedure and enforcement - approval, review and the penalty figures

Part 2 provides not only for making orders but also for testing, inspection, audit and sanctions. Section 28 states the purpose of an administrative monetary penalty as “promote compliance with this Act and not to punish”. The amounts in the table are copied as written in the provisions, which do not name a currency.

Four points stand out in the table.

  • Who approves. The body that approves a Part 2 order is the Intelligence Commissioner, not a court (subsection 7(2)). The Part 1 summary mentions authorization by a justice or judge in several places.
  • The limit on testing. Subsection 13(4) says assessment or testing must not have the effect of granting access to personal information.
  • Penalty or offence, not both. When conduct can be proceeded with either as a violation or as an offence, proceeding in one manner precludes the other (section 34).
  • The limitation period. A notice of violation cannot be issued more than two years after the day the Minister becomes aware of the conduct (subsection 33(1)).

The purpose and safeguards the government has stated in documents

Section 3 of the bill states the purpose of Part 2 in one sentence: “The purpose of this Act is to ensure that electronic service providers can facilitate the exercise of authorities to access information that are conferred on authorized persons.”

The Department of Justice Canada explanatory material on Part 1 opens by saying that criminal activity is increasingly being enabled by digital, globalized and sophisticated crime networks. It describes the bill as giving law enforcement new tools to investigate serious threats, disrupt organized crime and protect communities. It sets out the safeguards of the two new tools as follows.

  • Who the confirmation of service demand can be used against. Only telecommunications or internet service providers, and not hotels, car rentals or medical facilities.
  • The scope of the question. Only a yes or no confirmation of whether a provider serves the person in question, with no details about individuals, transmission data or evidence.
  • The requirement. Reasonable suspicion that an offence has been or will be committed, and that the information will assist the investigation.
  • The exclusions. It cannot be used where the confirmation would disclose medical information or information subject to solicitor-client privilege.
  • Response time and review. At least 24 hours must be given, the provider has five business days to apply to a court for review, and it need not hand over information until the court has made a final decision.
  • The subscriber information production order. It needs a judge’s authorization and reasonable suspicion, is limited to information identifying the subscriber such as name, alias, address, telephone number and email address, and cannot reach private communications or content.

The material says these two tools would not enable law enforcement to conduct warrantless searches of personal information. It covers Part 1 only. Within Part 2, the safeguards the government side points to are in the text: no compelled decryption (subsection 2(4)), the limits on retention (subsection 5(4)), no conferred authority to access personal information (subsection 5(6)), Intelligence Commissioner approval (subsection 7(2)), the systemic vulnerability carve-outs and the annual report (section 49).

Values to follow - what is not yet settled

The practical weight of the bill depends largely on values that are set outside the provisions. The following are blank in the documents or will be decided later.

  • Core provider classes. The schedule has none, and they are filled in by regulation (subsection 5(1)).
  • The content of the subsection 5(2) regulations. The specific demands on capabilities, devices, notices and retention are set by regulation.
  • The number and type of section 7 orders. The ESP is barred from disclosing an individual order by section 14, and under subsection 7(6) the Statutory Instruments Act does not apply to it. The route to the public that appears in the text is the aggregate in the annual report (section 49), which is itself published with any redactions the Minister considers necessary.
  • The Senate stage. Whether the second reading entry moves, and whether amendments are made, can be followed through the status shown on LEGISinfo.
  • In-force dates. Part 1 comes into force on the 180th day after royal assent (section 40 of the bill). Part 2 comes into force on a day to be fixed by order of the Governor in Council (section 47 of the bill), and no date appears in the documents.
  • How and when Psiphon would move. This is known only once the company gives its own account.

What could not be confirmed

  • The original Globe and Mail report. It reaches this text only through the Citizen Lab item, and the wording of the report itself was not viewed.
  • A direct statement from Psiphon. No item about Bill C-22 appeared on the first page of the psiphon.ca blog listing.
  • The destination country and date of a move, and the specific functions meant by “safety features”. The introductory item does not give them.
  • The body of the Public Safety backgrounder (Backgrounder – Securing Access to Information in Bill C-22). Its title is listed on LEGISinfo, but the connection was reset twice, so the government’s description of Part 2 rests on the bill text and the Department of Justice material on Part 1.
  • Citizen Lab’s assessment of the passed text. The retention figure in the 2 June submission matches the first-reading text, and no document assessing the passed text was viewed.
  • Developments after the Senate stage, and the specific content of the core provider classes and regulations.
