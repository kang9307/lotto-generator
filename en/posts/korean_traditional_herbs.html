Traditional Korean herbs are ingredients of Korean medicine (hanbang) that have long been used in health care. This article covers ten of them, ginseng, astragalus, danggui, goji, schisandra, cornus, cnidium, atractylodes, coptis and licorice, split into "what is traditionally said about their use" and "the evidence and cautions confirmed in primary sources."

This article does not describe how to treat or prevent any disease. Benefits we could not confirm are left out, and the safety information we did confirm (drug interactions, pregnancy, breastfeeding) is given with its source. The reference date is 2026-09-29.

The History and Significance of Traditional Korean Herbs

Traditional Korean herbal knowledge is said to have begun to take shape in the Three Kingdoms period and to have been compiled in the Joseon-era Donguibogam. The climate and geography of the Korean Peninsula supported a wide range of medicinal plants, and Korean medicine built its herb culture on them.

Korean medicine views the body as a whole and approaches health in terms of balance. But "used traditionally" and "shown to work in modern research" are different statements, and this article keeps the two apart.

10 Traditional Korean Herbs: Traditional Use and Verified Evidence

For each herb, "traditional use and forms" is separated from "verified evidence and cautions." Items whose evidence we could not confirm are marked as such.

1. Insam (Ginseng)

Insam (ginseng) is the herb most closely associated with Korea; ginsenosides are its best-known constituents.

Traditional Use and Forms

  • Fresh ginseng has been taken in decoctions, porridge and tea; red ginseng as concentrates, powders and preserved confections.
  • It is traditionally known as a tonic used when energy is low.

Verified Evidence and Cautions

  • The Korean government portal (korea.kr) lists the recognized functions of red ginseng health functional foods as immune support, fatigue relief, memory improvement, improved blood flow through inhibition of platelet aggregation, and antioxidant activity, with a daily ginsenoside intake that is set by function. These are approved functional claims, not treatment or prevention of disease. Follow the product label for actual amounts.
  • The U.S. NCCIH says Asian ginseng may improve cognitive function in middle-aged adults (not in young adults) and that a 2023 review found modest benefit for general fatigue, though results vary. It calls the evidence on blood sugar "inconclusive and conflicting."
  • Insomnia is the most common side effect. Ginseng may interact with blood thinners and diabetes medicines, and some studies suggest it may be unsafe when taken orally during pregnancy.

2. Hwanggi (Astragalus, Milk Vetch Root)

Hwanggi (astragalus) is a root that Korean medicine has classed as an herb that replenishes qi (vital energy).

Traditional Use and Forms

  • Used in teas, decoctions and soups.
  • Often included in multi-herb formulas.

Verified Evidence and Cautions

  • NCCIH states there is not sufficient reliable scientific evidence to know whether astragalus is useful for any health condition.
  • A 2023 review reported enhanced immune responses and lower inflammatory proteins, but the studies were small and varied in method.
  • People with autoimmune diseases should avoid it, and it may interact with immunosuppressive drugs. Animal research suggests it can be toxic to the fetus during pregnancy, and safety during breastfeeding is unknown.

3. Danggui (Angelica, "Female Ginseng")

Danggui is a root herb that has also been called "female ginseng."

Traditional Use and Forms

  • Used in decoctions, teas and herbal liquor (danggui-ju).
  • Traditionally said to be included in postpartum-recovery and blood-nourishing formulas.

Verified Evidence and Cautions

  • In the primary sources we opened this time, we could not confirm evidence for benefits in anemia, menstrual pain, menopausal symptoms or liver function. The earlier statements have been removed.
  • A 2021 Frontiers in Pharmacology review reports that danggui (Angelica sinensis) contains coumarin-type compounds similar to warfarin and may raise INR; the evidence is animal experiments and chemical analysis. We could not confirm whether the species studied is the same as the danggui sold in Korea.
  • If you take blood thinners or are pregnant or breastfeeding, consult a doctor, Korean medicine practitioner or pharmacist first.

4. Gugija (Goji Berry, Wolfberry)

Gugija (goji) is a red berry added to teas, porridge and medicinal rice.

Traditional Use and Forms

  • Dried and steeped as tea, or added to porridge and medicinal rice.
  • Traditionally known as an herb that supports the eyes and liver.

Verified Evidence and Cautions

  • In the primary sources we opened this time, we could not confirm evidence for benefits to eye health, liver function or blood sugar. The earlier statements have been removed.
  • The 2021 Frontiers in Pharmacology review lists goji (Lycium barbarum) as weakly inhibiting warfarin metabolism and possibly raising INR, with case reports of unexplained INR increases.
  • If you take blood thinners, consult a doctor or pharmacist before eating it.

5. Omija (Schisandra Berry)

Omija is a berry named for its five flavors: sweet, sour, bitter, salty and pungent.

Traditional Use and Forms

  • Made into tea, fruit punch, syrup (omija-cheong) and liquor.
  • Dried, it can be stored and used year-round.

Verified Evidence and Cautions

  • In the primary sources we opened this time, we could not confirm evidence for liver protection, respiratory health, stress or cognition. The earlier statements have been removed.
  • The sources we checked contain no safety information specific to omija on drug interactions or pregnancy. That means "not confirmed," not "no problem."
  • If you take medication or are pregnant or breastfeeding, consult a professional first.

6. Sansuyu (Cornus Fruit)

Sansuyu is a red fruit that is dried and used as an herb and in tea.

Traditional Use and Forms

  • Made into tea, syrup (sansuyu-cheong) and liquor, or dried and added to decoctions.
  • Traditionally known as an herb that nourishes the kidneys and liver.

Verified Evidence and Cautions

  • In the primary sources we opened this time, we could not confirm evidence for effects on the kidneys, liver, blood pressure or blood sugar. The earlier statements have been removed.
  • The sources we checked contain no safety information specific to sansuyu on drug interactions or pregnancy. That means "not confirmed," not "no problem."
  • If you take blood pressure or diabetes medicine, do not substitute an herb; talk to the prescriber.

7. Cheongung (Cnidium, Szechuan Lovage Root)

Cheongung is a rhizome that has traditionally been used in formulas related to blood circulation.

Traditional Use and Forms

  • Used in decoctions, pills and teas.
  • Traditionally said to be included in formulas for headaches and menstrual complaints, and usually combined with other herbs.

Verified Evidence and Cautions

  • In the primary sources we opened this time, we could not confirm evidence for benefits in headache, inflammation, menstrual pain or sleep. The earlier statements have been removed.
  • The 2021 Frontiers in Pharmacology review reports that cheongung inhibits CYP2C9 and CYP1A2 and may raise warfarin INR; the evidence is rat studies.
  • If you take blood thinners or are pregnant or breastfeeding, consult a professional first.

8. Baekchul (White Atractylodes Rhizome)

Baekchul is a rhizome traditionally used in formulas related to digestion and fluid balance.

Traditional Use and Forms

  • Used in decoctions, teas and pills.
  • Traditionally known as an herb that supports the spleen and stomach.

Verified Evidence and Cautions

  • In the primary sources we opened this time, we could not confirm evidence for benefits to digestion, immunity, diuresis or fatigue. The earlier statements have been removed.
  • The sources we checked contain no safety information specific to baekchul on drug interactions or pregnancy. That means "not confirmed," not "no problem."
  • If indigestion or diarrhea persists, see a doctor rather than relying on an herb.

9. Hwangnyeon (Coptis Rhizome, Golden Thread)

Hwangnyeon is an intensely bitter rhizome traditionally classed as an herb that clears heat.

Traditional Use and Forms

  • Used in decoctions, pills and topical preparations; its strong bitterness means it is often combined with other herbs.
  • Traditionally said to be included in formulas that clear heat.

Verified Evidence and Cautions

  • We could not confirm evidence for antimicrobial, fever-reducing, anti-diarrheal or liver-protective effects. The earlier claim that it is "effective for infectious conditions" has been removed.
  • Berberine, known as a constituent of coptis, is also found in goldenseal. NCCIH says the berberine in goldenseal can be harmful to newborns, that pregnant or breastfeeding women should not use goldenseal, and that it should not be given to infants. The same caution may apply to coptis, but we could not confirm guidance on coptis itself.
  • NCCIH describes a study in which goldenseal extract lowered metformin levels by about 25%. If you take diabetes medicine, consult a professional.

10. Gamcho (Licorice Root)

Gamcho (licorice) is an herb that harmonizes other herbs in a formula, also known by nicknames such as "the mother of medicines."

Traditional Use and Forms

  • Widely included in teas, decoctions and pills, and more often used with other herbs than alone.
  • Traditionally known for harmonizing the taste and action of the other herbs in a formula.

Verified Evidence and Cautions

  • NCCIH says the glycyrrhizin in licorice has been linked to serious adverse effects such as irregular heartbeat and cardiac arrest with large or long-term use, and can be a problem even in small amounts for people with hypertension or heart or kidney conditions.
  • Interactions with corticosteroids have been reported. Large amounts of licorice extract during pregnancy (about 250 g per week) are unsafe and can increase the risk of delivery before 38 weeks.
  • We could not confirm effects for gastric ulcer prevention or treatment, cough or inflammation. NCCIH says a mouth rinse may reduce pain and ulcer size in canker sores, but it is unclear whether licorice alone affects digestive symptoms.

Modern Applications of Traditional Korean Herbs

Health functional foods are not the same as herbal medicines

A health functional food may make claims only within the range of functions recognized by Korea's MFDS. Red ginseng, for example, is listed for immune support, fatigue relief, memory improvement, improved blood flow through inhibition of platelet aggregation, and antioxidant activity. Check the function, daily intake and cautions on the label. We could not confirm whether the other herbs in this article have such recognition.

Use in cosmetics

Some herb extracts are used as cosmetic ingredients, but we could not confirm their effects on skin. The earlier statement that they "support skin health and beauty" has been removed.

Use as foods

Herbs are also used as teas, syrups and liquors. That a product is sold as food does not mean the herb's effects have been verified, and the same cautions apply if you take medication.

Precautions When Using Traditional Korean Herbs

Consider your own condition

If you have a chronic illness, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or are giving an herb to an infant or older adult, consult a Korean medicine practitioner, doctor or pharmacist first.

Drug interactions

For warfarin, the 2021 Frontiers in Pharmacology review reports that danggui, goji and cnidium may raise INR, licorice results are mixed, and ginseng may lower INR according to mechanism studies. NCCIH describes interactions of ginseng with blood thinners and diabetes medicines, licorice with corticosteroids, and astragalus with immunosuppressants. Direction and level of evidence differ by herb, so the most reliable step is to show your full medication list to the professional you consult.

Quality and safety

Korea's MFDS regulation on herbal medicine safety and quality management covers heavy metals, pesticide residues, sulfur dioxide and mycotoxins. When buying, choose a seller that provides test results or manufacturing and distribution information, and if anything goes wrong while taking an herb, stop and see a doctor.

Conclusion

Traditional Korean herbs are a cultural heritage built on long experience, but what this article confirmed in primary sources is closer to "what to be careful about" than "what works." Read claims by separating traditional use from confirmed evidence.

Herbs are still medicine. If you take medication or are pregnant or breastfeeding, consult a professional first, and if you have symptoms, see a doctor instead of substituting an herb.
