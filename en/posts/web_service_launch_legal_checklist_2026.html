Five Questions to Answer Before You Launch - Legal Groundwork for Solo and Small Teams

Start from what the service does, not from a list of statutes - Korean law, checked July 2026

2026-07-30 Life & Law

Introduction: five questions instead of a reading list

Scope first, because it decides whether this article is useful to you. Everything below is based on the law of the Republic of Korea. The rules where you live or where your business is established will be different, sometimes fundamentally so, and nothing here transfers automatically to another jurisdiction. Read this as a worked example of how a small operator can turn statutes into a checklist, and confirm the equivalent rules for your own country with a local adviser.

The sentence people search most often before shipping a weekend project is 「does a solo developer need a privacy policy」. What comes back is usually a list of statute names: the Personal Information Protection Act, the Network Act, the E-Commerce Act, the Act on the Regulation of Terms and Conditions, the Copyright Act. The list is accurate and almost useless, because it never says which of them touches your service and which does not.

This article reverses the order. It starts from what the service does. In practice the obligations of a small service split along five questions. Is there a login? Is there a payment? Do users upload posts or files? Do you send advertising by email or push? Are there overseas users or overseas infrastructure? Answer those five and the set of provisions you have to check is almost fixed. Answer no to all five and the remaining work is smaller than you fear.

The second thing this article separates is timing. What must exist as written text before launch is not the same as what can be tidied up once you have users. Without that split you either never ship because the preparation never ends, or you produce documents nobody needed while missing the one that mattered.

The third axis is possible because this blog also covers engineering: the point where law turns into design. Retention periods, destruction procedures, the list of processors you rely on, automatic collection devices, cross-border transfer. The moment you write these into a document they become a specification the system has to satisfy. When the document and the system disagree, it is not the document that is wrong; it is the risk that has grown.

Finally, the stance of this article. It states only what was checked by opening the provision, and it says so plainly where nothing could be checked. Amounts and thresholds appear only where a basis was found; everywhere else the text says only that a breach is subject to sanction. Every service and situation described below is a hypothetical example and does not refer to any real operator.

2. A privacy policy: the contents are listed in the statute

A privacy policy is not a document you copy from someone else. It is a document whose contents a statute enumerates. Article 30(1) of the Personal Information Protection Act requires a controller to establish a privacy policy and lists the items it must contain. The table below sets out that list. Rows marked only if applicable are the ones the statute makes conditional.

ItemWhat must be statedCondition
1The purposes of processing personal dataAlways
2The processing and retention periodAlways
3Provision of personal data to third partiesOnly if applicable
4The destruction procedure and destruction methodAlways
(the article carves out cases where destruction after the retention period is not required, in which case the basis must be stated)
5The possibility that sensitive data becomes public and how to choose not to disclose itOnly if applicable
6Outsourcing of personal data processingOnly if applicable
7The processing of pseudonymised dataOnly if applicable
8The rights and duties of the data subject and legal representative, and how to exercise themAlways
9The name of the privacy officer, or the department name, together with contact detailsAlways
10Installation and operation of devices that collect personal data automatically, such as internet access log files, and how to refuse themOnly if applicable
11Other matters prescribed by Presidential DecreeCheck the Enforcement Decree

The cell that surprises people is item 10, because an analytics script added to see visitor counts, or a cookie used to keep someone logged in, can land there. The provision covers not only installation and operation but how to refuse. In other words the sentence does not end at 「we use cookies」; it has to reach 「and here is how to turn them off」.

Publication is in the statute too. Article 30(2) requires that where a policy is established or amended it be disclosed so that data subjects can easily check it, by the method prescribed by Presidential Decree. The operative word in practice is easily. Having written the document and having left it where someone can find it are different achievements, and the detailed method has to be read in the Decree.

The third paragraph holds an unexpected rule. Article 30(3) provides that where the content of the privacy policy differs from the content of a contract between the controller and the data subject, whichever is more favourable to the data subject applies. The practical consequence is sharp in both directions. Write the policy looser than reality and the loose sentence can be held against you; write it stricter than reality and the strict sentence becomes the standard. A privacy policy is therefore not a well-written document to be copied but a description of what the system actually does.

If this blog already has a piece devoted to drafting the policy itself, read that alongside this one. Here the topic is covered only as far as a pre-launch decision requires.

3. Why 「get consent for everything」 is the wrong instinct

This is the most common misconception: that consent always comes first when personal data is involved. But Article 15(1) of the Personal Information Protection Act, as summarised by the Easy Law service of the Korean Ministry of Government Legislation, treats consent as one ground among several. The subparagraphs are these.

  • 1 - Where the data subject has given consent.
  • 2 - Where a statute has a special provision, or it is unavoidable in order to comply with a statutory obligation.
  • 3 - Where a public institution cannot avoid it in performing duties prescribed by statute.
  • 4 - Where it is necessary to perform a contract with the data subject, or to take steps requested by the data subject in the course of concluding a contract.
  • 5 - Where it is manifestly necessary for the urgent life, bodily safety or property interests of the data subject or a third party.
  • 6 - Where it is necessary to achieve the legitimate interests of the controller and those interests manifestly outweigh the rights of the data subject.
  • 7 - Where it is urgently necessary for public safety and welfare, including public health.

For a small service the one that matters is subparagraph 4. If a user creates an account in order to use the service, and an email address is needed to keep that account working, the address has the character of data necessary to perform the contract. Yet countless sign-up screens put that field behind a checkbox and add 「you cannot register unless you agree」. Two things go wrong.

  1. Turning a non-consent basis into consent creates room for withdrawal. Once consent is the stated ground, that consent is treated as something that can be withdrawn, and data the service genuinely needs to operate gets dragged into the same logic.
  2. The line between mandatory and optional blurs. When every checkbox looks compulsory, the ones that must genuinely be optional end up effectively forced.

That line has an express rule behind it. The Easy Law guide, citing Article 16(3) of the Personal Information Protection Act, states that a controller must not refuse to provide goods or services because the data subject declines to consent to collection beyond the minimum necessary. In connection with Article 22(5) the same guide explains that service cannot be refused because the subject declines consent to use beyond the original purpose, to provision to third parties, or to processing for promotion or sales solicitation.

What you must disclose when you do rely on consent is also fixed. The four items of Article 15(2), as set out in the guide, are these.

  • The purpose of collection and use.
  • The items of personal data to be collected.
  • The retention and use period.
  • The fact that the subject has the right to refuse consent, and the disadvantages of refusing where any exist.

The last item is the one most often missing. The requirement is to state the existence of the right to refuse, which means one more line of text next to the checkbox.

Age belongs here too. The guide, citing Article 22-2, states that where a controller must obtain consent in order to process the personal data of a child under 14, it must obtain the consent of the legal representative. This is not a requirement you satisfy with a document. If you need a verifiable route to a legal representative, the screens and the data model change. That is why many small services decide at launch that under-14 users are out of scope and say so clearly in the terms and on the sign-up screen. Either way the decision belongs before launch; after you have users it is hard to reverse.

4. The privacy officer: being small is not an exemption, it is a promotion

This is the part most often reported wrongly. Article 31(1) of the Personal Information Protection Act requires a controller to appoint a privacy officer with overall responsibility for personal data processing, while also allowing an exception where the controller meets criteria set by Presidential Decree. Stop reading there and conclude 「we are small, so we do not need one」 and you have missed half of it.

The other half is paragraph 2. Where a privacy officer is not appointed, the business owner or the representative becomes the privacy officer. The effect of the exception is not that the role disappears but that the appointment step is skipped and the representative takes the seat.

The size threshold was checked in the Easy Law service of the Ministry of Government Legislation. That guide gives Article 32(1) of the Enforcement Decree as the basis for the exemption, describes the target as controllers qualifying as small enterprises, and reports the sectoral thresholds for regular employees as follows.

  • Mining, manufacturing, construction and transport - fewer than 10 regular employees.
  • Other sectors - fewer than 5 regular employees.

A caution goes with those numbers. Qualifying as a small enterprise is not settled by headcount alone, because a separate statute sets the requirements, so do not conclude from these figures alone that the exemption applies without reading the source. This article verified the employee thresholds through the Ministry guide and left the remaining requirements outside its checked scope.

Failing to appoint has two consequences that run in parallel. One is the effect of paragraph 2 already described: the representative becomes the officer. The other is sanction. The same guide states that where a privacy officer is not appointed, an administrative fine of up to 10 million won applies under Article 75(4)9 of the Personal Information Protection Act. This is the only place in this article where an amount appears, because it is the only one whose provision was confirmed alongside it.

In practical terms: for a one-person service, the representative name and contact details go into the privacy policy, because item 9 of Article 30(1) makes the officer name or department and contact details a required entry. What solo developers actually worry about here is exposing a personal contact channel. The provision asks for a channel that reaches someone, and a dedicated service mailbox is a common answer, but whether a given arrangement satisfies the provision needs professional confirmation.

For completeness, Article 31 is followed by Article 31-2 (designation of a domestic representative), which concerns operators without an address or place of business in Korea. It sits outside this article, so only the title is noted.

5. Advertising messages: four things to check before the first send

Sign-up consent and advertising consent are different documents drawn from different statutes. The advertising side comes from Article 50 of the Act on Promotion of Information and Communications Network Utilization and Information Protection. The table sets out the requirements as read in the provision.

ParagraphWhat it requiresWhat to check in practice
Paragraph 1Anyone transmitting advertising information for profit through an electronic transmission medium must obtain the recipient explicit prior consent. The provision carves out cases such as advertising the same kind of goods to contact details obtained in a transaction relationshipIs there a record of when and with what wording consent was taken
Paragraph 3Transmission between 9 pm and 8 am the following day requires separate consent. Media prescribed by Presidential Decree are exceptedCan scheduled sends or automated notifications drift into that window
Paragraph 4When transmitting, you must state the sender name and contact details and how to refuse receipt or withdraw consentIs the footer text an actual working link
Paragraph 5Prohibits measures that evade or obstruct refusal or withdrawal, that generate contact details automatically, that register contact details automatically, that conceal the sender identity or the source of the advertising, and that induce a reply by deceptionHave you built opt-out so that a human has to intervene
Paragraph 8You must check periodically whether recipients still consentIs the consent timestamp stored so the interval can be computed

As the right-hand column shows, this provision is a data problem rather than a wording problem. Paragraph 8 demands periodic checking, which is impossible if the consent timestamp was never stored. Paragraph 4 demands a withdrawal route, which is decoration if nothing records the withdrawal. So a service that plans to send anything should build the consent and withdrawal store before the first send. Section 11 covers that part.

One more distinction. Operational notifications and advertising are not the same thing. A password reset email or a payment failure notice has a different character, while a message that opens with 「we shipped a new feature」 but is in substance a sales pitch drifts toward advertising. How a borderline message is classified depends on content and context and needs professional confirmation. The practical safeguard is simple: keep operational mail and marketing mail on separate paths from the start.

The level of sanction for breach appears in this article without an amount. The fine and penalty figures could not be confirmed in the provision, so the verified statement stops at the fact that breach is subject to sanction.

6. Terms of service: a link is not the same as an explanation

Terms are effectively necessary once a service has accounts. Yet practical attention usually goes to what to write and rarely to how to present it. The statute weights the second. Here is what Article 3 of the Act on the Regulation of Terms and Conditions says, as read in the provision.

  • Paragraph 1 (drafting) - Standard terms must be drafted in Korean, use standardised and systematic wording, and mark important content clearly with devices such as symbols, colour, or bold and larger type so that it is easy to recognise.
  • Paragraph 2 (disclosure and copy) - When concluding a contract the business must make the content of the terms clear to the customer by a method generally expected for that type of contract, and must hand over a copy if the customer asks, so that the customer can know the content. Sectors such as passenger transport, electricity, gas and water supply, postal service and public telephone service are excepted.
  • Paragraph 3 (explanation) - The business must explain the important content of the terms so that the customer can understand it, unless explanation is significantly difficult given the nature of the contract.
  • Paragraph 4 (effect of breach) - Where a business concludes a contract in breach of paragraphs 2 and 3, it cannot assert those terms as part of the contract.

Paragraph 4 is the point of this section. Fail the procedure and, before any question of a fine arises, you lose the ability to rely on the clause. That hurts operators in an unexpected direction: the carefully drafted limitation-of-liability clause or suspension clause can become a sentence you cannot invoke at the moment you need it.

So the practical conclusion is plain. Drafting the terms well matters, and so does making the important clauses visible and letting the user see them at the moment of agreement. A common approach is to show the full terms in a scrollable view, present the key clauses as a short summary next to it, and record the version of the terms in force at the moment of consent. With version records you can answer the later question 「which sentences were shown at the time」.

Which clauses are unfair and therefore void is not this article axis. That test and the dispute routes are covered from the consumer side in a separate piece, so see the guide to void unfair contract clauses. From the operator side this article says only one thing: read your own terms as a consumer once. If a clause type that piece treats as void appears in your terms, that clause may as well not be there.

7. User content and open-source notices

If users upload text or images, two rights questions arrive together. How far can the service use what a user uploaded, and is the service using somebody else work.

The first resolves into the terms. Who owns the content, the scope in which the service may use it, and when content is deleted or access restricted all end up in the terms. And the requirement from Article 3 of the Act on the Regulation of Terms and Conditions applies here without modification. The more a clause can work against the user, the closer it is to important content, so it is safer to assume the marking and explanation duties bite harder.

The second concerns other people works that the service itself uses: icons, fonts, images and open-source libraries. Open source does not mean free of obligation; it means permission with conditions attached, so failing to give the notice a licence requires puts you outside the condition. What usually goes wrong in practice is not the code but the absence of a notices screen.

  • Keep the list of libraries and their licences in a file. Remembering does not survive a year.
  • Check whether a licence requires inclusion of the copyright notice and the licence text. Where it does, put a notices page inside the service.
  • Assume conditions can differ by form of distribution. Serving over the web and shipping a file are not the same act.
  • Treat fonts and icons separately. Web font licences differ in the scope of use they allow.
  • If you use output from an image generation tool, check that tool terms of use as well.

This article, however, did not open the provisions of the Copyright Act. So it says nothing about takedown requests, infringement reporting procedure, or the scope of liability. Copyright basics and practice are covered separately, so see the copyright basics guide. The two takeaways here are that user content raises the weight of your terms, and open source leaves you a notices obligation.

8. When data crosses a border - transfer abroad and overseas users

Small services almost always run on foreign infrastructure, and one fact gets missed: the fact that you did not choose the server location does not make the cross-border question disappear. Here is the structure of Article 28-8 of the Personal Information Protection Act as read in the provision.

Paragraph 1 first sets the rule that a controller must not provide, outsource the processing of, or store personal data abroad, and then lists the grounds.

  • Separate consent from the data subject.
  • A special provision on cross-border transfer in a statute, a treaty to which Korea is a party, or another international agreement.
  • Where outsourcing or storage is necessary for purposes such as performing a contract, and the matter has been disclosed in the privacy policy or notified by means such as email.
  • Where the recipient holds a certification designated and published by the Personal Information Protection Commission and has taken the protective and other necessary measures.
  • Where the transfer goes to a country the Commission recognises as having a substantially equivalent level of personal data protection.

The third ground is the one that fits small services most often, and the important part is that relying on it carries a condition: you must disclose or notify. Use foreign infrastructure while your privacy policy says nothing and the ground is simply empty. Read the other way round, the outsourcing and cross-border sections of your policy have to be a reflection of your infrastructure.

If you rely on consent instead, paragraph 2 fixes what to disclose: the items of personal data transferred, the destination country with the timing and method, the recipient name or title and contact details, the recipient purpose and retention and use period, and how to refuse the transfer, the procedure, and the effect of refusing. The last item is the heavy one, because being able to refuse means deciding what happens to a user who refuses, which lands back in design.

And here is what this article could not verify. It is widely noted that EU data protection rules may apply to a Korean operator that targets users in the EU, but no official EU source could be opened during this research. This article therefore states no EU article numbers and no applicability test, and leaves the detail outside its scope. If you intend to target users abroad, you need the official material of that region supervisory authority and professional confirmation. What is confirmed is the sequencing: under Korean law, the cross-border transfer provision bites first.

9. If there is a payment - the part this article could not verify

Going paid adds obligations: where and how business identity information must be displayed, how withdrawal of subscription must be handled and within what period, and whether mail-order business notification applies. This article set out to cover that topic and failed to open the text of the relevant statute. So the following rules were applied.

  • No article numbers were stated. The rule is not to cite a provision that was never opened.
  • No periods or amounts were stated. A withdrawal window or a notification exemption threshold written without a verified basis becomes a loss for the reader.
  • Only the place to confirm was left. E-commerce obligations and mail-order notification fall under the Fair Trade Commission, and the notification counter is the local basic municipality.

Independently of verification, the order of reasoning can still be written down. Ask first whether money changes hands, and if so whether you are the one selling to the consumer. If both answers are yes, treat e-commerce obligations as in scope and get confirmation before launch. Even when a payment gateway sits in the middle, the party bearing contract and disclosure duties is a separate question, so do not take the processor assurance that it is all handled at face value.

Leaving this section thin is uncomfortable, but writing unverified content as if it were verified is far more dangerous. Unlike every other section, here the absence of a basis is itself the information.

11. The privacy policy is a design specification

This may be the most useful section here. Look again at the items a privacy policy must contain and you see that they are not text to write but capabilities the system has to have. When document and system diverge, editing the document does not close the gap.

The sentence you end up writingWhat the system must therefore haveBasis
「Member data is destroyed immediately after withdrawal」A real deletion path on account closure, and a decision covering copies left in backups and logsArticle 30(1), items 2 and 4
「Access logs are kept for a set period」A job that removes expired records, or a retention policy, plus a timestamp column that lets the period be computedArticle 30(1), item 2
「We use cookies and analytics, and you may refuse」The actual list of tools in use, and code whose behaviour changes when refusal is expressedArticle 30(1), item 10
「We outsource work to the following providers」An external service list kept current, and a habit of amending the document when a tool changesArticle 30(1), item 6

「You may withdraw marketing consent at any time」A field that stores the withdrawal, and a send path that always passes through that fieldNetwork Act Article 50(4) and (5)

14. Frequently asked questions

Do I need a privacy policy if my site is just an information page with no sign-up?

The test is not sign-up but whether you process personal data. Article 30(1) of the Personal Information Protection Act requires a controller to establish a policy and lists purposes, retention period, destruction procedure and the officer contact details among the contents. The same article requires a statement on automatic collection devices where applicable, so a contact form or an access-log tool can put you inside the rule with no membership concept. Individual judgement needs professional confirmation.

I run a one-person service with no employees. Can I skip appointing a privacy officer?

There is an exemption from appointing, but the role does not disappear. Article 31(2) provides that where nobody is appointed the business owner or representative becomes the privacy officer. The Easy Law guide gives Article 32(1) of the Enforcement Decree as the basis, reports sectoral thresholds of fewer than 10 regular employees for mining, manufacturing, construction and transport and fewer than 5 for other sectors, and states that failing to appoint is subject to a fine of up to 10 million won under Article 75(4)9.

Can I make marketing consent a mandatory checkbox at sign-up?

No. The Easy Law guide, citing Article 16(3), states that service cannot be refused because the subject declines consent to collection beyond the minimum necessary, and it says the same in connection with Article 22(5) for processing aimed at promotion or sales solicitation. Actually sending the messages adds the prior consent, night-window consent and disclosure duties of Network Act Article 50.

Does using a foreign cloud provider or a foreign analytics tool count as a cross-border transfer?

If processing or storage happens outside Korea, Article 28-8 of the Personal Information Protection Act applies. It bars provision, outsourced processing and storage abroad, then allows separate consent, a statute or treaty, outsourcing or storage for performing a contract where the matter is disclosed or notified, a designated certification, and a recognised adequate country. Whether a particular tool falls inside depends on the contract structure, so confirm it with a specialist.

Is it enough to put a link to my terms of service on the sign-up screen?

A link and the procedure differ. Article 3 of the Act on the Regulation of Terms and Conditions requires the drafting method, disclosure with a copy on request, and explanation of important content, and paragraph 4 provides that a business concluding a contract in breach of paragraphs 2 and 3 cannot assert those terms as part of the contract. Skipping the procedure can strip the clause of force.

Conclusion: back to the five questions

Folded into a paragraph: starting from a list of statutes never ends, but starting from what the service does makes the list short. Is there a login, is there a payment, is there user content, do you advertise, does data cross a border. Those five answers almost fix the provisions you have to check.

So there are three things you can do today. First, write down the answers to the five questions. One sheet of paper is enough, and cross out every 「no」 on the spot. Second, for each remaining row, list the data you collect. Open the screens and the database side by side and write what is actually stored, and half of your privacy policy is already drafted. Third, take that list to someone for confirmation. A prepared list buys far more in the same hour.

One last sentence, the one worth keeping. A privacy policy is not a document that describes the service; it is a specification the service has to meet. That is exactly why Article 30(3) of the Personal Information Protection Act makes whichever is more favourable to the data subject apply when the policy and the contract diverge. Writing the document first and bending the system to it later looks easier and grows the risk.

To repeat: this article provides general information and is not legal advice. Do not treat anything here as fixing the scope of obligations for a particular service. Every service and situation described is a hypothetical example and refers to no real operator.

Consult a lawyer or another qualified professional about your own case. Whether you are a personal information controller, whether a flow is a cross-border transfer, and what filings you need all depend on the structure of the service and the contracts behind it. The payment and overseas-user topics in particular are areas this article left empty because they could not be verified, so separate confirmation is essential. Note again that this article covers Korean law; the rules of your own country of residence or establishment will differ.
