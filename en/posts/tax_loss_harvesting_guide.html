Introduction: When a Loss Can Become a Resource

When a holding is deep in the red, the instinct is usually to wait for it to recover. From a tax standpoint, however, an unrealized loss is not just a painful number but a potential tax resource. If you actually sell a losing position to lock in the loss, you can offset it against gains realized on other holdings in the same year, reducing your taxable capital gain. Deliberately realizing losses to manage your tax burden this way is called tax loss harvesting, sometimes described as tax-loss selling.

Simplifying it into a "free win," though, invites misunderstanding. Tax loss harvesting is not magic that erases tax; it is generally closer to deferring the tax burden or lowering the current year's taxable profit. Some markets, like the US, restrict repurchases with a wash sale rule; others, like Korea's overseas-stock regime, have no such rule but a different deduction-and-rate structure. This article lays out the mechanics of harvesting, the US wash sale rule, the Korea overseas-stock capital gains context, execution steps, and the limits, all oriented around decision criteria. It is for information only and does not recommend or guarantee any specific trade or savings amount.

1. What Tax Loss Harvesting Is - Concept and Principle

The core principle is offsetting realized losses against capital gains. As long as a loss sits only as an unrealized figure, nothing happens for tax purposes. For the loss to enter the tax calculation, you must actually sell to realize it. A realized loss is then netted against realized gains in the same tax year, lowering the net profit that forms the tax base. For example, if you gained 5,000 on one holding and lost 3,000 on another, realizing the loss brings the netted profit down to 2,000, and tax is figured on that reduced amount. That is the skeleton of the idea.

A common misconception is that harvesting "makes up for" the loss. It does not. The loss is still your loss; tax rules merely let you offset it against gains. The benefit of harvesting is the indirect effect of "tax not paid," not any recovery of the loss itself.

2. How It Works - Netting Losses Against Gains

A typical harvesting flow is commonly described as follows.

  • Step 1 - Map gains and losses: Identify the capital gains you have already realized, or expect to realize by year-end, in the same tax year.
  • Step 2 - Screen loss positions: Among holdings showing a loss, pick those you are comfortable trimming or rebalancing on investment merits.
  • Step 3 - Realize the loss: Sell the position to lock in the loss, which then becomes eligible for netting.
  • Step 4 - Keep your exposure: If you want to stay invested, a frequently cited approach is reallocating into a similar-but-not-identical asset, within the limits the rules allow.

In the US case, references also note that net losses remaining after netting can offset ordinary income up to an annual cap (commonly cited as 3,000 US dollars), with larger net losses carried forward to the next year. These caps and carryforward rules vary by country's tax law, so confirm the rules that apply to you.

3. The US Wash Sale Rule

Any discussion of harvesting in the US inevitably brings up the wash sale rule. It is described as a guardrail against realizing a loss purely for tax reasons and then immediately buying the same position back, effectively keeping your exposure intact.

3.1 The 30-Day Rule and Substantially Identical Securities

By common accounts, if you buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the loss sale (a 61-day window in total), the loss deduction is disallowed. The crux is the judgment of what counts as "substantially identical," which can extend beyond the same stock to instruments tracking effectively the same index, as commentators note.

3.2 A Disallowed Loss Is Not Lost - Basis Adjustment and Deferral

Crucially, a loss disallowed by a wash sale is not erased but added to the cost basis of the replacement security. The new asset's basis rises by the disallowed amount, so the loss effectively revives when that asset is later sold outside the wash sale window - it is deferred. That is why the wash sale rule is often described as a rule that shifts timing rather than one that takes the loss away.

3.3 Reaching Across Accounts, Spouses, and Tax-Advantaged Accounts

The wash sale rule is described as applying not only within a single account but also to repurchases in other accounts you control, a spouse account, and tax-advantaged (tax-deferred) accounts such as an IRA. Spouses trading the same security across separate accounts can trip the rule inadvertently, a frequently flagged pitfall. Specifics can change, so verify with official IRS sources.

4. The Korea Context - Overseas-Stock Capital Gains and Netting

For Korean residents, harvesting comes up most often with overseas stocks. As commonly described, overseas-stock gains are netted across the year, then a basic deduction of 2.5 million KRW is subtracted, and the remainder is taxed at 22 percent (20 percent capital gains tax plus 2 percent local tax) under separate taxation, with the prior year's realized results filed and paid the following May.

4.1 Netting Gains and Losses Within the Same Year

That is where room for harvesting appears. If a big winner and a loser coexist in the same year, realizing the loss to offset the gain can lower the netted profit and the tax base. Conversely, if there are only losers and no realized gains, there may be little tax reason to lock in the loss. Because netting occurs within the same tax year, managing realization timing - by settlement date around year-end, for instance - is frequently stressed.

4.2 The Difference: No US-Style Wash Sale Rule

Korea's overseas-stock capital gains regime is reported to have no repurchase restriction equivalent to the US wash sale rule. In principle, selling a losing position to realize the loss and immediately buying it back does not, by common accounts, void the loss netting right away. So "keep the position, realize only the loss" is described as less constrained in Korea than in the US. That said, this - along with rates, deductions, and netting scope - is subject to change, so do not treat this article as settled guidance; verify with current official sources such as the National Tax Service and consult a tax professional.

5. Execution Steps and Cautions

5.1 Repurchase Rules and Replacement Assets

To stay invested while realizing only the loss, US investors often reallocate into a different but correlated asset rather than the same one to avoid a wash sale - while taking care it is not "substantially identical." Where no wash sale rule applies, as with Korea overseas stocks, repurchase is less constrained, but repeatedly trading against your own investment discipline purely for tax is not advisable.

5.2 Trading Costs and Spreads

Harvesting involves real trades - a sale and, if desired, a repurchase. The resulting commissions, non-tax trading costs, bid-ask spreads, and currency-conversion costs (for overseas stocks) can eat into the tax benefit. It is repeatedly noted that harvesting only pays off if the tax saved clearly exceeds these costs.

5.3 The Dividend and Distribution Reinvestment Trap

If dividends or fund distributions are set to reinvest automatically, that reinvestment can count as an unintended repurchase inside the wash sale window, a pitfall frequently cited in the US context. When planning to harvest, it is safer to review reinvestment settings alongside your trade timing.

6. The Limit - Deferral, Not Exemption

The most important limit is that harvesting defers rather than erases tax. After realizing a loss and buying a similar asset back, that asset's cost basis (purchase price) is lower. So when you eventually sell it, the capital gain is computed larger, and tax can rise accordingly. In effect, the tax you cut today is partly offset by a larger future tax base.

So where does the real benefit come from? Commonly cited sources include the time value of deferring tax, adjusting your rate bracket or burden by offsetting a large current gain with a loss, and using annual deductions (such as Korea's 2.5 million KRW for overseas stocks). But the size of these effects varies greatly with your tax rate, the amounts realized, and future market moves, so it cannot be stated as a guaranteed savings amount.

7. Decision Criteria - When Harvesting Is Worth Considering

Harvesting is not a cure-all but a tool to weigh when it fits the situation. The more the following conditions overlap, the more it tends to be worth considering, per common discussion.

  • You already have, or expect, realized gains to offset in the same tax year.
  • The loss position is one you would trim or rebalance on investment merits anyway (investment logic first, not tax).
  • The tax benefit is clearly larger than trading, currency, and spread costs.
  • You can follow the repurchase rules precisely (the US wash sale rule and the like).

Conversely, if you would shake a long-term holding plan just to cut taxes, lock in losses with no gains to offset, or let costs exceed the tax saved, the payoff from harvesting may be small.

8. Conclusion: How to Approach Tax Loss Harvesting

Tax loss harvesting is a useful tool for turning an unrealized loss into a tax resource, but at heart it is about offsetting and deferral, not exemption. First, a loss must be realized by selling to enter the tax calculation. Second, it pays off most when offset against gains in the same tax year. Third, the US restricts repurchase with the wash sale rule, yet the loss is added to basis and deferred, while Korea's overseas stocks have no such rule but a different frame of 2.5 million KRW deduction, 22 percent, netting, and a May filing. Fourth, do not forget the trade-off that the replacement asset's lower basis can raise future tax.
