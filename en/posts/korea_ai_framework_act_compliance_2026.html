Korea's AI Framework Act Is in Force - What Your Company Actually Has to Prepare

A four-step applicability test, a document checklist per duty, and a 90-day order of work

July 27, 2026 Law & Legal

Introduction: the question people actually ask is a different one

There is no shortage of explainers on the Republic of Korea's AI Framework Act. Yet the questions that actually land on the desk of a compliance officer, an in-house lawyer, or whoever was handed the AI file are never what is this Act. They are two others: does it apply to us, and if it does, what do we have to produce. Legislative purpose and background only become useful after those two are answered.

So this article is written less as an explainer and more as a work order for starting an internal review. The sequence is as follows. First we pin down the current state of the law, its effective dates and amendment history. Then we test applicability in four steps. Then we list, per outcome, the documents that must actually exist as files. Then we separate what existing documents can already cover from what has to be written from scratch. Finally we assign owners and an order of work.

One premise has to be nailed down first. This law is already in force. If the material in front of you says it is due to take effect in 2026, that material is out of date. The Act and its Enforcement Decree have applied since 22 January 2026, and an amending act and amended decree took effect on 21 July 2026. The government is running a grace period, but as discussed below, a grace period defers sanctions, not duties.

1. Where the law stands - fixing the dates first

Before any internal review, everyone has to agree on a single reference date. This statute has several names and amends quickly, and a meeting where each person brings material from a different month goes nowhere.

1.1 Getting the name right

  • The formal title is the Framework Act on the Development of Artificial Intelligence and the Creation of a Foundation for Trust. Searching under this title returns the most reliable sources
  • It is commonly abbreviated to the AI Framework Act or the AI Basic Act. All three names refer to the same statute
  • Labels such as an AI promotion law or an AI regulation law are journalistic characterisations, not titles. Internal documents cause less confusion if they use the formal title

1.2 The confirmed timeline

DateEventWhat it means in practice
2024-12-26Passed the National AssemblyLegislation settled. Trust only material published after this point
2025-01-21Promulgated as Act No. 20676The date article numbers were fixed. Cite the act number alongside
2026-01-22Act enters into force plus Enforcement Decree (Presidential Decree No. 36053)The date duties began. Legal obligations exist from here
2026-01-24Part of the Decree's schedule relating to digital medical devices enters into forceMedical device operators face a two-day difference in commencement
2026-07-14Amended Decree approved by the CabinetAmendment content settled
2026-07-21Amending Act and amended Decree enter into forceNew procurement-related confirmation scheme and more. Bidders should re-review

1.3 What the July 2026 amendment changed

If the January 2026 package leaned toward trust and regulation, the package that took effect on 21 July 2026 leans toward promotion. Based on published material, the principal additions are as follows.

  • A new AI product and service confirmation scheme — a procedure allowing AI products and services to be preferentially considered in public procurement. For companies with public-sector revenue this may be more urgent than the regulatory side
  • Broader definition of AI-vulnerable groups — job seekers and women with career interruptions are reported to have been added to the existing categories such as persons with disabilities and older adults. This feeds directly into impact assessment design
  • Expanded support measures — support for talent at universities outside the capital region and for science and engineering personnel costs, and broader grounds for establishing AI research institutes. The appearance of branch-numbered provisions such as Articles 17-2, 22-2 and 22-3 reflects this direction
Practical tip. Put a reference date on the front page of every internal review document, for example the date you retrieved the statutory text in July 2026. This law gained an amendment within six months and its subordinate notices are still being tidied. A compliance document with no reference date will be trusted by nobody six months from now.

2. Step one - are we an AI business operator?

Everything else depends on this. If the answer is genuinely no, the later steps are moot. In practice, though, organisations wave through this step far too quickly.

2.1 Two kinds of operator

The Act broadly splits AI business operators into two. An AI development business operator develops and supplies artificial intelligence. An AI deployment business operator uses artificial intelligence supplied by a developer to provide a product or service. The category most often overlooked is the deployment operator.

Your situationFirst-pass viewWhy
You train and deploy your own modelsLikely a development operatorThe textbook case of developing and supplying AI
You attach a commercial model API to a customer-facing chatbotLikely a deployment operatorYou built nothing, yet you supply a service containing AI to outsiders
You switch on an AI feature inside a SaaS product to handle customersNeeds reviewTurns on whether the feature is visible to customers and supplied under your brand
Employees use generative AI only for internal workMay fall outside if nothing is suppliedBut re-examine if outputs end up in external deliverables
You only resell or distribute a product containing AICheck the contract structureWhose name it is supplied under, and who faces the user, is decisive
You are a foreign entity with no Korean address or place of businessStill potentially in scopeArticle 4 provides for extraterritorial application (below)

2.2 Foreign operators are not excluded - Article 4

Article 4, paragraph 1, which sets the scope of application, provides that the Act applies even to acts done outside Korea where they affect the domestic market or domestic users. In other words, where the servers sit and where the entity is incorporated are not the test. The test is whether Korean users are affected. Paragraph 2 of the same article provides that the Act does not apply to artificial intelligence developed or used solely for national defence or national security purposes, as prescribed by Presidential Decree.

2.3 Questions to ask at this step

  • Can you list every externally supplied product or service in which AI plays a part? If no such list exists, build that first
  • Does the list include ancillary SaaS features, internally built tools, and modules delivered to partners?
  • Is the involvement of AI visible on the user's screen, or is it running quietly behind it?
  • Does an AI-assisted decision affect a customer's rights or obligations — approval, rejection, tiering, ranking?
  • If you supply a foreign parent's product in Korea, who does the contract name as responsible toward Korean users?

A table of the answers to those five questions is effectively the first draft of your AI asset register. Every deliverable discussed below sits on top of that register. Start without it and something will be missed.

3. Step two - is it high-impact AI?

This is where the compliance burden diverges most sharply. Where a system is high-impact AI, a separate set of operator duties attaches.

3.1 Two conditions read together

The definition of high-impact AI in Article 2 subparagraph 4 reads as a combination of two conditions. First, is it used in one of the listed domains? Second, does it materially affect, or risk affecting, human life, physical safety and fundamental rights? Falling within a domain does not automatically make a system high-impact, and being outside the list is not conclusive either, because the Act leaves room for additional domains to be prescribed by Presidential Decree.

Listed domain (in substance)Cases that catch companies outSelf-check question
Energy supplyGrid operation support based on demand forecastingDoes the AI output feed directly into supply decisions?
Production and supply of drinking waterAnomaly detection and control in treatment processesIs there a path where equipment reacts without human confirmation?
Healthcare delivery and access systemsPatient triage and appointment prioritisationDoes it create differences in patient access?
Development and use of medical and digital medical devicesImaging read support, digital therapeuticsHave you run an AI Framework Act review separately from device approval?

The most common misjudgement. A company answers that it does not use AI in hiring, while in fact automated application sorting, duplicate-applicant detection and keyword-based ranking are running. HR simply never called those things AI. The test is not the label on the tool but what its output does to a person's rights and duties. The domains and wording in the table above are a summary drawn from published material, so check them against the statutory text before deciding.

3.2 The confirmation route - Article 33

Article 33 (confirmation of high-impact AI) requires a business operator to review in advance whether its artificial intelligence, or a product or service applying it, is high-impact AI, and allows the operator to request confirmation from the Minister of Science and ICT where the answer is unclear. Published explanations of the Decree describe a processing period of 30 days, extendable once by up to 30 days, with the reason and the length of any extension to be notified in writing.

What matters practically is that requesting confirmation is optional, not mandatory. No regulator is going to arrive and designate your systems for you. The first determination is the operator's own, and whether that determination was reasonable will later have to be shown from records. That is why the reasoning document matters more than the conclusion.

3.3 If it applies - Articles 34 and 35

Article 34 (duties of business operators concerning high-impact AI) requires measures to secure safety and reliability. Published material describes the following components.

  • Establishing and operating a risk management plan — which risks are identified and how they are mitigated
  • Establishing and operating an explanation plan — how decision criteria and the basis for outcomes are explained to users
  • Establishing and operating a user protection plan — how objections and correction requests are received
  • A framework for human management and oversight — at which points a person can check or halt an automated decision
  • Documenting the implementation of those measures and retaining the records — published material describes a retention period of five years

Article 35 (impact assessment of high-impact AI) is different in character. Published commentary describes it as a duty of endeavour for business operators. However, where state agencies intend to use a high-impact AI product or service they are to give preference to those for which an impact assessment has been carried out, which makes it close to mandatory in practice for companies selling into the public sector. Article 35, paragraph 1 also requires the assessment to reflect the characteristics of AI-vulnerable groups, and the July 2026 amendment is reported to have added job seekers and women with career interruptions to that category.

4. Step three - does the transparency duty apply?

Even where nothing is high-impact, this is the provision most companies actually collide with first, because it bites the moment generative AI is used.

4.1 Three strands, not one

Article 31 (transparency duty) is often summarised as a watermark rule, but three obligations of different character sit inside a single article. Treating them as one thing guarantees a gap in your response.

StrandWhatWhenSanction per published material
Advance noticeThe fact that the service operates on high-impact or generative AIBefore supplying the product or serviceAdministrative fine up to KRW 30 million (Article 43)
Output labellingThe fact that the output was produced by generative AIWhen the output is suppliedCorrective order (Article 40, paragraph 3)
Deepfake disclosureThe fact that virtual sound, images or video is hard to distinguish from realityOn supply, so that users clearly recognise itCorrective order (Article 40, paragraph 3)

4.2 Visible methods and machine-readable methods

Published explanations of the Decree divide labelling into two families.

  • Visible methods — captions, logos, on-screen watermarks and anything else a user can perceive with the naked eye
  • Machine-readable methods — metadata, digital watermarks and similar techniques a machine can parse
  • Where only machine-readable methods are used, a separate notice at least once, for instance at the point of download, is described as necessary. Putting it in the metadata is not the end of the matter
  • The age and physical condition of users are to be taken into account, meaning services aimed at children or with accessibility requirements need a different labelling design

4.3 Areas described as exceptions

  • Output consumed only inside the service interface and not distributed externally — in-game AI NPC dialogue and chatbot replies consumed on screen are described as capable of being covered by a notice at the start of the service rather than a label on every output
  • Artistic or creative expression, or output forming part of it, may be marked in a way that does not impair exhibition or enjoyment, per published explanations
  • Cases where AI involvement is already obvious, and purely internal use with no external supply, are described as being treated differently
Caution. These exceptions are drawn from published commentary and may be read narrowly. Before concluding that your chatbot is exempt because it lives inside a screen, check whether users can copy, screenshot or share answers into the outside world. The more a determination leans on an exception, the more carefully its reasoning must be recorded.

4.4 And yet this is the cheapest thing to fix

Counter-intuitively, the transparency duty is among the least expensive to prepare for. Building a risk management framework takes months; adding a line to a service entry screen and the help centre saying that the service operates on AI usually fits inside one sprint. Advance notice is also one of the items with an administrative fine attached. Its ratio of risk reduction to cost is the best in the whole package, which is a good reason to move it forward in the queue.

5. Step four - domestic representative and large-scale training

Most domestic companies come out of this step with a clean no. Even so, record the fact that you checked. Later on, having never considered a question and having considered it and concluded it did not apply are two very different positions.

5.1 Domestic representative - Article 36

Article 36 (designation of a domestic representative) requires operators with no address or place of business in Korea who meet certain thresholds to designate a domestic representative in writing and report it to the Minister of Science and ICT. Published material describes the thresholds as meeting any one of the following.

  • Total revenue of KRW 1 trillion or more in the preceding year
  • Revenue of KRW 10 billion or more from AI-related services in the preceding year
  • An average of one million or more daily domestic users

The representative is described as acting for the operator in matters such as submitting results relating to large-scale training, requesting confirmation of high-impact status, and supporting implementation of safety and reliability measures. Failure to designate carries an administrative fine of up to KRW 30 million. Domestic companies are not directly caught, but adding a check on whether an overseas vendor has designated a Korean representative to your due-diligence list gives you a contact route if something goes wrong.

5.2 Safety duty for large-scale training - Article 32

Article 32 (duty to secure AI safety) requires that, where the cumulative computation used in training exceeds a prescribed threshold, the operator identify, assess and mitigate risk across the lifecycle and submit the results of establishing a risk management framework to the Ministry of Science and ICT. Published explanations of the Decree put that threshold at 10 to the power of 26 floating-point operations (FLOPs), set with reference to international norms.

That scale corresponds to frontier model training at the global top end. The overwhelming majority of companies are nowhere near it. Fine-tuning and retrieval-augmented setups are far from this threshold. If your organisation is planning its own pre-training run, however, review this provision at the budget approval stage.

7. What existing documents can cover, and what they cannot

This is what practitioners most want to know and what explainer articles almost never address. Most organisations already hold compliance documentation. You do not have to rewrite all of it. You do need a test for when reuse is legitimate.

7.1 Three tests for reuse

  1. Same objective. What the existing document protects has to match what the Act protects. Information security risk and fundamental rights risk are different axes
  2. Coverage. If the existing document covers only certain systems, it cannot cover the whole AI estate
  3. Refresh cycle. A document revised every three years cannot govern models that change every quarter
Document you probably already haveReusabilityWhat has to be added
ISMS-P risk assessment resultsPartialSecurity and privacy axes are reusable. The fundamental rights axis and AI-specific risks such as hallucination, bias and automation bias must be added
Privacy impact assessment (PIA)PartialThe assessment skeleton and stakeholder mapping transfer directly. Scope has to widen from personal data to fundamental rights generally
Privacy policyLimitedUsable as a channel, but the fact of AI-based operation has to be stated explicitly. Buried in a policy, it may fall short of the purpose of advance notice
Terms of serviceNot recommendedTerms are not read. Advance notice is safer at the point of use, on screen. Terms should be supporting evidence only
Model cards and system cardsGoodAn excellent technical base for the explanation plan, but written for engineers, so a user-facing text has to be authored separately
ISO/IEC 42001 AI management system documentsMaps substantiallyRisk management, roles and documentation overlap heavily. But certification does not substitute for legal compliance; build an article-by-article mapping table
Internal control SOPs and quality management documentsPartialUsable as evidence of human oversight. Check that authority to halt and roll back an AI decision is stated
Existing customer complaint proceduresGoodReuse as the backbone of the user protection plan. Add objections to AI decisions as a distinct category with a handling deadline
Asset inventory or CMDBPartialA good starting point for the AI asset register. Add model source, training data and external exposure columns

In short, less has to be written from scratch than people fear. Most of the work is adding one column or one clause to something that already exists. Conversely, three things are never covered by existing documents: the AI asset register, the record of prior high-impact review, and the output labelling specification. These are requirements the AI Framework Act created, so they have to be authored.

8. Who owns what - allocating roles

AI compliance usually fails not because nobody understood the law but because nobody owned it. Legal does not know the systems, engineering does not know the articles, and between them nobody maintains the register.

For small organisations. Those eight rows do not need eight people. Naming one overall owner is the whole trick. One person maintaining the register and refreshing it quarterly produces a completely different outcome from nobody maintaining it. Compliance that runs on company-wide collaboration with no named owner stalls without exception.

9. Order of work and priorities - a 90-day arc

You cannot produce every deliverable at once, and the order is not arbitrary. Without a register you cannot run determinations, and without determinations you cannot know which documents you need. The sequence below follows that dependency.

  1. Days 1-15: build the AI asset register. Collect from every team anything that could be called AI. At this stage do not judge — just list. Mixing in judgement causes people to self-censor and omit
  2. Days 16-30: run steps one and two. Determine operator type and high-impact status per asset, with a line of reasoning each. Park ambiguous items on a separate list rather than forcing a conclusion
  3. Days 31-45: transparency gap analysis and immediate fixes. Find screens missing advance notice and add the wording. The best cost-to-effect ratio in the whole plan, so pull it forward. Draft the labelling specification in this window
  4. Days 46-65: reuse mapping. Lay out existing ISMS-P, PIA and SOP material and map which clause attaches where. This is where the list of genuinely new documents gets fixed
  5. Days 66-80: draft documents for high-impact assets. Risk, explanation, user protection and oversight, to draft stage. Draft, not final, is the goal
  6. Days 81-90: retention, roles and board reporting. Decide where documents live and who refreshes them. Residual risk gets an explicit acceptance signature and is recorded

If you can only pick one window, pick number three. Advance notice carries an administrative fine yet costs the least to implement. Conversely, number six is the easiest to skip, and skipping it leaves the first five windows sitting in one person's folder rather than becoming an organisational asset.

10. How to read the grace period

This deserves its own section because it generates more misunderstanding than anything else.

10.1 What the government said

  • The Ministry of Science and ICT stated it would run a grace period of at least one year alongside commencement
  • During that period, sanctions such as fact-finding investigations and administrative fines would in principle not be applied, and would be used only in a limited way in exceptional situations involving significant social harm such as loss of life or damage to human rights
  • The Ministry also said it would keep open the possibility of extending the period further, taking account of international developments and the pace of technology

10.2 So what is deferred and what is not

The grace period suspends enforcement, not obligations. Legal duties have existed since the effective date of 22 January 2026. What is deferred is the imposition of sanctions, not the duty itself. That distinction matters because the following risks persist throughout.
  • Civil liability — if an AI decision causes loss to a user, damages claims proceed regardless of the grace period
  • Other statutes — the Personal Information Protection Act, advertising rules and sector-specific regulation are unaffected
  • Contracts and procurement — public tenders and large-enterprise vendor reviews increasingly request AI compliance documents, so being unprepared can mean exclusion from bidding
  • The physical limits of preparation time — if you have high-impact assets, documentation takes months. Starting after the grace period ends is starting too late
  • Uncertainty about the end date — the at-least-one-year policy is confirmed, but no official announcement fixing an exact end date was found. Planning around an assumed end date is hard to recommend

10.3 Being precise about fine amounts

The ceiling set by Article 43 is up to KRW 30 million, and sources agree on that. Below that ceiling, the individual scale in the Decree's schedule is described in several places as starting in the low millions of won for a first failure to give advance notice, escalating with repetition, with a higher figure for failing to designate a domestic representative — but this article could not confirm the band-by-band amounts from a primary source. If you need a figure in an internal report, open the schedule to the Enforcement Decree directly.

11. Ten misconceptions seen in practice

  1. It has not commenced yet. It has. Effective 22 January 2026, with the amending act effective 21 July 2026
  2. There is a grace period, so we can skip it. Sanctions are deferred, duties are not. Civil, contractual and other-statute risk is unchanged
  3. We do not build AI, so we are out of scope. The deployment operator concept exists. A service built on a third-party API can be in scope
  4. The government will tell us if we are high-impact. The first determination is yours. Requesting confirmation is an option, not a duty
  5. A watermark discharges the labelling duty. Advance notice is a separate duty, and machine-readable-only labelling is described as requiring a separate notice
  6. KRW 30 million is an acceptable cost. That arithmetic ignores non-compliance with corrective orders, exclusion from procurement, contract termination and reputational loss, which are most of the real cost
  7. We complied with the EU AI Act, so Korea is covered. Different statutes. See section 12
  8. Our privacy documentation is enough. The overlap is large but the protected interest differs. The fundamental rights axis has to be filled in separately
  9. Small businesses are exempt. This article found no general exemption based on size. The size thresholds confirmed relate to designating a domestic representative. The Act does contain support provisions for smaller firms, but support is not exemption
  10. Internal-only means automatically excluded. It depends on external distribution and whether a listed domain is involved. Internal hiring screening is a real example of an internal system inside a high-impact domain

12. It is not the same law as the EU AI Act

This misconception surfaces constantly in Korean subsidiaries of global groups. A parent company's EU AI Act programme does not automatically deliver Korean compliance. The two instruments differ from their design philosophy up.

AxisRepublic of Korea AI Framework ActEU AI Act
CharacterA hybrid framework act combining promotion with trust-buildingA horizontal, risk-based regulation
ClassificationEnumerated domains for high-impact AIA risk-tier structure of prohibited, high-risk, transparency and minimal risk
SanctionsPer published material, centred on fines up to KRW 30 million and corrective ordersPenalties reaching a percentage of global turnover depending on the breach

On the EU side the key point is that deferral is not relaxation. Reporting indicates that enforcement powers over general-purpose AI model providers and the Article 50 transparency duties take effect on 2 August 2026, that the prohibition on generating non-consensual intimate imagery and child sexual abuse material and the machine-readable watermarking requirement crystallise around 2 December 2026, and that only the high-risk system rules have moved back. In other words, the content-labelling side tightens in Korea and the EU at roughly the same time. If you run a global service, designing your labelling specification against both regimes at once is more efficient. But compliance with one does not substitute for the other.

13. What this article left out because it could not be verified

The most dangerous thing in a legal article is writing an unverified item as though it were verified. The following were deliberately left unstated because they could not be confirmed from a primary source.

  • The subparagraph number for the definition of generative AI. That high-impact AI is defined in Article 2 subparagraph 4 was confirmed from multiple sources, but the subparagraph for generative AI could not be confirmed against the original text, so only the article number is cited
  • Band-by-band fine amounts in the Decree's schedule. Several sources describe escalation by number of violations, but the amounts could not be verified against the original
  • Final titles and status of subordinate notices and guidelines. It is confirmed that the Ministry released drafts of two notices and five guidelines for comment, but their final titles and current status could not be verified
  • The exact end date of the grace period. Only the at-least-one-year policy is confirmed; no announcement specifying a date was found
  • Whether a general size-based exemption exists. No provision exempting smaller enterprises wholesale was confirmed. The size thresholds that were confirmed relate to the domestic representative requirement
  • The exact statutory wording of the enumerated domains. The domain table above summarises published material; item-level wording was not collated against the original

If any of these is decisive for your decision, check the statutory text and the ministry's notices directly or obtain a lawyer's review.

Conclusion: three things you can do this week

The conclusion is not that the law is frightening. It is how to move one step forward from where you are. Whatever your size, three things can start this week.

  1. Build a one-page AI asset register. It does not have to be complete. Asking teams to write down anything with AI in it, and collecting the answers into one table, creates a floor for the discussion
  2. Check whether customer-facing screens carry advance notice. Chatbots, recommendations, automatic summaries, automated replies — anywhere. If it is missing, add the wording. Of the items carrying an administrative fine, this is the cheapest to implement
  3. Name one overall owner. An additional duty is fine. The moment a name is written down, this stops being something someone should do and becomes something someone does

Determinations, reuse mapping and documentation follow. But one thing matters more than following the order: the habit of recording why you decided what you decided. Subordinate rules under this Act are still being finalised and further amendments are likely. In that environment what protects an organisation is not a perfect conclusion but evidence that it reasoned sensibly and wrote it down at the time. A one-page register and a line of reasoning are where that evidence starts.
