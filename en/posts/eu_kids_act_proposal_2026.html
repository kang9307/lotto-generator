Context and Legislative Status — Proposal Phase and the Ordinary Legislative Procedure

On September 17, 2026, the European Commission formally presented a major legislative proposal titled the EU KIDS Act (Keeping Internet Digital Spaces Accountable and Trustworthy), aimed at creating a harmonized, EU-wide regulatory framework to protect children and adolescents online. According to the official press release (IP/26/1890) and accompanying policy briefings, this proposal addresses growing concerns regarding addictive platform designs engineered to capture young users attention for extended periods.

When analyzing this initiative, the primary legal reality to understand is that this document is strictly an executive legislative proposal, not an existing or immediately enforceable regulation. Under the European Union ordinary legislative procedure, the draft text must now be scrutinized, debated, and amended independently by both the European Parliament, representing EU citizens, and the Council of the European Union, representing member state governments. A final compromise text must be negotiated through inter-institutional trilogue sessions before it can be formally adopted into Union law. The European Commission is currently conducting a public consultation process to gather feedback from educators, parents, young people, and industry representatives, which remains open until November 26, 2026.

Consequently, the provisions detailed in the draft represent the policy architecture and regulatory intent envisioned by the European Commission, rather than settled legal requirements.

Tiered Age Framework for Social Media Access

The foundational pillar of the EU KIDS Act proposal is a tiered structural model known as the gradual uptake model, which establishes three distinct age tiers for online social media access. According to the official text, the material scope covers social media, video-sharing platforms, online games, and AI companions and chatbots used by minors.

Age Category Proposed Access Rules Platform Obligations Documented Basis
Under 13 years Prohibition of independent social media access Allow only child-friendly video sharing under parental accounts; verify age on sign-up Commission Proposal & Official Q&A
Ages 13 to under 15 Access permitted solely via parent-managed mini accounts Enforce 1-hour maximum daily usage ceiling; provide parental oversight and dashboard metrics Commission Proposal & Official Q&A
Ages 15 and older Permission to establish and operate independent personal accounts Maintain default safety-by-design baseline; mandate age verification on registration Commission Proposal & Official Q&A

The operational mechanisms across these three age tiers are structured as follows:

  • For children under 13 years of age, independent social media accounts are strictly banned, with exceptions permitted only for child-friendly video sharing services managed under a parent or guardian account.
  • For adolescents between 13 and under 15 years of age, platforms may only offer access through parent-managed mini accounts with restricted features.
  • Mini accounts are subject to a strict quantitative limit: total daily usage across the service must not exceed a cumulative total of one hour per day.
  • Young individuals reaching 15 years of age are permitted to open and manage their own independent social media accounts, while remaining protected by default safety configurations.

Mandatory Safety by Design Obligations Imposed on Digital Platforms

In addition to chronological age tiers, the EU KIDS Act imposes systemic safety by design obligations across all online services accessed by minors. The regulatory scope explicitly covers social media networks, video-sharing platforms, multiplayer online games, and commercial artificial intelligence services.

The Commission proposal directly targets exploitative product architectures that foster excessive screen engagement, mandating secure default configurations.

Regulatory Item Proposed Legal Obligation Conventional Industry Practice Covered Digital Services
Infinite Scrolling Restrict infinite scrolling and features driving excessive usage Continuous asynchronous feed loading to maximize session duration Social networking and video platforms
Reward Engagement Tricks Restrict reward tricks and gamified incentives driving excessive retention Daily streak badges and push incentives creating psychological retention Social networks, online gaming titles
Nighttime Push Alerts Restrict push notifications during sleeping hours to prevent excessive usage Unrestricted 24-hour notification delivery disrupting adolescent rest All digital services serving minors
Account Privacy Defaults Mandate minor profile configurations as private by default Public profile defaults with open indexability to maximize discovery Social networks, messaging software
Unsolicited Communications Strictly ban unsolicited contact from non-connected strangers Open direct messaging allowing contact without prior mutual authorization Social apps, multiplayer games

Under these design directives, platform developers cannot rely on opt-in safety menus. Instead, systemic protections must be embedded into the core user interface by default:

  • Platforms must restrict infinite scrolling, reward tricks, and features engineered to induce excessive digital engagement.
  • Notification delivery subsystems must restrict push notifications during sleeping hours to prevent late-night disruption.
  • All profile metadata and user uploads belonging to minors must be configured as private by default.
  • Platforms must strictly prohibit and block unsolicited communications from strangers to minors.
  • User interfaces must incorporate accessible controls allowing young users to easily block or mute other users.

Default Deactivation Rules for Artificial Intelligence Chatbots and Companions

The draft EU KIDS Act addresses conversational artificial intelligence interfaces and virtual companions accessible to minors through specific statutory mandates.

Based on the official text released by the European Commission, the obligations regarding artificial intelligence are focused on two primary requirements:

  • AI chatbots and companion features must be turned off by default within services accessed by minors.
  • Systems must be restricted from engaging in behaviors that cause children to form an emotional dependency on the AI.

These requirements prevent conversational agents from operating in an active default state upon application launch, while prohibiting digital interactions engineered to cultivate emotional reliance among young users.

Privacy-Preserving Age Verification Tools and Platform Obligations

The EU KIDS Act establishes that age verification procedures required to protect minors must not compromise the personal privacy of users.

The principles documented in the European Commission text regarding age assurance are structured as follows:

  • Online services and application stores are legally required to use age-verification tools that protect user privacy.
  • As an example, platforms may utilize privacy-preserving tools such as the EU age verification app, which does not store identity documents or biometric data.
  • Social media services and video-sharing platforms must verify the age of users upon the creation of new accounts.

By mandating verification tools that function without retaining identity records or biometric data on commercial platform servers, the proposal seeks to decouple age verification from data surveillance.

Reversal of the Burden of Proof and Duties for Very Large Platforms

From an enforcement perspective, a defining legal mechanism introduced by the EU KIDS Act is the formal reversal of the burden of proof. Under the Commission proposal, the responsibility shifts from regulators having to prove harm to platforms having to prove inherent safety.

The specific duties set forth in the Commission documentation are as follows:

  • The burden of proof is formally shifted from public regulatory authorities to online platforms.
  • Providers of Very Large Online Platforms (VLOPs) must actively prove that their digital services are safe for children.
  • Very Large Online Platforms must affirmatively demonstrate that their services are designed with child well-being in mind.
  • This burden of proof remains a proposal-stage provision until the European Parliament and Council have examined it.

Structural Comparison with Existing EU Regulations and General Protection Models

The EU KIDS Act proposal functions alongside the established Digital Services Act (DSA) framework, translating high-level principles into precise technical requirements for child digital safety.

Analytical Dimension Proposed EU KIDS Act EU Digital Services Act (DSA) General Protection Frameworks
Access Age Thresholds Ban under 13; mandatory 1-hour cap for ages 13 to under 15 No fixed chronological access ban; general duty to protect minors Uniform contractual baseline (typically age 13 or 14)
Design Architecture Rules Statutory restrictions on infinite scroll, reward tricks, sleep alerts Broad ban on manipulative dark patterns and minor targeted advertising Post-publication content review and administrative filtering
Artificial Intelligence Mandates Chatbots off by default; ban on emotional dependency Systemic risk evaluations and transparency obligations Absence of mandatory off-by-default requirements
Evidentiary Responsibility Platform must affirmatively prove safety by design before operation Platform must conduct annual systemic risk evaluations and mitigations Regulators or complainants bear the burden of proving harm

While the Digital Services Act established broad obligations prohibiting profiling minors for behavioral advertising, the KIDS Act proposal formalizes specific engineering benchmarks, including daily session ceilings and restrictions on continuous infinite feeds.

Furthermore, shifting the burden of proof to require digital platforms to demonstrate that their architectures actively respect child well-being represents a significant evolution in international digital governance.

Legal Disclaimer and Key Factors for Stakeholders to Monitor

The analysis set forth in this publication is strictly an objective overview of the legislative proposal and official explanatory documentation released by the European Commission. It does not constitute formal legal counsel or statutory advice. Because the proposal remains subject to multi-party negotiations across the European Parliament and Council of the EU, specific numerical thresholds and rules remain subject to revision.

Digital product architects, infrastructure engineers, compliance officers, and global service providers should track several critical developments as the legislative file advances:

  • First, whether the European Parliament and national delegations maintain the proposed 13 and 15-year chronological thresholds alongside the rigid 1-hour daily cap.
  • Second, the commercial adoption and deployment timeline of privacy-preserving age check tools that do not store identity credentials or biometric records.
  • Third, the exact methodology and documentation standards that the European Commission will define for platforms to satisfy the ex-ante burden of proof for system safety.
  • Fourth, the extraterritorial implications for non-European technology firms and app stores offering digital consumer services to end users located within the European Economic Area.

What Could Not Be Verified

  • The final legislative adoption date and formal implementation timetable, as the proposal is currently in its initial parliamentary reading phase without fixed deadlines.
  • The technical cross-platform aggregation mechanics for enforcing the 1-hour daily limit across multiple applications or concurrent devices, which is not detailed in the summary proposal.
  • The exact mathematical formulation and revenue-scaling criteria for statutory fines, which await detailed correlation with Digital Services Act enforcement schedules.
  • The specific legal precedence and grandfathering provisions governing interactions with preexisting national statutes in EU member states, such as French national laws on social media age gates.
