What was published — 5,463 responses collected from July 5 to September 5, 2026

State of Devs 2026 is a developer survey run by Devographics. The About page of the results site gives the collection window as July 5 to September 5, 2026 and the number of responses collected as 5,463. The Demographics page describes this as the second edition of State of Devs. According to the About page, the survey was created "in an effort to learn more about the non-technical aspects of life as a developer." Unlike surveys that ask which framework or language people prefer, this one asks about careers, workplaces, health, views on AI, worldview and hobbies, and the results site is divided along the same lines: Demographics, Career, Workplace, AI, Health, Worldview and Hobbies.

The overview text on the first screen of the results is a contributed piece. The site introduces its author as Miranda Heath, founding director and researcher at the Software Stewardship Lab. The survey was picked up on GeekNews on October 6, 2026, and the sentences often quoted in summaries are in that overview.

  • "Nearly half" of respondents had experienced job insecurity and insufficient wages.
  • "A quarter" had been laid off at some point in their career, and "nearly 1 in 10" had been laid off within the last 9 months.
  • "Over a quarter" anticipated needing to change careers altogether within the next 5 years.
  • 62% reported having had burnout at some point in their career, the same proportion as in last year's survey.
  • Views on AI were split, with 49% of respondents feeling positive towards it and 42% feeling negative.

The overview notes two firsts. This was the first Devographics survey to ask about developers' mental state, and it was also the first year in which the survey asked developers for their views on AI. As a result, the mental-state questions and the AI questions have no previous-year value to set beside them. Of the five sentences above, the first three are approximations hedged with "nearly" and "over", and behind each of the five sits one question and its answer options. The sections below attach to each sentence the question, the option, and the number of people who answered that question.

Who answered — recruitment and the makeup of the sample

Before any figure, the first thing to look at is how the respondents were gathered. The explanation on the About page comes down to three statements.

  • Open access. The survey was openly accessible online, and respondents were not filtered or selected in any way.
  • Recruitment channels. Respondents were primarily a mix of people who had taken past Devographics surveys such as State of JS and State of CSS, alerted through a dedicated mailing list, and traffic from social media.
  • A caveat about scope. The survey "should also be taken as a snapshot of a specific subset of developers, and is not meant to speak for the entire ecosystem."

The third statement is a limit that the organizer attaches to its own data. "This is not a representative sample" is therefore the operator's own description and not a reader's guess, and the numbers that back it up are published on the Demographics, Career, Workplace, Health and Metadata pages.

Four features of the sample stand out from the table.

  • Country of residence. The United States is the largest group at 22.83%, and adding up the ten largest countries (the United States, Germany, the United Kingdom, France, Canada, Australia, the Netherlands, Spain, Poland and Sweden) gives 2,987 respondents, about 64.9% of those who answered the residence question (calculated). Expanding the list shows India with 63 respondents (1.37%), Japan with 50 (1.09%) and China with 18 (0.39%). South Korea is not among the 40 countries and regions listed individually, and all remaining answers are grouped as "Other Answers", which holds 296 respondents (6.4%).
  • Experience. The median is 12 years, and respondents with four years of experience or less account for 8.67%. The Career page states directly that junior developers did not participate as much this year.
  • Type of work. In the question on day-to-day work areas, front-end development (JS/TS) was selected by 83.48% and front-end development (CSS) by 73.48%. The Workplace page explains the weight of front-end work by "the web-centric focus of the survey's audience."
  • Referral source. In the source chart on the Metadata page, "State of Devs (Devographics)" accounts for 3,615 respondents, which is 66.17% of all 5,463. The site says the source is computed from referrer data, URL tracking data and self-reported answers.

The Demographics page also contains a passage in which the organizers themselves point to the limits of the survey's reach. In the migration question, respondents living in Japan showed the highest share of immigrants, and the site writes that this is "almost certainly due to the survey failing to reach the native Japanese population more than to any real demographic trend." The same reasoning applies to other countries and to other questions. A few dozen respondents from a given country are not a scaled-down copy of that country's developers; they are the people who received word of a web development survey and chose to answer it. The language chart points in the same direction: 4,896 respondents, 89.62% of the total, filled out the survey in English.

The denominator of each percentage — not 5,463 but the people who answered that question

The About page states that all survey questions were optional. The number of people answering therefore differs from question to question, and under each chart the site shows the respondent count for that question together with its share of the whole survey. The question on workplace issues experienced over a career was answered by 5,352 people (98%), the question on states experienced recently by 4,484 (82%), the overall AI opinion question by 4,866 (89%), and the free-form question on AI outlook by 1,650 (30%).

The percentages in the charts are computed against these per-question counts. Dividing the 3,344 people who selected burnout by 5,352 gives 62.48%, which matches the chart (calculated). Dividing by all 5,463 respondents gives 61.21% (calculated). The gap is small only because this question had a 98% response rate; for a question answered by 82% of the survey, the choice of denominator moves the value considerably. The 2,943 people who selected reduced motivation are 65.63% of those who answered the question but 53.87% of all respondents (calculated).

Five points tend to trip readers up when working out the denominator.

  • Multiple-choice questions. The workplace issues question and the recent-state question both allow several selections. The option percentages do not add up to 100, and "nearly half had experienced job insecurity and insufficient wages" means that each of the two items was selected by close to half, not that the same people selected both. The site shows that respondents picked 4.3 items on average in the workplace issues question.
  • Questions with a selection limit. The question on emotions about the tech industry and the question on AI risks both limit respondents to four selections. A low percentage for an item is closer to "it seldom made someone's top four" than to "few people feel this way".
  • Different denominators on different pages. The language chart on the Metadata page shows 89.62% beside 4,896 English-language responses, and that value matches division by the full 5,463 (calculated). It cannot be assumed that such charts follow the same denominator rule as the charts in the main sections.
  • Subgroup comparisons. The discrimination chart in the overview splits respondents by gender, and its denominators are 678 for the group made up of women, non-binary or gender non-conforming respondents and those who chose "Not Listed", and 3,399 for men. Discrimination based on gender or gender identity was selected by 308 people in the first group (45.43%) and by 98 in the second (about 2.9%, calculated). The number 678 is also the count of people who selected "Discrimination" in the workplace issues question (678, or 12.67%), but the two are different values.
  • Partial responses. The survey completion chart shows a median of 96% and an average of 87%, and 458 responses (8.38%) filled out only 10 to 20% of the survey. This is the background to respondent counts that differ by several hundred from one question to the next.

In three places the wording of the overview and the wording of the chart options differ.

  • The "quarter" laid off. The option that stands at 25.0% in the chart is "Yes, sometimes before that". "Yes, during the past 12 months", at 9.32%, is a separate option, and since the response counts of the three options add up to the 5,299 people who answered, each respondent chose exactly one of them (calculated). The number of respondents who said they had been laid off at least once is the sum of the two options: 1,819, about 34.3% (calculated).
  • "The last 9 months". The overview says 9 months, while the option in the chart reads "during the past 12 months".
  • 42% negative on AI. Adding the four options on the negative side of the chart gives 1,914 respondents, or 39.33% (calculated). The sum of the four positive options, 49.77%, is close to the overview's 49%.

These differences can be handled as a matter of choosing which source to cite. When repeating a sentence from the overview, label it as the overview's wording; when repeating a chart value, give the question, the option and the respondent count along with it. That keeps the two kinds of value from blending into each other.

Job insecurity, layoffs and change — "have experienced" and "think it likely" are different questions

The question behind the 48.17% figure for job insecurity asks which workplace issues respondents have experienced "throughout your professional career". The time frame is the whole career and not the present, and several items can be selected. The most frequently selected items are "Bad management" with 3,374 respondents (63.04%), "Burnout" with 3,344 (62.48%), "Work-life balance issues" with 3,114 (58.18%) and "Boredom" with 2,905 (54.28%), while "None" was selected by 243 (4.54%). The Career page notes a correlation between having worked for more employers and reporting more workplace issues, and suggests this could simply be due to respondents experiencing more issues over time, adding that another factor could be junior hires lacking the perspective to identify problematic behaviors in the first place. The median experience among respondents is 12 years.

A separate question asks about the current workplace. In the multiple-choice question on the negative aspects of the working environment at the current employer (3,931 respondents, 72% of the survey), "Poor management" was selected by 1,748 (44.47%), "Compensation" by 1,710 (43.5%) and "Job security" by 1,224 (31.14%). The 48.17% for a whole career and the 31.14% for the current employer come from different questions with different denominators, so neither can be linked to the other as a "decrease" or an "increase". In the employment status question, "Unemployed" was selected by 419 respondents (7.78%).

Two questions ask about the future, and they differ by a single word. One asks about jobs and the other about careers. Both are five-step questions carrying the same condition: "within the next 5 years" and "due to reasons beyond your control".

  • Likelihood of having to change jobs. 5,310 respondents. "Somewhat likely" with 1,225 (23.07%) and "Very likely" with 1,025 (19.3%) add up to 2,250, or 42.37% (calculated). "Equally likely/unlikely" stands at 1,346 (25.35%), and the two unlikely options together come to 1,714, or 32.28% (calculated).
  • Likelihood of having to change careers. 5,334 respondents. "Somewhat likely" with 956 (17.92%) and "Very likely" with 440 (8.25%) add up to 1,396, or 26.17% (calculated). "Equally likely/unlikely" stands at 1,273 (23.87%), and the two unlikely options together come to 2,665, or 49.96% (calculated).

Two sentences came out of the same distribution. The overview wrote that over a quarter anticipated needing to change careers within the next 5 years, and the commentary on the Career page wrote that "most respondents still consider a forced career change unlikely". The first sentence leads with the 26.17% and the second with the 49.96%, and both are in the same chart. Repeating only one of them passes along only one side of the distribution.

The question does not ask what the "reasons beyond your control" are. The commentary on the Career page says that while AI anxiety and industry burnout play a part, for now at least traditional workplace issues are still the main factors making respondents worry for their jobs, and that apart from job insecurity itself, discrimination stands out as the issue most likely to make respondents think they will have to change jobs in the near future. Both statements are the site's commentary. The discussion of reading a profession's mood separately from its employment indicators is laid out in When a Whole Profession Stops Believing in Its Own Career.

Burnout at 62.48% and the "recently" question — two questions with different time frames

The 62% burnout figure is not the result of a standalone question; it is one option inside the workplace issues question described in the previous section. "Burnout" was selected by 3,344 people, 62.48% of the 5,352 who answered. The time frame is the whole career, and whether something counted as burnout was left to each respondent's own judgment. The overview notes that this proportion is the same as in last year's survey.

The question added this year uses a different time frame. "Which of the following have you experienced recently?" asks respondents to select everything they have experienced "recently", and the wording does not say how long "recently" is. The Workplace page explains that the response items were inspired by common signs of burnout and that respondents selected 4.5 items on average. The question was answered by 4,484 people, 82% of the survey.

  • Having reduced motivation or interest for work — 2,943 respondents, 65.63%
  • Feeling increasingly cynical about my work or industry — 2,700 respondents, 60.21%
  • Feeling emotionally drained at the end of the workday — 2,553 respondents, 56.94%
  • Finding myself procrastinating more — 2,389 respondents, 53.28%
  • Having trouble focusing on tasks I used to enjoy — 2,289 respondents, 51.05%
  • Having a reduced sense of accomplishment at work — 2,237 respondents, 49.89%
  • Feeling less effective at work — 2,021 respondents, 45.07%
  • Having trouble sleeping or irregular sleep patterns — 1,595 respondents, 35.57%
  • Having trouble disconnecting from work outside of work hours — 1,479 respondents, 32.98%

The overview's "around two thirds" who felt less motivated and more cynical corresponds to the 65.63% and 60.21% above, and its "around half" to the five items below them. The overview interprets these changes as indicative of burnout; that is the contributor's interpretation, and what the site says about the nature of the question goes only as far as "inspired by common signs of burnout". The figures for this question are self-reports of having selected a state as recent, and the 62.48% is a self-report of having selected burnout as something experienced during a career. The two values differ in both time frame and question, so they do not combine into a single line.

The related figures on the Health page are as follows. In the multiple-choice question on ongoing health issues (4,518 respondents), "Poor sleep" was selected by 1,866 (41.3%), "Weight issues" by 1,332 (29.48%), "Mental health issues" by 1,320 (29.22%) and "Back pain" by 1,176 (26.03%), while "None" was selected by 917 (20.3%). The average number of hours slept per night is shown as 6.9, and the site writes that 91% of respondents sleep between 6 and 8 hours. In the question asking for up to four emotions that describe how respondents currently feel about the tech industry (4,694 respondents), the order is "Exhausted" with 2,451 (52.22%), "Disillusioned" with 2,165 (46.12%), "Curious" with 1,916 (40.82%), "Overwhelmed" with 1,868 (39.8%), "Anxious" with 1,849 (39.39%) and "Hopeful" with 1,102 (23.48%).

The figures in this section are tallies of items that respondents selected themselves. How AI tools produce fatigue is covered in LLM Burnout - The New Fatigue of AI Coding Tools and The Human in the Loop Is Tired, so this section stops at the question wording and the figures.

The AI questions — 49% versus 42%, and what the chart options add up to

The AI section consists of questions on the share of AI-generated code, overall opinion, concerns, the employer's stance, the effect on mental state, and a free-form comment. The closed questions were each answered by between 4,625 and 4,866 respondents.

  • Share of AI-generated code. The question "What proportion of the code you produce is AI-generated?" (4,846 respondents) shows a median of 63% and an average of 56%. The answer 0 was given by 455 respondents (9.39%), the 87.5% bracket was chosen by 1,009 (20.82%) and the 75% bracket by 879 (18.14%). The site comments that developers who write almost all their code through AI represent the largest single segment.
  • Overall opinion. A nine-step scale running from fully negative to fully positive (4,866 respondents). The four steps on the negative side received 314, 489, 660 and 451 responses, the neutral step 530, and the four steps on the positive side 729, 1,087, 456 and 150. The most selected step is "Positive" with 1,087 (22.34%), and the median is shown as 50%.
  • Effect on mental state. A five-step agreement question on the statement "AI tools have had a positive impact overall on my mental state" (4,855 respondents). The results are "Strongly Disagree" 1,023 (21.07%), "Disagree" 1,189 (24.49%), "Neutral" 1,534 (31.6%), "Agree" 867 (17.86%) and "Strongly Agree" 242 (4.98%).
  • Employer's stance. 4,625 respondents. The option saying the company encourages AI use without it affecting the respondent's behavior was selected by 2,421 (52.35%); the option saying the company encourages AI use and the respondent uses AI more as a result by 1,402 (30.31%); the neutral option by 656 (14.18%); and the option saying the company discourages AI use by 146 (3.16%).
  • Risks of concern. Up to four selections, 4,849 respondents. The leading items are "Environmental impact" with 2,664 (54.94%), "Job displacement" with 2,653 (54.71%), "AI slop takeover" with 2,297 (47.37%) and "Negative cognitive impacts" with 2,191 (45.18%).
  • Free-form comments. 1,650 people, 30% of the survey, wrote something further about their outlook on AI. In the site's categorization the themes with the most matching answers are "Developer impact" with 428, "Anti-AI sentiment" with 403, "Industry impact" with 337, "Pro-AI sentiment" with 302 and "Neutral sentiment" with 265.

The value behind the overview's statement that nearly one half of respondents felt AI did not have a positive impact on their mental state is the sum of "Strongly Disagree" and "Disagree": 2,212 respondents, or 45.56% (calculated). In the same question "Neutral" is the largest single option at 31.6%, and the two agreeing steps together come to 1,109, or 22.84% (calculated). Disagreeing that the impact was positive is not the same statement as saying the impact was negative, and the statement the survey put to respondents was the former.

The category counts for the free-form comments are a different kind of value from the opinion distribution of the whole survey. They group the writing of the 30% who chose to write, so dividing 428 by 5,463 does not yield "the share of developers worried about developer impact". The site's commentary also describes differences between groups: older respondents tend to generate less of their code using AI yet tend to view the technology more positively, and freelancers reported lower AI usage and more negative attitudes towards AI. Because the site itself describes age as one of the key confounding factors in the dataset, statements of this kind are read as reports of correlation.

How far year-over-year comparison goes — from 8,717 respondents to 5,463

State of Devs was first held in 2025. The introduction on the 2025 results site gives the number of respondents as 8,717. The 2026 edition has 5,463, which is 3,254 fewer, a drop of about 37% (calculated). This is not a panel tracking the same people; it is a self-selected group recruited again each year, and both its size and its composition changed between the two years. According to the 2025 data carried alongside the 2026 data on the Demographics page, the share of the United States among those who answered the residence question moved from 25.76% in 2025 (1,823 of 7,077) to 22.83% in 2026 (1,051 of 4,603).

The year-over-year statements that the site makes in writing are the following five.

  • Burnout. The 62% who reported burnout at some point in their career is the same proportion as in last year's survey (overview).
  • Layoffs. Affirmative responses to the layoff question crept up slowly compared with last year. The site adds the caveat that this could also be due to the respondent pool changing from year to year (Career).
  • Job happiness. The majority remain happy with their current job, but the data shows a slight increase in unhappy respondents compared with last year (Workplace). This year's distribution is 2,746 respondents for "Happy" and "Very Happy" combined (57.69%, calculated) and 783 for "Unhappy" and "Very Unhappy" combined (16.45%, calculated).
  • Junior participation. Junior developers did not participate as much this year, with only 9% of respondents having four years of professional experience or less (Career).
  • A change to a question. In the question on negative aspects of the current workplace, "Poor management" was a write-in answer last year; adding it to the official selectable items this year sent it straight to the top (Workplace).

The fifth item is a case in which the site itself shows where a year-over-year comparison goes off course. The chart carries a "+ 8" marker beside this item, and that change does not mean management problems grew within a year; it is the consequence of an option becoming available to select. The small "+ 1" and "- 1" markers beside item names in other charts are read the same way. Checking the markers in the residence chart against the 2025 data shows Germany moving from third to second (+ 1), the United Kingdom from second to third (- 1) and Australia from eighth to sixth (+ 2), so the markers coincide with changes in rank. They show a shift in rank and not a change in percentage, and the ranks being compared belong to two years with different respondent pools.

A sentence such as "developer layoffs increased in 2026" therefore goes beyond what this survey covers. The sentence the survey does support ends here: of the 5,299 respondents to the 2026 edition's layoff question, 9.32% said they had been laid off during the past 12 months, and the site wrote that affirmative responses crept up slightly from last year while also stating that a changing respondent pool could be the reason.

"Out of scope" in the table does not mean the sentence is false. It means that the composition of this survey's respondents cannot back the sentence up. The rate of job insecurity or burnout among developers in South Korea is a value for a survey whose population is developers in South Korea, and the relationship between AI and burnout cannot be settled by self-reports taken at a single point in time. What State of Devs 2026 provides is a detailed distribution of the answers that 5,463 people wrote down themselves over two months in the summer of 2026.

An order for carrying the figures over

When moving figures from this survey into a document or a slide deck, following the order below avoids the mismatches seen above.

  1. Open the question wording first. Phrases that fix the time frame and the condition, such as "throughout your professional career", "recently", "during the past 12 months" and "beyond your control", determine what a figure means.
  2. Check how answers were selected. Whether a question is single-choice or multiple-choice, and whether it limits the number of selections, decides whether its percentages can be added together.
  3. Write the respondent count alongside. The count and its share of the whole survey appear under each chart. Attach the denominator, as in "62.48% of 5,352 respondents".
  4. Mark values that were added. For a value that combines several options, such as the 26.17% obtained from "Somewhat likely" and "Very likely", state which options were added.
  5. Use "respondents to this survey" as the subject. The overview writes "developers" in places, but the subject that fits the caveat on the About page is respondents.
  6. Carry over only the year-over-year statements the site makes in writing. Keep the caveat about the changing respondent pool attached to them.
  7. Keep the overview and the charts apart. Wording that appears only in the overview, such as "a quarter", "9 months" and "42%", is labeled as the overview's wording.

The general order for telling surveys, telemetry and experiments apart and for weighing the sample and the interests of whoever ran the study is in How to Read AI Usage Pattern Reports About Software Teams, and an example of reading the figures of a CSS survey from the same organizer together with its sample is in The Workarounds CSS Deleted.

In the conclusion of the results site, Devographics founder Sacha Greif writes that "there are some questions that a survey just can't answer", giving as examples whether programmers will still write code by hand five years from now and whether software engineering will even exist as a profession. The 26.17% from the question about having to change careers within five years is not a forecast answering those questions; it is the distribution of likelihoods selected by the people who answered this survey in the summer of 2026.

What could not be confirmed

  • The publication date of the results. The About page says the results were published on "September 25, 2025", a date earlier than the collection window (July to September 2026), so the actual publication date could not be pinned down.
  • Why the overview says "the last 9 months" while the chart option says "the past 12 months". The site gives no explanation.
  • Which tally the overview's 42% negative on AI comes from. The chart options add up to 39.33%.
  • The number of respondents living in South Korea. It is not among the individually listed items and is folded into "Other Answers", so the value is unknown.
  • The definition of decimal values such as "+0.59" shown beside chart items. What the value relates to cannot be read from the static page, so these values are not used in the body.
  • The official definition of the "+ 1" and "- 1" markers. Only the match with changes in rank against the 2025 edition in the residence chart was checked.
  • The figures behind the overview's statement that respondents who had experienced burnout were less happy and more likely to have ongoing health issues. The values of the corresponding cross-tabulated chart were not seen.
  • The name of the last option in the question on the share of AI-generated code. The static page shows it only as "8" (470 respondents, 9.7%).
  • The Worldview and Hobbies sections and the downloadable raw data. They were not opened.
  • Whether weighting or any adjustment was applied. The About page says nothing on the subject.
