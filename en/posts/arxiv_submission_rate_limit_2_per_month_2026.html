Overview — Enacting Limits of Two Monthly and Three Active Submissions

On October 1, 2026, arXiv instituted a comprehensive update to its submission rate limit policy affecting all submitters and categories across the repository. The core of this updated regulatory framework consists of two strict boundaries. First, each submitter is limited to a maximum of two submissions per calendar month. Second, any submitter may have at most three total active submissions under review or processing at any single point in time.

Since its founding by Paul Ginsparg in 1991, arXiv has served as the preeminent open-access preprint repository designed to facilitate rapid dissemination of scientific findings prior to formal peer-reviewed publication. While operational guidelines previously existed to restrict abusive submission volumes, those restrictions historically operated under the informal discretion of volunteer moderators rather than hard quantitative ceilings enforced uniformly by the submission infrastructure. When an individual account engaged in excessive volume, administrative interventions were conducted ex-post on a case-by-case basis. The policy enacted on October 1, 2026, represents a standardized hard limit applied across every research subject category without administrative distinction.

This structural change carries immediate operational ramifications across international academic departments. Research groups that routinely prepared batches of concurrent manuscripts to meet major conference deadlines, as well as teams engaged in modular reporting of specialized findings, now face concrete scheduling constraints. Navigating scholarly preprint dissemination now requires an exact understanding of how submission limits are measured, deducted, and reset across individual researcher profiles.

The scope of this regulatory update is universally inclusive. Authors publishing across physics, mathematics, computer science, quantitative biology, quantitative finance, statistics, electrical engineering and systems science, and economics are all governed by identical ceiling thresholds. Regardless of author seniority, institutional prestige, or subject matter complexity, every research team must structure preprint distribution strategies around calendar month boundaries and active queue balances.

Empirical Submission Surge — Doubling in Two Years and a Sixfold cs.AI Spike

The official announcement released by arXiv documented the precise empirical metrics that made quantitative rate limits necessary. According to published repository statistics, arXiv received 40,363 submissions in September 2026 alone. This monthly volume marks an all-time record in the history of the platform. Handling over 40,000 papers in a thirty-day window translates mathematically to processing an average intake exceeding 1,340 distinct manuscripts every single day.

Examining longitudinal repository data demonstrates the unprecedented acceleration of submission rates over recent cycles. In September 2016, total monthly intake stood at 9,869 submissions. Over the subsequent eight years, monthly intake climbed steadily to reach 20,569 submissions in September 2024. Expanding from 10,000 to 20,000 monthly papers required eight years of organic research community growth. However, between September 2024 and September 2026, submission volume doubled from 20,569 to 40,363 within a twenty-four-month window, representing a staggering 96% spike. A volume expansion that previously required decades occurred within two calendar years.

This massive intake surge extended far beyond digital server hosting requirements, creating severe human administrative bottlenecks. Published figures indicate that in September 2026 alone, staff and volunteer teams handled nearly 9,000 operational support tickets. Inquiries involving categorization appeals, verification flags, metadata adjustments, and technical formatting failures pushed volunteer review infrastructure and operational staffing past sustainable thresholds.

What Consumes the Quota and What Does Not — Rejections, Holds, and Deletions

To plan academic submissions without unexpected administrative blocks, authors must understand the precise criteria that govern monthly quota deductions. A common misconception among authors is that only accepted and announced preprints count against the monthly allowance. The actual operational policy operates under the opposite mechanism.

As clarified in the official policy FAQ, rate limiting is designed specifically to manage volunteer moderator time. Because verifying manuscripts consumes moderator attention regardless of whether a submission is ultimately approved, rejected papers consume quota. If an author submits two papers within a calendar month and both are subsequently rejected by moderators for failing scholarly standards, the author has exhausted their allowance for that month and cannot submit further manuscripts until the first day of the next calendar month.

To prevent procedural confusion during high-volume research cycles, authors should review the core operational tenets articulated in the policy documentation:

  • Immediate deduction on submission: Submitting a manuscript file deducts one unit from the monthly quota immediately upon completion, with no retroactive refund upon rejection.
  • Protection of human evaluation labor: Every manuscript that enters the pipeline demands moderator screening hours, validating quota consumption irrespective of editorial disposition.
  • Full quota restoration upon pre-announcement deletion: If an author voluntarily deletes an uploaded paper prior to formal announcement, both the monthly quota and active slot are restored.
  • Unified aggregation across disparate categories: Submitting across disparate disciplines, such as physics and computer science, counts against the singular user account quota without sector division.

Conversely, the policy provides a clear exemption for early corrections. If a submitter realizes after submission that a document contains critical errors or omissions and deletes the manuscript before it is formally announced to the public, that submission is excluded from both the monthly submission total and the active submission counter. This exemption applies regardless of author rationale, provided the deletion occurs prior to public posting.

When manuscripts are placed on hold for detailed moderation and remain unannounced when the calendar rolls into the next month, they do not reduce the new month quota of two papers. However, because their review remains unresolved, they continue occupying active submission slots. An author carrying two held submissions into a new month retains a monthly quota of two submissions, but can only submit one additional manuscript immediately before colliding with the concurrent active limit of three papers.

Submitter Quota Rather Than Author Quota — Multi-Author Project Strategies

A vital structural distinction in the revised policy is that restrictions attach strictly to the primary submitter account rather than to every co-author named in the manuscript metadata. In collaborative scientific research, this architectural choice creates meaningful procedural flexibility.

The official guidelines establish the following baseline principles:

  • When an author uploads and submits a paper, one unit of monthly quota is deducted solely from that submitter registered user account.
  • Other collaborating researchers listed in the manuscript metadata do not have their monthly allowance or active allowance deducted.
  • Account quotas and status tracking function as non-transferable individual account attributes.
  • Co-authors are expected to coordinate their respective submission workflows to balance intake demands.
  • Creating fictitious proxy user profiles or delegating submission to non-contributors violates repository terms of service.
  • Designated submitters must be authentic co-authors who contributed substantively to the underlying scientific investigation.

For example, if an academic research laboratory completes four distinct papers within a single calendar month, having the laboratory director submit all four manuscripts under a single account is impossible. The principal investigator account would hit the monthly ceiling upon the second submission. However, if four distinct co-authors each handle the submission of one manuscript through their individual verified accounts, all four projects can be processed simultaneously without violating policy.

Nevertheless, research teams must adhere to standard author responsibility guidelines. arXiv policies require that the individual acting as submitter must be a legitimate co-author who contributed materially to the work. Creating proxy user profiles or delegating submission to non-contributors to evade rate limits violates platform terms of service. Research groups must proactively assign submission responsibilities among genuine co-authors well in advance of conference deadlines.

Volunteer Moderation Systems and the Bottleneck Caused by AI-Generated Papers

The fundamental driver behind these quantitative constraints is a growing structural mismatch between automated manuscript generation and volunteer-driven scientific moderation. Unlike commercial publishing houses that fund operations through author article processing charges (APCs) or institutional subscriptions, arXiv operates as an open-access utility powered by volunteer subject-matter specialists.

Thomas Dietterich, Distinguished Professor Emeritus at Oregon State University and Chair of the arXiv Editorial Advisory Council, summarized the dilemma in the official announcement:

The heart of arXiv process for detecting and rejecting low-quality papers is our many volunteer moderators. We are so grateful that they donate their time and expertise every day. However, a relatively small proportion of authors are submitting a large number of low-quality papers and consuming a disproportionate fraction of the moderators time. This is unfair to authors who continue to submit quality papers — their papers can be delayed for days or weeks as a result.

Moderators report several specific categories of problematic submissions driving this administrative strain:

  • Thin papers of narrow scope: Manuscripts that present trivial, highly fragmented, or incremental technical variations lacking substantial scholarly consequence.
  • Salami papers: Single cohesive research investigations artificially divided into multiple disjointed micro-papers to inflate publication counts.
  • Dense, AI-written papers: Texts synthesized using advanced generative language models that present polished grammatical cadence while lacking rigorous experimental validation or scholarly value.
  • Uncontextualized raw benchmark tabulations: Manuscripts that merely report tabular outputs from open-source models without rigorous methodology, analytical depth, or interpretation.
  • Automated rephrasing and derivative submissions: Manuscripts generated by running existing literature through rephrasing utilities, creating redundant low-value submissions.

arXiv existing guidelines do not prohibit the ethical use of generative tools. Authors are permitted to utilize AI technologies to assist their research, provided that such usage is explicitly disclosed and the work meets standard criteria of scholarly interest and novelty. However, because generative tools reduce the friction of drafting technical prose to negligible levels, a tiny fraction of submitters flooded the platform with superficial manuscripts, creating severe backlog delays for legitimate research worldwide.

Evolution of Preprint Ecosystems — Transitioning from Honor Systems to Hard Quotas

The institution of formal monthly limits marks a major turning point in the history of open scientific communication. Since Paul Ginsparg established the platform in 1991 as an automated mailing distribution list for high-energy physics, arXiv operated on the implicit assumption that academic culture and the physical friction of research would naturally govern submission frequency.

Prior to advanced generative technologies, publishing an independent scientific preprint demanded extensive manual literature synthesis, mathematical derivation, experimental execution, and document preparation. The sheer cognitive and temporal cost of authoring acted as a natural rate limiter. Platforms required only lightweight anomaly detection to manage isolated boundary violations.

Generative AI completely inverted that operational dynamic. The marginal cost of producing seemingly competent technical prose collapsed toward zero, while the cognitive cost of human peer evaluation remained entirely unchanged. In an open-access environment without financial barriers, this asymmetry threatens systemic tragedy of the commons. Imposing a uniform hard quota of two papers per month represents an administrative containment mechanism to prevent total queue gridlock.

This structural transformation highlights critical realities confronting the wider scientific publishing architecture:

  • Extreme disparity between synthesis and verification: Producing plausible technical prose requires minimal effort, whereas establishing empirical truth requires sustained expert scrutiny.
  • Vulnerability of unpriced public commons: Absent financial friction, unconstrained automated submissions impose severe externalities on legitimate scientific contributors.
  • Necessity of structural rationing: When informal professional norms fail to curb excessive resource consumption, automated mechanical throttling becomes unavoidable.
  • Reevaluation of curation gateways: Preprint repositories originally designed for frictionless distribution are forced to construct protective filters to ensure basic utility.

This dynamic mirrors systemic issues documented across formal peer review networks (as detailed in What Peer Review Cannot Filter — What Academic Evaluation Guarantees and What It Does Not). Strain on volunteer reviewer capacity is no longer restricted to subscription journals; it directly impacts pre-publication open infrastructure.

Procedural Submission Checklist for Researchers

To avoid unexpected administrative barriers and scheduling disruptions prior to major conference submission deadlines, research teams should adopt standardized procedural verification workflows. The following checklist outlines key verification steps:

  • Account history audit: Verify whether the designated submitter account has completed any submissions during the current calendar month, taking into account that prior rejections count toward the quota.
  • Active slot audit: Count existing manuscripts currently pending moderation or held on review to ensure total concurrent active papers remain strictly below the cap of three.
  • Author allocation planning: In multi-paper research teams, identify distinct genuine co-authors to act as primary submitters for separate projects, ensuring distributed quota usage.
  • Pre-announcement defect review: If critical formatting or scientific errors are discovered immediately post-submission, trigger early deletion before formal public announcement to preserve quota.
  • Lead-time scheduling: Because moderation backlogs can introduce verification delays spanning days or weeks, schedule preprint uploads at least two to three weeks prior to conference target dates.
  • Consolidation of incremental outputs: Avoid fragmented micro-manuscripts by consolidating related findings into comprehensive, self-contained papers that minimize quota depletion.
  • Transparent disclosure of AI assistance: When generative language utilities are employed during manuscript preparation, provide clear methodological acknowledgments in accordance with submission guidelines.
  • Precise taxonomy selection: Select primary and secondary subject classifications rigorously to avoid moderation re-routing delays that cause prolonged active slot occupation.

Implementing disciplined internal submission tracking ensures that scientific work reaches the public domain smoothly while respecting shared repository boundaries.
