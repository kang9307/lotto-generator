The skeleton of the original - symptoms first, causes next

Most monitoring writing covers how to build a dashboard, and writing on how to read and interpret the graphs once built is rare. The original aims at that gap. Its subject is how to read graphs, diagnose and act. This article follows the flow of the original and rearranges what to look at, in what order and toward what conclusion into a single procedure.

The starting point of the original is a frame that groups metrics into four: the four golden signals from Google SRE, namely traffic, latency, errors and saturation. The key distinction that follows is between symptoms and causes. Latency and errors are symptoms that users experience, while CPU, memory, thread pools and connection pools are the state of the infrastructure, that is, candidate causes. The sentence of the original is that you must identify the symptoms first and narrow down the culprit through the causes.

  • Traffic. The volume of requests entering the system. It serves as the denominator for every metric that follows.
  • Latency. The time from request to response. The slowness users feel shows up here.
  • Errors. The share of failed requests. The failures users encounter show up here.
  • Saturation. How full the resources are. This is the side of the metrics that narrows candidate causes, and the CPU, pools and queues discussed later belong here.

This distinction carries over to alert design. The original says that putting alerts on arbitrary resource thresholds such as CPU 80 percent produces many false alarms, and that alerts should sit on symptoms, as when the error rate crosses a threshold, so that they match real user impact. That resource metrics lie when read alone is the shared premise of the sections that follow.

Traffic - how much is coming in

The first question is how much is coming in. A service without special events keeps a consistent traffic shape. The original says that learning that usual shape is the precondition for recognizing anomalies. The sentence that a metric at half of normal is wrong even if it is within the normal range is an example.

  • A vertical drop. A signal that requests are not reaching the servers. Suspect upstream problems such as the load balancer, DNS or the gateway. Counterintuitively, the server-side metrics are clean at that moment.
  • A vertical spike. A sudden rush. Candidates are a viral moment, crawler access or an attack, and if the spike repeats periodically, think of batch jobs or cron.
  • Traffic as the denominator. The same 500 errors mean something entirely different out of 1 million requests a day than out of 1,000. Read errors not as counts but as a ratio to traffic.

The procedure this section gives is simple. Before opening any other metric, check whether the shape of the request volume is the same as usual. If the volume has broken, postpone digging inside the servers.

Latency - what the average hides

The second question is how long it takes. The original says the average response time hides the shape of the distribution, because two servers with the same 100 ms average can have entirely different performance characteristics. That is why percentiles are used. P50 is the typical user experience, and P95 and P99 are used to set targets and catch early warnings. The original says anomalies almost always appear in P99 first.

  • Converting to scale. At 1,000 requests per second, P99 means 10 users per second receive a slow response, which is 864,000 a day. One percent is not a small number.
  • Calls multiply. In a service where drawing one screen uses more than 40 API calls, even if 99 percent of each call finishes within the target time, the probability that all 40 finish within target falls to about 67 percent.
  • Who owns the tail. Heavy users who have accumulated a lot of data naturally fall into the slow tail. The original puts it as P99 likely being the response time experienced by the best customers of the service.

The aggregation problem that arises when the same topic spans several servers is covered in the article on averaging the p99 of ten servers. This article follows the original only as far as the reason for reading P99.

Errors - fast failures and slow failures

The third question is how much fails. The original starts by splitting status codes. A 5xx is a server-side failure and is always a concern. A 4xx is an invalid request from the client, but it can also be a sign that the API contract has broken because an old app and a new server are out of version. The next distinction is the speed of failure.

  • Slow failures. The type that fails after waiting several seconds, like a timeout. It holds threads and connections while waiting, so errors and saturation appear together.
  • Fast failures. The type that fails within milliseconds, such as connection refused or a null reference from a dead dependency. Resource utilization stays perfectly healthy.
  • Survivor bias. There is a paradox in which the error rate soars while P99 actually looks better. It happens because requests that failed instantly mix into the samples as millisecond entries. Looking only at the latency graph at that moment gives false comfort.

The procedure in this section is to separate, when errors increase, whether the failures are fast or slow. If fast, look not at resource metrics but at whether the dependency is alive. If slow, go down to the resource and pool metrics of the next section.

The cheapest branch in this table is whether everything is slow or only part of it. The original says this one distinction halves the list of suspects. When several rows of the table fit at once, follow the hypothesis-driven investigation of the original. Instead of scanning twenty graphs, form one hypothesis and look at the one graph that would confirm or refute it. The original's example is that if the DB is slow, connection wait should be high. If confirmed, move to the next item of the symptom checklist, and if refuted, move to the next hypothesis. This cuts the time spent wandering through a dashboard without a purpose.

Resource metrics - never read CPU and memory alone

The sentence in the original is that resource metrics lie when read alone. CPU is always read overlaid on latency. The original gives three cases by the combination of the CPU pattern and latency.

Shape of CPULatencyDiagnosis in the original
Rising gentlyLow and stableHealthy scaling. Keep watching
Around 20 to 30 percentSoaringI/O wait. DB delay, locks, pool exhaustion, timeouts. Look at the blocked resource, not CPU
Flat against 100 percentHighCapacity exceeded or an infinite loop. If traffic is normal, review the code

The original says container environments hold one more trap that this table misses: throttling created by Kubernetes CPU limits. A container receives a CPU time quota every 100 ms period by default, which is 50 ms per period at a 0.5 core limit. Once the quota is used up the container is forcibly stopped until the next period. The CPU usage graph does not show the stopped time, so usage looks normal while only the P99 tail grows longer.

  • The normal signal, the sawtooth. In runtimes with a garbage collector such as the JVM or Node.js, memory rising and then dropping sharply at GC is a normal pattern like a heartbeat.
  • A leak shows in the floor. Look at the troughs, not the peaks. Flat troughs are normal, and troughs that creep up like stairs are a sign that objects the GC cannot reclaim remain each time.
  • A stair-step jump. A vertical jump above the sawtooth means a large amount of data was loaded into memory at once. Full table queries, large file processing and Excel downloads appear as examples. Switch to pagination or streaming before an OOM happens.

Pools and queues - the arithmetic of concurrency

One piece of arithmetic in the original is the center of this section. Little's law says the number of requests being processed at the same time equals the arrival rate per second multiplied by the average processing time. If 1,000 requests arrive per second and average processing takes 50 ms, there are 50 concurrent requests, and a pool of 200 threads is enough. But if the DB slows and the average becomes 2 seconds, concurrency becomes 1,000 times 2, which is 2,000, the pool runs dry and 1,800 requests queue up. The traffic is unchanged, yet the system collapses.

  • Cascading collapse. When every thread is waiting on the DB there is no thread left to handle new requests. Health checks time out, the load balancer removes the server, and the remaining servers take more traffic and fall faster. One slow query spreads into an outage of the whole cluster.
  • Retry storm. When clients resend failed requests, traffic grows two to three times and closes the window for recovery.
  • The gradient rather than the pool itself. Look at the gradient of thread pool usage rather than the absolute value. A gentle rise with traffic is normal, and a vertical rise means a delay elsewhere is creating the wait.
  • Event loop servers. Node.js and WebFlux do not keep a thread per request, and one execution thread runs the loop. If one place blocks, the entire process stops. The metric is event loop delay, read by maximum or P99 rather than average, and by trend against normal rather than an absolute standard. An average CPU of 40 percent across 8 processes can hide one at 100 percent, so look at the per-process maximum.

The same principle appears in the connection pool. The original's example is the HikariCP default of 10 connections against 200 application threads. When the DB slows, connection timeout errors appear. The wrong response is to enlarge the pool, and the right one is to ask why connections are returned slowly. The sentence in the original is that the cause is usually not the pool itself but a delay somewhere.

Bottlenecks, saturation, cache and timeouts - when two graphs disagree

The original offers a few last tools for when several metrics move together. The first is the shape of a bottleneck. Waiting piles up in front of the bottleneck, and behind it things are idle. The throughput of the system is set by its weakest link. The second is that the saturation curve is not a straight line. As utilization rises, waiting time grows nonlinearly.

UtilizationWaiting time (relative to baseline)Interpretation in the original
50%1xBaseline
80%4xIt gets steep from here
90%9xThinking there is still 10 percent left is dangerous
95%19xThe remaining 5 percent comes back as 19 times the waiting time
  • A queue is not free. A queue is healthy when it absorbs a temporary rush, but when arrivals keep exceeding throughput it becomes a debt of latency. With a throughput of 100 per second, 100 queued items mean the next request waits 1 more second, and 10,000 queued items mean 100 more seconds. Resources also get spent on requests the client has already given up on.
  • Backpressure and load shedding. A queue length that keeps rising without recovery is a sign of no flow control. If the queue rejects explicitly with a 429 when it overflows, instead of everyone slowing down a little, some fail fast and the rest are processed normally.
  • When the cache hit rate collapses. A hit rate of 97 percent means the DB receives only 3 percent of the traffic, and when the cache collapses the DB takes more than 30 times the load. Hundreds of requests going to the DB at once when a popular key's TTL expires is a cache stampede. First check whether the time of the DB load surge overlaps the time of the hit rate drop.
  • Timeout mismatch. If the gateway timeout is 3 seconds and the backend query timeout is 5 seconds, the user receives a 504 at 3 seconds while the backend quietly records a success at 5 seconds. A contradiction arises in which the backend metrics show no errors while the gateway shows 504s. The remedy is a timeout budget that shrinks going inward. The original's example is 3 seconds at the gateway, 2.5 seconds at the HTTP client and 2 seconds at the DB.

The diagnostic rule of this section boils down to one. If you see the contradiction of one layer showing errors while the next layer shows success, suspect timeout alignment first.

The purpose of a timeout budget is to make the inner layers fail cleanly before the outer layers do, so that errors propagate upward properly. If the outer layer gives up first, the inner layer keeps producing responses nobody will read and spends CPU and connections even during overload. The values of 3, 2.5 and 2 seconds are the original's example, so values for your own service have to be set by looking at the real response distribution, and the original gives no service-specific values.

Three time horizons - calm times, right after a deploy, during and after an incident

The original sums up that the same graph is read differently depending on when it is viewed. In calm times you learn the baseline, right after a deploy you are careful for 30 minutes, and during an incident recovery comes ahead of root cause analysis.

  • Five calm minutes. Go around the dashboard for 5 minutes a day. Once you know the traffic shape, the CPU rhythm and the normal P99 range, the problem meant by the sentence that without knowing the usual you cannot even recognize that something is off disappears. Set alerts to fire when the baseline is crossed. A change that worsens by 1 percent a day over 4 weeks never fires an alert, so a panel that overlays today's P99 on the same weekday last week is needed.
  • The 30 minutes after a deploy. Leave a marker for the deploy time on the graphs. The question of before or after the deploy then needs no other information, and the original says the return on cost is the greatest. A new process may see P99 temporarily double because JIT compilation is unfinished, the local cache is empty and the connection pool is being rebuilt. If it recovers over a few minutes it is warmup, and if it stays high for more than 10 minutes it is a regression.
  • Rollback criteria and canaries. Agree in advance on a rule such as rolling back without debate if the error rate after deploy exceeds X percent or P99 shows no recovery trend within 10 minutes. For a canary, deploy to one or two servers first and compare the canary and the stable version side by side under the same traffic conditions, split by version label.
  • During and after an incident. First settle the scope: the whole system, a specific API, or a region or customer. Then look for changes within the last 30 minutes to a few hours: deploys, configuration, feature flags, infrastructure work and marketing inflow. Recovery comes first, in the order of rollback, restart, scale out and turning a feature off with a flag, and the evidence, metrics, logs and heap dumps, remains after recovery. A postmortem draws a timeline from four moments, the first symptom, the alert, the start of the human response and recovery, and turns the gap in detection and the gap in response speed into action items. People are not written into the cause.

The summary of the procedure in this section is three lines. In calm times build the baseline, after a deploy separate warmup from regression by whether it recovers, and during an incident recover first and check one graph per hypothesis.

Six steps to apply to your own server

The six steps below carry the flow of the original into an order the reader can repeat on their own server. The basis for each step is in the earlier sections.

  1. Look at the shape of the request volume. Is it the same as usual, has it broken vertically, has it spiked? If it broke, look upstream before inside the servers.
  2. Read latency in percentiles. Look at P50, P95 and P99, not the average. Separate whether only a specific API is slow or everything is.
  3. Read errors as a ratio and separate their speed. Look at the ratio to traffic, not the count, and distinguish fast failures from slow ones.
  4. Overlay resource metrics on latency. If CPU is low and latency is high, suspect waiting, and if CPU is stuck flat, suspect capacity or a loop. In containers, put throttling on the candidate list too.
  5. Find where the waiting piles up. Look at pools, queues, the cache hit rate and timeout alignment. Waiting piles up in front of a bottleneck and things are idle behind it.
  6. Cross-check recent changes against the baseline. Put the deploy marker beside the same-weekday panel from last week, and if it does not recover, roll back by the criteria agreed in advance.

The concluding checklist of the original shortens these six steps to four questions: how much is coming in, how long does it take, how much fails, and what is full. When an alert sounds, stop and ask these four.
