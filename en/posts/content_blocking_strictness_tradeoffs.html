How the Web Changes When You Tighten the Blocker

What you gain and what breaks at each step up, how to narrow a fault down on your own, and where most people should stand

Aug 20, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: The Morning After You Tighten It

Last night you enabled four more filter lists and, while you were at it, blocked every script coming from another origin. Pages got noticeably faster and the screen got clean. This morning the login button on your bank site depresses and then nothing happens. The meeting link opens to a white page. Both worked yesterday.

This is where people split. Anyone who remembers what they changed last night finds the cause in five minutes. Anyone who does not concludes that the site is broken, and a few weeks later has twenty lines of exceptions, half of which nobody can explain. The real cost of raising strictness is not blocking performance; it is this maintenance bill.

Three things are covered here. What exactly gets caught and what breaks with each step up, in what order to narrow a fault down, and where it is rational to stand given your own conditions.

One disclosure first. The page you are reading is funded by ads. If you are reading it with a blocker on, this article earns nothing. That is written knowingly. So you will not find a lecture here telling you not to block, and you will not find a claim that advertising is evil either. The choice belongs to the reader, and what this article does is put the information that choice needs where you can see it. Section 7 lays out the publisher-side math without hiding it.

Why the blocking mechanism itself changed recently — the difference between intercepting requests and declaring rules up front, and the argument behind it — was covered in How Content Blocking Actually Works - The Browser Landscape After the Extension Permission Model Changed. This article sits on top of that and looks only at what happens when a user turns the dial.

1. A Blocker Really Does Three Different Things

The single phrase ad blocking covers three operations with entirely different characters. Get this distinction straight and you can name the guilty layer the moment something breaks.

1.1 It never sends the request

When the page tries to fetch a file from some address, the request never leaves. You save the data and the remote server does not learn you visited. It is the strongest layer and also the layer that can kill functionality. If what got stopped was an ad image, nothing happens; if it was a data request the page was waiting on, the screen stalls right there.

1.2 It removes what already arrived

The request went out and the response came back, but a particular region is made invisible. This is usually called cosmetic filtering. It is where the tidy layout comes from instead of an empty rectangle where the ad was. Of the three layers this one has the fewest side effects, because it never touches how the page behaves. A bad rule hides a paragraph you wanted, and you notice immediately.

1.3 It intervenes in the code the page runs

It blocks or alters what the page scripts try to do — neutralizing the function that opens a popup, or returning a different value to code that checks for a blocker. This is the most powerful of the three and the one that shatters most. The rest of the page is written assuming that code runs, so cutting one thread stops features that look unrelated.

This three-layer split is not one extension's quirk. When Apple introduced content blockers in WebKit, the action types it defined follow the same skeleton — stop the resource from loading, strip cookies from the request, hide elements by CSS selector. And that design write-up names as a core benefit that the extension cannot see the URLs of pages and resources. Blocking power and visibility always move together, and it shows here too.

2. Tracking and Advertising Are Not the Same Problem

Blurring the two muddies the discussion and, worse, pushes you to a stricter level than you need. Keeping them apart pays off practically.

  • Contextual advertising — placed to match the subject of the page, cookware next to a recipe. It works without knowing who the visitor is, so it needs no personal data
  • Behavioral tracking — stitching one person's movements across many sites into a profile. It is used to sell advertising, but it is not logically bound to advertising
  • Tracking that is not advertising — analytics tags, social share widgets, tools that record how you move through a page. These sit on sites that carry no advertising at all

There is a frequently cited data point on whether advertising without tracking actually works. The Dutch public broadcaster NPO dropped tracking-based targeting in early 2020 for contextual placement, and its ad revenue rose year over year for six consecutive months. Generalizing that number as it stands would be wrong, though. Critics who examined the same case point out that after consent prompts were introduced, the large majority of NPO's users had not opted in, so it was effectively serving targeted ads to only a small slice of its audience. The starting line was low. It is good evidence that contextual advertising can work, and weak evidence that it works better everywhere.

One more thing. The expectation that cross-site tracking is about to disappear on its own is not currently accurate. On 22 April 2025 Google said Chrome would continue to support third-party cookies and would not ship a separate standalone prompt for them. This is not a matter that waiting will resolve; it is a matter each person handles in settings.

The practical conclusion is simple. If blocking tracking is the goal, privacy lists plus the browser's built-in protections cover most of it. Disliking tracking is not by itself a reason to go all the way to hard mode. What the levels above buy is something else.

3. The Strictness Spectrum - What Each Step Buys and Costs

The levels and their names follow the blocking mode document in the uBlock Origin wiki, which calls them default, medium and hard. One list-adding step is inserted in front here, to match the order in which the dial actually turns.

LevelWhat decides a blockWhat you gainBreakage rateMaintenance
0. DefaultKnown advertising listsAds gone, pages lighterRareAlmost none
1. Extra listsPrivacy, malicious URL, annoyance listsThe bulk of cross-site tracking removedOccasional embeds and widgetsAn exception every few months
2. Medium modeAll third-party scripts and frames, globallyCatches things you have never heard ofSomething fails on many sitesWeekly touch-ups
3. Hard modeEvery third-party requestThird-party profiling structurally foiledNew sites usually need fixingA judgement call per visit

There is a character break between levels 0-1 and levels 2-3. The first two are decided by lists; the last two are decided by origin. List-based blocking only catches what is known, but the web mostly keeps working. Origin-based blocking catches things without knowing their names, and takes down what the page needed along with them. The point where the dial stiffens is exactly between level 1 and level 2.

The wiki says of hard mode that it ensures profiling by third parties is completely foiled, at the expense of having to fix most websites. That is a warning written by the people who built the tool, not marketing copy. There is a reassuring passage too: stepping back down is one cell wide. A single no-op rule on the third-party cell demotes hard mode to medium, and the same treatment on the scripts and frames cells demotes medium to default. Strictness is a reversible setting, not an irreversible commitment.

4. What Concretely Breaks as You Go Up

The phrase the site is broken is too blunt. In reality the failures land in a fixed set of places, with predictable symptoms.

What breaksSymptomGuilty layerUsually from
External account sign-inButton clicks, nothing happens; a popup opens blankThird-party scripts and framesLevel 2
Payment entry frameThe card number field never appears; verification stallsThird-party framesLevel 2
Map and video embedsA grey box or an empty slotFrames and scriptsLevels 1-2
Comment and support widgetsThe whole section is absentFramesLevel 2
Cart and stock lookupsThe click registers but no number changesBlocked data requestLevels 2-3

News site article countersThe article never opens, or the notice will not closeScript interventionLevel 2

Two rows deserve to be pulled out.

Payments behave differently from everything else. Other faults are fixed by a reload; a payment cut in the middle leaves state behind. The authorization can succeed while the browser never returns to the order screen, leaving you unable to tell what happened. So in front of a payment screen it is better to switch the blocker off for that moment than to engineer a precise exception. There is no reason to turn a few seconds of judgement into a permanent rule.

A misbehaving article counter on a news site is not a result this article recommends. Raising strictness does not open paid content; far more often the opposite happens and the article itself will not load, or a notice refuses to close. And methods for reaching paid content without paying for it are not covered here. That is not a strictness question, it is a different question.

Finally, the most annoying property of all this in practice. Half of these failures are silent. No error message, no red line in the console, simply nothing happens. That is what makes it hard to connect the fault to the setting you changed yesterday, which is what the next section is for.

5. Diagnosing a Break and Writing Narrow Exceptions

5.1 Turn off the outer layers first

Blocking usually sits in several layers at once. Browser built-in protection, the blocking extension, DNS blocking on the device or router, and a corporate network filter can all be live together. Poke at a random one and you wander with four suspects. Fixing the order removes that cost.

  1. Reproduce the action in a window where extensions do not run. If it works there, the cause is inside the extension settings. If it still fails, it is outside the extension
  2. If it was outside, suspect DNS settings and the network. Open the same page on a different connection or on mobile data and compare
  3. If it was inside, disable the blocker for that site only and check
  4. Then drop the strictness by one step. Medium becomes default, hard becomes medium
  5. Roll back the filter lists you added recently, one at a time. Turn off several at once and you will never learn which one it was

5.2 Open the logger and reproduce the failure

The blocker's logger shows the requests that passed through that tab and which rule caught each one. The procedure is short. Open the logger, reload the problem page, perform the failing action, and read the lines marked as blocked. It is several times faster than opening domains one by one on a hunch, and more importantly it points you at exactly one thing to allow.

The domain name you get here becomes the raw material for the exception. Skip the logger and your exceptions get wide, for the reason in the next section.

5.3 Keep the exception as narrow as you can

When something breaks, most people whitelist the whole site. It is fast and it is expensive. The blocker is effectively absent on that site from then on, and a few months later the length of the allow list is your real strictness. Hard mode with frequent wide exceptions can be genuinely leakier than default mode with none.

Work in this order.

  • Allow that one domain on that one site. If the logger identified it, this usually ends it
  • If not, allow third-party scripts only on that site. Leave frames and everything else blocked
  • If that still fails, drop the strictness by one step for that site alone
  • Whitelisting the whole site is last, and should be treated as temporary

And when you add an exception, write down one line about why. Use a comment if your rules support them, a plain note if not. Exception lists are the kind of list nobody ever prunes, and without a stated reason nobody ever can.

6. When the Extension Itself Becomes the Risk

A blocker must in principle be able to act on every page, which places it in the broadest permission class the browser has. Here is the part that gets discussed less — that permission transfers with ownership of the extension. Users install based on a developer, but updates ship from whoever owns it at the time.

Two cases are documented in public reporting.

  • Nano Adblocker / Nano Defender — October 2020. After the original developer transferred the rights, the new maintainer was reported to have shipped an update containing code that sent user data to a remote server. Chromium-build installs numbered more than 300,000 at the time, and the extensions were pulled from the store
  • The Great Suspender — sold in June 2020, malicious code reported that November, removed from the store in February 2021 and disabled on machines where it was already installed. Not a blocker, but structurally the same event: ownership transfer of a broadly permissioned extension

What both share is that nothing was wrong on the day of installation. There is no basis for assuming the judgement you made at install time stays valid. The practical rules that follow are few.

  • Run exactly one blocker. Two or more interfere with each other's results, making diagnosis effectively impossible while multiplying the number of parties holding permissions
  • Watch for similarly named copies. The more widely used a blocker is, the more separate extensions with near-identical names surround it
  • Prefer ones whose source is public and whose maintainers are identifiable. When ownership moves, that community is usually where it surfaces first
  • Sweep the extension list twice a year and delete what you no longer use. Abandoned extensions are the dangerous ones — nobody is watching, and the permissions are still there
  • Be suspicious of permissions that are wide relative to the function. A tool used on one site rarely needs to read and change data on all sites

The WebKit-style declarative model mentioned at the end of section 1 is a design aimed squarely at this risk. The extension hands over rules and the browser decides, so the extension never sees the addresses. In exchange, the logger and any judgement that depends on context become impossible. Power and visibility shrink together, and which side is better depends on what you are afraid of.

7. The Publisher-Side Math - What Fills the Space Ads Leave

As stated in the introduction, this page runs on advertising. So this section is written by an interested party, and saying so seemed better than not. On that footing, here are the facts.

When ad revenue goes away, what was being made does not simply continue. It moves to other revenue paths, each with its own side effects.

  • Subscriptions — there is a real ceiling on how many one person can hold. Large outlets fill that ceiling first, so smaller sites are squeezed out first
  • Donations — a minority carries the majority's share. Continuity becomes tied to a few people's circumstances, and those few people's tastes reach the editorial line easily
  • Sponsorship and native placements — the advertiser moves closer to the editing. Disclosure obligations attach, but it remains harder to tell apart from the body text than a banner is
  • Affiliate links — revenue only appears if the reader buys, so recommendations tilt toward things worth buying. A conclusion of do not buy this becomes harder to reach
  • Paywalls — it means leaving the open web. Not in search results, not shared by link

So the space where advertising was does not automatically fill with something cleaner. It fills with something more commercial, or more closed, or simply less of it. Usually one of those three arrives.

The other side is equally factual. Ad slots have served as a distribution path for malicious code, and a user's position that they never consented to behavioral tracking is legitimate. And the NPO case from section 2 remains valid as evidence that advertising can work with the tracking stripped out. It is a place where advertising itself was arguably not the problem — what came attached to it was.

So the conclusion here is not do not block. It is that this is not all-or-nothing. Allowing ads on the handful of places you read weekly while blocking everywhere else is entirely feasible technically, and plenty of people run exactly that. There is no requirement to apply one strictness everywhere.

8. Mobile Offers a Different Set of Choices

Trying to carry your desktop strictness onto a phone usually hits a wall, because the layer structure itself differs.

  • Declarative blockers — rule bundles registered through an app. Light and easy on the battery, but there is no logger. When something breaks you have no way to see which rule did it, so turning the whole bundle off is often the only lever available
  • Mobile browsers that support extensions — you get tools close to the desktop ones. In exchange your browser choice narrows and the supported extension catalogue is smaller than on desktop
  • Device-wide DNS blocking — its great advantage is that it covers apps outside the browser. Its cost is that the unit of judgement is only a hostname, so when one domain serves both what you need and what you do not, there is no way to split them. Block it and the feature dies, allow it and nothing is blocked

There is one hazard peculiar to mobile. The things that break are the urgent ones. Paying at a counter, verifying identity at a gate, checking a delivery. No diagnostic tools and no time. So the practical advice is to keep mobile one step below your desktop level. You give up little, and not stalling when it is urgent is worth a lot.

How far you can strip things at the device level and what you give up in return is covered more broadly in The Real Cost of a De-Googled Phone - What You Actually Give Up in Daily Use 2026.

9. Where Most People Should Stand

Strictness is not a performance grade, it is a maintenance contract. Choose level 3 without weekly time to spend on it and exceptions accumulate until your effective strictness has sunk below level 1. Running level 1 precisely gives a better result than that.

If this is youSuggested levelWhy
You do not want to spend time on settingsLevel 0Most of the felt benefit arrives here
Tracking bothers youLevel 1Cross-site tracking is mostly caught at the list layer
Work sends you to unfamiliar sites all dayLevels 0-1Predictability matters more than strictness
Data allowance and battery are the pointLevel 1The request layer already delivers that
You are curious and have time to tinkerLevel 2Reversal is one cell wide, so experiments are cheap
You have something specific to protectLevels 2-3But in a profile separate from daily use

Three rules hold regardless of level.

  • Split your profiles. Keeping a daily profile loose and tightening a separate one is steadier than raising and lowering strictness in a single browser. It also lets you narrow a fault to a profile
  • Keep account and payment sites out of the experiment. Banks, government services, the shops you use often. What you can lose there outweighs what stricter blocking buys
  • Log every change. A list enabled, a rule added — one line with the date and the reason. This one habit removes most of your future diagnosis time

Conclusion: You Are Choosing a Maintenance Bill, Not a Strictness

Arguments about blocking strictness usually go wrong because the question is posed badly. Ask how much can be blocked and the answer is always hard mode. Ask what you want to block and how many minutes a week you can pay for it, and the answer differs by person, which is the accurate question.

Three things to keep.

  • Decide the goal first. If tracking is the goal, the list layer nearly finishes the job. What you buy above that is something else
  • Breakage is predictable. The failure sites are a known set, and outside-in plus the logger usually takes minutes. Exceptions as narrow as possible, with the reason written down
  • There is no need to apply one level everywhere. Open the places you read often, block the rest. Mobile one step lower, account and payment sites outside the experiment

And one last note on what this article does not cover. Methods for reaching paid content without paying, or for defeating blocker detection, are not here. Those are not questions about the strictness dial, and they are outside this article's scope.
