Complete Guide to Hair Loss: Causes, Treatments & Management

From male, female, and alopecia areata to minoxidil, finasteride, dutasteride, and hair transplants — an evidence-based overview

Introduction: Why Understanding Hair Loss Matters

Hair loss affects hundreds of millions of people worldwide. Androgenetic alopecia alone accounts for approximately 95% of all hair loss cases, making it one of the most prevalent chronic conditions in dermatology. Yet despite its prevalence, many people delay evidence-based treatment in favour of unverified folk remedies, often allowing the condition to progress further than necessary.

Hair loss is not merely a cosmetic concern. Research published in Mayo Clinic Proceedings has documented significant psychological distress and reduced quality of life associated with hair loss in both men and women. Early intervention matters: the sooner appropriate treatment begins, the more hair follicles can be preserved.

This guide draws on sources including NIH StatPearls, peer-reviewed dermatology journals, and Mayo Clinic data to explain the major types of hair loss, their causes, how they are diagnosed, what treatments have the strongest evidence, and which popular beliefs lack scientific backing. All treatment decisions should be made in consultation with a qualified medical professional.

1. Types of Hair Loss

Understanding the type of hair loss is the essential first step, as each has different causes and treatment approaches.

1.1 Male Pattern Hair Loss (Androgenetic Alopecia in Men)

The most common form of hair loss in men, male pattern baldness is driven by a genetic predisposition combined with the action of DHT (dihydrotestosterone). DHT is converted from testosterone by the enzyme 5-alpha reductase and causes genetically susceptible follicles to progressively miniaturise. The classic pattern follows the Hamilton-Norwood scale (Stages I–VII), typically beginning with a receding hairline or thinning at the crown.

1.2 Female Pattern Hair Loss (FPHL)

Female pattern hair loss differs from its male counterpart in several important ways. According to NIH research, serum androgen levels are normal in most women with FPHL, suggesting increased follicular sensitivity to androgens rather than elevated hormone levels. The frontal hairline is usually preserved, with diffuse thinning at the crown and a widening centre part (Ludwig Scale I–III). Associated conditions such as thyroid dysfunction, iron-deficiency anaemia, and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) must be ruled out.

1.3 Alopecia Areata

Alopecia areata is an autoimmune condition in which immune cells attack hair follicles. It typically presents as well-defined, coin-sized patches of sudden hair loss on the scalp, though it can progress to total scalp hair loss (alopecia totalis) or complete body hair loss (alopecia universalis). Comorbid autoimmune thyroid disease is reported in 5–10% of cases. Spontaneous regrowth occurs in some patients, but widespread or recurrent disease requires active treatment.

2. Causes of Hair Loss

Hair loss is multifactorial. The main contributing factors include:

2.1 Genetics

Genetic predisposition is the strongest risk factor for androgenetic alopecia. Multiple genes — including the androgen receptor (AR) gene — influence follicular DHT sensitivity. A family history of hair loss on either side increases individual risk, but genetics alone do not determine outcome.

2.2 Hormonal Factors

DHT is the primary hormonal driver of male pattern baldness. In women, post-menopausal oestrogen decline, postpartum hormonal shifts, and androgen excess from PCOS can trigger or accelerate hair loss. Thyroid hormone imbalance (both hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism) can cause diffuse hair shedding.

2.3 Nutritional Deficiencies

Deficiencies in iron, zinc, vitamin D, and biotin have been linked to hair loss. Iron deficiency is a common cause of telogen effluvium in women. Supplementation in the absence of a confirmed deficiency, however, does not have robust evidence for treating androgenetic alopecia.

2.4 Stress and Telogen Effluvium

Significant physical or psychological stressors — major surgery, high fever, childbirth, extreme dieting — can push a large proportion of follicles into the resting (telogen) phase simultaneously, causing diffuse shedding weeks to months later. This condition, called telogen effluvium, is generally reversible once the triggering cause is resolved.

2.5 Medications

Chemotherapy agents, anticoagulants (warfarin), certain antihypertensives, high-dose vitamin A, and systemic steroids are among the medications associated with hair loss. Any new or worsening hair loss following a medication change should be discussed with the prescribing physician.

3. Diagnosis

Accurate diagnosis by a dermatologist is essential before starting treatment. Self-diagnosis risks selecting the wrong treatment for the wrong type of hair loss.

3.1 Clinical Examination and Dermoscopy

A dermatologist will assess the pattern and extent of hair loss, rate of progression, family history, and current medications. Dermoscopy (trichoscopy) allows detailed visualisation of follicular structure. Scalp biopsy may be needed to confirm alopecia areata or rule out scarring alopecias.

3.2 Blood Tests

For women with diffuse hair loss or when an underlying cause is suspected, laboratory work typically includes TSH (thyroid function), serum ferritin, complete blood count, and hormone panels (LH, FSH, androgens). Treating an underlying condition takes priority over hair loss-specific medication.

4. Evidence-Based Treatments

The following treatments have the strongest clinical evidence base. All medications require medical consultation and, where applicable, a prescription.

4.1 Minoxidil

Originally developed as an antihypertensive, minoxidil was found to promote hair growth by increasing scalp blood flow and prolonging the anagen (growth) phase. It is FDA-approved for androgenetic alopecia in both men (2% and 5% topical) and women (2% topical). A 2024 meta-analysis indicated that 5 mg oral minoxidil produces comparable results to 5% topical minoxidil in men.

  • Efficacy: Approximately 60% of users experience slowing of hair loss and some regrowth. Results require consistent use for at least 3–6 months.
  • Caution: Effects reverse upon discontinuation. Initial temporary shedding is common during the first few weeks. Oral minoxidil requires a physician's prescription.

4.2 Finasteride

Finasteride 1 mg/day is an FDA-approved oral 5-alpha reductase inhibitor for male androgenetic alopecia. It reduces scalp and serum DHT by approximately 64–68%, slowing hair loss progression and promoting regrowth in most users.

  • Efficacy: Clinical studies report halting of hair loss in ~80–90% and visible regrowth in ~65% of men.
  • Caution: Approved for men only. Women of childbearing potential must avoid contact with crushed tablets due to risk of foetal abnormalities. Side effects including decreased libido and sexual dysfunction are reported in a minority of users. Discuss risks and benefits thoroughly with a physician before starting.

4.3 Dutasteride

Dutasteride 0.5 mg/day inhibits both type 1 and type 2 5-alpha reductase isoenzymes, suppressing DHT more completely than finasteride. Some studies have shown faster hair growth compared to finasteride, and it is approved for androgenetic alopecia in South Korea and Japan.

  • Caution: Similar side-effect profile to finasteride. Lowers PSA levels, which must be communicated to physicians overseeing prostate health screening.

4.4 Alopecia Areata Treatments

  • Topical and intralesional corticosteroids: First-line options for mild-to-moderate alopecia areata.
  • JAK inhibitors: Baricitinib and ritlecitinib received FDA approval (2022–2023) for severe alopecia areata, demonstrating significant hair regrowth in clinical trials. High relapse rates upon discontinuation mean long-term therapy is often required.
  • Contact immunotherapy (DPCP): Used for chronic extensive cases.

4.5 Hair Transplantation

For patients with insufficient response to medication or extensive hair loss, surgical transplantation is an option. DHT-resistant follicles from the occipital donor area are relocated to thinning regions.

  • FUE (Follicular Unit Extraction): Individual follicular units harvested without linear scarring. Preferred in most modern clinics.
  • FUT (Follicular Unit Transplantation): Strip excision method; lower cost but leaves a linear scar.

Graft survival rates of 90–95% are reported in healthy individuals. Results are generally permanent, but concurrent medical therapy is recommended to protect existing non-transplanted hair.

4.6 Low-Level Laser Therapy (LLLT)

FDA-cleared as an adjunctive device for androgenetic alopecia. Evidence supports modest hair density improvement, particularly when combined with pharmacological treatment.

5. Lifestyle and Hair Care

  • Maintain a balanced diet with adequate protein, iron, and micronutrients. Avoid extreme calorie restriction.
  • Minimise physical trauma to hair: avoid tight hairstyles (traction alopecia), excessive heat styling, and repeated chemical treatments.
  • Treat scalp conditions such as seborrhoeic dermatitis that may compromise follicular health.
  • Smoking has been associated with reduced scalp circulation and oxidative stress — a reason to quit beyond hair health.

6. Myths and Unproven Remedies

The hair loss market is saturated with products and methods that lack robust clinical evidence. Be cautious of the following:

6.1 Black Bean Juice, Ginger, Seaweed

While a balanced diet supports general health, there is currently insufficient clinical evidence that these foods alone can halt or reverse progressive androgenetic alopecia. Experts caution against using them as primary treatments.

6.2 Aggressive Scalp Massage

Evidence is currently insufficient to support scalp massage alone as a treatment for androgenetic alopecia. Overly vigorous massage may cause microtrauma to follicles. Applying evidence-based topical medication is more productive than repeated massage.

6.3 Anti-Hair-Loss Shampoos

Some shampoos may support scalp health or reduce shedding related to scalp conditions, but no shampoo has been proven to reverse androgenetic alopecia. Treating shampoos should complement, not replace, pharmacological or surgical intervention.

6.4 The Real Risk of Delaying Evidence-Based Treatment

The greatest danger of folk remedies is the delay in starting proven treatment. Androgenetic alopecia is progressive, and each follicle that miniaturises beyond the point of recovery cannot be restored by medication — only by transplant. Starting effective treatment early maximises the number of salvageable follicles.

7. Conclusion: Act Early, Act on Evidence

Hair loss is a manageable condition when approached systematically. Key takeaways:

  • Diagnose early: See a dermatologist at the first signs of unusual shedding or pattern changes. The type of hair loss determines the treatment.
  • Use evidence-based therapies: Minoxidil, finasteride, dutasteride, JAK inhibitors for alopecia areata, and hair transplantation have the strongest evidence base.
  • Be consistent: Most pharmacological treatments require months to show results and continued use to maintain them.

The information in this guide is provided for general educational purposes. Individual outcomes vary. Always consult a qualified dermatologist before starting, changing, or stopping any treatment for hair loss.
