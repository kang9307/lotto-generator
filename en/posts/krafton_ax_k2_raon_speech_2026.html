Introduction: the transcript always breaks in the same place

Anyone who has run a recorded meeting through automatic transcription recognizes the pattern in the output. Plain Korean sentences come out surprisingly clean. Then the transcript hits a sentence that means please merge this PR and cut a rollback branch, where the English technical terms sit inside Korean grammar, and everything goes sideways. Merge turns into something else entirely, rollback disappears, and if you are unlucky the whole clause vanishes.

The recognizer is not weak at Korean. It collapses where the language changes inside a single sentence. And in Korean engineering and business speech, that boundary gets crossed hundreds of times a day.

That is the target KRAFTON aimed at with A.X K2 Raon-Speech-21B-A3B. It is a Korean-English bilingual speech model built by stacking a self-trained speech encoder and speech codec on top of SK Telecom's A.X K2 Light 20B-A3B text backbone. Roughly 21.2B total parameters, roughly 3.5B active.

Here is the judgment up front. The value of this release is not the leaderboard position. It is that a Korean speech model with language-boundary handling as an explicit design goal now exists in open form. And that value is currently redeemable only in research and evaluation, because of one line in the license: CC BY-NC 4.0, non-commercial.

This article works through the boundary problem first, then looks at what the model assembled and from where, what a 21.2B parameter count actually costs in a speech workload, and which jobs justify reaching for this versus staying on a commercial API. How to measure the local text-to-speech models that already exist is a separate question, covered in the open-source benchmark that compares 55 local TTS models through three lenses. This piece is not about measurement. It is about the design of a newly assembled thing.

1. The break happens at the boundary, not in the language

Saying that Korean speech processing is hard is too blunt to act on. What is actually hard is a short list of specific boundaries.

  • Language boundary. Korean and English alternate inside one sentence, usually at the granularity of a single word
  • Morpheme boundary. Korean is agglutinative, so particles and endings chain onto stems. Whitespace units and meaning units do not line up
  • Orthography boundary. Whether a given sound gets written in Hangul or in Latin script is a matter of house style
  • Register boundary. One sentence-final ending changes the formality of the whole utterance, and the two main polite registers differ in sound and in usage

The first one is the expensive one. HiKE, the benchmark built to measure Korean-English mixing, labels code-switching at three levels: word, phrase, and sentence. It splits them because the difficulty is different at each level. Sentence-level mixing can be handled by cutting the audio and processing each half; word-level mixing has nowhere to cut.

The conclusion the paper reports is blunt. Most multilingual speech recognition models do not perform adequately on code-switched speech out of the box, and that capability has to be enabled through fine-tuning with synthetic code-switching data. In other words, training on many languages does not automatically produce competence at hearing two of them braided together. Knowing several languages separately and following two in one breath are different skills.

1.1 Agglutination blurs the boundary further

The moment a Korean ending attaches to an English stem, the problem gains another layer. Forms meaning merged and then, although I deployed it, or have to roll back put two languages inside one orthographic word. Separating the languages there requires going below the word level, and at that granularity a language identifier is useless.

For a pair like English and Spanish, with closer typology and comparable morphology, this would be a much shallower problem. Korean and English are far apart in word order, morphology, and phonology. That is why data for the pair is scarce, and why a model built specifically for it has a reason to exist.

2. Loanwords and code-switching are different problems

Conflate these two and evaluation falls apart before modeling even starts. When someone says the Korean equivalent of I wrote it in React, what is that word?

TypeExampleWhy the expected output splits
Established loanwordcomputer, bus, projectThe Hangul spelling is standard. Latin script looks wrong
Technical proper nounReact, Kubernetes, PostgreSQLHouse style is Latin script. In Hangul it stops being searchable
Unsettled middle groundDocker, SlackSpelling varies inside the same organization
Genuine code-switchingthis one is deprecatedWriting the English word in English is close to the only correct answer
Mixed word formmerged-and, committed-itThe spelling rule conflicts inside a single word

HiKE keeps loanword labels separate from its code-switching level labels precisely because the first and fourth rows must not be judged by the same yardstick. Writing an established loanword in Latin script and mangling a genuine English word into Hangul are completely different failures, yet a single word error rate flattens both into one point lost.

A practical judgment falls out of this. When choosing a recognizer for a mixed Korean-English environment, a single published error rate tells you close to nothing. It is faster to build a small evaluation set that treats your own organization's spelling conventions as ground truth. Thirty minutes of meeting audio is enough, and it already contains every term your team actually uses.

3. Numbers, units, and politeness levels

Text normalization hurts in different ways depending on which direction you are going.

Going from audio to text, one sound maps to several valid spellings. A spoken date can be written as 2026-08-05, as August 5, 2026, or spelled out in words. Which one is right depends on where the text is headed. If it feeds a calendar it has to be digits; if it is a subtitle, spelling it as spoken may be better.

Going from text to audio the arrow reverses: one spelling maps to several valid readings. Korean has two numeral systems, native and Sino-Korean, and the counter that follows decides which one applies.

  • 3:20 reads with a native numeral for the hour and a Sino-Korean numeral for the minutes, in the same breath
  • Three items takes the native numeral; item number three takes the Sino-Korean one. Same digit, different reading
  • 1,200 won reads as a quantity; the digits 1200 in a phone number read one by one
  • 3.5B reads as three point five B or as a billions figure, and without context you cannot decide
  • 80GB is read as gigabytes by some teams and as the letter pair by others

Politeness is another layer entirely. In recognition, dropping one syllable at the end of a sentence changes its register. In synthesis, the same sentence read in the formal register and in the polite conversational register has a different intonation contour. In English text-to-speech, politeness lives mostly in word choice. In Korean it is wired into grammar and prosody at once.

These items matter because they are the kind of problem that scale does not fix. Multiply the parameters by ten and the right way to read 3.5B is still a decision that lives inside your organization. Scale solves pulling language out of sound. This category is the convention layer that comes after.

4. Why one bilingual model differs from two stitched together

This is the core of the article. Why not take the best Korean model and the best English model and put a language identifier in front? The answer is not that it fails outright. It is that the switching granularity is far too coarse to follow real speech.

The last two rows are the honest cost. And this release left that cost visible in its own numbers. In the model card's aggregate table, A.X K2 Raon-Speech ranks first in Korean at 0.72 while placing third in English at 0.75. A model listed simply as Raon-Speech sits in the same table at 0.70 Korean and 0.79 English, taking first place in English.

The card does not state the lineage between the two, so no firm claim can be made about how they relate. But the contrast in the table is unmistakable. Korean went up by 0.02 in the same place English went down by 0.04. That is the price of bilingual integration, and it is the row you should always look for when choosing a multilingual model. Read only the phrase about ranking first and you will adopt it without knowing what you give up if your workload leans English.

4.1 A pipeline loses more than accuracy

Laying out spoken dialogue as voice activity detection, then speech recognition, then a text dialogue model, then speech synthesis is the long-standing approach and still powers many products. The Moshi paper names two problems with it. One is a latency of several seconds between interactions. The other is that passing through text discards non-linguistic information that modifies meaning, such as emotion or non-speech sounds.

The second matters more than it first appears. A single-syllable acknowledgment can be agreement, a request for repetition, or resignation depending entirely on intonation. Once it lands as text, that information is gone and no downstream component can recover it. The case for going straight from speech to speech rests here more than on accuracy.

5. What A.X K2 Raon-Speech actually assembled

This model was not built from scratch end to end. It is an assembly that mixes borrowed parts with self-trained ones, and the model card draws that line clearly.

ComponentSpecificationOrigin

If the training-hours figure has no anchor for you, there is one available. The Whisper paper reports training on 680,000 hours of multilingual and multitask weakly supervised data, transferring to many benchmarks without fine-tuning. The roughly 1 million hours behind this encoder is a larger number, but it is not a comparable one. The training objective and data composition differ, and above all this encoder was aimed at two specific languages from the start. Hours are a hint about scale, not a proof of quality.

5.1 What the codec is actually for

In a speech language model, the codec turns sound into discrete tokens the model can handle. It does for audio what a tokenizer does for text. Mimi, the reference design, compresses 24 kHz audio down to 12.5 representations per second at 1.1 kbps in a fully streaming manner, with a latency of 80 ms equal to the frame size.

That is where the frame rate reveals its importance. The frame period is the floor on response latency. At 12.5 frames per second, one frame is 80 ms, and no amount of faster hardware gets you underneath it. Raise the frame rate and latency drops, but the language model now has proportionally more tokens to chew through. The most naked trade in speech model design lives right here.

6. 21.2B total, 3.5B active - the trade you are making

The two numbers have to be read together. Roughly 21.2B total, roughly 3.5B active. In the mixture-of-experts layout, 8 of 128 experts fire per token.

What that structure gives and withholds is cleanly separable.

  • Gives. Arithmetic in the 3.5B class. Per-token processing time far below a dense 21.2B model
  • Gives. Knowledge capacity from the full 21.2B. Different experts can hold different things
  • Withholds. Memory scales with total size. You cannot know which expert fires, so all of them stay resident
  • Withholds. Batching efficiency is messier than a dense model, since different requests activate different experts

The model card's runtime requirements show this directly. BF16 weights come to about 42.4 GB, so an 80 GB GPU or a multi-GPU setup is required, and the vLLM-Omni path is documented as needing roughly 96 GB of VRAM on a single GPU. The accompanying conditions are tensor parallelism of 1, a maximum model length of 4,096, and 4,096 max batch tokens.

Put plainly: you buy quality at 21.2B, speed at 3.5B, and pay the bill at 21.2B. Which is another way of saying this is not something to try on a personal workstation.

Where real-time latency actually comes from. Throughput is not what makes a conversation feel alive; time to first sound is. The Moshi paper reports a theoretical latency of 160 ms and 200 ms in practice. Natural human turn gaps sit near that range, which is why the figure gets used as a baseline. How to split latency into parts and pick a model against it is covered separately in the range where the faster model wins.

The codec table is the most informative part of this release. PESQ, a signal fidelity measure, dropped hard to 83.5%, while Korean speech recognition through the codec rose to 112.6%. Two numbers pointing in opposite directions, in the same table.

That is not a defect. Read it as an intentional trade. The purpose of a codec inside a speech language model is not to reproduce the waveform faithfully but to shrink the token count while preserving linguistic content. Tune toward keeping language and shedding fidelity and you get exactly this shape. The same table also draws a boundary: do not repurpose this codec for music or general audio compression. What matters in those applications is precisely what has been traded away.

8. Open weights, closed license

The license on the model card is CC BY-NC 4.0. NC means non-commercial. Being able to download weights and being able to ship them are entirely different statements.

Here is the spot where mistakes actually happen. The same card also mentions Apache License 2.0, but its scope is explicitly limited to the included A.X K2 Light implementation code. Apache-licensed code does not make the weights commercially usable. The release policy of SK Telecom, which built the backbone, and the release policy of this speech model are separate matters.

  • Weights — CC BY-NC 4.0, non-commercial
  • Included backbone implementation code — Apache 2.0, within that scope only
  • Speaker consent — the card states that users bear responsibility for obtaining consent before voice conditioning or generation involving identifiable speakers
  • Stated intended use — bilingual speech recognition and generation, audio understanding, and voice assistant research, all inside the non-commercial boundary

The third item carries weight independent of the license. Speaker-conditioned synthesis transfers the characteristics of a specific person's voice from a short reference clip, and this model exposes that capability as an explicit API. The gap between what is technically possible and what is permissible is at its widest in this feature. Voice and likeness disputes have already made that gap visible in more than one jurisdiction.

How many distinct layers the phrase open weights actually covers came up before in the piece on the Krea 2 open-weight image model. The trap is the same here. The presence of a download button says nothing about the license.

9. When to reach for this and when a commercial API wins

Compress the criterion into one line: if revenue depends on it, use a commercial API; if you are in the validating-and-learning phase, this is worth the trouble. The license has already drawn that line, so there is less to deliberate than it might seem.

SituationChoiceReason
You want to measure mixed Korean-English recognition yourselfThis modelOpen models built for mixing are rare
Prototyping a spoken dialogue agentThis modelRecognition, synthesis, and dialogue live in one model

Shipping in a customer-facing serviceCommercial APIA non-commercial license makes it a non-starter
Real-time call captioningCommercial APIYou need streaming, reconnection, and partial results
You need diarization and timestampsCommercial APIThe model card does not mention those features
No 80 GB class GPU availableCommercial APIThe weights alone are 42.4 GB

One thing the table does not hold. The real cost of an open-weight speech model is not the GPU, it is everything around it. Speaker diarization, punctuation restoration, custom vocabulary, partial-result streaming, resuming a dropped connection, chunking and stitching long audio. More than half of what a commercial speech API sells is that list rather than the model. Getting the model for free still leaves you building all of it, and that usually takes longer.

10. Where it sits in the Korean speech AI landscape

Three things about the landscape are confirmable from this release.

  1. The text backbone is SK Telecom's A.X K2 Light while the speech encoder and codec are KRAFTON's own. One Korean organization built the speech layer on another Korean organization's backbone
  2. Naver's HyperCLOVA X 8B Omni appears in the comparison table with a Korean aggregate of 0.60, meaning Korean omni models can now be compared against one another
  3. Qwen3-Omni sits third in the same table with a Korean aggregate of 0.65. Chinese open models are already a direct comparison point on Korean performance

The third carries the most practical weight. The assumption that a domestic model must be better at Korean is now contradicted by a table. The gap between 0.65 and 0.72 is real, but whether it justifies accepting a non-commercial license is a separate calculation.

It is natural to reach for character voices or NPC dialogue when explaining why a game company shipped a speech model, but that is not something the public materials confirm, so this article leaves it as speculation. The intended uses the card states are bilingual speech recognition and generation, audio understanding, and voice assistant research.

Why chasing model names and rankings is mostly a losing use of attention was the subject of the piece on not memorizing every AI model name. The same applies here. What to remember from this release is not the name but the component list and two lines of license. Know those two and the next model six months from now reads the same way.

11. What could not be confirmed

The following items lack a primary source I could point to, so they appear nowhere in this article as figures or assertions.

  • Absolute error rates. The published aggregates are normalized to 0 to 1. Word or character error rates on individual datasets are not on the card
  • The composition of the 46 benchmarks. Only the counts are confirmed, 24 Korean and 22 English. Which datasets they are could not be cross-checked
  • Measured latency and real-time factor. The 160 ms, 200 ms, and 80 ms figures in this article belong to Moshi and Mimi, not to this model. No latency figure for this model appears on the card
  • What the Raon-Speech row in the comparison table is. Only its name and scores are confirmed. Its lineage, size, and training conditions could not be verified, so only the Korean-versus-English contrast was cited
  • Dedicated code-switching results. No results from a mixing-specific benchmark such as HiKE appear on the card. Designing for mixing and having measured mixing are different claims
  • Whether a commercial license can be negotiated. No separate commercial licensing path was found in public documents
  • Diarization, timestamps, and custom vocabulary. They are absent from the supported task list. Whether that means unsupported or merely undocumented could not be determined

Conclusion: read the two tables, not the two names

What will outlast this release is not the ranking. It is that a speech model with mid-sentence Korean-English mixing as an explicit design goal now exists in open form, and that the cost of the attempt was left in the same document as a number. Korean went up 0.02 where English went down 0.04. Publishing a table like that is more trustworthy than hiding it.

So there are two things worth carrying away. The first is the codec table. PESQ down, Korean recognition up: that shape explains the nature of this component more precisely than any prose. It is a part for a speech language model, not an audio compressor. The second is one line of license. Put CC BY-NC 4.0 on a roadmap without reading it and there is no way back later.

One thing worth doing today. Build a small evaluation set from thirty minutes of your own team's meeting audio. Every English term your team actually uses is already in there, and your spelling conventions are already settled. That one set will tell you more about an adoption decision than someone else's 46 benchmarks. The bonus is that the set outlives the model, and the bonus is usually worth more than the principal.
