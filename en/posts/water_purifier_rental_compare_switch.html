How to Compare Water Purifier Rentals and Switch Providers

Compare the full-term total and the cost of quitting early rather than the monthly figure, and two contracts finally line up against each other

August 16, 2026 Life & Practical

This article is about reading contract structure. It recommends no company or model and excludes none, and it quotes no actual fee or penalty figures, because those vary by company, model and date and have to come from your own contract. The statutory sections below describe Korean law and are a general explanation, not legal advice for any particular case. The rental market described here is a Korean arrangement; if you are elsewhere, treat the arithmetic as portable and the statutes as not.

Put two brochures side by side and one number stands out: the monthly fee. A few thousand won apart and the choice seems made. Then you open the contracts and one commits you for 36 months while the other runs 60. One charges a setup fee and the other does not. One hands you the unit at the end of the term and the other keeps it. The monthly figure carries none of those three.

Change the unit of comparison and the fog clears. You need two numbers: the total paid across the whole committed term, and what it costs to walk away partway through. Pull both out of each contract and comparison becomes possible, and the ranking you get is usually not the one the monthly fee suggested.

Choosing the filtration itself — reverse osmosis versus hollow fibre, tank versus direct-flow hygiene — is covered in how to choose a water purifier, and filter replacement timing in filter replacement cycles and doing it yourself. What this article adds is how that choice lands in the contract, and what order to do things in when you move from one contract to another.

1. What a rental actually rents you

A rental looks like one thing but is two things bundled onto one line. One is the right to use the machine; the other is a maintenance service. The monthly fee is their sum, and the brochure normally prints only the sum.

Separating them matters because their value differs enormously from household to household. The value of the usage right is more or less set by what the machine costs to buy. The value of the maintenance service depends on whether you can change a filter yourself and whether you have time to be home for a visit. That is why an identical contract is cheap for one household and expensive for another.

  • Usage right — what you pay to use the machine. Its character changes completely depending on whether ownership transfers at the end
  • Maintenance service — scheduled visits, filter changes, sanitising, inspection. The interval and the item list are written in the contract
  • Fault handling — whether free repair sits inside the service plan or comes with separate conditions
  • Upfront costs — setup, registration or activation fees paid once at the start

If the contract does not break these four out and shows only a single monthly line, the quickest move is to ask whether a self-managed plan exists for the same machine. The gap between the managed and self-managed prices is exactly what you pay each month for the visits.

2. Ownership transfer versus perpetual rental - the day after expiry decides

The biggest dividing line is what happens the day after the committed term ends. Both types show up as the same monthly figure on a brochure, but the total-cost arithmetic works differently.

Ownership transfer type

Finish the term and the machine becomes yours. The substance of this arrangement is closer to instalment payment with a service plan on top. Afterwards you simply keep using the unit and handle filters, and if you want the visits to continue you usually sign a separate service agreement. When totalling cost, it is only fair to credit the fact that at expiry you hold a machine with residual value.

Perpetual rental type

The term ends and the machine stays company property. Keep paying to keep using it; stop and you return it. In this arrangement, no matter how long you stay, nothing accrues to you as an asset. Some contracts do reduce the fee after the initial term, and whether that clause exists swings the long-run total considerably.

Checking is simple. Find the clause covering disposition of the unit at expiry. Does it say ownership transfers, does it say return, or does it set out a renewal or extension rate? That one clause separates the two contract types. What a salesperson says out loud is not enough; it has to be on paper.

3. Totalling the cost - start from monthly fee times committed months

The skeleton is easy. The hard part is not the arithmetic but collecting every line without missing one.

total = (monthly fee x committed months) + setup fee + registration fee + other upfront costs

Two corrections attach to that. For an ownership-transfer contract, subtract the residual value of the machine you keep. For a perpetual rental you intend to keep past the term, add those extra months. And watch for a promotional period shorter than the committed term. If the first twelve months are discounted and month thirteen reverts to list price, multiplying one monthly figure by the term is simply wrong; you have to add the segments separately.

Where gifts and free months get clawed back

Cash gifts, vouchers and a few free months are not thrown in for nothing. They are the price of completing the term. Run the contract to the end and the clawback never becomes visible, which is exactly why it feels free. The problem arrives if you stop early.

So the question to ask about a gift is not what it is worth but which line it returns through if you cancel. Open the early-termination settlement clause and look for anything charged in addition to the remaining fees. Clause names differ between companies, so search for the clause rather than the amount. The larger the gift, the harder this deserves to be read: receiving more means having more to give back.

4. Finding the break-even against buying

Whether renting or buying wins is not a matter of taste but a matter of how long you will use it. The calculation is one line: find the month at which cumulative purchase cost catches up with cumulative rental cost.

  • Purchase side — purchase price + install cost + (filter set price x replacements so far) + paid servicing if you use it
  • Rental side — upfront costs + (monthly fee x months elapsed)
  • Where the two lines cross is break-even. Past that point buying keeps pulling ahead
  • For an ownership-transfer contract, subtract residual machine value from the rental side at expiry and look again

The shape is usually this. Renting is overwhelmingly cheaper early because no lump sum leaves your account; the gap narrows over time and flips at some point. Which is why the decision compresses down to how many years will this purifier stay in this kitchen.

One thing gets missed often: leaving filters out of the purchase side. A purifier is a machine with a running consumable, and dropping filter cost makes buying look far better than it is. The mirror-image mistake is leaving early termination out of the rental side. If a move, a marriage or a job change is already on the calendar within three to five years, that exit cost is part of the total.

5. An exit charge has three parts

People say penalty as though it were one item, but an invoice normally splits it. Three parts, and you will not miss any.

  1. Penalty on the remaining term — usually scaled to months left. The longer the remainder, the larger it grows
  2. Discount and gift settlement — returning the value of discounts or gifts already received. The clause from the previous section
  3. Removal and collection cost — the cost of taking the machine away, which applies where the unit has to go back

Some contracts carry all three and some only one. A contract marketed as having no penalty can still cost real money if the other two parts are alive. So compare on the sum of the three lines you actually found, not on whether the word penalty appears.

Do this before signing rather than after. By the time you need to cancel, your options have already narrowed, and opening the contract for the first time at that moment is too late.

6. The floor the law sets - the right to cancel and a ceiling on penalties

The contract is not the whole story. Korean law draws a floor under transactions supplied continuously over a period, and a contract cannot go below it.

Article 31 of the Act on Door-to-Door Sales provides that a consumer who has entered into a continuous transaction contract may terminate it at any time during the contract period, unless another statute provides otherwise or a presidential decree carves out an exception for transaction safety. So the line that cancellation is simply impossible during the committed term does not hold for contracts this article reaches. Cancellation is not blocked; it carries a cost.

There is a limit on that cost too. Article 32 paragraph 1 of the same Act provides that even where the contract is terminated or rescinded for reasons not attributable to the business, the business may not demand a penalty significantly exceeding the loss arising from the termination, and may not unfairly refuse to refund money received in excess of the price of goods actually supplied. Where money already received exceeds the price of what was supplied plus the penalty, the difference must be refunded, with delay compensation if it is late (Article 32 paragraph 3). The Fair Trade Commission may issue standards for calculating penalties and refunds (Article 32 paragraph 4).

There is also the Consumer Dispute Resolution Criteria, a Fair Trade Commission public notice that includes water purifier rental as its own item. Its character matters: it is a basis for agreement or recommendation, applied when the parties have not expressed a different intention. It is not enforced directly the way a judgment is; it is a reference line for negotiation and mediation. That is still useful in practice, because it gives you something to point at when talking to the company.

The provisions above are shown to explain structure and do not mean they apply as written to any particular contract. Whether a given contract is a continuous transaction, and whether a demanded penalty significantly exceeds the loss, are decided case by case. A real dispute is better handled through the Korea Consumer Agency or a legal advice service.

8. What the service plan buys and what it does not

Maintenance is not a small share of a rental fee, yet what the visit actually consists of is rarely explained at signing. What you can confirm on paper is the interval and the item list.

  • Visit interval — how many months apart. Some contracts state visits per year instead
  • Filter replacement — which filters at which visit. Not every filter is changed every time
  • Sanitising and cleaning — which parts and how. Tank and direct-flow units differ in what needs it
  • Inspection — leaks, plumbing connections, cold and hot function checks
  • Water checks — what is measured on site and whether the reading is explained
  • Wearing parts — whether the tap and seals fall inside free replacement
  • Missed visits — what happens to the fee for a month when nobody was home

Whether that service is worth buying varies by household. One test does most of the work: can you change the filter yourself? If the machine takes push-in cartridges and the job does not bother you, the service loses most of its value. If the unit has a tank that needs internal cleaning, or filter changes involve tools and draining, the visit earns its keep.

This is where a self-managed plan becomes meaningful: the same machine at a lower rate with no visits, filters ordered by you. The gap between the two rates is the price tag on the visits, and you decide whether that number is worth doing it yourself for. If you are unsure, starting managed and converting later is the safer order. Whether conversion is possible sits in the plan change clause.

Worth noting for the Korean context: a household purifier carries no notification duty. The installer notification under Article 8-2 of the Drinking Water Management Act applies to those installing purifiers in multi-use facilities to supply drinking water to many people. If you are putting one into an office or a shop that qualifies as such a facility, check that point in advance.

9. Separating labelling from marketing

Purifier advertising mixes two kinds of sentence: labelling you can confirm on paper, and claims with no way to check them. Telling them apart is close to the whole job here.

The baseline is already in statute. Article 3 item 7 of the Drinking Water Management Act defines a water purifier as a device made to supply drinking water meeting the drinking water quality standard through physical, chemical or biological processes. The target, in other words, is not better than the competition but compliant with the standard. That standard is laid out in annex 1 of the Rules on Drinking Water Quality Standards and Inspection, organised into microorganisms, inorganic substances harmful to health, organic substances harmful to health, disinfectants and disinfection by-products, substances affecting aesthetic quality, and radioactivity.

There is also a procedure for confirming a device meets it: the water purifier quality inspection under Article 43 paragraph 2 of the same Act and Article 35 paragraph 11 of its enforcement rules, carried out by the Korea Water Cluster certification body designated under Article 19 of the Act on Water Management Technology and Water Industry Promotion. So when an advertising phrase turns up, the questions change into these.

  • Has this model passed the quality inspection, and can you see an inspection identifier or certification mark?
  • Which substances is it labelled as reducing, and by how much, under what test conditions?
  • Is there a list of target substances, or only adjectives like clean and pure?
  • If a health benefit is being claimed, where is the document that supports it?

That last item is where mineral phrasing belongs. Some methods remove more dissolved matter and some remove less — that is a difference in method and does not by itself make one healthier than the other. What minerals in water do for health is outside what this article can settle, and it is certainly not something you can learn by reading a contract. So rather than ranking two products on that phrase, judge on whether the four questions above come back with paper answers.

Marketing in the opposite direction gets the same treatment. Framing tap water as dangerous is a claim, not verifiable labelling. If you want to know about the tap water where you live, published water quality information from the responsible utility is the accurate route, and evaluating tap water was always a separate decision from choosing a purifier.

10. Switching - get the order wrong and you pay twice for a month

Moving from an existing contract to a new one is fiddlier than signing fresh, because two timetables have to be aligned. There is an order.

  1. Establish the exact expiry date. It may run from installation rather than signing, and if the machine was swapped mid-contract the term may have restarted. Ask support and get the remaining month count in writing or by message
  2. Confirm what happens to the unit at expiry. If ownership transfers, there is less reason to rush. Running to the end, keeping the machine and then starting fresh is usually the better sequence
  3. Price out quitting now, in three parts. Remaining-term penalty, discount and gift settlement, removal cost. If that sum exceeds what you would still pay to expiry, the reason to switch disappears
  4. Set the new start date after the old expiry. An install brought forward creates a month where both contracts bill. Install scheduling is often flexible, so say so early
  5. Book the collection. On return-type contracts the account is not closed until the machine is picked up. Keep evidence that collection completed
  6. Watch the final invoice. Settlement charges typically run a month or two past cancellation. Cancelling the direct debit immediately can create an arrears record, so close it after the final bill clears

Moving house - relocation install comes before switching

A house move is a common reason to think about cancelling, but most contracts contain a relocation install clause that moves the existing contract to the new address. Three things to check: whether relocation is free or charged, how many times it is allowed within the term, and whether the new plumbing adds materials cost. Paying an exit charge when relocation was available is usually a loss.

Contract transfer - handing it over without cancelling

If you no longer need the contract but somebody else can take it on, it is worth asking whether a transfer of contract is possible instead of cancellation. The remaining term is assumed by the new holder, so no exit charge arises. Whether it is allowed, and on what conditions — screening of the incoming holder, whether an address change can be processed at the same time — sits in the assignment clause and varies by company. This route is worth the most when a lot of term remains.

12. Frequently asked questions

Is a water purifier rental just an installment purchase in disguise?

It depends on the type. If ownership passes at the end of the term, the substance is instalment payment with a service plan attached. If the machine stays company property and you must keep paying to keep using it, it is a lease throughout. Both print as the same monthly figure, so the post-term disposition clause is the only reliable way to tell them apart.

Where do free gifts and free months get paid back?

They are tied to completing the term. Run to the end and the clawback never surfaces; cancel early and the value of the discounts or gifts is commonly billed as a separate settlement line. Names differ by company, so look for the existence of a termination settlement clause rather than for an amount.

What are the components of an early termination charge?

Remaining-term penalty, discount and gift settlement, and removal and collection cost. Some contracts carry only one of the three, some all of them. To know the real exposure you have to find each part and add them up, and since amounts and formulas vary by company, model and date, they have to come from your own contract.

Can a contract forbid cancellation during the committed term?

Under Korean law, Article 31 of the Act on Door-to-Door Sales provides that a consumer in a continuous transaction contract may terminate at any time during the contract period, unless another statute provides otherwise or a presidential decree makes an exception. Article 32 paragraph 1 adds that even where termination is not the business's fault, a penalty significantly exceeding the loss caused by termination may not be demanded. Whether a given contract qualifies, and whether an amount crosses that line, are decided case by case, so a dispute belongs with a consumer agency.

How should I read marketing claims about minerals in the water?

Framing it as good or bad leaves nothing verifiable. Article 3 item 7 of the Drinking Water Management Act defines a purifier as a device made to supply water meeting the drinking water quality standard, so the baseline is fixed. The questions worth asking are whether the model passed the quality inspection under Article 43 paragraph 2, whether the inspection identifier is visible, and which substances the labelling says it reduces. Those three have paper answers; health-benefit phrasing does not.

When should I cancel the direct debit after ending a rental?

After settlement clears. Final fees and settlement charges normally run a month or two past cancellation, so stopping the debit at the moment you give notice can produce a missed-payment or arrears record. Confirm the final invoice has been processed, then close it.
