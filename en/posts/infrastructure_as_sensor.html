Reading Installed Infrastructure as a Sensor

The physical signals that rails, fiber, base stations and phones carry as a side effect, and the bill that comes with pulling them out

Aug 20, 2026 Tech & IT

The moment a train becomes a scanner carriage

A flatbed scanner works on a simple principle. A light source and a single row of sensor pixels crawl beneath the glass, and the rows they read are stacked in time order into one image. The height of that image is set by the pixel count of the sensor. The width is set by how far the carriage travelled.

So what happens when the subject is far too large to lay on the glass? You invert the arrangement. Instead of moving the subject, move the sensor. And carriages of that kind are already installed in every city. A train runs a fixed track at a nearly constant speed and in a straight line. Put one camera on it and the whole city is effectively lying on the glass.

The idea does not stop at photography. Fiber buried for telecommunications also carries ground vibration. Radio beams crossing between base stations leave a record of the rain that fell between them. The accelerometer in your pocket picks up earthquake shaking on the same axis it uses to rotate the screen. The premise is the same throughout: instead of installing something new, pull information out of what is already there.

What follows looks at how far this idea has been validated, where it is still exploratory, and what it costs. That includes facing the fact that people get recorded incidentally, though not how to build a system that does so deliberately.

What the original project actually did, and the limits its author wrote down

The prompt for this piece was a personal line-scan camera project. The builder rides a moving vehicle, continuously captures a single vertical line out the window, and stitches those lines into an ultra-wide image. The starting point is described in one sentence: what if the entire camera moved and the subject did not?

The kit is a combination of industrial parts.

  • Monochrome line camera — a 1×2048 pixel sensor read roughly 19,000 times per second
  • Color line camera — 3×2098 pixels at roughly 10,000 reads per second, with R, G and B on physically separate rows
  • Lens — a 28mm f/2.8 manual lens adapted onto a C mount
  • Auxiliary sensors — a six-degree-of-freedom accelerometer and gyro plus a GPS receiver, both polled by a microcontroller

The largest output is a 56,894 by 2,048 pixel grayscale image taken on the San Francisco to Oakland ferry in February 2026. Subway runs produced others. Post-processing goes through a homegrown pipeline called grindstone, whose first version took hours and failed by exceeding JPEG size limits before being rewritten around NumPy.

The aspect ratio is only correct if you know the speed

The weak point is exact. If you do not know how much real distance one line covers, the image stretches or compresses. In other words, travel speed is pixel size.

GPS reads only ten times a second, and by the author's own description a single gap there covers 400 entire lines of camera output. On most trains it never got a fix at all. That leaves integrating the accelerometer, which needs an initial velocity. The author assumes zero because the trip starts at a station, while noting he cannot be certain of that. On top of it all, the microcontroller's serial buffer overflowed at times, wiping out close to half a second of data outright.

Color and perspective repeat the same problem

Because the color sensor's R, G and B rows are physically separated, they never see the same point at the same instant. The author corrected by shifting the red channel ten pixels right and blue ten pixels left, but noted the background cannot be fully corrected because objects at different distances move at different angular rates. Parallax has the same root: near objects sweep past faster than far ones, so the pixels-per-distance figure has to be tuned by hand per scene. The color sensor's sensitivity to infrared across all channels was suppressed with a filter passing only the 400 to 700 nm band.

So this is a hobby project rather than a measurement instrument, and the author presents it that way. His stated next goal is not accuracy but to make it not dependent on a laptop to capture images. Yet the list of difficulties it surfaced is precisely the list the scientific cases below run into.

The general form: what is installed already carries something

Using infrastructure as a sensor splits into two branches. In the first, the infrastructure supplies motion. Trains and ferries carry a sensor along steadily without detecting anything themselves; they are freight platforms. In the second, the infrastructure is itself the medium. Light in a fiber, radio between antennas, an accelerometer inside a handset — each does its original job while surrounding physical quantities leave traces in how it behaves.

The second branch is overwhelmingly the larger one, for three reasons. It is already deployed at national scale, it stays powered around the clock because of its original purpose, and the operator maintains it out of commercial self-interest.

For that branch to work, three conditions have to hold at once.

  1. Physical coupling — the quantity you care about genuinely affects how the infrastructure behaves, the way raindrops absorb radio energy
  2. Recordability — that effect is already logged somewhere, or can be made to be. Operators record received power per link as a matter of course
  3. A stable inverse — you can recover the quantity from the record, and that recovery is not wildly sensitive to noise

The third is usually the bottleneck. The first two are questions of physics and operations and resolve quickly; the third collapses every time conditions shift. Most of the figures below are report cards on condition three.

Fiber is already listening to vibration

The principle: phase in the returning light

Distributed acoustic sensing fires short optical pulses down a fiber and watches the light scattered back by microscopic irregularities in the glass. When a section of cable stretches slightly under vibration, the phase of the light returning from that section changes. Round-trip time tells you where it happened, so a single strand behaves like thousands of strain sensors lined up end to end.

The key point is that the cable was never built to be a sensor. You attach an interrogator to a strand that was buried for telecom and read what comes back.

Trackside cable: measured on a German high-speed line

The line between Erfurt and Halle shows in numbers how far this goes. A commercial DAS interrogator was attached to 35 km of standard telecom single-mode fiber lying in a trough beside the rails, and the first 20 km were analyzed because signal strength degraded past that point.

  • The original 2.5 kHz sample rate was averaged down to 156 Hz, or 6.4 ms intervals
  • One channel per 0.68 m of cable
  • Signal-to-noise ratio stayed above 10 across the first 20 km
  • What was tracked: train position, velocity, and the number of bogie clusters

For a train at 160 km/h, three methods were compared. Following the train gave a velocity uncertainty of plus or minus 4.5 km/h over a 15-second average; watching one point on the rail gave plus or minus 4.8 km/h over a 341 m average; counting the spacing of bogie clusters gave plus or minus 0.8 km/h over a 681 m average.

The limits the same study reported are more telling. Where the fiber looped or crossed the track, velocity errors ran as high as 70 km/h, and some methods lagged by up to 22.5 seconds. If you do not know exactly where and how the cable was laid, the numbers drift. And since it was not laid for measurement, there is no reason that map should be accurate.

Unused strands in the city

Scaling to a whole city changes the question. What fraction of the street network needs accessible fiber before a given application becomes possible? A 2026 preprint framed this in terms of coverage thresholds and percolation, and separated three regimes.

  • Below 10 percent — already sufficient for earthquake early warning, groundwater monitoring, geological mapping and urban activity tracking
  • Around 51.6 percent — the percolation threshold, where the city becomes effectively connected and statistical traffic monitoring opens up
  • Approaching 100 percent — infrastructure monitoring, individual vehicle tracking and pedestrian movement analysis

The obstacles the same paper named were not technical. Fiber ownership is fragmented between commercial operators and government organizations, mapping can be inaccurate particularly for older installations, and data privacy concerns limit the potential for collaboration. This document is a preprint, so it does not carry the standing of peer-reviewed work.

Submarine cable, with no added hardware at all

The most extreme version sits on the seafloor. On a roughly 10,000 km submarine cable linking Los Angeles and Valparaiso, researchers detected seismic and water waves by monitoring the polarization of ordinary optical telecommunication channels over a nine-month observation period.

Unlike DAS, which attaches a dedicated interrogator at the end, this reads an incidental state variable of traffic that is already flowing. Deep-water cable being quieter than its terrestrial counterparts is what made the observation feasible. Given how large the observational gaps are at sea, and how expensive filling them with new instruments would be, the appeal of this direction is obvious.

Reading rain from attenuation between base stations

Mobile backhaul contains many microwave links running straight between two antennas. When rain falls along that path, drops absorb and scatter the signal and received power drops. Operators already log received power per link, so path-averaged rainfall intensity can be inverted out of that attenuation.

A 2013 study covering the Netherlands validated the approach at country scale.

  • Target area of about 35,500 square kilometers, with roughly 2,400 links feeding the real-time algorithm
  • Validation used 1,751 link paths out of 2,902 commercial links
  • The period was June to September 2011, with 12 days of calibration data and 12 days of validation data
  • Against radar, daily rainfall depth showed near-zero bias, a coefficient of variation of 0.53 and a coefficient of determination of 0.73
  • At 15 minutes over 81 square kilometers, the coefficient of variation was 1.13 and the coefficient of determination 0.49

The gap between those two figures is the whole character of the approach. It degrades sharply as you slice time and space more finely. Daily areal rainfall is usable; a 15-minute local downpour is not something to take at face value.

The error sources the study listed all come from the same root, that this was never built for measurement. Recording precision is limited to 1 dB. A film of water on the antenna adds attenuation of its own. Atmospheric humidity variation alone contributes errors on the order of 1 dB. In the lower 7 to 13 GHz bands the relationship between attenuation and rainfall turns nonlinear and errors grow. Wet snow is not measurable at all.

The reason the direction keeps being pursued is the motivation the paper states outright. Most of the earth's land surface lacks accurate surface precipitation measurement, and in many regions gauge network density is declining rather than growing. Where the observing network is thin, the telecom network is already there.

When the accelerometer in your pocket becomes a seismometer

A phone's MEMS accelerometer exists to rotate the screen. Earthquake shaking arrives on the same axis. The difficulty is that this signal is buried in walking, riding in a car, and setting the phone down on a desk.

Separating everyday motion from earthquakes

A 2016 smartphone seismic network study handled that separation with a neural network taking three inputs: an amplitude measure (interquartile range), a frequency measure (zero crossing rate) and a multi-component amplitude measure (cumulative absolute velocity).

  • Detection is possible for magnitude 5 or larger within 10 km in the 1 to 10 Hz band
  • 98 percent of earthquake records within 10 km of the epicenter were classified as earthquakes and 93 percent of everyday motions as everyday, for roughly 7 percent false alarms at the device level
  • A network-level event is declared only when more than 60 percent of active phones within a 10 km radius trigger together. In 1,000 simulations without earthquakes, no false event was generated

The limits the same study reported are just as clear. Because a phone slides on whatever surface it sits on, recorded amplitudes are lower bounds on the actual value, and magnitude estimates based on peak shaking saturate for very large earthquakes. Values can be clipped exactly at the events that matter most.

What changes at operational scale

When the same idea moves into the operating system, the scale is different. The published figures for Android Earthquake Alerts are these.

  • More than 18,000 earthquakes detected since April 2021, ranging from magnitude 1.9 to 7.8
  • 790 million alerts sent worldwide across more than 2,000 earthquakes significant enough to warn people, with the system active in 98 countries by the end of 2023
  • Median absolute error of the first magnitude estimate improved from 0.50 to 0.25
  • Population with access to early warning grew from roughly 250 million in 2019 to 2.5 billion
  • Of more than 1.5 million survey respondents, 85 percent found the alerts very helpful
  • Alerts are sent based on a privacy-preserving, coarse location of the device

The point here is not a contest over accuracy. It is reach — warnings arriving in countries that never built a dedicated seismic network. The jump from 250 million to 2.5 billion is what that looks like.

Why it is appealing: not zero cost, but density and geography

People often describe this approach as free to install, which is inaccurate. Interrogators, data pipelines, storage and staff all cost money. The real advantage lies elsewhere.

  • Density — no budget anywhere buys tens of thousands of strain observation points at 0.68 m spacing as separate instruments. One strand supplies that array already
  • Geography — it is already in places people cannot easily reach: kilometers below the sea surface, under city streets, inside tunnels
  • Continuity — you get an unbroken time series rather than a temporary campaign. And because the operator keeps cables and links alive for its own business, maintenance is externalized
  • Filling the gaps — it becomes an option exactly where gauge and seismometer density is thinning, which is where observation is most needed

All of these hold only when access is granted. And access is a matter of contracts and policy, not engineering.

Why it is hard: noise, missing calibration, and the right to the data

Using something not built for measurement to measure costs you along six axes. The cases above left a concrete instance on each one.

The sixth axis differs in kind from the rest. The first five leave room for improvement through signal processing and physics, but access is institutional territory. That is why the urban fiber preprint named ownership fragmentation and privacy concerns as real obstacles. An operator has little incentive to open state data about its own assets, and opening it attaches liability.

One more thing rides alongside: no quality guarantee. What a telecom operator commits to is communication quality, not observation quality. Reconfiguring a link or swapping equipment is normal operations for them and an unannounced discontinuity for anyone building a time series on top. The change logs and advance notice that would be routine for an instrument simply are not part of the arrangement.

And calibration has a circularity problem. Accuracy of values pulled from infrastructure is usually confirmed against existing networks — radar, gauges, seismometers. Where those networks are thin, there is nothing to check against. The places that need this approach most are precisely the places hardest to validate it in. It is no accident that the Dutch study was carried out in a country with dense radar coverage.

The data that appears as a side effect, and purpose limitation

Infrastructure sensing does not record only the quantity you targeted. A cable listening for vibration cannot filter out everything but seismic waves. Passing vehicles and footsteps land in the same record. That is not a defect but an inevitable consequence of how it works.

What kinds of data appear incidentally

  • Vibration based — fiber running beside roads or under pavements receives traffic and footfall along with everything else. That is exactly what the preprint meant when it placed individual vehicle tracking and pedestrian movement analysis in the near-total coverage band
  • Device based — phone accelerometer data originates in a personal possession by definition. The character changes completely the moment precise location is joined to it
  • Link based — received power on a microwave link is a property of a path, not a person, which makes it different in kind. Even so, that data lives inside an operator's systems, alongside other information that does connect to individuals

Why purpose limitation is decisive

Conventional privacy models turn on notice and consent. Here that axis does not function. Someone walking beside a track or driving over a buried cable has no way of knowing what is being recorded, and there is no defined population to notify. The structure for asking consent does not exist.

What remains is control at the output rather than the input: stating the purpose narrowly and keeping the products only in the form that purpose requires.

  • Aggregate to segment-level statistics rather than individual trajectories
  • Keep event summaries rather than raw waveforms, with retention limited to what the purpose needs
  • Handle location at the minimum precision required, which is the axis behind Android Earthquake Alerts stating it works from coarse location
  • Route any purpose change through review, and do not retain data merely because retaining it is technically possible

That last item matters most in practice. Once data accumulates it invites proposals to use it for something else, and by then the decision cannot be unwound. The research literature deliberately separates statistical monitoring from individual tracking as different layers precisely because the two need different kinds of data.

For balance: the urban fiber preprint judged the risk of privacy-invading applications extremely low at current performance levels, reasoning that individual tracking effectively requires near-total coverage and that such a state is not expected in the near future. The same paper added that in a more distant future, designing optimal networks that still preserve privacy may become a question requiring attention. Low risk now and safe later are not the same claim.

How far the data a device generates can be controlled shows up in the same shape inside the home. That axis is covered device by device in Smart Home & IoT Security Complete Guide.

Who uses it: early warning, asset diagnosis, weather

The places this approach earns its keep mostly wear the face of a public good.

  • Disaster early warning — detect the faster P wave and alert before the stronger shaking arrives. In regions without a dedicated network the change in reach is dramatic. Directly above the epicenter, though, the alert cannot beat the shaking
  • Infrastructure condition — trackside cable yields train position, speed and bogie counts. The urban fiber work pointing to infrastructure monitoring in its high-coverage band runs in the same direction
  • Weather — coarse-resolution areal rainfall where gauge networks are sparse or shrinking

In all three the benefit spreads widely and does not accrue to any one operator. That is where the structural problem sits. The cost falls on the infrastructure owner while the benefit goes to society. Observation only exists if the telecom operator opens the data, and what opening it produces is mostly not theirs. Even with the technology ready, unless that mismatch is resolved this stays a matter of agreements and public funding rather than a business.

An organization considering this direction has items to settle before technical validation.

  1. Who holds the cable route map for the target section, and how accurate is it
  2. Is there terminal space, with power, where an interrogator can sit
  3. Does a section exist where results can be overlapped with an existing observing network
  4. Can the purpose and retention scope of the outputs be fixed in writing
  5. Is there a channel for being notified when the operator changes its configuration

Start without items four and five and the project stalls in operations even after succeeding technically. On the point that the observation method decides which questions can be answered at all, this is the same problem covered in Seeing Network Traffic.

Conclusion: rereading is cheaper, but it is not free

Pulling signal out of what is already installed gives two things at once: a density no new network could buy, and a position in places people cannot go. What it gives up in exchange is calibration, installation records, and any guarantee that the data will keep arriving.

So the judgment splits by condition.

  • Favorable — the existing network is sparse, infrastructure already runs along the section of interest, the required space and time resolution is coarse, and change detection matters more than absolute values
  • Dedicated instruments are better — fine resolution is required, absolute accuracy will underpin regulation or contracts, or continuity of data access cannot be guaranteed

Going back to the scanner settles it. Removing the glass is an appealing move, but the work the glass was doing does not disappear: a known speed, a known distance, a known light. Using infrastructure as a sensor is the problem of deciding who does that work instead. A train supplies the motion but will not tell you its speed. Fiber transmits vibration but will not tell you where it is buried.

What filled those gaps is, in the end, what the observation's credibility rests on. When you meet a number, the first thing to check is not the accuracy figure but the section it came from and what it was compared against.
