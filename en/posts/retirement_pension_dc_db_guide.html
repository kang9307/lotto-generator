Korea's DB and DC Retirement Pensions: What Actually Differs

Whether the employer or the worker carries the investment risk — from that one line follow the formulas, the wage peak shock, and the one way street of conversion

Aug 29, 2026 Finance & Economy

This article is general information about the retirement pension system of the Republic of Korea and does not recommend any financial product or plan conversion. It is not investment or tax advice. Base any decision on your own workplace retirement pension rules and on advice from a qualified tax or labour professional.

1. One letter apart, and the money piles up differently

When a consent form for a DC conversion goes around a Korean workplace, two reactions are usual. One is which plan pays more. The other is that everyone else is signing. Both go wrong at the same place. These are not a generous plan and a stingy plan. They are two different machines for accumulating money.

DB is the defined benefit plan, DC the defined contribution plan. Article 2, items 8 and 9 of Korea's Employee Retirement Benefit Security Act define them, and Articles 13 and 19 govern how each is established. Half the answer is already in the names. In DB the benefit is fixed first; in DC the contribution is fixed first. Whatever is not fixed first is what moves later.

Everything below is stated as of August 2026 and applies to the Korean system only. Readers outside Korea will find their own occupational pension rules differ. Article numbers are given so that any conflict with an employer's summary can be resolved against the statute. Severance calculation and retirement income tax are covered in the severance pay guide, and the practical question of investing a DC account is in the DC account guide. What follows is the dividing line between the two plans.

2. One sentence splits them — who carries the investment risk

If you remember only one thing, remember this.

In DB the employer carries the investment risk. In DC the worker does.

Under DB the employer owes a defined amount at the moment you leave. It invests the reserve to meet that promise, and if returns disappoint it must top the reserve up. If returns are strong, it contributes less. Investment outcomes are absorbed on the company's books, while the worker's number is set by wage and length of service instead.

Under DC the employer's duty ends at paying in. Article 20, paragraph 1 requires the employer to pay in cash at least one twelfth of the participant's annual total wage into the participant's account. From then on the worker chooses the holdings and takes the result. Gains and losses both belong to the account holder.

Everything else follows from that line. It explains why the formulas differ, why a wage peak arrangement strikes only one side, and why conversion runs in one direction. The sections below trace those consequences one at a time.

3. The DB formula — the last wage reaches back over everything

Article 15 sets the defined benefit level at no less than 30 days of average wage for each year of continuous service. Average wage is measured near the moment of departure, so in practice the formula looks like this.

DB benefit ≒ average wage just before leaving × years of service

The part that is easy to miss is that this is multiplication. The final wage does not apply only to the final year; it is applied retroactively across every year already served. What you earned in your first year never enters the calculation.

  • While pay rises, the value of past service rises with it
  • A promotion that lifts pay in a step raises the valuation of the whole period at once
  • If pay falls, past service is cut by the same proportion
  • Whatever the reserve earned has no direct effect on what the worker receives

So in DB the role of a rate of return is played by wage growth. Whatever markets do, a worker whose pay rises six percent a year sees a retirement benefit that grows at roughly the same pace. That is why DB is called stable, and also why DB quietly weakens when pay stops moving.

4. The DC formula — cut yearly, and invested from that day

DC does not reach back. One twelfth or more of that year's total wage enters the account in that year, and from that moment the money runs on its own. Article 20, paragraph 3 requires the employer to pay in at least once a year on a regular basis, and where payment is late it adds delay interest for the days of delay, at a rate set by Presidential Decree within a ceiling of forty percent a year.

Because of that structure, the same lifetime pay produces a different result depending on when it was earned. A contribution taken from a junior salary has decades of compounding ahead of it; one taken from a senior salary a year before retirement has almost none.

  • Early contributions are small in size but long in compounding time
  • Late contributions are large but have almost no time to work
  • A big raise late in a career leaves past contributions untouched
  • Under Article 20, paragraph 2 a participant may add voluntary contributions of their own
  • When retirement or a similar event occurs, Article 20, paragraph 5 requires unpaid contributions within 14 days

Put simply, DB values the whole career from a single photograph taken at the end, while DC adds up a photograph taken every year. Which total is larger depends on how those photographs turned out.

5. Which is better — axes instead of an answer

Plenty of writing sells an answer here. The closest thing to an answer is that none exists, because the deciding variables lie in the future and neither wage growth nor investment results can be known in advance. What can be decided now is which axes to look along.

The axes often point in opposite directions. When they do, look first at the axis that cannot be reversed: the timing of a wage peak scheme, and the irreversibility of the conversion itself.

6. Why a wage peak scheme turns the board over

A wage peak scheme hits the DB formula head on, because of the multiplication in section 3. If pay is cut by thirty percent three years before retirement, the reduced wage is multiplied by all twenty five years of service. The loss is not confined to the last three years.

Korean law recognises this. Article 32, paragraph 4 requires an employer who adjusts wages by reference to an age, a service milestone or a wage level through a collective agreement or workplace rules to tell workers in advance that their retirement benefit may fall, and, in consultation with the worker representative, to take measures preventing that fall, such as converting to a defined contribution plan or improving the benefit calculation basis. An agreement to cut contractual working hours by at least one hour a day or five hours a week falls under the same provision. An employer who gives no notice and takes no measure faces a fine of up to five million won.

That is the background to the common line that it is better to move to DC before the wage peak begins. The line carries conditions.

  • Timing is everything. Move after pay has already been cut and the reduced DB benefit is locked in as it stands
  • Some employers block the fall through an improved calculation basis or a separate top up, which changes the value of converting
  • After conversion the investment result is yours. Avoiding one reduction means taking on a different kind of variability

Wage peak schemes are designed differently at every employer. The reduction rate, the trigger age and the compensation method sit in the workplace rules and the retirement pension rules, so borrowing another workplace's example will mislead.

7. A worked example on stated assumptions

The numbers below are assumptions used to show the structure. They are not forecasts of wage growth or investment returns, and nothing here predicts or promises any outcome.

Take a simplified three year model. Assume a first year total wage of 36 million won, one contribution at each year end, a constant annual return, and average wage approximated by that year's monthly average.

AssumptionCase A: wage growth 6% · return 2%Case B: wage growth 1% · return 8%
Year 3 annual total wage40.4496 million won36.7236 million won
Year 3 monthly average wage3.3708 million won3.0603 million won
DB benefit (monthly average × 3 years)10.1124 million won9.1809 million won
Year 1 contribution of 3 million, valued at year 33.1212 million won3.4992 million won
Year 2 contribution, valued at year 33.2436 million won3.2724 million won
Year 3 contribution3.3708 million won3.0603 million won
DC total9.7356 million won9.8319 million won
DifferenceDB higher by 0.3768 million wonDC higher by 0.6510 million won

Same person, same three years, and the direction of the result flips. What flipped it was not the plan but the relative size of wage growth and investment return. Over a longer career the gap compounds, so a difference of a few hundred thousand won across three years becomes far larger across twenty.

The point is not that one side wins. The point is which two numbers you are actually comparing. Put your employer's wage growth over the last five years next to the investment result you believe you can live with, losses included, and you have the raw material for a decision.

8. Conversion runs one way

This is the section to be careful with. Changing the plan is procedurally straightforward. Article 4, paragraph 3 requires the employer to obtain the consent of the union representing a majority of workers, or of a majority of workers where there is none, before changing an established retirement benefit plan into another type. Paragraph 4 requires the employer to hear their opinion even when the change is not unfavourable. Skipping consent or consultation is punishable by a fine of up to five million won under Article 46, item 1.

The difficulty is not the procedure but the result. Separating what becomes fixed from what does not come back gives this.

  • The retroactive scope is set by the workplace rules. Korea's labour ministry has explained that whether past service is credited in one step or in stages, in full or in part, may be settled by labour and management and written into the retirement pension rules, taking the employer's financial burden into account. Conversion terms therefore differ by employer
  • Retroactive contributions have a stated basis. On the same guidance, they are calculated from the total wage received in the year before the date the retroactive crediting was decided, must be at least one twelfth of the annual total wage, and must amount to at least 30 days of average wage for each year credited
  • The road back is closed. Where a workplace converts to a defined benefit plan again, retroactive crediting for the defined contribution period is difficult, so transferred DC reserves cannot be moved into the new DB account. The plan can change, but the calculation basis for periods already moved should be treated as not returning
  • The wage at conversion sets in place. DB entitlement up to the conversion is fixed on the average wage at that date, and later raises no longer feed into it
  • Consent operates collectively. The change is made through the worker representative, so an individual objection does not keep the old plan. The moment the consent form circulates is effectively the last chance to argue

So the question to ask before signing is not whether it is better but what becomes fixed. Retroactive scope, the reference date for retroactive contributions, and the treatment of the existing DB account: check how those three read in the rules, and where the wording is unclear, ask a labour attorney or the competent regional labour office.

9. What it really means when the investment duty moves to you

Moving to DC creates one decision that did not exist under DB: what to hold. Not deciding is also a decision.

The system knows about that gap. The default option under Article 21-2 applies a pre approved investment method where the participant chooses nothing. Where a previously chosen method matures and the participant does not select within two weeks of notice, the default option takes effect, and the same rules apply to individual retirement pension accounts.

The statistics show where the money actually sits. According to the 2025 retirement pension investment white paper published by Korea's Ministry of Employment and Labor in May 2026, total reserves stood at 501.4 trillion won at the end of 2025, of which market linked holdings made up 24.6 percent. Roughly three quarters remained in principal and interest guaranteed products. By plan type, DC and IRP together passed half at 54.3 percent, and the 2025 annual return was reported at 6.5 percent.

Balance matters here. A heavy tilt to guaranteed products can work against long horizon accumulation, but market linked products can lose principal. There is no assurance that one year's return repeats, and if you retire in a losing year, the balance on that day is what you receive. Whether you can absorb that variability, which DB never asked of you, is one axis of the conversion decision. How to set an allocation in practice is covered in the separate DC article.

10. If the employer fails — the safeguards and their limits

Under DB the reserve backs the employer's promise to pay, which makes the insolvency question heavier on that side. The safeguards that can be confirmed are these.

  • Priority repayment. Article 12, paragraph 2 ranks the final three years of retirement benefits ahead of claims secured by pledge or mortgage and ahead of taxes and public charges against the employer's total assets. Paragraph 1 gives other retirement benefits priority over taxes, public charges and other claims apart from secured ones
  • Minimum reserve. Article 16, paragraph 1 requires a defined benefit employer to hold, at each business year end, at least the standard liability reserve multiplied by a ratio set by Presidential Decree at no less than sixty percent. Article 5 of the Enforcement Decree sets that ratio as no less than eighty percent, as prescribed by Ordinance of the Ministry of Employment and Labor
  • Shortfall notice. Article 16, paragraph 2 requires the pension provider to check within six months of the business year end whether the reserve exceeds the minimum and to inform the employer, and to inform the worker representative as well where it falls short. Paragraph 3 obliges the employer to close the gap
  • Protection of entitlement. Article 7, paragraph 1 prohibits assigning, attaching or pledging the right to receive retirement pension benefits
  • Deposit protection. Korea's deposit protection ceiling rose to one hundred million won on 1 September 2025, and defined contribution, individual retirement pension and small business retirement pension fund reserves held in protected deposit products receive a ceiling separate from ordinary deposits

The limits are equally clear. The minimum reserve need not mean one hundred percent of the standard liability reserve, because the statute sets a floor and delegates the actual ratio to subordinate legislation. Priority repayment is a ranking rule that operates on assets that remain; it does not guarantee payment. The separate deposit ceiling applies to protected deposit products, so market linked holdings fall outside it. For a DB workplace, the practical signal is whether the Article 16, paragraph 2 notice has ever reached the worker representative.

11. Can you take money out early — different doors by plan

Here the two plans are asymmetric. Article 22 addresses defined contribution plans, and there is no corresponding early withdrawal provision for defined benefit plans.

Article 14 of the Enforcement Decree sets the grounds for a defined contribution withdrawal. They include buying a home as a person without one, meeting a housing deposit as a person without a home, disaster damage, bearing medical expenses exceeding 125 thousandths of the participant's annual total wage, a declaration of bankruptcy or a decision commencing individual rehabilitation within five years of the application date, and repaying the principal and interest of a secured loan. Each ground needs documentation, and where none applies the door does not open at all.

Pledging works differently. Article 7, paragraph 2 provides that despite the prohibition in paragraph 1, a participant meeting grounds and requirements set by Presidential Decree may pledge the right to receive benefits within a limit set by Presidential Decree, and that a registered pension provider shall cooperate so that a loan against the pledged benefit can be made. Article 2 of the Enforcement Decree sets the grounds, and the limit is fifty percent of the participant's reserve.

  • For a DB participant who needs cash, the door that can open is pledging, not early withdrawal
  • Withdrawal and pledge grounds are decided by whether the situation matches an enumerated item, not by how difficult things are
  • The medical expense test is set as a proportion of your own total wage rather than an absolute amount
  • Withdrawing shrinks the retirement pot, and the tax treatment of the amount withdrawn is a separate matter. Tax varies with individual circumstances, so confirm it with Korea's National Tax Service or a tax professional

Whether a provider actually offers such loans, and what documents they require, varies by provider, so ask the financial institution holding the account first.

12. Finding out which plan you are in, and what to read in the rules

Surprisingly many people do not know their own plan. It rarely appears on a payslip, and the line in the joining pack is quickly forgotten. There are three routes.

  1. The retirement pension rules. The most reliable. Articles 13 and 19 set out what the rules for each plan type must contain, so the document reveals the plan type, the benefit calculation method and the contribution schedule. Ask the HR or general affairs team to let you read it
  2. The financial institution. If an account is being managed, it will show which plan the account belongs to
  3. The integrated pension portal of Korea's Financial Supervisory Service. It lists the pension contracts registered in your name

Once you have the rules open, these are the items to read.

  • Plan type and whether both run. Some workplaces operate both plans or let workers choose. Confirm which one applies to you
  • Benefit calculation method. For DB, how average wage is measured; for DC, which wage elements enter the contribution base
  • Conversion clause. How retroactive scope and the reference date are set, and whether the once a year contribution schedule under Article 20, paragraph 3 is being kept
  • Wage peak clause. When the reduction applies and what preventive measure is written down
  • For DB, the most recent funding notice. Whether an Article 16, paragraph 2 result has ever been passed to the worker representative
  • For DC, the default option. Which product is designated and whether it currently applies to your account

13. Closing — three questions to answer first

Neither plan is superior. There is only a choice about whether someone else carries the risk or you do, and the value of that choice is set by your own circumstances.

  1. Which way does your wage curve run over the remaining years? If it rises, the DB multiplication works for you. If it is flat, or a wage peak is scheduled, the same multiplication runs the other way
  2. Can you absorb the variability of investment results? Not only the gains, but the case of retiring in a year that lost money
  3. Have you read what the consent form actually fixes? Retroactive scope, reference date, and the part that does not come back

If one of the three has no answer, that is the reason to delay signing and ask. The wider picture of retirement income, including the public scheme, continues in the National Pension guide and the IRP structure article.

This article is general information about the Korean retirement pension system and does not recommend any financial product or plan conversion. It is not investment or tax advice. Retirement income tax and pension income tax depend on individual circumstances, so confirm them with the competent tax office or a tax professional, and decide on any plan conversion after reading your own workplace retirement pension rules and consulting a labour professional.

What could not be confirmed

Stated plainly. The current minimum funding ratio for defined benefit plans is delegated to an Ordinance and a public notice of the Ministry of Employment and Labor, and that text could not be opened. Section 10 therefore reports only the statutory floor in Article 16, paragraph 1 (no less than sixty percent) and the delegating language in Article 5 of the Enforcement Decree (no less than eighty percent, as prescribed by Ordinance), without stating the percentage in force. Whether pledge backed lending is offered on defined benefit entitlements in practice is left open, since what a statute permits and what providers offer can differ. The ceiling on substitute payments under the Wage Claim Guarantee Act is set by public notice and split by age band, so no figure is given here. Wage peak designs, workplace rules and provider fees vary by employer and by date and do not generalise.
