Introduction: Defamation in the Digital Age

Social media, online communities, video platforms, and messaging apps have made it easier than ever for damaging statements to spread at scale. When someone posts false or harmful content about you online, knowing your rights and the proper response channels is essential. This guide outlines key concepts in online defamation law — primarily from a South Korean perspective — and provides a step-by-step approach to evidence preservation and legal response. Because laws differ significantly between countries and change over time, this is general information only and should not be relied upon without professional legal advice.

1. What Is Online Defamation?

Defamation is the public communication of a false or reputation-damaging statement about an identifiable person. Online (or cyber) defamation refers specifically to defamatory content distributed through the internet — social media posts, blog articles, comments, video descriptions, or messages.

In South Korea, online defamation is primarily governed by two statutes: Article 307 of the Criminal Act (general defamation) and Article 70 of the Act on Promotion of Information and Communications Network Utilization and Information Protection (the "Network Act"), which imposes heavier penalties for defamation conducted through information and communications networks. The aggravated penalties reflect the wider reach and faster propagation of online content.

2. Defamation Based on True Facts vs. False Statements

2.1 Defamation by True Statement

Under Network Act Article 70(1), even a true statement posted online can constitute defamation if it publicly damages someone's reputation and the poster acted with the purpose of harming them. The criminal penalty is up to three years of imprisonment or a fine of up to 30 million Korean won. This surprises many people who assume that truth is an absolute defense — it is not under Korean law.

However, Criminal Act Article 310 provides an exemption: if the statement is true and made solely in the public interest, criminal liability is excluded. Criticism of a public figure's official conduct, whistleblowing on genuine public concern, and similar disclosures may qualify. Private matters or disclosures unrelated to the public interest generally do not.

2.2 Defamation by False Statement

Network Act Article 70(2) imposes significantly heavier punishment — up to seven years of imprisonment, up to ten years of disqualification, or a fine of up to 50 million Korean won — for spreading false information that damages reputation. Both the falsehood and the purpose of harming the victim must be established.

2.3 The "Purpose of Defamation" Requirement

Both paragraphs of Article 70 require that the accused acted with a purpose of harming the victim's reputation. This element is separate from the fact that the statement damages reputation; courts evaluate intent based on context, relationship between the parties, and the nature of the content. Genuine public-interest reporting and good-faith criticism may negate this element.

3. Defamation vs. Insult: Key Differences

A closely related offense in Korea is insult under Criminal Act Article 311. The dividing line is whether a specific, verifiable fact was stated.

4. Elements Required for Criminal Defamation

4.1 Publicity (Public Accessibility)

The statement must be made in a way that unspecified or multiple persons can perceive it. Public posts and comments clearly meet this threshold. Group messaging chats with many members can also satisfy the publicity element. Even one-to-one messages may qualify if the content foreseeably spreads to third parties (the "transmission possibility" doctrine).

4.2 Identifiability

The victim must be identifiable to third parties — not necessarily by name. Nicknames, photographs, job titles, or contextual details that allow identification are sufficient. Statements that criticize an unidentifiable general category of persons typically do not satisfy this element.

4.3 Reputation-Damaging Content

The content must be capable of lowering the target's social standing. Pure opinions or value judgments — "I think this product is bad" — are generally not factual assertions and may be treated as insult rather than defamation. "Fact" in this context means a specific, time-bound, verifiable statement, as opposed to abstract commentary.

5. Evidence Collection and Preservation

Online posts can be deleted at any time. Act immediately upon discovering potentially defamatory content.

5.1 Screenshots

  • Capture the full page with the URL bar visible. Partial screenshots have lower evidentiary value.
  • Include the posting date, author's username or ID, and visible metrics (likes, comments, views).
  • Save files at original resolution; include the date in the filename.
  • Back up to multiple devices and cloud storage immediately.

5.2 URL and Metadata Records

  • Copy the full URL into a separate text file and take an additional screenshot showing the address bar.
  • Archive the URL with a web archiving service (e.g., web.archive.org) to create an independent third-party record.
  • For emails or messages, preserve complete headers — sender IP, timestamps, and routing information.

5.3 Notarization

Having an attorney or notary personally access, capture, and certify the defamatory content creates a legally robust record that is difficult to challenge as fabricated. This is especially valuable for major disputes where the original may be deleted before legal proceedings conclude.

5.4 Platform Data Preservation Request

Contact the platform's support to request data preservation before deletion. Once a criminal complaint is filed, law enforcement can formally request the platform to preserve relevant records. Preservation requests through legal channels are more reliable than informal contact.

6. Response Procedures

6.1 Takedown Request to the Platform

The fastest remedy is using the platform's own reporting mechanism. Under Network Act Article 44-2, victims can request service providers to take temporary measures (blinding or deletion). Platforms typically review requests and may temporarily restrict access. Note that the poster can appeal temporary measures, so this step should be combined with formal legal action when necessary.

6.2 Report to the Korea Communications Standards Commission (KCSC)

The KCSC (방심위) can order platforms to remove or restrict access to content after review. Reports can be filed online at cyberclear.kr. This is an administrative channel distinct from criminal prosecution and often produces content removal faster than civil litigation.

6.3 Criminal Complaint

Defamation and insult are complaint offenses in Korea — criminal prosecution can proceed only if the victim formally files a complaint. Network Act defamation is also a victim-withdrawal offense, meaning prosecution stops if the victim expressly states they do not wish punishment. Steps to file:

  1. Prepare a written complaint including: the respondent's identifying information (username, IP if known), the specific content, date, platform, and a list of attached evidence.
  2. Submit to the Cyber Investigation Unit at a police station or online through the Cyber Bureau.
  3. Police investigate (interview complainant, summon respondent), then refer to the prosecutor or decline referral.
  4. The prosecutor decides whether to indict or drop charges.

6.4 Civil Damages

Independent of criminal proceedings, victims can sue for civil damages (compensation and consolation money) under tort law. In Korea, the statute of limitations is three years from when the victim knew of the damage and the perpetrator, or ten years from the date of the unlawful act (Civil Act Article 766). Awarded amounts vary widely — typically hundreds of thousands to tens of millions of Korean won — based on the severity of harm, duration, and breadth of dissemination.

6.5 Request for Poster Identification

When the poster is anonymous, courts can order platforms to disclose user information under Network Act Article 44-6. Law enforcement may use warrants for the same purpose. Complete anonymization or overseas VPN use can make tracing difficult or impossible.

7. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Is a true post really illegal?

Under Korean law, yes — even a true statement publicly damaging someone's reputation can constitute defamation. The public-interest exemption (Criminal Act Article 310) may apply to genuine whistleblowing or criticism of public figures' official conduct, but not to private-life disclosures unrelated to the public interest.

Q2. Does Korean law apply to content posted on overseas platforms?

If the victim is in Korea, the content is in Korean, and it targets a Korean audience, Korean law may apply. Practical enforcement depends on the platform's willingness to cooperate and the poster's location. Overseas servers or VPNs can complicate prosecution.

Q3. Can I just request removal without filing a criminal complaint?

Yes. Platform reporting, KCSC complaint, and civil injunction for content removal are all available without filing a criminal complaint. You can pursue removal independently and decide later whether to escalate to criminal or civil proceedings.

Q4. What should I avoid doing when responding to online defamation?

Avoid retaliating with your own online posts that could constitute defamation or insult in return. Do not try to coerce or threaten the poster to delete content — this may itself constitute a criminal offense. Let legal processes handle enforcement. Also avoid filing complaints based on unfounded claims, as this can result in a counter-complaint for false reporting.

8. Key Takeaways

  • Online defamation in Korea covers both true and false statements if they damage reputation publicly.
  • False-statement defamation carries significantly heavier penalties.
  • Insult (no specific fact) and defamation (specific verifiable fact) are distinct offenses.
  • Collect and preserve evidence immediately — screenshots with URLs, metadata, and notarization for key cases.
  • Multiple response channels exist: platform takedown, KCSC report, criminal complaint, and civil damages suit.
  • Defamation is a complaint offense — only the victim can initiate criminal prosecution.
  • Laws vary by country; always consult a qualified attorney for your specific situation.
