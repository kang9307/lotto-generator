Lower Back Pain Relief Complete Guide — Causes, Stretches, Posture & Prevention

From Muscle Strain to Disc Herniation — What You Can Do at Home and When to See a Doctor

2026-06-25 Health & Wellness

Introduction: Why Is Lower Back Pain So Common?

Lower back pain affects an estimated 80% of adults at some point in their lives, making it one of the leading causes of disability worldwide. The rise of remote work, increased screen time, and sedentary lifestyles has made the problem even more widespread in recent years. Most episodes of acute lower back pain resolve on their own with appropriate self-care, but improper management can lead to chronicity or, in rare cases, mask a serious underlying condition.

This guide draws on information from the NHS, Mayo Clinic, and Spine-Health (as of June 2026) to give you a clear, evidence-based overview of lower back pain — its causes, what you can safely do at home, how to set up your workspace, and which warning signs demand prompt medical attention. This is general health information only and does not replace professional medical advice.

1. Common Causes of Lower Back Pain

1.1 Muscle and Ligament Strain (Most Common)

The majority of lower back pain is caused by strain of the muscles or ligaments supporting the spine. This can happen suddenly — lifting something heavy with poor technique, a sudden awkward twist — or gradually through repetitive strain and prolonged poor posture. Muscle strain typically responds well to rest, gentle movement, and over-the-counter pain relief, resolving within days to a few weeks.

1.2 Poor Posture and Prolonged Sitting

Sitting for long periods, especially with a rounded lower back or a forward-jutting neck, places sustained load on the lumbar spine. The lumbar region has a natural inward curve (lordosis); when this curve collapses during slouching, the muscles and discs absorb abnormal stress. According to Spine-Health, standing up and moving for at least one to two minutes every half hour is one of the most effective simple interventions for office-related lower back pain.

1.3 Herniated (Slipped) Disc

Between each vertebra sits a disc that acts as a shock absorber. When the soft inner material of a disc bulges or ruptures outward and presses on a nearby nerve, it causes back pain that often radiates down one leg (sciatica). According to the NHS, most herniated discs improve without surgery as the body gradually reabsorbs the disc material, usually within six to twelve weeks. Staying active within pain limits is generally encouraged.

1.4 Spinal Stenosis and Osteoarthritis

As we age, the spinal canal can narrow (stenosis) and the cartilage protecting joints can wear away (osteoarthritis). Stenosis characteristically causes pain and leg cramping that worsens with walking or standing and eases with sitting or leaning forward. These age-related conditions are a leading cause of back pain in people over 50.

1.5 Other Causes

Less common but important causes include compression fractures from osteoporosis, kidney stones, infections (discitis, vertebral osteomyelitis), and — rarely — tumours. Psychological factors such as chronic stress, anxiety, and poor sleep are increasingly recognised as significant contributors to persistent pain by sensitising the nervous system.

Current guidelines consistently recommend maintaining normal activities as much as possible during acute back pain. Complete bed rest delays recovery and leads to muscle deconditioning. The goal is to keep moving — gently and within your pain tolerance — rather than avoiding all movement.

3. Home Relief: What You Can Do Right Now

3.1 Heat vs Cold — Which to Use?

Cold therapy is generally recommended in the first 48–72 hours after a new injury. Wrap an ice pack in a cloth and apply for up to 20 minutes at a time, several times a day, to reduce swelling and numb acute pain. After the initial period, or for chronic muscle stiffness, heat therapy (a heat pad or warm bath for 15–20 minutes) relaxes tight muscles and improves blood flow. Some people find alternating heat and cold helpful. Never apply ice or heat directly to bare skin.

3.2 Five Effective Back Stretches

Gentle stretching relieves muscle tension and improves flexibility. Hold each position for 30–60 seconds, breathe steadily, and stop if pain sharpens.

  • Knee-to-Chest Stretch: Lie on your back with knees bent. Gently pull one knee toward your chest with both hands, hold 30 seconds, then switch. Eases lumbar muscle tension.
  • Cat-Cow Stretch: On hands and knees, inhale as you drop your belly and lift your head (cow), then exhale as you round your back toward the ceiling and tuck your chin (cat). Repeat 10 times. Improves spinal mobility.
  • Child's Pose: Kneel and sit back toward your heels, extending both arms forward on the floor until your forehead rests on the mat. Holds for up to 1 minute. Stretches the lower back and hip flexors.
  • Pelvic Tilt: Lie on your back, knees bent. Gently flatten your lower back against the floor by tightening your abdominal muscles. Hold 5 seconds, release. Repeat 10–15 times. Activates deep core muscles and corrects lumbar alignment.
  • Supine Spinal Twist: Lie on your back, draw both knees to your chest, then lower them slowly to one side while turning your head the opposite way. Hold 30–60 seconds per side. Releases tension in the lower back and hips.

3.3 Core Strengthening Exercises

Strong core muscles — the deep abdominals, back extensors, and pelvic floor — act as a natural corset for the spine. A 2021 randomised controlled trial published on PubMed found that four weeks of core stabilisation exercises significantly reduced pain and disability in patients with sub-acute non-specific lower back pain, outperforming general strengthening exercises alone.

  • Bird-Dog: On hands and knees, simultaneously extend your right arm and left leg until parallel to the floor. Hold 3–5 seconds. Return and switch sides. Builds spinal stabilisers without loading the lumbar spine heavily.
  • Dead Bug: Lie on your back, arms pointing to the ceiling, knees bent 90° above your hips. Slowly lower your right arm and left leg toward the floor without letting your back arch, then return. Alternate sides. Targets the deep transverse abdominis.
  • Glute Bridge: Lie on your back with knees bent. Press through your feet to lift your hips until your body forms a straight line from knees to shoulders. Hold 5–10 seconds, lower slowly. Strengthens glutes and core together.
  • Forearm Plank: Rest on forearms and toes, keeping your body in a straight line from head to heels. Start with 20–30 seconds, progress gradually. Engages the entire core without spinal loading.
Important: Avoid intense core exercises during the first 48–72 hours of an acute flare. Begin with gentle stretches, then progress to strengthening as pain allows. If any exercise worsens your symptoms, stop and seek professional advice.

4. Posture and Ergonomics

4.1 Sitting Correctly

Set your chair so your feet rest flat on the floor (or on a footrest), your knees are at or slightly below hip level, and your lower back is fully supported by the chair back. Keep your monitor at eye level so your neck stays neutral. Shoulders should be relaxed and pulled back slightly — not hunched forward. The single most impactful ergonomic habit is simply getting up to move for one to two minutes every 30 minutes.

4.2 Safe Lifting Technique

When lifting objects from the floor, stand close to the load, lower yourself by bending at the knees and hips (not the waist), grip the object firmly, and rise by pushing through your legs while keeping your back straight. Never twist your spine while lifting — pivot with your feet instead. Split heavy loads into smaller, lighter ones where possible.

4.3 Sleep Position

Side sleeping with a pillow between your knees keeps the spine neutral and reduces stress on the lower back. If you prefer to sleep on your back, place a pillow under your knees to maintain the lumbar curve. Stomach sleeping is generally not advised as it hyperextends the lower back and strains the neck.

5. Red Flags — When to Seek Emergency Care

Go to an emergency department immediately if you notice:
  • Sudden weakness or paralysis in one or both legs, or difficulty walking
  • Loss of bladder control (incontinence or inability to urinate / urinary retention)
  • Loss of bowel control (faecal incontinence)
  • Numbness or altered sensation around the groin, inner thighs, perineum, or buttocks (saddle anaesthesia)
  • Fever over 38°C (100.4°F), unexplained night sweats, or significant unintentional weight loss accompanying back pain
  • Severe back pain following a fall, road traffic accident, or other trauma
  • Back pain in someone with a known cancer history, immunosuppression, or long-term steroid use

The NHS warns that bladder/bowel symptoms combined with saddle numbness may indicate cauda equina syndrome — a rare but serious condition requiring emergency surgery. Delay in treatment can result in permanent paralysis and loss of bladder and bowel function.

Additionally, back pain that is progressively worsening, does not improve after 6 weeks of self-care, or is associated with pain that is worse at night warrants a non-urgent but timely medical consultation.

6. Medical Treatment Options

If self-care is insufficient, the following treatments may be offered depending on your diagnosis:

  • Medications: NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen) reduce inflammation and pain. Muscle relaxants may be prescribed for short-term spasm. Nerve pain medications (e.g., gabapentin) are used for radiating leg pain.
  • Physical Therapy: A physiotherapist provides a personalised exercise programme, manual therapy, and education. Evidence supports physical therapy as more effective than medication alone for long-term outcomes.
  • Imaging: X-rays, MRI, or CT scans are recommended only when red flags are present or when conservative treatment has failed after 6 weeks — not for routine acute back pain.
  • Injections: Epidural steroid injections can provide short-term relief of severe radiating leg pain but do not alter long-term outcomes.
  • Surgery: Reserved for structural problems unresponsive to conservative care, or for emergencies such as cauda equina syndrome. The vast majority of lower back pain resolves without surgery.

7. Prevention — Building Long-Term Back Health

7.1 Stay Regularly Active

Low-impact aerobic exercise — walking, swimming, cycling — performed for at least 30 minutes five days a week maintains muscle endurance and helps with weight control. Yoga, Pilates, and tai chi have been shown to improve both core strength and flexibility, and are supported by multiple studies for their role in reducing recurrent lower back pain.

7.2 Maintain a Healthy Weight

Excess body weight, particularly around the abdomen, shifts the centre of gravity forward and increases the load on lumbar vertebrae and discs. Even modest weight reduction has been shown to reduce lower back pain intensity.

7.3 Quit Smoking

Smoking impairs blood flow to spinal discs, accelerating degeneration. Smokers have a significantly higher incidence of disc herniation and chronic back pain compared to non-smokers. Quitting is one of the most evidence-backed lifestyle changes for spinal health.

7.4 Manage Stress and Sleep

Chronic stress and poor sleep amplify pain perception. Aim for 7–9 hours of sleep per night and practise stress-reduction techniques such as mindfulness meditation, breathing exercises, or regular leisure activities.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Should I rest or keep moving with lower back pain?

Current evidence strongly supports staying gently active during most episodes of acute lower back pain. Strict bed rest leads to muscle weakness and delays recovery. Walk at a comfortable pace, do gentle stretches, and avoid activities that sharply worsen your pain — but do not stop moving altogether.

Q. How long does lower back pain usually last?

Most acute episodes of lower back pain improve significantly within 2–4 weeks and resolve fully by 6 weeks with appropriate self-care. Around 20–30% of people develop sub-acute or chronic pain, particularly if they have psychosocial risk factors such as high job dissatisfaction, depression, or catastrophising about pain.

Q. Is a herniated disc always painful?

Not necessarily. Many people have disc herniations that show on MRI but cause no symptoms. Pain occurs only when the herniated material presses on or irritates a nerve. When symptoms do occur, most resolve with conservative treatment within 6–12 weeks as the body reabsorbs the disc material.

Q. Can I take ibuprofen for back pain?

NSAIDs such as ibuprofen can be effective for short-term back pain relief. However, they should be used at the lowest effective dose for the shortest necessary period. Consult a pharmacist or doctor first if you have stomach ulcers, kidney problems, heart disease, or are pregnant. Pain medication addresses symptoms, not underlying causes — combine it with movement and, where appropriate, professional therapy.
