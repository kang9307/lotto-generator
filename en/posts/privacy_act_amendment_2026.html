10% of Revenue - What Korea's Amended Personal Information Protection Act Requires of Companies From September 11, 2026

Punitive fines, a mandatory reduction tied to preventive investment, board-level CPO governance, and the new breach-possibility notice — what is new, what is an expansion, and what waits until July 2027 (September 2026)

2026-09-12 Law & Legal
⚠️ Legal information notice — This article explains a publicly announced regulatory change in the Republic of Korea and is not legal advice. Whether any obligation applies to a particular organisation, and what sanction follows, is determined by the governing statutes and by the Personal Information Protection Commission. Introduction: What Changed on 11 September

If you handle personal data compliance for an organisation operating in Korea, one item landed on your desk in the second week of September. The amended Personal Information Protection Act, passed in March, and the amended Enforcement Decree that fills in the details it delegated, both took effect on 11 September 2026. The fact that the Act and the Decree entered into force on the same day matters. The Act sets direction and the Decree supplies thresholds and procedure, so the two have to be read together before you can tell what actually applies to your organisation.

What changed is not a handful of clauses but where responsibility sits. The ceiling for punitive administrative fines moved to within 10% of total revenue. Ultimate accountability is now explicitly attached to the business owner or chief executive. Appointing a Chief Privacy Officer, referred to throughout Korean practice as the CPO, became a matter for the board rather than a personnel memo. And running in the opposite direction inside the very same amendment is a mechanism that gets far less attention: organisations that invested in prevention beforehand are subject to a mandatory reduction of the fine. The penalty ceiling gets quoted everywhere; this half rarely does. For anyone building a security budget, this is the half that is actually actionable.

This article is not about what an individual should do after receiving a breach notice. That axis is covered separately in Data Breach Response Guide — How to Check, Act, Report, and Prevent Harm. What is covered here is the obligations that fall on companies and public bodies from 11 September: which of them are genuinely new, which are expansions of duties that already existed, why there are two different commencement dates, and what the published material does not yet say. The source is a single press announcement issued by Korea's Personal Information Protection Commission on 10 September 2026. Figures that are not in that announcement are not in this article either.

1. The Shape of the Amendment — Four Strands

The announcement gives the background in one sentence: large-scale personal data breaches have occurred one after another in sectors closely tied to everyday life, and public anxiety and concern have persisted. It does not name a company or an incident. It is a sentence explaining why the rules were changed, not a sentence identifying a particular case, so this article does not identify one either.

The substance splits into four strands. The first three commenced together on 11 September. Only the fourth has a different date.

StrandContentsIn force
① Sanction and incentive for breachesPunitive administrative fine (within 10% of total revenue) · mandatory fine reduction linked to proactive preventive investment2026-09-11
② Accountability and governanceExplicit ultimate accountability of the owner/CEO · strengthened powers for the CPO · mandatory board resolution and filing when a CPO is appointed, changed, or removed2026-09-11
③ Remedies for data subjectsNew breach-possibility notice · expanded breach notification items · forgery, alteration, and destruction brought within reporting and notification2026-09-11
④ Mandatory certificationMandatory ISMS-P certification for significant personal data controllers in the public and private sectors2027-07-01 (scheduled)

The three subheadings of the announcement repeat the same skeleton. First, firm enforcement where breaches are repeated or large-scale and caused by intent or gross negligence, with incentives for preventive investment. Second, stronger powers and responsibilities for the CPO, with a mandatory board resolution and a filing to the Commission. Third, the introduction of the breach-possibility notice regime, together with an expansion of both the items and the scope of breach notification. Sanction, accountability, and remedy moved as one package.

2. Reading Within 10% of Total Revenue Precisely

This is the most-quoted number in the amendment and also the one most often carried across inaccurately. The original wording is a punitive administrative fine — within 10% of total revenue — for personal data breaches that are repeated or caused by intent or gross negligence. Three expressions inside that sentence deserve to be pulled apart.

  • Repeated, or caused by intent or gross negligence — the provision is not structured so that the mere fact of a breach attaches it. A behavioural qualifier sits in front: repetition, or intent, or gross negligence.
  • Total revenue — the announcement uses total revenue, not revenue attributable to the part of the business connected with the violation. When the base widens, the same percentage produces a very different absolute figure.
  • Within 10% — this is a ceiling, not a rate. Once within is the operative word, an actual imposition can sit anywhere below it.

So what decides where below it an imposition lands? The announcement says only that the amended Enforcement Decree specifies the criteria and procedure for imposing the fine. There is no formula, no set of rate bands, and no published list of aggravating and mitigating factors in the body of the announcement. This article therefore does not fill that gap. Whether an individual incident could fall under the provision, and if so at what level, is a matter decided by the Personal Information Protection Commission under the criteria and procedure the Decree establishes.

What this sentence changes in practice is not a calculation but a reporting line. The moment a ceiling is indexed to total revenue, a personal data incident stops being solely an information security matter and becomes a financial exposure. If your risk register either has no personal data entry or carries one graded only in qualitative terms, this amendment is effectively an instruction to rewrite that row.

3. The Half Nobody Quotes — Mandatory Fine Reduction Tied to Preventive Investment

It was introduced in the same paragraph of the same announcement, yet the two receive incomparable amounts of attention. The original phrase is a mandatory administrative fine reduction scheme linked to proactive personal data protection investment. It is short, but every element points somewhere specific.

  • Proactive, or ex ante — what is assessed is investment executed before an incident, not cooperation or remediation after one. Timing is part of the qualification.
  • Linked to investment — the hook is not a vague notion of maturity or attitude but investment, which is a measurable act with a date and an amount attached.
  • Mandatory reduction — the scheme is named mandatory rather than discretionary, which signals a structure where meeting the criteria produces a reduction rather than merely allowing one.

How large is the reduction? The announcement states only that the amended Decree specifies the reduction criteria for proactive preventive investment. It does not give a percentage, does not say which categories of spending count as preventive investment, and says nothing about caps or how multiple grounds interact. If you encounter material that states those numbers confidently, it is worth checking where the numbers came from before relying on them. This article goes no further than the sentence the announcement actually published.

3.1 Why This Axis Matters More to the Budget Holder

When the ceiling on a fine rises, the absolute value of any reduction rises with it. If you read only the ceiling, preventive spending stays framed as a cost centre and the conversation with finance stays defensive. If you design the same spending so that it maps onto the reduction criteria, the expenditure reduces risk and simultaneously becomes material that can be put forward at the sanctioning stage. The structure the amendment creates is not simply harsher penalties; it is harsher penalties with relief for those who prepared in advance. Miss the second half and you have read half the amendment.

3.2 Having Invested and Being Able to Prove It Are Different Things

Whatever the criteria turn out to be, investment has to exist on paper before it can be considered. That is not an interpretation of this particular scheme; it is a general property of administrative process. Here are the pieces most often missing when organisations look for them under pressure.

  • Date of execution — contract, approval, and acceptance dates that show the spending happened before the incident
  • Amount and accounting treatment — where security budget is blended into general IT budget, separating it after the fact is painful
  • Scope of application — which systems, which databases, which business processes the control actually covers
  • Operational evidence — a control that was purchased and then left disabled is hard to present as an investment
  • Review cadence and outcomes — periodic assessment records, findings raised, and whether remediation closed
  • Reporting trail — what was reported to executives and to the board, and when
  • People and training — hiring and assignment of specialist staff, and training completion records
  • Vendor and processor oversight — evidence that assessments of processors were actually carried out rather than merely contracted for

None of this is a claim that the list above constitutes the statutory criteria. The criteria are set by the Decree. What does not change, whichever criteria apply, is that without these records there is nothing to explain with.

4. Accountability Moved — CEO Responsibility and CPO Powers

The second strand bundles three things. It makes the ultimate accountability of the business owner or chief executive explicit, strengthens the CPO's remit — specifically covering management of specialist personnel, securing budget, and a duty to report to the board — and makes a board resolution and a filing mandatory when the CPO is appointed, changed, or removed.

Taken individually each of the three sounds unremarkable. Taken together the design intent shows. Accountability moves upward, authority moves to the person who actually does the work, and the reporting line is wired to the board. Remove any one of the three and the structure collapses. Push accountability upward alone and the CPO keeps the blame without the levers. Grant authority alone and ultimate responsibility becomes diffuse. Omit the reporting line and the authority quietly evaporates during budget review.

The explicit mention of managing specialist personnel and securing budget as part of the CPO's remit targets a long-standing practical complaint precisely. Privacy functions have historically been the ones held responsible after an incident while holding weak grounds on which to demand headcount and money beforehand. The duty to report to the board belongs to the same logic. A reporting duty also creates an obligation on the side that receives the report, which is often the more consequential half.

5. A Preparation Sequence for Organisations That Now Need Board Approval

This is the item with the most immediate operational load. Once choosing a CPO shifts from a personnel decision to a board agenda item, the speed at which you can change the person is bound to the board calendar. For an organisation within scope, the following sequence works through it quickly.

  1. Establish whether you are in scope at all. The announcement states only that the Decree specifies the scope of organisations subject to the board-resolution and filing requirement, along with the filing method. The thresholds are not in the announcement, so they have to be read straight from the Decree.
  2. Find the document your current CPO appointment rests on. Whether it is an HR order, an internal regulation, or nothing more than a line in the published privacy policy changes what the next step looks like.
  3. Draft the board agenda template. A standard item covering the rationale, qualifications, scope of duties, and reporting structure means you are not writing it from scratch each time the holder changes.
  4. Confirm the filing route that follows the resolution. The resolution and the filing are separate steps. Where and how the filing is made is set out in the Decree.
  5. Reflect changes and removals in the HR process. Covering the appointment and forgetting the replacement or the departure is the single most common gap.
  6. Decide how to handle a vacancy. Write into internal rules how the gap between a departure and the next board meeting is managed.
  7. Design the board reporting cadence. With a reporting duty in place, put the reporting dates and the standing agenda items into the annual calendar rather than improvising them.
  8. Keep the records. Resolutions, filing receipts, and reporting packs belong in one place. The point about provability from the previous section applies here without modification.

Groups with multiple legal entities or branches need a per-entity assessment. Whether the threshold applies at entity level, and whether a group-wide shared CPO arrangement satisfies it, are questions to be answered against the text of the Decree and guidance from the Commission rather than assumed.

6. The Data Subject Side — Breach-Possibility Notice and Expanded Items

The third strand is about remedies, and it contains one genuinely new regime and one expansion.

6.1 New: the Breach-Possibility Notice

The existing notification architecture engaged after a breach was confirmed. What the amendment adds is a notice regime for the possibility of a breach, pulling the pre-confirmation window inside the statutory framework. The practical difference is not small. The stretch during which an intrusion has been detected but its scope and volume are not yet pinned down is the longest and most chaotic phase of incident response, and it is precisely that phase that now carries a notification judgment.

The announcement states that the requirements and procedure are set out in the Decree. How much evidence, and of what kind, triggers the duty is therefore something to settle in advance from the text of the Decree rather than in the middle of an incident.

6.2 Expanded: Breach Notification Items

The items that a breach notification must contain have been expanded to include how to apply for dispute mediation and how to claim damages. That shifts the notification from a document that tells people what was exposed to a document that also routes them toward redress. Drafting a standard notification after an incident starts is too late. These are the pieces that can be prepared in advance.

  • A template with slots for the categories of data affected and the timeline
  • Standing wording explaining how to apply for dispute mediation
  • Standing wording explaining how to pursue a damages claim
  • A contact channel and a response script for the people staffing it
  • Channel-specific versions (email, SMS, website notice) and a fallback where delivery fails
  • A method for recording the notification trail — who was notified, when, and through which channel

7. Ransomware Counts — Forgery, Alteration, and Destruction Brought In

This is the quietest item in the third strand and the one that most concretely rewrites incident response procedure. Forgery, alteration, or destruction of personal data — with ransomware given as the example — is now within the scope of breach reporting and notification.

The older intuition was simple. A breach meant data went out; data that was encrypted and rendered unusable was an availability incident. A great many incident response playbooks are built on top of that split. This amendment erases the boundary. An incident where nothing left the perimeter but data was damaged inside it now falls within reporting and notification.

The points in the playbook that need attention are usually these.

  • In the incident classification matrix, a category reading no exfiltration, encryption damage only no longer implies an exemption from reporting
  • A notification and reporting decision gate belongs early in the ransomware response flow, not at the end of it
  • Restoring from backup does not erase the fact that data was damaged — successful recovery is not the disappearance of the event
  • Forgery and alteration are hard to detect; without integrity verification, an incident can pass unnoticed entirely
  • Short log retention makes it impossible to establish when the alteration occurred

8. Two Commencement Dates — 11 September and 1 July 2027

This is where confusion shows up most often. The amendment also covers mandatory ISMS-P certification — Korea's combined information security and personal information management system certification — for significant personal data controllers in the public and private sectors. That provision did not commence on 11 September. The announcement states it is scheduled to take effect on 1 July 2027, in view of the time organisations need to secure the budget required to obtain the certification.

There is a second layer here. The announcement also states that the Enforcement Decree amendments relating to mandatory ISMS-P will be pursued separately, before that statutory provision takes effect. In other words, you can read the Decree that commenced on 11 September from beginning to end and still not find the scope or procedure for mandatory ISMS-P. Knowing that structure in advance saves a great deal of time otherwise spent searching for text that is not there.

9. Separating Genuinely New Duties From Expanded Ones

This distinction is useful when you are sequencing the work. A genuinely new duty requires building a process that did not exist, which takes time. An expansion of an existing duty usually means changing an input or a piece of wording inside a process that already runs, which is comparatively fast.

Read the table across and the priority order emerges. Revising notification wording and the incident classification matrix is work you can start this week. Building the board agenda machinery and organising investment evidence is quarterly work. ISMS-P is next year's budget cycle.

10. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Is 10% of total revenue an automatic penalty whenever a breach happens?

No. The wording is a punitive administrative fine, within 10% of total revenue, for breaches that are repeated or caused by intent or gross negligence. The 10% is a ceiling expressed as within, and a behavioural condition sits in front of it. Whether a given incident could fall under the provision, and at what level, is decided by the Personal Information Protection Commission under the imposition criteria and procedure set out in the Enforcement Decree.

Q2. What kind of investment qualifies for the reduction?

The announcement says only that the Decree sets out the reduction criteria for proactive preventive investment. Neither the qualifying categories nor the size of the reduction appears in the text. Two things are clear: the assessment point is before the incident, and the scheme is named a mandatory rather than discretionary reduction. The operative criteria have to be read from the Decree and from Commission guidance.

Q3. Does our organisation need a board resolution to appoint a CPO?

The announcement states that the Decree sets the scope, but the thresholds themselves are not in the announcement, so they have to be checked against the Decree directly. For organisations within scope, the same procedure applies to changes and removals as to the initial appointment, which needs to be reflected in both the HR process and the board calendar.

Q4. How is the breach-possibility notice different from existing notification?

Existing notification engaged after a breach was confirmed. The new regime brings the pre-confirmation possibility stage inside the framework. Combined with the expansion of notification items, the trigger moves earlier while the content grows. The requirements and procedure are set out in the Decree.

Q5. Should we start preparing for mandatory ISMS-P now?

Commencement is scheduled for 1 July 2027. Since the stated reason for the deferral is the time needed to secure budget, an organisation that may fall within scope would sensibly place the item in the next budget cycle. That said, the Decree amendment defining scope and procedure is to be pursued separately before the provision takes effect, so the detailed criteria should be treated as not yet published.
