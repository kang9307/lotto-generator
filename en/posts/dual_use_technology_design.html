Where the decision to keep one log line gets made

The place this judgement actually happens is not a meeting room. It is one ticket, one review comment. A payment failure cannot be reproduced, so someone proposes logging the whole request body, the change is three lines, and it merges the same day. Retention follows the default, and the default is usually the generous one.

A few months later that log is in the warehouse, on an analytics dashboard, and inside a dozen backup snapshots. The original decision was three lines; undoing it takes three teams. Defaults outlive decisions.

There is a post from November 2013. It is called Civic Hygiene, and the claim is simple. Data gathered today for a good reason can end up in other hands later, so do not build systems suited to that use in the first place. Thirteen years have passed. This piece runs that comparison first, then moves the result into three implementation decisions: logs, identifiers and deletion.

What the 2013 essay actually argued

The original is a short post by Terence Eden. The argument folds into three steps.

  • Collection for a good purpose does not keep that purpose. The example is a national register of sexual orientation. It has value for health planning, but if a faction hostile to that attribute takes power, the same data becomes a list for selecting targets.
  • So the test is not the current operator. In the essay author's own phrasing, it is about not trusting the next government rather than the current one. What you are trusting is not an organisation but time, and that is the core.
  • Not every database is the same grade. A vehicle owner register, with clear public benefit and little value as an instrument of persecution, is treated as reasonable. Central accumulation of attributes that have historically been the basis for selective persecution is what the essay says to avoid.

The phrase civic hygiene is not the author's own. It quotes the security researcher Bruce Schneier, rendered there as It is bad civic hygiene to build technologies that could someday be used to facilitate a police state. The metaphor was well chosen. Hygiene is not aimed at a particular pathogen; it is the habit of removing in advance the conditions in which illness spreads.

There is a weak link from the start, though. Because hygiene is a habit, nobody keeps it unless who is responsible is settled. This essay placed that subject on the individual builder, and a norm that lives only as personal professional ethics is generally weak against market pressure and an employer's instructions.

What held up 13 years later - principles became statute, limits became numbers

The norm crossed into enforceable text

The biggest change is the subject of the sentence. What hung on the builder's conscience in 2013 is now written as an obligation placed on the operator.

  • GDPR Article 5 states data minimisation, purpose limitation and storage limitation as principles of processing. All three used to be design advice.
  • GDPR Article 25 requires them to be implemented at design time and in defaults. The article is literally titled data protection by design and by default. It has applied since 25 May 2018.
  • ISO 31700-1:2023 was published in February 2023 as a privacy by design standard for consumer goods and services. If the seven principles of 2009 were the conceptual layer, this standard is the layer that comes down as 30 requirements.
  • EU AI Act Article 5 goes one step further. It is not manage the risk but do not do it. Applicable since 2 February 2025, its eight items include untargeted scraping of facial images to build recognition databases, biometric categorisation inferring race, political opinion, religion or sex life, and emotion recognition in workplaces and educational institutions.

In the 2013 essay's own terms, civic hygiene crossed from recommendation into statute. A judgement in the same direction arrived even earlier. On 8 April 2014 the Court of Justice of the European Union declared the communications data retention directive invalid, not because the purpose was improper but because the interference went beyond what was strictly necessary. That was five months after the essay.

The belief that anonymisation suffices broke on a number

The other thing it got right is the intuition that removing a name does not remove the person. A 2019 study in Nature Communications turned this into something estimable. Its conclusion was that 15 demographic attributes are enough to correctly re-identify 99.98 percent of Americans in any dataset.

The implication is simple. Dropping the identifier column is not anonymisation. If the remaining combination of attributes narrows the field enough, the dataset still points at a person; it merely has no name attached. The U.S. Census Bureau applied differential privacy to the published statistics of the 2020 census and gave reconstruction attacks as the reason: published aggregate tables alone can revive a substantial share of individual records.

What missed 13 years later - where it piled up, and how

As much as it got right, some of it missed. More precisely, the essay was not wrong so much as the shape of the threat differed from the forecast.

Premise of the 2013 essayShape observable as of 2026
Risk takes the shape of a central register built by a stateThe AI Act prohibition list includes face recognition databases built by untargeted scraping. Accumulation does not only take the form of an official register
If you never create the sensitive field, that information does not existFifteen attributes are enough for re-identification. There is a range in which removing one field does not remove linkability
If you do not collect it, you cannot know itThe same list covers categorisation that infers sensitive attributes from biometric data. That is a signal that the focus moved from collection to inference
Deleting makes it disappearData used for training stays in the model even after the row is gone. A 2021 USENIX Security paper showed that language models can reproduce training data
Encryption will stay the exception for a long timeTransport encryption became the default. By a free certificate authority's 2025 retrospective, the global share of HTTPS page loads climbed from under 30 percent to around 80 percent in five years

The most important mismatch is the third row. The 2013 logic rested on the premise that if you do not create a field, that fact is not in the system. Today the premise holds only in part. Without a religion column, location history plus payment times plus access patterns will still produce an estimate of that attribute. That information not stored can be derived is the biggest change of the past 13 years.

So today there are two defensive lines. What not to store is the first, and what not to leave linkable is the second. The second axis was absent from the 2013 essay, and it is now the one that fails more often.

Getting the design vocabulary right - minimisation, purpose, retention, pseudonymisation

These words overlap with everyday speech and blur easily. Here they are, precise enough to write into a design document.

TermWhat it settlesWhere it splits in practice
Data minimisationLimit to what is adequate and necessary for the purposeThe test sentence is whether the feature fails without this field. Whether it is convenient is not the test
Purpose limitationNo further processing incompatible with the specified, explicit purposeWhat actually trips is not new collection but reuse: the moment a new feature reaches for existing logs
Storage limitationDo not keep it in identifiable form beyond the necessary periodThe qualifier is identifiable form. Besides destruction there is the option of converting to a form that points at nobody
PseudonymisationMake individuals unidentifiable without additional informationWhile that additional information exists it stays inside the regime. Korea codified this in the Data 3 Acts in force from 2020-08-05
AnonymisationMake reversal impossibleIf it holds, it leaves the regime, but judging whether it holds is hard. That is exactly what the re-identification study targets
Differential privacyMathematically bound the effect one record's presence has on the outputIt trades accuracy for noise. It protects the source data; it does not remove it

This vocabulary earns its keep in review. Opposing a new field with this feels a bit risky turns the discussion into a clash of taste. Asking what the purpose is, how long it is kept and what it becomes afterwards brings the discussion back to design.

Decision one - how much to log

Logging is the area in this subject decided most often and reviewed least. Five things actually split.

  • Field width. Request path, status code and latency are used in nearly every incident. Full query strings, request bodies and complete headers are used only when something goes wrong. Put both behind the same default and the second group piles up quietly.
  • Tiered retention. Seven days raw plus 400 days aggregated saves more than 30 days for everything. Incident work usually ends within the last few days, and trend judgements need only aggregates that point at nobody.
  • Sampling. A fraction of normal traffic, all errors and slow requests. It cuts cost, but the more important effect is that no per-person record accumulates in ordinary times.
  • Identifier form. Keep the raw IP, truncate the last octet, or hash with a rotating salt. Truncation merges several users on the same range; a rotating hash breaks tracking past the rotation period. That break is a gain if it is the point, and a loss if long-horizon abuse work needs it.
  • The threshold for schema changes. Adding a log field is the change that clears review most easily. One rule requiring why it is needed and until when it is kept works better than ten lines of principle.

Where log levels and field design tangle is covered separately in why WARN becomes a landfill.

Decision two - how to build identifiers

An identifier, once chosen, is effectively irreversible. After the value has spread through tables, event streams and external integrations, changing it costs as much as a rewrite.

  • Do not use natural keys. Make an email or phone number the primary key and that value rides every join path, every log and every outbound transfer. Erasing it means sweeping the whole system, and because the value is itself a means of contact, a leak realises harm immediately.
  • Use opaque identifiers. Sequential numbers leak the total user count and signup order; mixing in a birth date carries an attribute in the key itself.
  • Global or per-context. This is the biggest decision. When every service shares one user ID, linkage becomes easy, and that ease is both the convenience and the risk. Issuing a different value per relying party exists in standard authentication protocols, and its purpose is precisely to stop records in different places from being joined.
  • Hashing is not anonymisation. A narrow value space with fixed format and length, such as a phone number, can be reversed by exhaustive search in practice. Unless a secret key and key management come with it, the fact that you hashed is not a defence.
  • Leave room for reset. Letting users change the value breaks the accumulated history. That is why advertising identifiers are designed that way.
  • Deliberately not creating a join key. Without a common key, merging later becomes hard. That is a cost and simultaneously a defence. The call turns on whether the product actually needs that join.

How far IPs, identifiers and logs are treated as personal data is laid out from the user's side in personal data access requests. Read from the builder's side, it is a list of what you will later have to explain.

Decision three - implementing deletion as deletion

Deletion is the classic item whose spec is one line and whose implementation is ten places. Count the points one request has to reach and it usually looks like this.

Point to reachWhy it is commonly missedUsual handling
Primary databaseImplementation often stops hereHard delete or anonymising update
Read replicas, caches, search indexesPeople assume the source is enoughPropagate invalidation events
Event streams and message queuesThe original survives the retention windowShorter topic retention, key-based compaction
Warehouse and analytics tablesThe pipeline belongs to another teamPeriodic application of the deletion list
Backup snapshotsThey cannot be overwritten immediatelyPut beyond use and let the normal cycle expire it
Processors and third-party recipientsIt is in the contract and not in the codeDeletion propagation API and confirmation
Derivatives such as aggregates and modelsThey are not recognised as deletion targetsConfirm aggregates cannot revive individuals; judge models separately

For backups there is useful public guidance. The right to erasure guidance from the UK Information Commissioner describes putting backup data that cannot be overwritten immediately beyond use and letting it expire on the normal cycle. It adds that the data must not be used for any other purpose meanwhile, and that people must be told how their data is handled in backups. In implementation this becomes a suppression list of erased identifiers that the restore procedure has to check. If restore has never been rehearsed, that path effectively does not exist.

Three implementation options genuinely compete.

  • Hard delete. The most honest, but it collides easily with referential integrity and record retention duties.
  • Anonymising update. Keep the record and replace only the values that point at a person. It preserves order history statistics while removing the individual, and it only works if you check that the remaining attribute combination does not narrow back down.
  • Crypto shredding. Encrypt under a per-user key and destroy the key on deletion. It neutralises data scattered across stores in one move, and key management complexity is the price.

There is a paradox here. Proving you deleted something requires a record. To keep that deletion history from becoming new personal data, you also have to decide what goes into it.

The other side - delete everything is not the answer

The cost of minimisation is usually billed to someone else

Read only this far and the conclusion is that minimisation is always right. It is not. The costs on the other side are real and are usually billed to someone else.

  • Without logs you cannot catch failures. And which field you will need afterwards cannot be known in advance. That is the intrinsic difficulty of logging, and the part minimisation arguments often ignore.
  • Short retention weakens abuse response. Account takeover and payment fraud frequently show up only in long-horizon patterns. Shorten the window and the cost moves to the victim's side.
  • Noise cuts accuracy. Differential privacy trades accuracy for privacy by construction. The census case exposed that trade publicly because those statistics drive apportionment and redistricting.
  • Some records the law requires, and anonymisation degrades quality. Items such as accounting and transaction data must be retained, so a deletion request does not carry straight through. Anomaly detection and personalisation lean on individual-level signal, so keeping only aggregates makes some analysis impossible.

The practical answer is therefore not minimum. It is as much as needed, for as long as needed, with the reason written down. The third of those goes missing most often. Without a stated reason, a proposal to shorten retention and a proposal to extend it are equally unfounded, and the stronger party wins.

Where dual use is useful as a term, and where it is not

One objection always follows. Any technology can be misused, so misusability alone filters nothing. That is half right. It cannot serve as a binary test. It can, however, work as an axis of degree, and three axes are worth using.

  • Linkability. Does the system join records that were previously apart? The stronger the joining power, the easier the repurposing.
  • Selectability. Can it single out only people with a given attribute? A system that answers only in statistics and one that produces a list are different grades.
  • Reversibility. Can it be undone when it goes wrong? Accumulation that cannot be erased fixes the moment of a decision permanently.

The 2013 intuition that a vehicle register is fine while a register of beliefs is not reconstructs along these three axes. The former has low selectability and a narrow purpose. For the latter, selectability is the reason it exists. Bring the same axes into a design meeting and the question changes. Are we building a system that produces statistics, or one that can produce a list?

Even if the answer is the latter, that does not mean do not build it. It means access control, audit records and retention have to be designed at a different grade. It is the same reason debates about surveillance technology converge on the controls rather than the equipment. Those debates are handled case by case in the vigilante movement against Flock cameras and the EU Chat Control legislation.

Read the legal axis only to the level of principles

Developers do not need to interpret statutory text. But knowing a few principles gives design discussions a shared language. Korea's Personal Information Protection Act runs on the same axes: minimal collection, limits on use beyond the purpose, and destruction once the purpose is met. The pseudonymised data regime opens up use while bringing linkage inside a procedure.

Provisions that require design-time treatment are not satisfied by after-the-fact patching. It means the default itself is what gets assessed. A prohibition list goes a step further and presumes that a design review can end in cancellation. Interpreting text and judging lawfulness belong to legal counsel, and there is no need to go that far. Even this much shared changes the conversation in review. Asking a proposed field about purpose, period and its state afterwards is not a compliance activity; it is just design.

What could not be verified

  • The first source and exact wording of the civic hygiene quote. The 2013 essay quotes it as bad civic hygiene, the widely circulated variant reads poor civic hygiene, and the verb splits between build and install. Several quotation collections cite a book from 2000, but the original page could not be checked directly.
  • What became of the individual UK policies the 2013 essay cited, and current operating statistics for Korea's pseudonymised data linkage regime. The former is out of scope here; for the latter only the fact of the regime was confirmed.
  • The latest primary observation for the HTTPS share. The figure here relies on a free certificate authority's 2025 retrospective; the browser vendor's transparency report figure could not be checked.
  • Actual adoption rates for crypto shredding and machine unlearning. Only the concepts and research findings were confirmed.

Conclusion - three things to take away

First, the 2013 imperative became a descriptive sentence 13 years later. An appeal not to build it moved into principles of minimisation, purpose and retention, then into a provision requiring implementation at design time, and in part into a prohibition list. The core is less that the norm got stronger and more that the subject changed from personal conscience to organisational obligation.

Second, the defensive line moved. The fact that you did not create the sensitive field is no longer a complete answer. Fifteen attributes suffice for re-identification, uncollected attributes get inferred, and deleted rows persist inside models. The question today is not only what you stored but what you left linkable.

Third, this judgement is made at the granularity of a code review. Log width and retention, identifier scope, deletion reach. All three are settled in tickets rather than policy documents, and once settled they become the default that stands in for the next decision. That is why the hygiene metaphor still works. It is not a grand resolution but a small habit repeated.

The sentence the 13-year-old essay left behind was about not trusting the next government rather than the current one. In the language of people who live in code it reads like this. Design for the team that inherits this table three years from now. Nobody knows who that team will be, and that is exactly the point.
