PM Hiring Has Changed - The Era of the 60-Second Demo Instead of a Resume

Why LinkedIn turned APM into APB, whether "the resume is dead" is overstated, and what applicants and employers should do

Jul 5, 2026 Trends & Culture

Introduction: A Job Application With No Resume Field Is No Longer Unusual

It would be jarring to open a job application and find no resume upload field at all. Yet in 2026, exactly that started to appear. LinkedIn renamed its entry-level PM program from Associate Product Manager (APM) to Associate Product Builder (APB), and began asking applicants to show, in a 60-second video, a real product that real users are already using - instead of submitting a resume. Around the same time, PM job postings at Amazon, Google, Anthropic, and Cursor started asking, in one form or another, "have you actually built something?"

This piece analyzes, as of July 2026, what has actually changed in PM hiring based on job postings and public statements, and how much truth there is to the "the resume era is over" narrative. This is worth reading beyond the PM role alone - it is a signal of where hiring criteria across the labor market are shifting.

1. What Actually Happened - Big Tech PM Job Postings Changed

Let's start with the facts. What we're seeing is not an isolated stunt but a pattern showing up simultaneously across multiple companies.

1.1 LinkedIn: From APM to APB, the Resume Field Itself Disappeared

LinkedIn's entry-level PM program was long known as APM. In 2026 it was restructured into APB (Associate Product Builder). It wasn't just a rename - the application process itself changed. There is no resume upload field. Instead, applicants must show a product real people are actually using in a 60-second video, and answer six questions covering the problem defined, what was built, their role, the impact, the tech stack used, and customer insight. LinkedIn's Chief Product Officer described this as part of a broader shift toward a "full-stack builder" organization that lowers boundaries between functions.

1.2 Amazon and Google: PMs Who Build Prototypes Directly

Amazon's Ring team hires under the title "Senior Product Manager, Builder," requiring hands-on experience building prototypes with AI coding tools like Cursor, Claude, and Replit. Amazon's Rufus team gives its GenAI-focused PM the job of directly designing AI-quality evaluation systems such as "LLM-as-a-judge." Google's posting for a PM on advertiser and business intelligence AI likewise specifies at least a year of hands-on GenAI/LLM experience as a requirement. All three cases share the same pattern: what's expected of a PM has shifted from "writing good requirement documents" to "directly producing results with AI tools."

1.3 Cursor and Anthropic: "We Don't Hire Traditional PMs"

Cursor (Anysphere), the AI coding tool company, states plainly in its job posting that it does "not hire traditional PMs," requiring candidates to be able to build and ship apps themselves. Anthropic's Claude Code PM posting similarly lists direct experience building agent evals and daily habitual use of Claude Code as requirements. At these companies, the PM role is defined less as a planner and more as someone who builds the product.

2. Why Now - The Structural Shift AI Created

It matters that this change didn't just become fashionable overnight. My reading is that three structural forces converged at this particular moment.

2.1 The Cost of Building a Prototype Collapsed to Near Zero

Just a few years ago, a PM turning an idea into a tangible form required borrowing time from a designer and an engineer. Now, a single AI coding tool lets one person build a working prototype in a day. This collapse in cost structure is the core driver behind the shift in hiring criteria. Once being able to build something becomes common, it's a natural next step for "have you actually built something before" to emerge as the differentiator.

2.2 The Resume Itself Lost Credibility

At the same time, the resume as a medium lost trust. As generative AI let anyone produce a polished resume and cover letter in seconds, recruiters found it harder to judge whether resume text actually reflected a candidate's real ability. Many hiring surveys report that a significant share of recruiters no longer view the traditional resume as a reliable signal - a reflection of the same trend. Doubt has grown over whether the ability to describe oneself in text still correlates as strongly with the ability to actually produce results.

2.3 The Evaluation Criterion Shifted From "Words" to "Output"

With both trends converging, recruiters naturally gravitated toward signals that are easier to verify. Resume text can be exaggerated or fabricated relatively easily, but an actually working prototype and the ability to explain it in 60 seconds are comparatively hard to fake. This is the backdrop for why the old hiring adage "show, don't tell" has regained force in the AI era.

3. Is "The Resume Is Dead" Overstated? A Balanced View

There's a point worth flagging here. Headlines declaring "the resume is dead" are catchy, but in my view they are only half right.

3.1 Why the Resume Is Still Needed

In large-scale hiring, the resume is still used as a first-pass filtering tool. When hundreds or thousands of people apply to a single posting, there simply isn't the time or headcount to watch every applicant's demo video individually. Structured information such as years of experience, prior companies, and the scale of projects handled is hard to glean from a demo video alone, and a resume remains the most efficient way to convey that information.

3.2 The Limits of Demos and Portfolios

Demo videos aren't a perfect signal either. A single well-produced 60-second video cannot show collaboration ability, judgment under crisis, or the skill of coordinating multiple stakeholders. It's also not easy to distinguish, using demos alone, between an applicant who recycles the same flashy side project across many companies and one who has quietly handled tedious but important coordination work on a real team.

3.3 The Reality: Resume Plus Demo, Not Resume Instead of Demo

Most job postings observed in practice don't eliminate the resume outright - they add a portfolio or demo submission field on top of it. Even LinkedIn's APB, which declared a full resume elimination, is closer to a special case; postings from Amazon and Google still assume a resume submission while additionally verifying hands-on experience and prototyping ability. So "the resume is no longer sufficient on its own" is a more accurate description than "the resume is dead."

4. Beyond PM - A Trend Spreading Across Job Functions

Reading this change as a PM-only quirk misses the bigger picture.

4.1 Developer Hiring Went Through This First

In fact, output-based hiring took root in software engineering roles first. GitHub commit history, open-source contributions, and coding tests have long been trusted more than resumes. What's happening in PM hiring is closer to this same trend arriving late - but in a more dramatic form, namely the outright removal of the resume field.

4.2 Spreading to Designers and Marketers

Portfolios have long mattered more than resumes in design hiring. More recently, a growing number of marketing job postings ask candidates to submit campaign performance data and content they've actually produced. As long as AI tools keep lowering the "cost of producing output" across functions, this spread is unlikely to stop at PM.

4.3 Korea's Job Market Is Still at an Early Stage

Most domestic PM job postings in Korea remain centered on career summaries and resumes. Still, postings from domestic AI startups and Korean offices of global tech firms that ask for a portfolio or prototype have begun to appear, gradually. Given that shifts in hiring standards at global tech companies have historically reached the Korean market with a lag of roughly six months to two years, it's worth keeping an eye on whether similar language spreads more broadly in Korean postings from the second half of 2026 onward.

5. Three Things Applicants Should Do Now

There's no need to see this trend purely as a threat. There are concrete ways to prepare.

5.1 Have Something You Built, However Small

It doesn't need to be an impressive full-scale service. A small tool built with an AI coding tool to solve a recurring problem at work, or a single prototype from a side project, is enough. What matters isn't scale but whether you have experience defining a problem and actually shaping it into something real.

5.2 Practice Explaining It in 60 Seconds

Compressing what you built, why, how, and what the result was into 60 seconds is a skill that takes more practice than it seems. Drafting a script around a three-part structure - problem, process, impact - and rehearsing it repeatedly makes a real difference when it counts.

5.3 Don't Hide Your Use of AI Tools - Show It

There's no reason to hide the fact that you used AI tools to build a prototype. On the contrary, being transparent about which tool you chose and why, and how you reviewed and refined an AI-generated draft into something of real value, is increasingly viewed favorably by recruiters.

6. Traps Employers Can Easily Fall Into

Companies that follow this trend uncritically can run into problems of their own.

6.1 Without a Scoring Rubric, Demos Just Introduce Bias

Judging candidates solely by "an impressive demo," without a standardized rubric, makes it easy to confuse strong presentation skills with strong problem-solving ability. Before introducing demos, employers need as clear an evaluation rubric as they would for resume screening.

6.2 A Demo Doesn't Show Collaboration Skill or Judgment

A 60-second demo is strong at showing something one person built alone, but skills like coordinating priorities across teams or exercising judgment under pressure need to be verified separately. It's safer to treat the demo not as a replacement for the resume, but as one signal among several used alongside resumes, reference checks, and behavioral interviews.

6.3 Avoiding the Loss of Strong Candidates From Non-Traditional Backgrounds

Making a demo or prototype an absolute requirement risks discouraging strong PM candidates who had less access to coding tools, or who built their careers outside English-speaking or technical backgrounds, from applying at all. Employers should make clear that the bar is "willingness to try lightly with an AI tool," not "professional development ability," to avoid inadvertently losing talent.

Conclusion: The Resume Isn't Dead - It's Just No Longer Enough on Its Own

As of July 2026, the facts are clear. Multiple big tech companies - LinkedIn, Amazon, Google, Cursor, Anthropic, and others - are requiring PM candidates to show something they actually built, alongside their resume and sometimes ahead of it. This is the result of two forces converging: AI tools lowering the cost of building prototypes, and generative AI simultaneously eroding trust in the resume as a text-based medium.

But the phrase "the resume is dead" is an overstatement. More precisely, we've entered an era where a resume alone is no longer a sufficient signal. Applicants should have something they built, however small, and the ability to explain it briefly. Employers, if they adopt demos, should pair them with a clear evaluation rubric and design the process so candidates from non-traditional backgrounds aren't shut out. This shift is unlikely to stop at PM and will likely keep spreading across job functions - its pace and reach are worth continuing to watch.
