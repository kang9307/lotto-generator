Wrapping the Unpredictable Genius - What AI Harnesses and Talent Management Share

Reading Melody Koh's essay for the common principle behind AI harness engineering and managing brilliant people

Jul 16, 2026 Trends & Culture

Introduction: Why Talk About "Wrapping" Right Now

As of July 2026, a short phrase is circulating among people building AI products: wrapping. It comes from an essay by product builder Melody Koh, "Wrapping the Unpredictable Genius," posted on her newsletter. The title alone sounds like an HR essay about managing brilliant, difficult talent. In practice, though, the "unpredictable genius" the piece is actually about isn't a person at all - it's an LLM, the AI model itself.

Koh's argument is clear: the durable advantage in an AI product doesn't come from the model, but from the deterministic code that wraps around it. She draws this insight from a small but telling experience of her own. She wrote rules for Claude as documentation, and the model kept breaking them - until she moved those rules into executable code, a hook, and only then did they actually hold. That experience became her framework for understanding AI products.

Rather than simply relaying Koh's framework, this piece takes it as a starting point and goes two steps further. First: if you design AI systems, how should this wrapping concept actually apply in practice? Second - an extension Koh doesn't make herself, but one I think fits the metaphor surprisingly well - there is a structural resemblance between how organizations handle brilliant but unpredictable people and how systems handle brilliant but non-deterministic models.

1. What "Wrapping" Means - Melody Koh's Framework

1.1 The Real Asset Isn't the Model - It's Everything Around It

Koh's starting point is counterintuitive. The most visible layer of an AI product - the model itself - is actually the least defensible one, because anyone can reach the same model through the same API; as she puts it, it's "the part anyone can copy." What compounds over time, instead, is how you operate that model - what runs automatically, what a human reviews, what mistakes are prevented from recurring. Koh points to the coding tool Cursor as a case in point: its multi-billion-dollar revenue doesn't come from having the best model, but from proprietary wrapping infrastructure - codebase indexing, custom autocomplete training, enterprise integration.

1.2 Harness, Documentation, Hooks - The Layers of Wrapping

Koh's layers of wrapping break down roughly like this. At the center sits the unpredictable model. Around it is the harness - the program that actually operates the model. Around that are the documentation and rules that tell the model what to do, a "soft" layer the model can still ignore. On the outside are hooks - executable code that enforces rules with no room for negotiation. Koh's own experience proves the structure exactly: rules written as documentation went unheeded, and only the rules moved into hooks actually held.

1.3 Why This Metaphor Extends

That's as far as Koh's essay goes. What I want to add here is that this four-layer structure - an unpredictable core, the frame that operates it, soft rules, and hard enforcement - overlaps strikingly with how organizations handle brilliant talent. Organizations, too, don't try to eliminate the uncontrollable core: a person's creativity, intuition, and quirks. Instead, they design roles, boundaries, and evaluation systems that wrap that core, preserving the strength while containing the risk.

2. The AI Systems View - Wrapping Non-Determinism in Practice

2.1 The Cost Called the Verification Tax

There's a cost commonly overlooked when deploying AI systems in production: the time a human spends checking model output - the verification tax. Even if a model answers fast, if a human must confirm correctness every single time, the overall pipeline never actually speeds up. This shows up repeatedly in 2026 industry discussion: LLMs are fundamentally non-deterministic, so identical prompts can produce different outputs, which breaks the assumptions traditional debugging relies on. The way to reduce the verification tax isn't making the model smarter - it's pulling the point of verification upstream and absorbing it into the design.

2.2 Harness Engineering - What Gets a Guarantee, What Gets Freedom

This design work is what the industry now calls harness engineering. The core task is dividing decisions: which task paths should be left to the model's judgment, and which must be guaranteed to behave identically every time? Reviewing code and flagging style violations, for instance, can be left to the model's judgment - but blocking a deploy when code fails lint should be enforced in code, not requested of the model as a polite ask. In one reported coding-agent case, changing only the harness - not the model - produced a large benchmark jump. Deterministic infrastructure turns a probabilistic generator into a predictable worker.

2.3 Keeping Human-in-the-Loop From Becoming "Safety Theater"

The step where a human reviews output - human-in-the-loop - is often treated as the final safeguard in wrapping. But 2026 practitioner discussion raises a pointed warning: when human approval and review can't keep pace with a multi-step workflow running at machine speed, human-in-the-loop degrades from a real safeguard into formal "safety theater." Mature designs instead tier by risk level: low-risk work executes automatically, medium-risk work goes through secondary validation, and only high-risk work requires mandatory human approval. The point of current discussion is that this is resilient design, not inefficiency.

3. The Talent Management View - Wrapping Brilliant but Difficult People

3.1 The Autonomy vs. Predictability Trade-off

Brilliant people - especially those who redefine problems on their own terms and take paths others don't - tend to be unpredictable. The mistake organizations commonly make is cutting that person's autonomy to eliminate the unpredictability. The result is predictable in its own right: predictability goes up, but so does the loss of exactly the trait that made the person valuable to begin with. It's the same logic as over-constraining an AI model with rigid prompts - you get flat, generic answers instead of creative solutions. The real task isn't erasing unpredictability; it's designing what to wrap around it to contain the risk.

3.2 Psychological Safety as a Form of Wrapping

Management research keeps confirming the same point: psychological safety - the trust that mistakes and dissenting opinions won't be punished - is a core condition for high-performing teams. Research has also found a clear correlation between empathetic leadership and psychological safety. What's interesting is that psychological safety is itself already a form of wrapping. It leaves a person's unpredictable core - their quirks, bluntness, unconventional ideas - intact, while containing the social risk so it doesn't harm the team, and so the person themselves doesn't shrink. Recent research also notes the relationship between safety and performance is non-linear, not simply proportional: safety without clear standards can actually lower performance. That leads directly into the next point.

3.3 Clear Boundaries - The Rules That Must Become Hooks, Not Documentation

Psychological safety alone isn't enough. Borrowing Koh's framework, organizations need the same split between "documentation" and "hooks." Some rules are soft and left to discretion - "please meet the deadline." Others must be hard, with zero room for negotiation - "customer data does not leave without specific approval." Organizations that wrap brilliant people well design for both, separately. Areas that need creativity are left to autonomy at the documentation, or guideline, level; high-risk areas are nailed down with an approval process or a systemic constraint - a hook. The problem is that many organizations sit at one extreme or the other: leaving every rule as documentation, or locking everything behind approval.

4. The Principle That Runs Through Both Domains

4.1 Don't Remove Unpredictability - Wrap It

Combine the lessons from AI system design and talent management into one sentence: don't force an uncontrollable core into becoming controllable - design a controllable layer around that core instead. Whether it's a model's probabilistic generation or a person's intuition and creativity, that very unpredictability is often the source of the value. Strip it out, and the value goes with it. Instead, wrap a risk-containing layer around it - whether that's a harness, or psychological safety plus clear boundaries.

4.2 Risk-Tiering Is the Practical Answer in Both Domains

The AI system pattern of "low-risk runs automatically, high-risk requires approval" applies just as directly to talent management. Experimental attempts, minor decisions, and creative output are left to individual judgment; only decisions that directly affect customers or are hard to reverse go through prior approval or a second reviewer. Require approval for everything, and brilliant people leave the organization. Require approval for nothing, and the organization is exposed to risk. Tiering isn't a compromise between the two - it's a design that reflects the actual character of each domain.

4.3 Wrapping Is Ultimately an Engineering of Trust

Koh's final insight, in my reading, comes down to this: the entire discipline is telling apart the situations where you need the model to think for itself from the situations where you need a hard guarantee instead. Replace "model" with "person" in that sentence, and it still holds up perfectly. In the end, wrapping isn't a technique for suppressing an unpredictable entity - it's an engineering of trust that lets an organization or a system actually rely on and use that entity.

5. Applying This - What to Check

5.1 If You Design AI Systems

List out the paths in your system where the model is left to judge freely, versus the paths that need a 100% guarantee. If the latter still sit at the level of a prompt or documentation - a polite "please follow this" - they're candidates for moving into a hook or code. Find where the verification tax is highest, keep a human in that loop only, and automate the rest according to risk.

5.2 If You Manage Talent, or Manage Yourself

If you have a brilliant but unpredictable team member, ask before cutting their autonomy: is this an area that needs creativity, or one where the risk is irreversible? For the former, give psychological safety and room. For the latter, draw the boundary with a clear approval process, not a polite document. If you're the unpredictable type yourself, meeting the organization's minimum "guarantee" - honest status updates, agreed deadlines - while keeping your own way of working everywhere else is itself a viable strategy.

6. Limits and Criticism

This metaphor has clear limits. The moment you compare a person to a model, you risk instrumentalizing that person. A model has no feelings, no career, no life. If the rules that "wrap" a person become excessive, that stops being management and becomes control - and brilliant people leave. The same risk exists on the AI side: nail down every path with a hook, and you lose the model's flexibility, the very strength wrapping was meant to protect. Wrapping is a means, not an end, and over-wrapping can suffocate the exact value it was supposed to preserve.

Conclusion

Melody Koh's essay is about AI product architecture, but the principle inside it - don't try to eliminate what you can't control, wrap it so it becomes manageable - meets old wisdom about handling people and organizations head-on. Organizations that wrap unpredictable genius well don't dull the genius; they contain the risk around it. It's no accident that, as of July 2026, the discourse around AI harness engineering and the discourse around talent management are converging on the same conclusion. Both are just giving the same answer, in different languages, to the same old problem of unpredictability.
