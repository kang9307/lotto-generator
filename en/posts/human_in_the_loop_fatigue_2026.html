The Human in the Loop Is Tired - Supervision Fatigue in the AI Coding Era

The more code AI produces, the more humans shift from creators to reviewers - why supervision fatigue happens, and how to keep working together without burning out

Jul 19, 2026 Dev & Infra

Intro: Why Can't We Step Outside the Loop

There was an expectation that as AI coding tools handled more work, the burden on developers would shrink. What is actually being reported on the ground is closer to the opposite: the time spent writing code has fallen, but the time spent reading, judging, and ultimately taking responsibility for that code has barely moved. An essay Pydantic published in July 2026, "The human in the loop is tired," names this mismatch directly. The author points to a colleague who now has to review around 30 pull requests every morning, and argues that as AI raises the volume of output, what is left for the human is not creation but supervision.

This post takes that framing as a starting point, but rather than summarizing the original, it works through - in my own terms - why "human in the loop" as a design is simultaneously a safeguard and a burden, what "supervision fatigue" actually consists of, and what practical steps can ease it.

1. A Safeguard and a Burden - The Paradox of the Human in the Loop

"Human in the loop" was originally a safety principle: automation can be wrong, so let a human make the final call. That principle still holds. The problem is that the volume of output automation produces has started to outrun the speed at which a human can absorb it.

1.1 The Asymmetry Between Output and Review Capacity

A survey by Faros AI covering organizations with heavy AI adoption found that PR size grew about 51.3% and the number of files touched per PR grew 59.7% at high-adoption teams. Meanwhile the median time to review rose 441.5%, and bugs per PR increased 54%. Generation speed has comfortably outrun human limits, but the speed of understanding and verifying that output remains bound to the same physical ceiling of human cognition it always was. The traffic the safeguard has to absorb has exploded.

1.2 Where the Safeguard Flips Into a Bottleneck

The reason to keep a human in the loop is to protect quality. But the moment output exceeds what that person can handle, the same mechanism starts working in reverse. Either the review queue backs up and delays shipping, or a reviewer under deadline pressure clicks approve without really looking. Either way, the principle that "a human makes the final call" survives in form but empties out in substance. The very presence installed for safety gets pushed, by its own limits, toward becoming the thing that threatens safety.

2. What Supervision Fatigue Actually Consists Of

Pydantic's analysis is worth citing for how it names this: a human reward function problem. Traditional coding was dense with small satisfactions - understanding a problem, working out the logic, getting something to compile. In the AI era, the argument goes, that source of satisfaction disappears, leaving only the grinding work of review. I largely agree with that diagnosis, but I want to break supervision fatigue down into finer pieces.

2.1 Constant Context Switching

Every time AI offers a suggestion, the developer's brain has to switch from "implementation mode" to "evaluation mode." Each autocomplete suggestion is its own micro context switch, and even an interruption as short as 300 milliseconds can break the thread of thought that flow depends on. Repeat that switch hundreds of times a day, and you can end the day drained even though no single task was especially hard.

2.2 Stuck Between Trusting and Distrusting

Humans get placed in an awkward middle ground - unable to fully trust AI output, unable to fully ignore it either. Trust it completely and you fall into automation complacency and miss errors; doubt it from scratch every time and you burn enough time that using AI stops paying off. Research also shows that once a large share of output turns out correct, human reviewers tend to relax their own monitoring effort. Holding that ambiguous tension continuously is itself a source of fatigue.

2.3 Accountability Never Moves

If AI writes the code and a human approves it, responsibility when something breaks falls on the person who approved it. The burden of writing shifted to the machine; the burden of accountability did not shift at all. If anything, as the volume of output to review has grown, it is more accurate to say the total accountability any one person carries has actually increased.

2.4 From Creator to Reviewer - A Shift in Identity

The least visible but heaviest factor, I think, is the shift in role identity. Many developers chose this work for the pleasure of building something themselves. When more than half of a day's work becomes judging whether something someone (or something) else made is right or wrong, that person gets redefined - not as a creator, but as a quality gate. When the role has changed but the person still defines themselves by the old identity, that gap is where the exhaustion comes from.

3. Why Productivity Gains Don't Translate Into Satisfaction Gains

3.1 Less Room for Flow to Take Hold

Flow is a state that emerges from pushing through a self-directed, controllable task. Multiple studies of AI coding assistants point out that the loop of suggest-evaluate-accept/reject-revise can structurally erode flow on its own - the cycle of evaluating and verifying every suggestion functions as ongoing interruption. Productivity metrics are measured in throughput; satisfaction tracks the duration of sustained flow, not throughput. There is no guarantee the two move in the same direction.

3.2 More Work to Start, the Same Capacity to Finish

Pydantic's analysis can fairly be read as naming this gap an increase in work intensity. AI has sharply increased how many tasks a person can start at once, but human capacity to thoughtfully finish and verify those tasks has not changed much at all. The wider the gap between starting and finishing grows, the more unfinished items pile up mentally, and the harder it becomes to resist the pull of "let me try one more prompt." Getting pulled along by that urge is draining enough that it has been compared to a slot machine repeating a dopamine hit.

3.3 The Problem of Meaning - Output Without Your Hands Moving

Productivity numbers are calculated from the count or speed of output, but the meaning a person draws from work comes from how involved they were in the process. The satisfaction of seeing code you did not write yourself pass review is qualitatively different from the satisfaction of solving a problem you were stuck on yourself. I think this is exactly where productivity can double without satisfaction doubling: the numbers went up, but the total sense of "I did this" did not.

4. Connecting to the Recent Thread - From Understanding Debt to Supervision Fatigue

This discussion links directly to a thread BrainDetox has been following the past few days. Four days ago, in Understanding Is the New Bottleneck, I noted that as AI writes code faster, the bottleneck shifts from writing to understanding, and that when understanding is not kept current, an "understanding debt" quietly accumulates. I read supervision fatigue as exactly the mark that understanding debt leaves on a person's body and mind. When the habit of clicking approve without understanding repeats, debt piles up in the codebase, and the review labor needed to pay that debt down converts, one for one, into human fatigue.

The senior-premium scenario from just yesterday's Are Senior Developers About to Get Rich? can also be reread in this light. One reason senior compensation is rising is not that seniors endure supervision fatigue better, but that they can quickly tell what needs a close look and what can be waved through. The ability to shrink the "amount" of supervision needed is itself what commands a premium. The role shift covered in From Executor to Architect is another piece of the same picture: that piece's argument - that design and judgment remain once execution moves to the machine - maps almost exactly onto this post's "creator to reviewer" shift. My own judgment, though, is that the reviewer role, unlike the architect role, does not fill the satisfaction gap on its own - so the role shift is not unambiguously good news.

5. Five Practical Ways to Reduce Supervision Fatigue

Eliminating fatigue is not possible. But reducing its total volume and changing the quality of what drains you is, I think, achievable. Here are five methods that are either proven or provable in practice.

5.1 Set Explicit Trust Boundaries

Reviewing every piece of output with the same intensity is the single biggest cause of fatigue. Lower the trust boundary on low-risk areas - test code, internal scripts, repetitions of an already-verified pattern - by delegating to AI and skimming lightly, and reserve close scrutiny for high-risk areas like the production path, security, and data handling. Documenting this boundary at the team level takes the burden of deciding it off each individual, every time.

5.2 Offload the Verification Burden to Automation

Tests, static analysis, type checks, and lint catch a large share of the errors a human would otherwise have to spot by eye, upstream. The key is narrowing human review to what automation cannot catch - alignment with requirements, design judgment, edge cases that need domain knowledge. Investing in wider automated-verification coverage is, in the end, the most reliable way to cut the total amount of human supervision needed.

5.3 Tune Agent Autonomy to Task Difficulty

Granting maximum autonomy on every task only increases the review burden. Raise autonomy so an agent can run familiar, repetitive work to completion; lower it with tight checkpoints for unfamiliar or high-impact work - adjust the dial per task. Limiting scope so that one agent handles one thing at a time follows the same logic.

5.4 Cut Context Switching With Batch Review

Reviewing every PR the moment it lands maximizes context-switching cost. Batching review into one or two set windows a day reduces the number of switches and raises the density of focus available for each review. If giving up a little real-time responsiveness buys back that much fatigue, it is a good trade for most teams.

5.5 Choose Intervention Points by Importance

Scale intervention intensity to a change's blast radius - how many users are affected, how hard it is to roll back, how security-sensitive it is. Sharing just one principle across a team - go light on easily reversible changes, go heavy on hard-to-reverse ones - already makes the allocation of review energy far more rational.

6. Individual, Team, Tool - Three Layers for Sustainable AI Collaboration

6.1 Individual: Accept the Reviewer Identity, but Guard Time for Flow

It is better for your own mental health to accept, rather than deny, that your role has already shifted substantially toward reviewing. At the same time, I recommend deliberately keeping part of each day for solving problems yourself, without AI. It looks inefficient, but it is the minimum mechanism for not fully losing the reward circuit that flow and a sense of accomplishment run on.

6.2 Team: Design Review as a System, Not an Individual Skill

Leaving supervision fatigue as a matter of individual willpower eventually burns someone out, and the whole team inherits the gap left behind. Total review volume, batching cadence, trust boundaries, and automated-verification coverage need to be treated as a team-level design problem to be sustainable. If the structure requires one person to absorb 30 PRs a day, that is not a tooling problem - it is a process failure.

6.3 Tools: They Need to Evolve Toward Reducing the Supervision Burden

There is a lesson here for tool builders too. A tool optimized purely to increase output volume ends up growing users' supervision fatigue and eroding trust in the tool itself. Presenting diffs in smaller pieces, explaining the reason for a change before the raw diff, and learning from past review comments as rules so the same note doesn't have to be repeated are all design choices that genuinely reduce the supervision burden.

Conclusion: No Loop Is Tireless, but a Sustainable One Is Possible

Removing the human from the loop entirely is not the answer - that eliminates the safeguard itself. But leaving today's supervision burden unaddressed is not an answer either. Supervision fatigue is not a sign of individual weakness; it is the result of a structural mismatch: output volume exploded while verification systems and reward structures stayed put. The five mitigations laid out here - trust boundaries, automation offload, autonomy tuning, batch review, importance-based intervention - will not erase that mismatch entirely, but they can slow how fast it wears people down. As of July 2026, the race for AI coding productivity is still red-hot, but my conclusion is that the truly scarce resource in that race is not the speed of code generation - it is a sustainable supervision capacity that lets people keep exercising judgment.
