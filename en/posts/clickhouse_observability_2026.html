Why ClickHouse Is Winning the Observability War

As telemetry volume grows exponentially, why a columnar analytical database is becoming the new standard for logs, metrics, and traces

2026-07-05 Dev & Infra

Introduction: When Telemetry Explodes, Cost Becomes the Bottleneck

As of July 2026, the total volume of logs, metrics, and traces a single microservice generates in a day is on a different scale than it was just a few years ago. Container counts have grown, every request now carries a distributed trace, and AI agents that call their own tools and leave execution logs behind have turned telemetry itself into a big-data workload. The real problem is where you put that data and how fast you can query it. The moment the balance between storage cost and query speed breaks down, observability flips from a nice-to-have into a line item that eats the budget.

This post starts from a topic discussed on GeekNews — "Why ClickHouse is winning the observability war" — and works through the underlying question directly: why is the columnar analytical database ClickHouse rising as the standard backend for logs, metrics, and traces? The short answer, and my own conclusion after digging into it, is that this is not a fashion trend. It is a structural fit between the nature of telemetry data and columnar storage that happened to line up almost perfectly.

1. Why Observability Is Being Redefined as an Analytics Problem

Observability was treated as a collection problem for a long time — instrument the code, ship the logs, pipe them into a dashboard, done. But as data volume grew into multiple terabytes per day, the real bottleneck shifted from collection to what comes after: storage and querying. ClickHouse's own materials frame this plainly, describing observability as a high-performance data analytics problem rather than a data collection problem. Every log line, every metric point, every trace span is ultimately a time-ordered event record, and the ability to filter and aggregate that record set quickly is what actually differentiates an observability stack today.

Layered on top of this is the AI agent era. When a human was the one reading the dashboard and deciding what to do, sampling or pre-aggregating data to cut cost was a reasonable tradeoff. But when an AI agent is expected to diagnose and act on an operational anomaly by itself, it needs full-fidelity signal, not a sample. ClickHouse's recent positioning around full-fidelity observability for AI-driven operations reads as a direct response to exactly this shift.

2. ClickHouse's Technical Edge - Columnar Storage and Compression

2.1 Why Columnar Storage Suits Log Queries

A traditional row-oriented database stores each record as a whole unit on disk. If a log record has 40 fields and a query only needs three, the other 37 still get read along with them. Columnar storage inverts this: values for the same field are stored together, so a query only has to read the columns it actually needs. Querying three fields out of an 800GB log table can, in theory, mean scanning something closer to 40GB. Given that the overwhelming majority of observability queries are narrow — "just the error rate," "just this service's latency" — this structural advantage is not a coincidence. It is a design that happens to map exactly onto how observability queries actually look.

2.2 Compression Ratio - Log Data Characteristics and ZSTD

Columnar storage also compresses better. Values packed into the same column tend to be similar — the same log level, a repeated service name, a narrow range of status codes — so type-specific codecs combined with ZSTD compression work very effectively. ClickHouse reports average compression ratios of 15-20x depending on data characteristics, and practitioner write-ups describe observing 10-14x, sometimes over 20x, on log workloads specifically. A case Cloudflare has publicly shared is particularly striking: a log document that took roughly 600 bytes in Elasticsearch shrank to about 60 bytes after moving to ClickHouse — a roughly 10x reduction in storage footprint for identical data, measured in production.

2.3 Linear Scaling - The Simplicity of "Just Add Shards"

Blogger Mat Duggan, writing from a decade of experience running observability platforms, argues that ClickHouse's decisive differentiator is that the architecture does not change as data grows. In his words, ClickHouse "at 10 TB a day looks like ClickHouse at 1 TB a day" — a sharp contrast to stacks that require redesigning the cluster topology itself every time volume steps up an order of magnitude (matduggan.com). His core claim is that whether you're at 1TB or 10TB a day, the operational model converges on one thing: add shards.

3. What Sets It Apart from Elasticsearch

3.1 Inverted Index vs. Column Scan - Search-Optimized vs. Analytics-Optimized

Elasticsearch is, at its core, a full-text search engine. It builds an inverted index on every field to answer queries like "find documents containing this word." The problem is that the vast majority of log and metric analysis queries are not search — they are aggregation. "What was the 5xx error rate over the last hour" is a filter-and-aggregate operation, not a text search. An inverted index is over-engineered for this kind of query, and the index itself tends to balloon larger than the raw data it indexes. It has been observed that 1TB of raw logs can expand to 3-5TB in Elasticsearch purely from index overhead.

3.2 Cost Comparison - The Gap Widens With Scale

This difference shows up directly as a cost curve once scale increases. Mat Duggan's scale-by-scale comparison makes the direction unmistakable.

Independent research repeatedly reports 60-70% lower cost for ClickHouse versus Elasticsearch on comparable log analytics workloads, which is best understood as the compression-ratio gap flowing directly through to the infrastructure bill.

3.3 Where Elasticsearch Still Wins

To be fair, there is a place where the inverted-index architecture of Elasticsearch remains genuinely stronger: free-text full-text search, fuzzy matching, and complex relevance ranking. Multiple organizations reportedly run a hybrid setup — log storage on ClickHouse, search-heavy workloads on Elasticsearch. It is more accurate to say ClickHouse is taking over the majority of log and metric analytics workloads than to say it fully replaces Elasticsearch.

4. What Sets It Apart from Time-Series Databases Like Prometheus

4.1 The Cardinality Problem - A Structural Limit of Prometheus

On the metrics side, the comparison is different. A traditional time-series database like Prometheus creates a distinct series for every combination of metric name and label set. As label combinations grow — for instance, using high-cardinality values like user ID or request ID as labels — the number of series explodes exponentially. This is the well-known cardinality explosion. Prometheus pays this cost at write time: the moment cardinality rises, the ingest path itself consumes far more resources.

4.2 ClickHouse's Approach - Treating Metrics as Flexible Rows

ClickHouse takes a different approach. It stores metrics as table rows carrying attributes and measurements, and performs aggregation at query time rather than storage time. In other words, ClickHouse pays the cardinality cost at query time instead of write time. That distinction matters a great deal in modern microservice environments that need to handle high-cardinality dimensions, like per-user-ID latency. Industry reporting suggests a mid-scale organization running a Prometheus/Grafana stack, including engineering overhead, spends roughly $450K annually — and a meaningful share of that is the operational burden of managing cardinality.

4.3 This Is Not an Argument Against Prometheus

None of this means Prometheus has become unnecessary. In environments where cardinality is controlled and predictable, and for traditional monitoring centered on real-time alerting, Prometheus remains highly efficient, and its integration with the Kubernetes-native ecosystem is effectively the standard. ClickHouse's advantage shows up specifically in the growing set of workloads that need to correlate high-cardinality metrics with logs and traces in one place.

5. Integrating With OpenTelemetry and Grafana - ClickStack as a Standard

5.1 The OpenTelemetry Collector and the ClickHouse Exporter

ClickHouse does not become an observability stack on its own. What drives actual adoption is its integration with OpenTelemetry. The OpenTelemetry Collector is a vendor-neutral layer for receiving, processing, and routing telemetry, and its built-in ClickHouse exporter lets OTLP logs, metrics, and traces load directly into ClickHouse tables without a separate broker. Metrics scraped by Prometheus are typically routed through the Collector, converted to the OTel protocol, and pushed to ClickHouse as well — a common pattern in practice.

5.2 ClickStack - Logs, Metrics, and Traces in One Engine

The combination ClickHouse is officially pushing in 2026 is ClickStack. It bundles OpenTelemetry for collection, ClickHouse for storage and querying, and HyperDX as the visualization layer into a single open-source stack. Logs, metrics, traces, and session replays are stored in tables split by signal type, but they remain fully correlatable within one database. ClickStack's headline claim is that you can join an error log, the trace that produced it, and the infrastructure metrics from the same time window in a single SQL query. Compared to the old pattern of viewing three separate signals in three separate tools and stitching them together mentally, solving correlation at the database level itself is a meaningful shift.

5.3 Grafana Plugin 4.0

Grafana has effectively become the standard visualization layer. The officially maintained ClickHouse Grafana data source plugin, version 4, adds a query builder that treats logs and traces as first-class citizens, raising usability to the point where SREs no longer need to hand-write SQL for every query. It also recognizes OTel schemas natively, which means a migration to a ClickHouse backend can happen without tearing up existing Grafana dashboard assets.

6. The Economics of Log Analytics at Scale - Where the Economies of Scale Flip

6.1 The Trap of "Building a System to Reduce the Bill"

The fundamental problem with SaaS-style observability tools is that their pricing unit is usually tied to volume or cardinality. As data grows, the vendor's own infrastructure cost grows, and that cost passes straight through to the bill. Mat Duggan points out that this eventually traps teams into building a system whose entire purpose is reducing the bill — tuning sampling rates, filtering log levels, shortening retention, sometimes with a team whose ongoing job is exactly that.

6.2 The Counterargument - Self-Hosting Is Not Free

Self-hosting is, of course, not free. A ClickHouse cluster still requires hardware, operational staff, and fixed costs around schema, TTL, and partitioning design. The key point is that the slope of that cost curve is far gentler than a SaaS or inverted-index-based stack. Across multiple reported cases, a 10x increase in volume roughly translates to a 10x increase in ClickHouse cost, while vendor-locked stacks tend to jump non-linearly because volume growth pushes you into an entirely different contract tier.

6.3 The AI Agent Era Makes Discarding Data Harder to Justify

There's economic pressure coming from another direction too. In the past, sampling logs or shortening retention to save cost was an obvious tradeoff. But as workflows where AI agents diagnose and remediate incidents on their own become more common, it's becoming clear that sampled data or pre-aggregated summaries aren't enough for an agent to reason correctly. The traditional tradeoff of "store less to spend less" is becoming harder to sustain, and that is another structural factor tilting toward columnar databases that win on compression and scalability.

7. Limits and Caveats - ClickHouse Is Not a Silver Bullet

Listing only advantages is not analysis. There are real limits worth stating clearly.

7.1 Operational Complexity and Learning Curve

ClickHouse is not as plug-and-play as a managed SaaS tool. Partitioning keys, TTL policy, choosing the right MergeTree engine variant, and designing a sharding/replication topology are decisions a team has to make for itself. A poor initial schema decision gets expensive to unwind later. Teams used to throwing schemaless data straight into Elasticsearch or a managed Datadog will genuinely face a learning curve.

7.2 Full-Text Search Workloads Remain a Weak Spot

As noted above, workloads centered on free-text search, fuzzy matching, and complex relevance ranking are still structurally better served by inverted-index-based Elasticsearch. If a team's primary usage pattern is "finding" logs rather than analyzing them in aggregate, a hybrid setup is a safer bet than a ClickHouse-only migration.

7.3 Prometheus Remains Strong for Real-Time, Low-Cardinality Monitoring

For low-cardinality, predictable real-time alerting — infrastructure health checks and the like — Prometheus's simplicity and Kubernetes ecosystem integration remain practical. Moving every metric to ClickHouse is not always the right call; a selective migration starting with workloads that genuinely need high-cardinality correlation is the more rational path.

8. Conclusion - Three Things You Can Apply Today

My conclusion is that ClickHouse is winning the observability war not because of clever marketing but because of structural fit. The characteristics of a columnar engine designed for clickstream analytics — high compression, column-level scanning, near-linear scaling — happen to line up almost exactly with the nature of telemetry data: high volume, append-only, time-ordered, mostly queried in aggregate. Combined with the open ecosystem of OpenTelemetry and Grafana, it is settling in as a standardized stack without vendor lock-in.

8.1 Classify Workloads by Volume and Cardinality First

Rather than migrating everything at once, start by identifying which signal — logs, metrics, or traces — accounts for the largest share of your current observability cost and volume. High-cardinality metrics and high-volume logs are your top migration candidates.

8.2 Pilot - Move One Log Pipeline to ClickStack and Compare

Instead of swapping the entire stack at once, move one relatively low-risk log pipeline to an OpenTelemetry Collector plus ClickHouse exporter setup and run it side by side with your existing stack. Measuring compression ratio, query response time, and actual infrastructure cost on your own data is more reliable evidence than any vendor's marketing numbers.

8.3 Validate Search-Heavy Workloads Separately With a Golden Query Set

If your team's primary usage pattern leans toward text search, pull 20-30 of your most frequently run queries into a golden set and compare response quality and speed side by side between ClickHouse and your existing search engine. Separating analytics workloads from search workloads is what lets you make a decision — hybrid or full migration — you won't regret later.
