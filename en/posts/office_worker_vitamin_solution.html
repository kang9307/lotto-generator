You wake up at 7 a.m. and don't get home until after 10 p.m. Even on weekends, work is on your mind. You find yourself anxiously checking your email on your phone. Sound familiar?

Chronic fatigue is a common complaint among office workers, but the causes range from lack of sleep and workload to illness, medication and nutrition. Nutrients are only one of them. This article sets out what three often-mentioned nutrients do and do not do, and what to check before using them.

Three nutrients office workers often reach for when tired

1. Vitamin B Complex - Involved in Energy Metabolism

What role does it play?

  • Involved in converting carbohydrates, protein, and fat into energy
  • Involved in maintaining normal nervous system function
  • Evidence that extra supplements reduce fatigue in people who already get enough from food is limited

Deficiency symptoms that can occur (examples; other causes are more common): severe fatigue, poor concentration, depressed mood, tingling in hands and feet

Intake reference: It differs by vitamin (B1, B2, B6, B12 and others), sex and age, and the 2025 Korean Dietary Reference Intakes adjusted vitamin B6 among others. This article could not verify the individual figures in the 2025 source and does not list them. Check your age group in the official tables.

2. Vitamin C - An Antioxidant Nutrient

What role does it play?

  • Involved in protecting cells through antioxidant action
  • Needed for collagen synthesis
  • Involved in maintaining immune function
  • Getting it first from fruit and vegetables is the baseline

Deficiency symptoms that can occur (examples; other causes are more common): slow wound healing, bleeding gums, skin problems

Intake reference (US NIH ODS, checked at publication, not re-checked this time): men 90 mg, women 75 mg, Tolerable Upper Intake Level 2,000 mg per day. The earlier "200-500 mg during stress" figure was removed because no source could be confirmed.

3. Magnesium - Involved in Muscle and Nerve Function

What role does it play?

  • Involved in muscle and nerve function
  • Involved in over 300 enzymatic reactions
  • Involved in blood pressure regulation and heart rhythm
  • Benefits for sleep or anxiety are not established, so this article does not claim them

Deficiency symptoms that can occur (examples; other causes are more common): muscle cramps, insomnia, headaches, anxiety, irregular heartbeat

Intake reference (US NIH ODS, adults 31 and over, not re-checked this time): RDA men 420 mg, women 320 mg. The adult Tolerable Upper Intake Level for supplemental magnesium is 350 mg per day; it does not apply to magnesium from food.

Pre-use checklist

The items below do not recommend a dose. They are what to check if you already take or plan to take a supplement. The earlier "vitamin C 500 mg" and "magnesium 200-400 mg" schedule was removed because the top of the magnesium range could exceed the 350 mg limit.

If you take a B-complex product

  • Follow the directions and daily amount on the label
  • Add up the amounts in other products such as multivitamins
  • If you take medication, ask a pharmacist or doctor whether it can be combined

If you take a vitamin C product

  • Check that the label's daily amount does not exceed the upper intake level (2,000 mg per day, NIH ODS)
  • First check how much you already get from fruit and vegetables
  • This article could not confirm any difference in effect by time of day

If you take a magnesium product

  • Check that the label amount does not exceed the supplemental upper limit (350 mg per day); the limit does not apply to magnesium from food
  • If you have kidney problems or take medication, talk to a doctor before starting
  • This article could not confirm that taking it in the evening improves sleep

Precautions

- If you are already taking medication, consult your doctor before adding supplements.
- Pregnant or nursing women and those with specific health conditions should seek medical advice first.
- Do not exceed the label amount, and add up the amounts across products.
- Supplements are an aid, not a replacement for a balanced diet.

What to check when you look for changes

The earlier "week 1 to week 8 expected effects" timeline was removed because no evidence for it could be confirmed. What people notice varies widely, people who are not deficient may notice nothing, and if symptoms persist you should see a doctor rather than rely on supplements.

  • First check whether sleep, workload or meals changed at the same time
  • If fatigue continues, do not raise the amount; see a doctor
  • If other symptoms appear, such as weight change, fever or a pounding heart, see a doctor promptly
  • Writing down the product, amount and start date helps at a consultation

Wrapping up: what to check and what to hold off on

The first step with fatigue in a busy working life is not choosing a supplement but narrowing down the cause. Look at sleep, meals and workload first, and see a doctor if the fatigue continues.

If you decide to take a supplement, check the label amount and the upper intake level and add up the amounts across products; that is the safer starting point.

# Pre-use checklist for supplements when you feel tired

As of: 2026-09-29
This is a list of things to check, not a dose recommendation. Talk to a doctor or pharmacist before taking supplements.

## 1. Look at these first

- [ ] Have your sleep, workload or meals changed recently?
- [ ] Are you taking any medication? (If so, ask a doctor or pharmacist about interactions.)
- [ ] Are you pregnant, nursing, or do you have a medical condition, especially kidney problems? (If so, consult a specialist.)
- [ ] Do other symptoms overlap, such as weight change, fever or a pounding heart? (If so, see a doctor.)

## 2. Check by nutrient

| Nutrient | Known role | Reference to check | Source and status |
|---|---|---|---|
| B vitamins | Involved in energy metabolism and nerve function | Differs by vitamin, sex and age | 2025 Korean Dietary Reference Intakes (B6 and others adjusted); check individual values in the official tables |
| Vitamin C | Antioxidant, collagen synthesis, immune function | Men 90 mg, women 75 mg, upper limit 2,000 mg/day | US NIH ODS, checked at publication (not re-checked 2026-09-29) |
| Magnesium | Involved in muscle, nerve, blood pressure and heart rhythm | Adults 31+: men 420 mg, women 320 mg; supplemental upper limit 350 mg/day (food excluded) | US NIH ODS (not re-checked 2026-09-29) |

## 3. Label check

- [ ] Are you following the daily amount on the label?
- [ ] Did you add up the amounts across products such as multivitamins?
- [ ] Is the magnesium supplement at or below 350 mg per day?
- [ ] Is the vitamin C amount at or below 2,000 mg per day?

## 4. When you look for changes

- A week-by-week effects timeline is not used because no evidence for it could be confirmed.
- If fatigue continues, do not raise the amount; see a doctor.
- Writing down the product, amount and start date helps at a consultation.
