The Fastest Way to Notice a Package Got Smaller

Shrinkflation versus skimpflation, the one number that exposes both, and where disclosure rules actually stop

Aug 11, 2026 Finance & Economy

Paying the same and getting less

You open the bag and something is off. That impression is usually correct. The trouble is that memory is not evidence. Nobody wrote down how many grams were in that bag three years ago, and the shelf shows only today's price. Downsizing is not hard to notice. It is hard to prove.

A scene from the UK made the point better than any statistic. Someone clearing out a house found a chocolate bar with a 1991 best-before date and set it next to the version sold today. The old bar weighed 62.5 g. The current one weighs 40 g. A 22.5 g gap, with the old bar roughly 56 percent heavier. Thirty-five years compressed into a single photograph, and suddenly it was obvious. The reason nobody notices this during a weekly shop is the same reason it took a photo to reveal: the 22 grams did not vanish at once. They left three at a time, seven times over.

This piece is about catching those three grams in real time. The short version is that there is exactly one reliable method, and in much of the world it is already printed on the shelf. What you need to know is where it is printed, and where it is not.

Shrinkflation and skimpflation are different problems

Both are effective price increases. What differs is how a shopper detects them, and mixing them up sends you looking in the wrong place.

Shrinkflation is a quantity change. 500 mL becomes 450 mL; a twelve-pack becomes a ten-pack. The number is printed on the package, so a unit-price calculation exposes it immediately. Skimpflation is a composition change. The weight holds while the recipe moves down a grade: less cocoa, a flavoured oil in place of the real one, more starch and less meat. The unit price does not move at all.

A third variant deserves a name. Bundleflation withdraws single units and leaves only multipacks, raising the basket size per trip. The unit price can even look better while the shopper buys more than they need. That pattern is part of why unit-price rules have been extended into online retail in several markets.

Why quantity moves instead of price

The explanation that companies are simply deceiving shoppers is half right and explains nothing about the other half, which is the shopper's own reaction. If there are two ways to raise the effective price and only one triggers backlash, the structure of that backlash is worth understanding.

  • Prices are memorised, quantities are not. Most people can say what a box of cereal costs. Almost nobody can say how many ounces are in it. Touch the remembered number and resistance is instant.
  • The shelf compares per package, not per gram. Standing in front of two competing products, the eye lands on the large retail price, not the small unit-price line. The visual hierarchy of the tag becomes the hierarchy of competition.
  • Psychological price points are real. Crossing a round number produces a step change in volume, not a smooth one. Holding that line while absorbing input costs leaves quantity as the only free variable.
  • Trade terms and promotions are built on pack price. Feature pricing and discount depth are negotiated on the retail figure, not on the unit price.
  • It is reversible. Cutting a price back down sends a loud signal and is hard to undo. Restoring a pack size when input costs ease can happen quietly.

That asymmetry is the stated rationale behind regulation. When Korea's competition authority amended its rules in 2024, the reasoning was information asymmetry: if the price and packaging look identical, even an attentive shopper struggles to detect a quantity change. Notice the direction. The rule is not do not downsize but disclose that you did. Pricing stays a commercial decision; what regulation addresses is whether the shopper can know about it.

Unit pricing, the one tool that always works

The unit price restates a product's cost per fixed amount: per ounce, per 100 g, per litre, per count. That single figure does a surprising amount of work.

  • It translates downsizing into a price increase. A 400 g pack at $4.00 that becomes 360 g at $4.00 keeps its shelf price, but goes from $1.00 to $1.11 per 100 g. An 11.1 percent rise, in plain numerals.
  • It makes different sizes comparable. The assumption that bigger is always cheaper fails often, especially when a smaller pack is on promotion.
  • It strips the illusion out of bundles. Buy-one-get-one, bonus-size packs and gift sets can only be compared on a common denominator.
  • It quantifies brand premium. When two products in the same category differ twofold per 100 g, you at least know the size of the question you are answering.

The arithmetic is one division. Shelf price divided by net quantity. The obstacle is that nobody does this in front of every shelf, which is precisely why regulation exists to print it for you.

Where unit-price labels are mandatory, and where they are not

The European Union

  • Directive 98/6/EC of 16 February 1998 requires traders to indicate both the selling price and the price per unit of measurement for products offered to consumers, so that comparison is possible.
  • The indication must be unambiguous, easily identifiable and clearly legible, and the selling price is the final price including VAT and other taxes.
  • The Directive was later amended by Directive (EU) 2019/2161, which added rules on announcing price reductions, requiring the prior price to be the lowest applied in at least the previous 30 days.
  • Implementation, including exemptions for small retailers and for goods where unit pricing would not be meaningful, is handled through national transposition and therefore varies by member state.

The United States

  • There is no federal unit-pricing mandate. Unit pricing is set by state and local law, which is why the label is routine in some states and absent in others.
  • The Fair Packaging and Labeling Act of 1967 directs the FTC and FDA to require that consumer commodities disclose net contents, product identity, and the manufacturer, packer or distributor. It governs what the pack must declare, not whether a reduction must be announced.
  • In the 118th Congress, the Shrinkflation Prevention Act of 2024 (S.3819 and companion H.R.7825) would have directed the FTC to establish shrinkflation as an unfair or deceptive act or practice. The Shrinkflation Reduction Act (H.R.9279) would have required on-pack labeling to inform consumers of it. Neither received a vote.
  • Practically, that leaves the net-quantity declaration as the reliable artefact. It is always there, and it is the input to your own division.
Where no unit price is printed, the phone calculator is faster than an argument. Divide shelf price by net quantity for two or three candidates. Then write the winning figure into a notes app. From that trip onward you are comparing, not recomputing.

Disclosure duties, and the countries that have them

Unit pricing tells you today's cost per gram. It does not tell you that the pack was bigger last year. A handful of jurisdictions have added a second layer that does.

France

  • An order of 16 April 2024, amended 28 June 2024 and applicable from 1 July 2024, requires food-led stores with a sales area above 400 square metres to inform consumers when the quantity of a prepacked product has decreased while its price per unit of measurement has risen.
  • The notice appears on the packaging or on a label attached to or placed near the product, and runs for two months per item.
  • It covers both branded and private-label goods, and excludes items sold loose or whose quantity varies during preparation.
  • Consumer groups have criticised the placement, arguing that a shelf-adjacent notice is weaker than a mark printed on the pack itself.

South Korea

  • The competition authority amended its designation of unfair consumer transaction practices under the Framework Act on Consumers, with the change issued on 3 May 2024 and effective 3 August 2024.
  • Manufacturers of listed everyday goods that reduce quantity, size, weight or count must disclose it for at least three months from the date of change, via on-pack marking, a notice on the company website, or a notice at the point of sale including online listings.
  • Two carve-outs matter. Disclosure is not required where the price falls alongside the quantity so that the unit price is unchanged, or where the change is 5 percent or less.
  • The website option is the weak link. A duty satisfied on a corporate notice board does not reach the person standing at the shelf.

The 5 percent threshold deserves a second look wherever it appears. Two consecutive 5 percent cuts compound to 9.75 percent, and neither one triggers a duty. A disclosure regime is a floor, not a substitute for checking.

Where disclosure duties do not reach

Half of knowing a rule is knowing what it does not cover. Everything below is lawful, or at minimum falls outside the disclosure regimes described above.

  • Goods outside the covered list. Every regime that exists works from a schedule of products. If an item is not on it, no duty attaches.
  • Composition downgrades. Skimpflation is not a change in quantity, size, weight or count, so downsizing rules do not touch it.
  • Repeated cuts under the threshold. Where a de minimis carve-out exists, each round can sit safely inside it while the cumulative effect does not.
  • Relaunches as a new product. Change the name and specification and it is a launch, not a reduction of an existing line.
  • Import and distribution layers. Duties typically bind the manufacturer or the party commissioning production, not everyone in the chain.
  • The reach of the notice itself. A duty discharged on a corporate website is legally satisfied and practically invisible to the person holding the pack.
  • Restaurant and foodservice portions, where no net-quantity declaration exists to compare against in the first place.

That last structural point is why the French approach, which puts the notice next to the product in the aisle, is meaningfully different from a website posting even though both are called disclosure. It is also why consumer groups pushed for an on-pack mark instead: the closer the notice sits to the decision, the more it changes behaviour.

What a shopper can actually do

Not resolutions. Actions that fit inside thirty seconds at the shelf.

Manage the ten items that matter

There is no need to track everything. A handful of products usually accounts for most of a grocery bill, and writing down their price per 100 g creates a personal inflation record that is more useful than any headline number. Three habits finish the job.

  1. Treat a repackaged product as a prompt. Redesigns often arrive with a quantity change, and they destroy your ability to compare by eye.
  2. Divide once for the large and the small size. Testing the bigger-is-cheaper assumption costs a few seconds and fails more often than expected.
  3. Convert every promotion to a unit price before comparing. Bonus packs and multibuys cannot be judged against each other on any other basis.

The CPI does not miss downsizing

Every shrinkflation discussion attracts the claim that official inflation statistics ignore it. At the level of methodology, that claim is wrong.

Statistical agencies build a consumer price index by tracking goods of constant quality over time. When a surveyed item changes, quality adjustment separates the part attributable to quality from the pure price movement, so that only the latter enters the index. The standard toolkit includes direct comparison, quantity conversion, expert judgement and hedonic regression.

The worked example published by Statistics Korea is unambiguous. An ice cream moving from 100 mL at 1,000 won to 110 mL at 1,200 won is not recorded as a 20 percent increase. Converted to a common quantity it is a 9.1 percent increase. Downsizing is the same calculation with the sign reversed, so a smaller pack at an unchanged price registers as a unit-price rise.

So why does measured inflation feel wrong? The gap comes from elsewhere.

  • Weights. The index reflects average household spending. If your spending mix differs from the average, your experience will too.
  • Purchase frequency. Groceries are bought weekly and remembered vividly; a utility bill leaves the account silently.
  • Basket composition. The index tracks representative items, not the specific brand and size you reach for.
  • Asymmetric memory. Increases are remembered. Decreases are not.
  • The genuine hard case. Quantity is easy to convert. A downgraded formulation is not, and that is a real methodological limit rather than a rhetorical one.

The honest summary is that shrinkflation is captured by the index and missed by human memory. Which means personal defence starts not with doubting the statistics but with keeping your own unit-price record. How the major jurisdictions compare

Two distinct approaches exist. One secures comparability through unit-price labelling; the other requires the reduction itself to be announced. Very few places have both.

JurisdictionUnit price displayDownsizing disclosure
European Union Directive 98/6/EC requires selling price and unit price to be unambiguous and clearly legible No single EU-wide rule identified; handled at member-state level
France Unit pricing under the EU directive as transposed Yes. From 1 July 2024, food-led stores above 400 square metres, notice for two months per item
United States No federal mandate; state and local rules vary No federal rule. FPLA covers net-contents declaration only. 2024 bills did not pass
South Korea Ministry notice covering large stores and, since 2025, large online marketplaces, across 114 listed goods Yes. Effective 3 August 2024, listed goods, at least three months, 5 percent carve-out
Japan Not a national mandate but local ordinance. Tokyo covers 68 items in stores of 300 square metres or more No disclosure duty. Misleading representations are handled under premiums and representations law
China Clear price marking rules require product name, price and pricing unit to be displayed No dedicated disclosure rule identified. Prepackaged goods must declare net content

The American case is instructive. Several 2024 bills aimed either to have shrinkflation declared an unfair or deceptive practice or to mandate on-pack labelling, and none reached a vote. Korea moved faster in the same year for a structural reason rather than a political one: its consumer framework statute already delegated the power to designate unfair consumer transaction practices, so a notice amendment sufficed and no new legislation was needed.

Frequently asked questions

The pack shrank and nobody told me. Can I get a refund?

Where a disclosure duty exists, breaching it is an enforcement matter against the seller, not an automatic individual remedy. A refund claim needs a different basis, typically a mismatch between what was advertised or labelled and what was delivered.

My store never shows unit prices. Is that illegal?

In the EU it would be unusual, since the Directive requires it. In the US it depends entirely on the state or municipality, and in many places no such requirement applies. Absence of the label is not by itself proof of anything.

Which is worse for me, a price rise or a smaller pack?

If the unit price moves by the same amount, the economic result is identical. The difference is the cost of noticing. A price rise is free to detect; a quantity cut takes work. Disclosure rules exist to shift that cost back onto the seller.

The packaging is unchanged but the taste is different. Where do I look?

The ingredient panel. Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight, so a change in order means a change in the recipe. Where percentage declarations are required, compare those against a photo of the older pack. This is skimpflation, and it sits outside downsizing disclosure rules.

Do bonus-size promotions ever reverse shrinkflation?

Temporarily and selectively. A bonus pack raises quantity at a fixed price for the duration of the promotion, which is a genuine unit-price improvement while it lasts. It says nothing about the standard pack, and comparing the promotional unit price against a competitor's standard unit price flatters it.

The takeaway

The chocolate bar went viral because it collapsed thirty-five years into one frame, not because anything covert happened in between. Each year's change was printed on the shelf. It is just that nobody read the small line.

So the practical list is short.

  1. Read the price-per-unit line. That single habit is most of the defence.
  2. Record the unit price of your ten most frequent purchases. Your own log becomes the evidence you will otherwise lack.
  3. Reread the net quantity whenever packaging is redesigned. A redesign wipes out the baseline you were comparing against.
  4. Check the ingredient order when something tastes different. Unit pricing will never catch that one.
  5. Use the reporting channel where a disclosure duty exists. It is a compliance mechanism, not a personal claim.

The regulatory picture is now two layers in the jurisdictions that have both: unit pricing makes products comparable, and disclosure rules make changes knowable. Both layers carry scope limits and exemptions, so neither is complete. Inside their scope, though, they work. The rest is thirty seconds of reading small print.
