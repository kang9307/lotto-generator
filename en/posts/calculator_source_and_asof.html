A Calculator Never Tells You It Is Wrong - Input Definitions, Rules, and the As-Of Date

What to check before trusting an online calculator, and where to look first when two screens disagree

Sep 6, 2026 Life & Practical

A collection of calculators that print the governing statute and the as-of date next to every result went public recently. The stated reason for building it was short. Calculators floating around the web were still using superseded rates and stale policy interest rates.

That observation is more useful to the people using calculators than to the people building them. When a calculator produces a wrong figure the screen does not turn red. No error is raised. You enter a value, a number appears, and the number always looks plausible. What follows takes apart, in order, when that number quietly stops being right and how you can catch it using the screen in front of you and about ten minutes of your own arithmetic.

This is a general explanation offered for information purposes. Every worked example below comes from the rules of the Republic of Korea, verified against published statutes and public agency material as of September 2026, and the equivalent rules in your own country will differ in substance and in structure even where the reading technique carries over. The amount of tax, the fee or the eligibility that applies to any specific case is determined by the responsible agency and by professionals qualified to advise on it. Nothing here is about paying less; it is about filing and checking correctly.

Three things have to be right at once

From the outside an online calculator is a few input fields and a button. Behind it three separate things are stacked, and they fail for three separate reasons.

  • The input definition - what is this field actually asking for. Is pay gross or net, does it include allowances that are not taxable, is floor area the exclusive area or the supplied area that includes shared space
  • The rule - what has been transcribed into the arithmetic. It may be an article of primary legislation, a percentage buried in a presidential decree, a ministerial notice, a committee resolution, or an index that lives entirely outside the statute book
  • The as-of date - which version of that rule this is, and when the thing being calculated happened. These two dates are not the same date, and neither of them is necessarily today

Break any one of the three and the calculation does not stop. A number comes out. It is simply a different number. The reason no exception is thrown is trivial once stated: to the program, a rate of 3.3 percent and a rate of 3.5 percent are equally valid floating point values. Which one is correct is information that does not exist anywhere inside the code.

So the useful move for a user is not to distrust the output. It is to check whether the three axes are visible on the screen. When they are, verification is a short exercise. When they are not, verification becomes entirely your problem, and most people do not notice that it has been handed to them.

Input definitions - identical labels, different contents

The most common mismatch is not in the rule at all. It is in the input, because two fields carrying the same label accept different things.

Korea's Ministry of Employment and Labor published the minimum wage applicable in 2026 on 5 August 2025: an hourly rate of 10,320 won, with a monthly equivalent of 2,156,880 won stated alongside it. That monthly figure carries a basis, and the basis is printed with it: 40 hours a week, 209 hours a month. The number 209 is not a count of hours worked. It is 40 contractual hours a week plus 8 hours of paid weekly holiday, converted to a monthly figure.

In other words, folded behind the single field labelled monthly pay are two assumptions, one about contractual hours and one about paid weekly rest. Change the schedule from 40 hours a week to 30 and the denominator changes with it, and so does everything downstream. If a screen does not print 209 and the basis behind it, then when two calculators disagree you have no way to locate the disagreement. The structure of the wage floor itself is covered in 2026 South Korea Minimum Wage Increase Impact Analysis.

Social insurance has the same trap in a different place. The monthly income you feed into a contribution calculation is supposed to be the figure after non-taxable allowances are removed. Put the gross total from the payslip in instead and the contribution comes out too high. The calculator says nothing, because the number you typed was a perfectly valid number. How each of those contributions is assessed is set out in Korea's Four Major Social Insurances Complete Guide 2026.

None of this is peculiar to tax or insurance. The same shape appears wherever a familiar unit hides an unfamiliar definition, which is the subject of The Same 20x Is Measuring Different Things. Sharing a unit is not the same as sharing a definition.

Where a single age field splits in two

The cleanest illustration of a splitting definition is age. Article 7-2 of Korea's Framework Act on Administration provides that, except where another statute or regulation specifically provides otherwise, age for administrative purposes is counted from the date of birth in completed years and expressed in years. It took effect on 28 June 2023.

The important part is the clause at the front. Where another statute specifically provides otherwise, that statute wins. Article 2 of the Youth Protection Act defines a young person as someone under 19 in completed years, and then adds that a person who has reached 1 January of the year in which they turn 19 is excluded. Their birthday has not arrived, but once that 1 January passes they are no longer a young person for the purposes of that Act. This is what is usually called calendar age, as distinct from completed age.

So the same date of birth entered into two calculators can return 18 in one and 19 in the other, and both are correct. They are computing different rules. Which one you need depends on what you intend to do with the number, and that is exactly the thing the calculator cannot know. Where the thresholds diverge from statute to statute is laid out in Complete Guide to Age Standards for Youth and Children in Korea.

One rule falls out of this. If a calculator does not say which definition it picked, it has not given you an answer; it has made a choice on your behalf. Whether that choice suits the use you have in mind is something only you can judge.

The rule - what exactly was copied in

The rules sitting inside calculators are not all of one kind. Depending on where a rule came from, how often it changes, who changes it, and how the change is announced are all different. Knowing the categories tells you in advance which calculators go stale fastest.

Source of the ruleExampleWho changes itHow fast it goes stale
Primary legislationTax period and filing window in the Income Tax ActLegislature, then promulgationSlowly. But supplementary provisions attach their own application rules
A percentage in a decreeThe rate in Article 9 of the Housing Lease Protection Act Enforcement DecreeGovernment rulemakingMedium. Each amendment sets its own scope of application
Ministerial noticeThe annual minimum wage noticeThe responsible ministryYearly. The notice date and the application period are different dates
Committee resolutionThe annual health insurance contribution rateA deliberative committee, then follow-up procedureYearly. The resolution date and the date it starts to apply are different
An index outside the lawThe published Bank of Korea policy rateThe monetary policy committeeFastest of all. No law changes at any point

The last row is the trap. When a provision points at an index, the answer changes without a single character of the law changing. You can follow legislative news as closely as you like and this movement will never appear in it.

Promulgation, entry into force and application are three different days

When people talk about an as-of date they usually collapse several distinct dates into one. At minimum three have to be separated.

  • Promulgation - the day the finalised text is published in the official gazette
  • Entry into force - the day the rule starts having legal effect. Article 53(7) of the Korean Constitution provides that a statute takes effect twenty days after promulgation unless otherwise provided, but in practice drafters set an explicit date in the supplementary provisions
  • Application - which facts the rule reaches. It may reach only events occurring after it takes effect, or it may reach relationships that already existed

The Korean Ministry of Government Legislation publishes drafting guidance that sorts the ways an entry-into-force date can be written into several types: effective on promulgation, effective after a stated grace period, effective from a named date, effective alongside another statute, different dates for different provisions, and, listed explicitly among them, an application date that differs from the entry-into-force date. The example formula the guidance gives reads roughly: this Act takes effect on the date of promulgation, but applies from a specified date.

So a rule labelled as taking effect in 2026 carries no guarantee whatsoever that it governs things that happened in 2026. That answer is not in the operative article. It is in the supplementary provisions.

A real provision where force and application came apart

Abstractions are easy to nod at, so here is a case where the two genuinely came apart. The capped rate used when a rental deposit is converted into monthly rent was lowered from 3.5 percent per year to 2 percent per year.

The supplementary provision of that amending decree reads, in substance: the amended Article 9(2) also applies to lease contracts subsisting at the time this Decree enters into force, but applies from the point at which all or part of the deposit is converted into monthly rent after this Decree enters into force.

Two conditions sit inside one sentence. When the contract was signed is not asked. When the conversion happened is. And then the question of what the phrase about conversion actually points to split again. In an interpretation issued on 3 March 2021 the Ministry of Government Legislation concluded that the reference point is the date the renewal contract effecting the conversion was concluded, not the date the contract term begins.

Now suppose a calculator encoded this as a single branch: after the effective date use the new rate, before it use the old one. It will produce a wrong answer across the window where a contract signed before the effective date is converted after it. And that wrong answer will not look strange as a number.

The general lesson: a rule is not only the operative article. There is a second rule attached in the supplementary provisions, and the second rule is the one calculators most often fail to copy across.

Attribution - this year's calculator is not this year's income

Income tax has this distinction built into its plumbing. Article 5(1) of Korea's Income Tax Act sets the tax period as 1 January to 31 December. Article 70(1) requires a resident with global income for that period to file the tax base with the head of the competent district tax office between 1 May and 31 May of the year following that tax period.

So the year you file and the year the income arose are always one year apart. A return filed in May 2026 deals with income attributed to 2025, and the rates and deductions applied to it are those that governed the 2025 tax period. If some rule started applying in 2026, it first shows up in income attributed to 2026, which is filed in May 2027.

This is where a bare 2026 on a screen becomes ambiguous. It can mean at least three things.

  • The calculator was built in 2026
  • The calculator computes a return filed in 2026, in which case its subject is income attributed to 2025
  • The calculator computes income attributed to 2026, in which case it will not actually be used until the following year

The three readings are each a year apart from the next. This is why a well-built screen does not say 2026 calculator. It writes both axes: attributed to 2025, filed in May 2026. The moment both axes are on the screen the ambiguity disappears.

Deadlines have their own rule. Article 5 of the Framework Act on National Taxes provides that where a filing or payment deadline falls on a Saturday, a Sunday, a public holiday, a substitute holiday or Workers' Day, the following day becomes the deadline. So even though the calendar says 31 May, the real last day varies from year to year. The filing procedure itself is covered in Korea Comprehensive Income Tax Guide 2026 and Hometax Explained, and a worked case of peeling the hidden assumptions off a calculator is in Computing Overseas Stock Capital Gains Tax by Hand. This article does not repeat those calculation methods.

Where the answer moves although the law does not

The moment a rule references an external index, the shelf life of a calculator stops being the amendment cycle of the statute and becomes the movement cycle of that index.

Article 7-2 of the Housing Lease Protection Act provides that where all or part of a deposit is converted into monthly rent, the monthly rent may not exceed the converted amount multiplied by the lower of two rates. One is a rate fixed by presidential decree with reference to bank lending rates and regional economic conditions. The other is the policy rate published by the Bank of Korea plus a rate fixed by presidential decree. Article 9 of the Enforcement Decree sets the first at 10 percent per year and the add-on in the second at 2 percent per year.

The first figure moves only when the decree is amended. The second does not wait for that. When the published policy rate moves, the ceiling moves with it while the article stays word for word the same. On the Bank of Korea published series the recent segment runs as follows.

One calculator has to produce three different answers at three points in time. A calculator built once and left alone goes stale precisely here. The structural comparison between the two rental forms is in Jeonse vs Monthly Rent.

There is a second instance of the same shape: annual contribution rates. Korea's health insurance contribution rate for 2026 was set at 7.19 percent by resolution of the Health Insurance Policy Deliberative Committee on 28 August 2025, up 0.1 percentage points from 7.09 percent the previous year. The long-term care contribution rate settled at the same meeting moved from 0.9182 percent to 0.9448 percent. Here too the day of the resolution and the period it governs are different. A figure fixed in August is used from January of the following year.

Rounding is a regulation, not a preference

When two results differ by a few units of currency the difference usually gets waved away as a rounding artefact. In the public sector, though, how fractions are handled is frequently set by law rather than chosen by a developer.

Article 47 of Korea's State Treasury Management Act is that place. Paragraph 1 provides that where there is a fraction of less than 10 won in treasury receipts or payments, that fraction is not counted, and where the whole amount is less than 10 won, the whole amount is not counted. Paragraph 2 provides that where there is a fraction of less than 1 won in computing the tax base of a national tax, it is not counted. Paragraph 3 allows local governments and designated public bodies to apply the same treatment.

Two consequences follow. First, truncation and rounding produce different results. Under a rule that drops fractions below 10 won, 12,345 becomes 12,340, not 12,350. Second, the stage at which fractions are handled changes the result too. Truncating each line item and then summing is not the same as summing and then truncating once.

There is an example that runs the other way. The 209-hour basis behind the monthly minimum wage equivalent comes from converting 40 contractual hours plus 8 paid weekly holiday hours to an average month, which lands a little above 208.5, and the figure is then taken upward. Fractions were not dropped here; they were rounded up. Which direction is correct is decided by the scheme itself. It is not a matter for the calculator to settle.

The four things to look for before you trust it

That is the inventory of places where things come apart. Now narrow it to what can be checked from the screen alone. Before you use a result, look for four things.

What to checkWhat you are looking for on screenWhat it buys youWhat to suspect if it is absent
Cited basis Article numbers of the statute or decree, the notice number, the name of the index being read You get a coordinate you can check against the original text You cannot tell which rule was transcribed. Finding out what this calculator relies on is now your job
As-of date The effective date of the rule or the date the data was refreshed, plus the period the calculation is attributed to Which version of the rule you are getting becomes settled fact You cannot tell when it was last touched. A superseded rate may still be sitting in there
Stated exclusions What was left out of the computation, and which situations it does not handle You can judge whether your own case is inside or outside the covered range You cannot tell whether surcharges, reductions or exemptions were applied. It may only fit simple cases
Estimate framing Words like estimated, indicative or simulated, and a note that the figure is not a determination You use the output as a starting point rather than a conclusion The number reads as settled. This is most dangerous where eligibility is involved

None of the four guarantees accuracy. What they guarantee is checkability. With a cited basis and an as-of date you can point at where the error is when there is one; without them you find out late that there was one at all.

What to suspect when the disclosures are missing

Absence of disclosure does not mean the calculator is wrong. Plenty of carefully built tools simply skip the labelling. What is unambiguous is where the cost of verification lands when it is missing.

  • No cited basis - if the result looks odd you have nothing to check it against. Verification has to be replaced with comparison against an official agency calculator
  • No as-of date - you end up inferring the refresh date from other clues: a year printed elsewhere, the amounts used in examples, the names of schemes referenced in the help text. None of those is conclusive
  • No stated exclusions - you cannot tell what range the computation covers, so anything even slightly unusual makes the result unusable. Reductions and surcharges tend to live in exactly the excluded territory
  • No estimate framing - the screen reads as a conclusion. Where eligibility is involved this either talks someone out of applying at all, or persuades them to plan around a figure that was never settled
  • All four missing - it is better not to use the result for anything beyond orientation. It can point you in a direction, but the residual uncertainty is too large to base a decision about money or eligibility on

Conversely, when all four are present the only remaining work is to check once that the labelling is true. That procedure is the next section.

Reconciling one case by hand

You do not need to verify everything. For a calculator you expect to use repeatedly, getting one case to tie out builds an instinct that carries over to the rest.

Put the same input into two places

Start by finding the official simulation or auto-calculation service. In Korea, national taxes have the tax authority portal, local taxes have the local tax portal, and each social insurance body runs its own screen. The input you use should be a deliberately simple hypothetical rather than your real case. Reducing the room for exceptions is what lets you narrow a discrepancy down to one cause.

Split the intermediate values

Comparing only the final figure tells you nothing about where the paths diverged. Most of these computations proceed in stages, and official screens frequently expose them. When you can see a tax base, a computed amount, deductions and a final assessment, line up stage against matching stage. The first stage that disagrees is where the cause lives.

Narrow the gap to one field

Once you have the offending stage, change one input into that stage at a time. If both screens move in the same direction by the same margin, the rule is shared and the input definitions differ. If only one moves, the rules themselves differ.

Reconciliation procedure

1. Choose one simple hypothetical input   (nothing that triggers an exception)
2. Run it through the official screen and write the result down
3. Enter the identical values into the calculator under test
4. If the final figures match, you are done
5. If not, lay the stage-by-stage intermediate values side by side
6. Find the first stage that diverges
7. Perturb the inputs to that stage one at a time to isolate the cause
8. Classify the cause: definition, rule, or point in time

The order to check when the numbers disagree

Poking at random takes a long time. Ranking the candidates by how likely they are and how cheap they are to check gives you a fixed order.

OrderWhat you checkThe gap looks like thisHow to check it
1 Definition The gap is large and proportional. One side is some multiple of the other, or larger by a constant fraction Open the field help. Reconcile gross against net, inclusion of non-taxable items, which area measure, which hours basis
2 Rounding The gap is a handful of currency units, at most a few tens Determine truncation versus rounding, and whether fractions are cut per line item or once at the end
3 Exceptions The gap appears only in certain ranges. Some inputs agree, others do not Look for reductions, surcharges, exemptions, caps and bracket boundaries. Probe just above and below a boundary
4 As-of date The gap runs across the whole range at a constant ratio Compare the rates each side uses and the date each rate belongs to. Separate the year of attribution from the effective date

Most cases resolve at step 1 or step 2. If a gap survives step 4, the problem is usually not the calculator at all but a case that is genuinely not simple. From that point it is faster to ask the responsible agency or take the judgement of a qualified professional.

Limits the official services state about themselves

Simulation screens run by public bodies tend to be quite explicit about their own limits. Those notes are not a sign of a weak service; they are an accurate one. Knowing what such a note looks like is what makes its absence visible elsewhere.

ServiceThe limit it statesWhat to read out of it

Rather than listing which items are covered, it leads with the fact that coverage is partial
National Pension Service estimated pension simulation Income and contribution periods are entered freely; the computation assumes contributions continue to age 60 at the latest; people aged 60 or over are outside the contributory scheme and cannot be computed; the minimum contribution period for an old age pension is ten years; a figure reflecting the actual contribution record requires authenticated access It puts its assumptions on screen as numbers. The age 60 endpoint and the ten-year minimum make the character of the output unmistakable
Wetax local tax pre-calculation Pre-calculation is offered separately per tax item, covering acquisition tax, vehicle tax, registration and licence tax, local income tax, resident tax and the regional resource facility tax among others It makes you pick the tax item first. The three screens share a habit. They call the output an estimate rather than a determination, and they keep the assumptions behind that estimate on the same screen as the estimate. A tax item like vehicle tax, where the assessment basis tangles with age-based relief and pro-rata calculation, is a particularly good demonstration; the structure is set out in Korea Vehicle Tax.

Following the flow through one hypothetical case

To see how the three axes overlap inside a single computation, here is one deliberately simple hypothetical. Real cases vary by region, by contract terms and by other provisions, so the figures below exist only to show the shape of the flow. Whether the rule applies at all, and at what amount, depends on the responsible agency's judgement and on the contents of the individual contract.

Assume 50 million won of a rental deposit is converted into monthly rent. The rule is Article 7-2 of the Housing Lease Protection Act, and the ceiling is the lower of the two rates.

Hypothetical - deriving the conversion ceiling

[definition]  amount converted = KRW 50,000,000  (the converted portion, not the whole deposit)
[rule]        ceiling = min( the rate under Enforcement Decree Article 9(1) ,
                             published BOK policy rate + the rate under Article 9(2) )
[as-of]       published policy rate from 2026-08-27 = 3.00% per year

  (1) 10 percent per year               = 10.00% per year
  (2) 3.00% + 2%                        =  5.00% per year
  the lower of the two                  =  5.00% per year

  annual ceiling = 50,000,000 x 5.00%   = KRW 2,500,000
  monthly ceiling = 2,500,000 / 12      = KRW 208,333.33...
                                         -> the last digits depend on how fractions are handled

[if a calculator had 2.50% frozen into it]
  (2) 2.50% + 2%                        =  4.50% per year
  monthly ceiling                       = KRW 187,500
  Same input, different answer. The gap is over 20,000 won a month.

All three axes are visible at once here. The definition is that the amount converted is the converted portion and not the entire deposit. The rule is that you take the lower of two rates. The as-of date is that one of those two rates depends on today's published policy rate. The final block is the point of the whole article: a calculator carrying a stale policy rate issues no warning at all. Looking only at the output, there is nothing to distinguish the stale figure from the current one.

The minimum duty on the builder

Having taken the user's side throughout, one section for the other side. Publishing a calculator means putting out a number that other people will make decisions on. Given that, four things belong on the screen at minimum.

  • Cite the basis - article numbers, notice numbers, the source of any index, with links where possible. The point is to hand the user a coordinate they can check the original text against
  • State the as-of date - separately for the effective date of the rule and the refresh date of the data, because those are different dates. Adding the period the calculation is attributed to removes the remaining ambiguity
  • State the limits - say what was not taken into account. Naming what was excluded is more useful to a reader than listing what was included
  • Frame it as an estimate - use words like estimated, indicative or simulated and say that it is not a determination. Public agency screens do this for a reason

One of the four is a different kind of obligation: not asserting eligibility. Being slightly off on an amount and being wrong about eligibility are not the same failure. Eligibility is decided by the responsible agency after an application and a review, and all a calculator knows is the handful of values that were typed in. Assets, dependency relationships, overlap with other benefits, the circumstances at the moment of application: most of that lives outside the input fields entirely.

A screen that asserts eligibility is dangerous in both directions. Told flatly that they do not qualify, a person gives up on applying at all; told flatly that they do, they plan around a figure that was never settled. The safer construction attaches the language to the requirement rather than to the person. This input meets this requirement, or it does not, is where the calculator's remit ends. What follows is the business of an application and a review.

Stated again. This is a general explanation offered for information purposes, and all of it describes the rules of the Republic of Korea. The provisions and figures above were taken from material published as of September 2026; readers elsewhere should expect their own jurisdiction's rules to differ in substance. For any specific case, the tax, the fee or the eligibility is determined by the responsible agency's review and by professionals qualified to advise. If money turns on the decision, treat the number on the screen as the starting point of a question rather than the conclusion.

What could not be confirmed
