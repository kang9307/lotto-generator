Making Something Nobody Asked For

Having no one ask is both a freedom and a bill — the arithmetic worth doing first

Aug 28, 2026 Trends & Culture

Who is this for, exactly?

Say an idea for something you want to build turns up. The questions that follow are fairly predictable. Who is going to use it? Who is the audience? What are you going to do with it afterwards? If the answers are thin, the idea folds on the spot. It folds not because the idea was bad but because a question you cannot answer has been placed at the entrance.

It is worth asking when that became the first question. At work it is unremarkable. Things built inside a company start from someone's request, and without a request there is no budget. The trouble is that the ordering has leaked out of work. Something built alone on a Saturday is now also expected to arrive with an audience, an outcome, and a distribution plan.

On 14 August 2026 Zach Chmael published a piece called Make Something Nobody Asked For, which surfaced on GeekNews a few days later. The argument compresses into two sentences. The professional standard of usefulness has crossed over into creative work and pushes us to evaluate ideas far too early. And uncommissioned projects are precisely what train attention, sequencing, the ability to finish, and taste. He proposes a five-question Nobody Asked Test: would you still care if it were never published, is there a question you genuinely want to learn about, is it small enough to finish before explaining it, are there constraints doing real work, and can you defer extracting value until after the thing is made.

What follows is not an assessment of whether he is right. It sits one level below that. What does the absence of a request actually do to the work? What becomes possible, what breaks, and what is worth deciding in advance if you take the condition on. This is a structural question before it is a question of temperament.

A request comes with a definition of done and a judge

Taking a request is not taking on one piece of work. Two things come bundled with it. The first is a definition of done: someone else decides where the finish line sits. The second is a judge: someone exists whose call determines whether the result was any good.

Both look like burdens and both are actually scaffolding. Because the end is fixed, scope does not expand without limit. Because a judge exists, you do not fall into an endless loop of grading your own output. A large share of the reason work projects finish more often than personal ones is not skill. It is this scaffolding.

Without a request you have to erect both yourself, and the difficulty is that people are terrible judges of their own work. The failure splits two ways. One side is too lenient and declares victory at an arbitrary point. The other is too harsh and never ships. The second is more common and far quieter, because what happens is not that the work fails to finish but that the definition of finished keeps rising. The person doing it is usually the last to notice they have stopped moving.

The last row is the point of the table. With a request, taste becomes negotiable. Without one, taste is the only rudder you have. This is exactly where Chmael's line about training taste and identity lands. Taste is built through repeated choices, and in requested work someone else has already made a good number of those choices for you.

What only appears when nobody asked

Some things cannot come out of request-driven work as a matter of structure. There are three kinds.

Things the market has no language for yet

Requests only arrive from people who can put their problem into words. A friction with no name, an annoyance everyone has quietly accepted, a gap that is not even registered as a problem because no substitute exists — none of these show up as requests. Demand research inherits the same limit: people answer poorly about objects that do not exist. In this territory the demand only acquires language after somebody has built the thing and put it in front of people.

Things that do not summarise

Approval processes have a filter, and what that filter screens for is not the quality of the idea but its summarisability. Anything that cannot be explained in five minutes does not survive the meeting. So organisations keep picking the easily explained, and anything that has to be built before it can be explained keeps sliding down the list. Unrequested work never has to pass that filter.

Things with exactly one user

No scale means no budget. A tool whose user base is you, or one family member, or the colleague at the next desk, will never win priority anywhere. Yet such a tool often takes days to build and fits that one person better than any commercial product could. The reason this territory sits empty is not technical. It is the cost of justification. With no request there is nothing to justify.

What you can see is only what survived

That was the bright side. The other side deserves equal weight.

Start by calibrating the scale. Building things outside work is not rare. In figures Stack Overflow published from its developer survey, 68% of professional developers said they write code outside work as a hobby. What is rare is not the building. It is the building leaving anything behind. And the only cases visible to us are the ones that did.

The archetype of this problem is the work the statistician Abraham Wald did during the Second World War. Damage patterns had been collected from bombers that returned from missions, and the emerging conclusion was to armour the areas where the hits clustered. Wald's answer inverted it. The places to reinforce were the areas with the least damage on returning aircraft, because planes hit there did not come back and were therefore absent from the sample. The error of studying only what passed a selection process, while what failed it goes missing, carries the name survivorship bias.

A list of people who built something unasked and did well is a list of returning aircraft. Extract a method from it and you lose every failure cause that never entered the data. The bias bites even where records are meticulous. In mutual fund performance, Elton, Gruber and Blake reported in 1996 an overstatement on the order of 0.9 percentage points per year, the gap created by liquidated funds dropping out of the table. If a field where everything is recorded runs that far off, then in personal projects, where the vanished leave no record at all, the gap cannot even be measured.

So the honest sentence is this. Most things built because nobody asked end up used by nobody. And that is not failure, it is the default. Calling the default a failure breaks two things at once.

  • Starting looks far more dangerous than it is — score the default as failure and the expected value goes negative, at which point starting nothing looks like the rational move
  • You learn the wrong causation from the winners — many traits shared by surviving cases were equally present in the ones that died. Telling them apart needs the failure sample, and there is no failure sample
  • Advice comes from one side only — the people writing advice are mostly the ones who survived. The others do not write, and when they do, nobody reads it

The practical response is not to stop reading success stories but to lower how strongly you read them. Not do it this way and it works, only doing it this way worked for someone. The piece that prompted this article is itself built on projects its author actually finished, and is no freer of the bias for that. Neither is this article. Far more work has passed all five of those questions and amounted to nothing.

Being your own first user

When no request exists, the criterion reached for most often is I am the first user. It genuinely does three things. The feedback loop collapses to nothing, because you do not have to ask. The requirements are real rather than imagined. And the abandonment point is unambiguous: if you stop using it, that is the end of it.

The trouble is that this criterion turns into self-deception quietly.

  • The pleasure of building gets counted as utility of use — using your own work mixes need with attachment, and from the inside they are not separable
  • A sample of one gets multiplied by n — needing it yourself is not evidence anyone else does, yet that leap happens almost automatically
  • Friction gets papered over by learning — you adapt fast to the odd corners of your own tool, and after adapting you can no longer see that they are odd

Two questions help separate them. First, do you still use it after enough time has passed that you have forgotten building it? Use immediately after building carries almost no information. Second, do you keep using it once an easier alternative shows up? If you will not switch, what keeps you there is more likely attachment than need. Attachment is not a flaw. But mistake it for demand and every decision that follows will be wrong.

As building gets cheaper, choosing gets expensive

Tooling has clearly improved. The distance from an idea to a first working something has shrunk visibly. Yet completion rates do not feel like they moved with it. The reason is that the bottleneck did not disappear. It relocated.

The old bottleneck was implementation ability. Whether you could build it was the gate, and that gate did the choosing for you. When only a handful of things are buildable, there is nothing to deliberate about. Once the gate dropped, the option space exploded, and more options means a higher cost of choosing. That is not an advantage. It is a change of conditions.

  • Cheap to start means cheap to abandon — three days of work is easy to walk away from. Any dip in interest moves you to the next thing, which is also three days away
  • The last stretch did not speed up — the first 80% got faster while edge cases, deployment, configuration and documentation did not. As the whole got shorter, that stretch grew as a share of it
  • Working and usable drifted further apart — a prototype that runs early looks like proximity to done. That illusion causes chronic underestimation of what is left
  • Nothing supplies the grounds for choosing — tools add to your ability to build, but they do not hand you a reason to build one thing rather than another

Two of Chmael's five questions — is it small enough to finish before explaining it, and are there constraints doing real work — aim precisely here. Neither asks about capability. Both ask about the discipline of choosing. In a world where far more is buildable, that is the side where the value has moved.

Do you publish it?

Publishing is the most misread item in this subject. Publishing helps and you should publish are different claims, and the second one is poorly supported.

What publishing gives is clear enough: validation by an actual person rather than the user in your head, the completion pressure that comes from knowing someone will look, and a record that later explains you to others. All three arrive with an invoice attached.

  • Maintenance expectation — once someone starts using it, they expect it to keep working. There is no legal obligation and the psychological cost is real anyway
  • Response load — not answering questions and issues is also a decision, and it is one you have to make again every time
  • Silence — more common than criticism and usually harder. Publishing something and having nothing happen is the default outcome
  • Requests appear — publish and requests arrive, and the premise of this whole piece, that nobody asked, dissolves. That is not a paradox. It is a trade
Level of releaseWhat you getWhat it costs
Not publishedFull freedom, zero cost to stopNo outside validation, no completion pressure
Only to people with the linkA handful of real usage signalsEven a handful generates expectations
Source only, no support statedA record, reuse, something to point atDocumentation, config separation, licence choice
Published with intent to maintainUsers, feedback, reputationIssue response, compatibility, expectation management

The middle two are most often the right answer and least often chosen. The third in particular — publishing the source while stating up front that it is unsupported — pays the expectation-management cost once, at the front, and then stops paying. Not publishing at all is also a complete option. Publishing is not part of the definition of finished. But if you are not going to, it is better to build it that way from the start, because preparing for release is not free and changing your mind halfway means doing the work twice.

Checks worth doing if you have an employer

This article does not advise quitting your job to build. If anything the opposite fits the subject better. A steady income removes the pressure to make the thing pay, and the absence of that pressure is exactly the condition under which unrequested work exists at all. The moment your livelihood rides on it, it has already become requested work.

There is, though, something to check. Who ends up owning what you build while employed cannot be answered in general, because the contract and the workplace rules decide it. So what follows is not an answer but a list of places to look. The statutes below are the floor of the analysis, not its conclusion (Korean law, as of August 2026).

Copyright. Article 9 of the Korean Copyright Act treats the corporation as the author of a work made in the course of employment and published under the corporation's name — but the provision carries the qualifier where a contract or workplace rules do not provide otherwise inside the article itself. In other words, where documents exist, the documents come first. A proviso adds that computer program works are not required to have been published, meaning programs fall inside the article's frame regardless of release.

Patents. Article 10 of the Invention Promotion Act gives the employer a non-exclusive licence where the employee obtains a patent on an employee invention (paragraph 1), and permits advance assignment of rights or an exclusive licence through a contract or workplace rules for employee inventions specifically (paragraph 3). Where rights are assigned, the employee holds a right to reasonable compensation.

Turned into a procedure, it looks like this.

  • Reread the employment contract and anything signed at hire in the original. Memory of these documents is usually wrong
  • Ask to see the workplace rules. Employers are required to keep them available, so the request is a normal one
  • Where anything is ambiguous, put the question to HR or legal in writing and keep the reply. A verbal answer is not evidence later
  • Separate personal hardware, personal time and personal accounts, and let that separation leave a trail
  • Do this before publishing. Undoing a release is far more expensive than delaying one

To restate it: the list above is a set of places to check, not a conclusion. Ownership in any specific case turns on the wording of the contract, the workplace rules and the concrete facts, so anything that snags should go to your employer's own process and to professional advice.

What can be recovered from what you never finished

Strip the romance out first. Unfinished is unfinished. Covering it with at least I learned something guarantees that the next one dies in the same place the same way. Saying something is recoverable is not the same as saying recovery happens by itself.

What is genuinely recoverable comes down to about four things.

  • Skill in the hands — only what you actually repeated stays. What you merely read does not
  • Estimation sense — you learn what is harder than it looks. Most of the improvement in later scheduling comes from here
  • Reusable fragments — code, structure, documents, material. They let you skip day one next time
  • Negative knowledge — confirmation that this direction does not work. It looks cheap until the same idea returns six months later

Recovery has one precondition. Write nothing at the point of stopping and nothing survives. A few months on, how far it got, why it halted and what blocked it will have evaporated entirely. One paragraph is enough.

  • How far it worked and what was left
  • Why it stopped — interest, a technical wall, or time. The three call for different responses
  • Where you would restart, and whether restarting is worth anything

When do you stop?

Harder than starting, and the structure above explains why. There is no judge for stopping either. Nobody declares the project over, so most personal projects are not finished, they are forgotten. Forgotten projects do not disappear; they stay on the books as debt. When you go to start the next thing, I never finished that other one arrives first. What actually blocks starting is not the memory of failure but the accumulation of unclosed states.

So the method that works is writing down a stopping condition when you start. The condition has to be checkable. When I get bored is true whenever you want it to be, so it is not a condition. Four weekends, if this feature is not working by month end I stop, three consecutive weeks without opening the file — those can be judged from outside. Fix the condition first and stopping becomes the execution of a plan rather than a failure. That is the real effect: the emotional cost disappears.

Stopping also has varieties. If deletion is the only one you know about, the decision gets harder than it needs to be.

  • Shrink — cut the scope until it counts as finished. Lowering the definition of done is usually the best available move
  • Freeze — halt with a record. The procedure is mostly about telling yourself explicitly that this is a hold, not a burial
  • Hand over — if someone will use it, pass it on. Publishing while stating you will not maintain it belongs here too
  • Delete — remove it, after writing the one paragraph worth keeping

A warning in the other direction is needed too. A pre-set condition also works as an excuse to quit. Enter a hard stretch and the condition suddenly looks reasonable. So set it at the start, with the hard stretch anticipated, and once set, avoid revising it from inside that stretch.

What could not be verified

  • The 68% figure for coding outside work comes from a page where Stack Overflow presents developer survey results, and that page does not state which survey year the number is drawn from
  • No reliable comprehensive statistics on completion or abandonment rates for personal projects turned up. The statement that most end up used by nobody is an inference from the structure of survivorship bias, not a measurement
  • The personal projects cited in the article that prompted this one are the author's own account and were not cross-checked
  • Ownership rules for works made in employment and employee inventions outside Korea are not covered here. Defaults differ by jurisdiction
  • Individual employers' workplace rules and undertakings are not public documents, so no generalisation is possible. The table above lists places to check, not sample clauses

Summing up

The absence of a request is not another word for freedom. It is the condition of having to erect the definition of done and the judge yourself, and the freedom is a by-product of that condition. The by-product is large enough to make the condition worth taking on, but ignore the condition side and what accumulates is a pile of things started and never closed.

The condition opens three doors: things the market has no language for yet, things that do not summarise, and things with exactly one user. None of the three can emerge from request-driven work as a matter of structure. And the same condition creates one danger. Most of it ends up used by nobody, and that is the default rather than a failure. Cover the default with success stories and you will learn the wrong causation from them.

Three lines survive as practice.

  • At the start, write one stopping condition in a form somebody else could check
  • Decide whether to publish at the beginning. Changing your mind halfway means doing the work twice
  • If your employer could be implicated, reread the contract and workplace rules before publishing

Who is going to use it does not need an answer. That question belongs to the request-driven world and this is not that world. But when is it done and who decides does need one — because the content of this choice, from the start, was that nobody else would be supplying it.
