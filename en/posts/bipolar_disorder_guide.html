Understanding Bipolar Disorder and How Treatment Is Approached

Not a condition you can judge for yourself - observation, assessment, and the route to care

2026-08-01 Life & Health

Introduction: the sentence that keeps coming up

Some months everything moves. You fall asleep at three, wake at six, and feel fine. Projects that had been sitting untouched for a year all start at once. Then a few weeks later nothing moves at all. Getting washed is heavy. Messages from people you like go unanswered for days. Almost everyone who describes this pattern adds the same sentence at the end of it: I honestly cannot tell whether this is just who I am or whether something is wrong.

The internet answers that question far too readily. Explanations of the form big mood swings equal bipolar disorder are everywhere, and so are tables asking you to count how many items apply to you. That is exactly what this article is not going to do. Bipolar disorder is not sorted out by the visible size of a swing, several other states look similar from the outside, and above all, the period when a person can least accurately see their own state sits right at the centre of this condition.

So this article has one purpose. Not to help you reach a verdict, but to shorten the distance between the question and a clinic. What is worth observing, why the memory of people around you matters so much, and what to bring to an appointment so the conversation starts further along. The verdict is not issued here.

What this article is. This is general health information provided for informational purposes and it does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Any judgement about an individual state, and any decision about treatment, must be made through consultation with a qualified clinician. This article does not name medicines, doses or schedules, and nothing written here should be used as grounds for self-diagnosis.

Sourcing rule. Everything below is written from material that was opened and read directly on the sites of the World Health Organization, the US National Institute of Mental Health, the UK National Health Service, and the counselling service run under the Korean Ministry of Health and Welfare. Figures and claims that could not be verified were left out.

1. If you are in danger right now, read this first

If any of the following applies, making contact matters more than finishing this article — whether it is about you or about someone sitting next to you.

Situations where you should not weigh it up alone

  • Thoughts of self-harm or suicide. Do not audit yourself over whether the thought is specific enough. The thought itself is reason enough to reach out
  • Agitation severe enough that sitting still is difficult. This carries more weight if it follows several days with almost no sleep
  • The line between thought and reality becoming unclear. Hearing what others do not hear, becoming convinced that people intend harm, or becoming convinced of special powers all belong here
  • Behaviour that could injure you or someone else has already started. Driving with impaired judgement, sudden large spending, and impulsive severing of relationships are included
  • People around you repeatedly saying you are not yourself. Even if you feel fine, the repetition of that observation is itself a signal

If someone near you appears to be at risk, do not try to talk them round alone. Calling together is far more useful than telling them not to think that way.

2. Where is the line between mood change and a condition

One thing first. Mood going up and down is not in itself an illness. Lifting when something good happens and sinking when something bad happens is baseline human operation. Being shaken for a few days by an exam result, a breakup, a promotion or a move is the same.

What public health sources treat as a condition is structurally different. The World Health Organization describes it as a state in which mood, energy, activity and thought move together. That is, it is not mood alone that changes; there are periods when the whole way a person operates shifts as one block. The UK National Health Service describes those periods as usually lasting a few days or weeks at a time, with stable stretches in between that can run for weeks, months or even years.

Two axes actually carry the weight here.

  • Duration. Swinging several times within a single day and holding one state for days or weeks are different in kind. When describing the low side, the World Health Organization refers to a pattern present most days, for at least two weeks
  • Functional impact. What matters is whether actual life was disrupted during that stretch: work, study, relationships, handling money. I felt terrible but got everything done and I left my job during that period are entirely different pieces of information
  • Amplitude, not direction. Change toward the good side is also worth observing. My mood improved, so it is fine is the judgement that goes wrong most reliably in this particular condition
  • The gap between how you see it and how others do. If you keep experiencing peak condition while people around you keep worrying, that gap is itself information worth writing down

Do not use these two axes as a self-assessment tool. Duration and functional impact are raw material for assessment, not the assessment itself. The same length and the same degree of difficulty can come from entirely different causes, and separating those is what the appointment consists of. The axes above are best used only as a guide to what is worth describing in a consulting room.

3. What is observed in manic, hypomanic and depressive periods

This section is deliberately written as prose rather than as a list, because a format that invites you to count items is harmful on this particular subject.

3.1 The elevated period, described as mania

Taking the World Health Organization and UK National Health Service guidance together, this period is described as one in which mood is markedly elevated, or alternatively irritable, and in which energy is noticeably increased. A reduced need for sleep without corresponding tiredness is reported alongside it, and speech becoming rapid or thoughts running one into the next so that staying on one thing is difficult is described as observable. Self-appraisal is described as inflating well beyond the usual, and decisions taken without weighing consequences — spending that cannot be sustained, abrupt changes of plan — are reported as increasing. Where the state is severe, convictions that do not match reality, or perceptual experiences others do not share, are described as sometimes accompanying it.

What this article wants to emphasise is not the list of features but the subjective experience of that period. It is frequently not remembered as a period of suffering. It is more often remembered as a stretch when things went well and the mind worked quickly. So when a clinician later asks how things were then, the patient has no material to report as a problem. That structure is the root of the diagnostic delay covered in the next section.

3.2 The state described as hypomania

The UK National Health Service separates elevated states by degree and describes the less severe side under the name hypomania. The direction is similar, but it is described as less intense, without life collapsing outright. That is precisely the problem. Because life does not collapse, it is not recognised as a reason to see anyone, and people nearby tend to file it under they seem to be on good form lately. It is practical to understand this as the period most easily missed.

3.3 The low period, described as a depressive episode

The low side is comparatively easier to recognise. The World Health Organization describes it as low mood together with a loss of interest in things that were previously enjoyable, present most days for at least two weeks, alongside difficulty concentrating, increased guilt or worthlessness, changes in sleep and appetite, and reduced energy. The UK National Health Service explicitly notes that thoughts of self-harm or suicide can occur during this period. That sentence is why section 1 of this article sits where it does.

The important point is that these two periods are described not as two illnesses affecting different people but as phases that can alternate within one person's course. Which means that, looked at in isolation, the low period is hard to distinguish from other states. That is why this article exists separately from the article on depression, and why the two should not be read as overlapping material.

4. Why diagnosis is delayed

Delay in this condition is not down to individual carelessness. It is structural. In order:

  • What drives people to a clinic is the low period. It hurts, so help is sought. The first appointment therefore tends to be filled in entirely from the depressive side
  • The elevated period does not get reported as a problem. If it is filed in memory as a good stretch, there is no reason to raise it. A clinician cannot see what is never mentioned
  • Several states overlap in appearance. The UK National Health Service states that the condition affects everyone differently and that its symptoms are similar to those of other mental health conditions, which makes it harder to identify
  • One appointment does not settle it. The same guidance describes a specialist carrying out a detailed assessment of mood, behaviour, health and family history. Time is required because it is necessary, not because anything is being done poorly

Reports that assessment can take years appear across research literature and professional bodies. This article does not quote a specific average figure, because the primary material containing those numbers could not be opened and read directly, and copying an unverified number would break the rule this article set for itself. The conclusion needed here is a direction, not a number. If delay is common, and the reason for it lies in a structure where the elevated period never gets reported, then the way to break that structure is already determined. Get information about the elevated period into the consulting room. That method is section 9.

The World Health Organization notes that stigma and discrimination against people with this condition are widespread, in communities and in health services alike. It also describes treatment coverage worldwide as low. This is why delayed assessment should not be framed as purely an individual failing.

5. Treatment: a combination, not a single method

The public sources are consistent on this. It is several elements used together, not one method. The World Health Organization frames effective management as medicines combined with psychosocial interventions, naming psychoeducation, cognitive behavioural approaches, lifestyle adjustment and peer support alongside. The UK National Health Service likewise presents mood-stabilising medicines and talking therapy side by side. The US National Institute of Mental Health states that an effective treatment plan can help people manage symptoms and improve quality of life.

ElementRole as public sources describe itCommon misreading
MedicationTreatment aimed at stabilising the swing is described as available. Both continuous use and use as needed are mentionedMedication removes your emotions — the described aim is handling amplitude, not removing feeling
Psychosocial interventionTalking therapy, psychoeducation and cognitive behavioural approaches are described as used alongsideTherapy is what you do when medication is off the table — the sources place them in parallel, not as substitutes

Support from othersPeer support and family involvement are named as components of managementGood family support cures it — support assists treatment, it does not replace it

One thing must be said plainly here. This article does not claim that any one approach is better. There is no basis for saying so, and the moment it were said, this article would be imitating a clinic. The composition and weighting of the combination differ between people, and adjusting them is itself the content of treatment.

Second. This article does not name medicines, doses or schedules. Those are settled against an individual's state, other health conditions and everything else being taken, and that information does not transfer into an article. Reading someone else's account of a specific medicine online and mapping it onto yourself is unsafe for the same reason.

5.1 Why consistency matters

This condition is described as running a course that improves and worsens repeatedly. Which also means that being well now does not mark the end of treatment. The US National Institute of Mental Health describes it as usually requiring long-term treatment. Reading a long stable stretch as evidence that management is working is closer to the facts.

6. Relapse and maintenance: feeling well is the risky point

The thing that happens most often in practice is this. Once things are stable, the reason to continue stops being obvious. That is an understandable drift. Side effects are uncomfortable, there is a cost, and attending appointments is itself a recurring reminder of one's own status.

UK National Health Service guidance is unambiguous at this point. The instruction that you should not stop taking your medicine unless a doctor tells you to, even if you feel better, appears there directly.

That sentence will not be used here to frighten anyone. What is needed is not fear but the next action.

  • If the thought of stopping arrives, bring the thought into the appointment. That is the whole of this section. The only thing to avoid is hiding the thought and adjusting alone
  • Side effects are for reporting, not enduring. What is uncomfortable, since when, and in what way becomes the raw material for adjustment
  • Cost and time pressures are legitimate subjects for an appointment. A plan cannot fit a life whose real constraints were never described
  • If you have already skipped or reduced doses, say so as it happened. Withheld information makes the next judgement wrong

A relapse is not a failure. In a condition with a long-running course, worsening again is part of the management process. What actually changes outcomes is not whether a relapse happened but how quickly it is noticed and how quickly it is reconnected to care. The records in the next section, and the observations in section 8, are what make that noticing possible.

7. Daily management: a support, not a substitute for treatment

Nail this down before starting. Nothing below replaces treatment. The way public sources mention lifestyle management is not as an alternative to care but as something that runs alongside it. Inverting that order is dangerous.

The UK National Health Service lists, as things a person can do themselves, keeping a regular routine, getting enough sleep, eating a balanced diet, exercising and managing stress, and also mentions avoiding influences that can destabilise mood. The World Health Organization similarly names regular sleep, physical activity, diet, stress reduction and mood monitoring as self-care.

  • Treat sleep as the fixed variable. Bedtime and waking time drifting is reported as an early signal of state change. If sleep itself is the difficulty, that is also a subject for the appointment (a separate article on sleep problems exists)
  • The simpler the record, the longer it survives. One line a day is enough: the day's mood as a number from zero to ten, the time you fell asleep, the time you woke. A few months of that becomes genuinely useful material
  • The purpose of the record is not self-assessment. It is not there so you can conclude something because the number ran high for a few days. It is there so that the appointment does not have to run on memory.
  • The larger the change, the more worth postponing a big decision. Changing jobs, moving, major spending, ending relationships — decisions that are hard to reverse are safer deferred until things are steady
  • Alcohol and stimulating substances cloud judgement. Moderation here is something the public sources consistently advise

8. What family and friends can do, and what to avoid

This may be the most practical section in the article. Because of the structure in which the elevated period is not recognised as a problem from the inside, observation by the person nearby becomes information nobody else can supply.

SituationResponse that helpsResponse that harms
Unusual elevation appearsWrite down what you observed, with dates: sleep, speed of speech, spending, changes of planDeclaring on the spot that they are not right and starting an argument
They are too low to do anythingOffer something small and concrete: book the appointment together, cover one mealBe strong, it is a matter of willpower — this turns a health state into a character flaw
They say they want to stop treatmentListen to the reason, and help them say that reason in the appointmentDeciding for them, or checking up covertly and confronting them
Risk signals appearDo not carry it alone; connect to the channels in section 1. Make the call togetherAgreeing to keep it secret when asked. Safety comes first
You yourself are worn downAcknowledge the exhaustion and look for support. Caring is not a solo jobEnduring until the relationship itself breaks

It is worth setting out separately why the language of willpower is particularly damaging.

  • It redefines a health state as insufficient effort. Asking for help then feels like confessing to failure
  • It adds guilt during an already hard period. Increased guilt during low phases is a feature the sources consistently describe
  • It is the stigma the World Health Organization points to, reproduced inside the closest relationships
  • It burns the opportunity to build the information that actually helps — observation and records — on an argument instead
  • During elevated periods the backfire is larger still. Persuasion is not the goal; connection to care is.

One addition: the carer's own state is also something to manage. Long-running care overlaps with the problem covered in the article on burnout.

9. What to prepare for an appointment

From here a checklist is appropriate, because this is a list of material to bring, not a list of symptoms to count. This preparation is how the structural delay described earlier actually gets broken.

  • Mood record. One line a day is enough. The date, the day's mood as a number, and one sentence if something unusual happened
  • Sleep record. Time asleep, time awake, and whether there were days with little sleep that did not produce tiredness
  • Since when. An exact date is not needed. Roughly since last autumn is already useful in an appointment
  • Notes on the elevated periods. This is the most important item. When sleep shortened, when spending departed from the usual, when plans were suddenly scaled up
  • Observations from people around you. Bring what a family member or close friend wrote down. Bring it even where it contradicts your own memory
  • Everything currently being taken. Include medicines unrelated to mental health. Supplements and herbal preparations too
  • Other health problems. Diagnosed conditions, and recent test results if you have them
  • Family history. Whether anyone in the family has been treated for mood-related difficulty. The UK National Health Service notes that a specialist checks family history
  • Alcohol and caffeine intake. Both affect judgement and sleep, so record them as they are
  • The one thing worrying you most. Appointments are short. A single line written in advance means that question gets addressed first

There is one thing to do after the appointment as well. Write down briefly, that same day, what you were told. Being able to open the next appointment with last time we agreed on this, and here is what happened changes the density of the conversation entirely.

10. Closing: the aim is care, not classification

Back to the opening question: is this who I am, or is something wrong?

What this article can offer is not an answer to that question but somewhere to take it. The reason has already been laid out. At the centre of this condition sits the period when a person can least accurately see their own state, and that period is exactly where the information needed for assessment lives. Which is to say, it is a structure that self-observation alone cannot close.

Three sentences remain.

  • Do not reach a verdict from the size of the swing. That judgement comes from a consulting room, not from this article
  • Keep a record of the elevated periods. It is the one reliable way to break the structure that delays assessment
  • If you are in danger now, stop reading and use the channels in section 1. Not deciding alone is the first principle

Stated once more. This article is general health information provided for informational purposes and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Nothing written here should be used as grounds for self-diagnosis or for a treatment decision. Judgements about an individual state, and the starting, changing or stopping of treatment, must all happen through consultation with a clinician. Separate articles exist on depression, anxiety, and adult attention difficulties.

## 5. Points to remember

- Do not reach a verdict from the size of the swing
- The elevated period is often not felt as a problem from the inside, so observation and records from others matter
- Treatment is a combination, not one method, and no source ranks one above another
- Do not adjust anything **without discussing it with your clinician**, even if you feel better
- Daily management (sleep, routine, records) is a **support**, not a substitute for treatment
- If you are in danger now, stop reading and use the channels in section 1
