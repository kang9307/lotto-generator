Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD): Complete Management Guide - Stages, Causes, Diet, Slowing Progression

An evidence-based strategy to protect your kidneys - from understanding stages to diet, medication and lifestyle

2026-06-15 Health

Introduction: kidneys fail quietly

Many people are taken aback when a health check report says their "kidney function (eGFR) is low" or that there is "protein in the urine." They feel fine, the urine looks normal, and suddenly the kidneys are said to be in trouble. This is the great pitfall of chronic kidney disease: in its early stages it causes almost no symptoms. The kidneys have a large reserve and may send no clear signal even after losing nearly half of their function, so many people are diagnosed only after the disease has progressed substantially.

There is no need to panic, though. Chronic kidney disease is a classic example of a condition where, if detected early and managed consistently, progression can be slowed dramatically. Especially in early and middle stages, simply controlling blood pressure and blood sugar, adjusting diet, and avoiding kidney-toxic drugs can protect the kidneys for a long time. The key is not a magic trick to revive damaged kidneys, but habits that preserve the remaining function for as long as possible.

This guide covers, in order, what the kidneys do, what CKD is and how its stages are defined, why diabetes and hypertension are the main culprits, symptoms and diagnosis (serum creatinine, eGFR, proteinuria), blood pressure and blood sugar control, low-sodium, protein, potassium and phosphorus diet, medication caution and avoiding nephrotoxicity, how to slow progression, and finally end stage (dialysis and transplant). It is not a guide that decides for you, but one that gives you the background to work smarter with your doctor and dietitian.

⚠️ Medical disclaimer. This article is for education and information only and is not medical advice. Diagnosis of CKD, staging, diet adjustments (sodium, protein, potassium, phosphorus) and medication choices or changes must be decided in consultation with your doctor, nephrologist and clinical dietitian. Recommended diet and medicines vary greatly with stage and lab results, and in particular restricting potassium, phosphorus, protein or fluids on your own can be dangerous. The figures shown here (GFR stages, sodium and protein targets) are general references based on published guidelines and do not constitute a self-prescription. Seek medical care promptly if you have swelling, shortness of breath, a sharp drop in urine output or severe fatigue.

1. What the kidneys do - more than a "urine factory"

The kidneys are a fist-sized pair of organs that sit toward your back, above the waist. Every day they filter your blood dozens of times and do far more than most people realize.

  • Waste removal: filter out protein-metabolism products (urea, creatinine), drugs and toxins into the urine.
  • Fluid and electrolyte balance: finely regulate body water and electrolytes such as sodium, potassium, phosphorus and calcium.
  • Blood pressure control: deeply involved in blood pressure via hormones like renin and the regulation of water and salt.
  • Acid-base balance: keep the body from becoming too acidic.
  • Hormone production: make erythropoietin (which drives red blood cell production) and active vitamin D for bone.

So when the kidneys fail it is not just waste that builds up; anemia, weakened bones, high blood pressure, electrolyte abnormalities and swelling can appear together. That is why kidney care spans diet, blood pressure, anemia and bone health.

2. What is chronic kidney disease - definition and diagnostic criteria

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is structural damage or a decline in kidney function that lasts at least three months. The word "chronic" is key: it is distinguished from acute injury, in which kidney numbers worsen temporarily over a few days.

The international kidney guideline (KDIGO) considers CKD present when one of the following persists for three months or more.

  • Reduced GFR: estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) persistently below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2.
  • Markers of kidney damage: persistent proteinuria (albuminuria), urine abnormalities, imaging or biopsy abnormalities, electrolyte abnormalities and the like (these count even if eGFR is 60 or above).

In other words, judgment is not based on the eGFR number alone; both filtering function (GFR) and proteinuria (degree of damage) are considered. Looking at both axes together is central to modern CKD classification.

3. Stages of CKD - GFR 1 to 5 and proteinuria

CKD is divided into stages 1 through 5 according to GFR. Below are the general GFR stages by KDIGO classification. Expressions can differ slightly by guideline and time, so interpret your own numbers with your care team.

Table 1. GFR (glomerular filtration rate) stages (mL/min/1.73 m2)

G190 or aboveNormal or high (CKD only if a damage marker such as proteinuria is present)

In addition, the degree of proteinuria (albuminuria), graded A1 to A3, is assigned together to assess risk. It is measured by the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR), roughly A1 (normal to mild, below 30 mg/g), A2 (moderate, 30-300 mg/g) and A3 (severe, above 300 mg/g). For the same GFR, heavier proteinuria carries a higher risk of progression and cardiovascular events. That is why managing proteinuria is so important in protecting the kidneys.

4. Causes - diabetes and hypertension are the two main culprits

Worldwide, the number one cause of CKD is diabetes and number two is high blood pressure. Both slowly destroy the tiny blood vessels and filtering units (glomeruli) inside the kidney.

4.1 Diabetes

Long-standing high blood sugar damages the glomerular microvessels so that protein leaks out and filtering function eventually declines (diabetic kidney disease). Diabetes is the most common cause of end-stage kidney failure. If you have diabetes, you should regularly check urine albumin and eGFR to monitor the kidneys. Blood sugar control is the starting point of kidney protection (see the prediabetes blood sugar control guide).

4.2 Hypertension

High blood pressure directly damages the kidney's microvessels, and conversely worsening kidneys push blood pressure higher, creating a vicious cycle. That is why blood pressure control is almost always the top priority in CKD care (see the how to lower blood pressure naturally guide).

4.3 Other causes

  • Glomerulonephritis: immune or inflammatory disease of the kidney's filtering units.
  • Inherited disease: polycystic kidney disease (many fluid-filled cysts) and others.
  • Obstruction and recurrent infection: stones or an enlarged prostate blocking urine flow.
  • Drugs and toxins: long-term or excessive use of some painkillers (NSAIDs), certain contrast agents, unverified herbal products and supplements.

5. Symptoms - few early on, appearing late

As emphasized, CKD is largely symptomless early on. By the time symptoms appear, the disease has often progressed substantially. Symptoms commonly seen in advanced stages include the following.

  • Swelling: feet, ankles, legs and sometimes the face swell (fluid and salt retention).
  • Fatigue and poor concentration: waste buildup and anemia cause easy tiredness.
  • Urine changes: foamy urine (proteinuria), more nighttime urination, and reduced output as it progresses.
  • Poor appetite and nausea: appetite falls as waste accumulates.
  • Itching and muscle cramps: related to electrolyte and waste imbalance.
  • Shortness of breath: from fluid overload or anemia.

These symptoms are not unique to CKD and are easily confused with other conditions. The key is to not rely on symptoms; if you have risk factors, find it early with regular tests.

6. Diagnosis - creatinine, eGFR and proteinuria

CKD is usually found with simple blood and urine tests. Even without symptoms, it is wise to check regularly if you have risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension or older age.

6.1 Serum creatinine and eGFR

Creatinine is a waste product from muscle that the kidneys filter into the urine. When kidney function falls, blood creatinine rises. Because creatinine is affected by muscle mass, age and sex, the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), which corrects for these, is more useful. eGFR expresses the kidney's ability to clear waste as a number around 0 to 120; a value below 60 persisting for three months or more suggests CKD.

6.2 Proteinuria (albuminuria)

Healthy kidneys release almost no protein into the urine. Albumin (protein) leaking into the urine is an early sign of kidney damage, and even if eGFR is still normal (60 or above), proteinuria can mean CKD. It is usually measured by the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR). Proteinuria is an important predictor of progression and cardiovascular risk, so it is also used as a treatment target.

6.3 Other tests

Electrolytes (potassium, phosphorus, calcium), hemoglobin (anemia), parathyroid hormone, urinalysis and kidney ultrasound are checked to identify the cause and complications. A single low value is not diagnostic; the principle is to confirm that it persists for three months or more. For how to read a checkup report overall, see the health checkup results interpretation guide.

7. Management 1 - blood pressure and blood sugar control come first

The most proven measures in CKD care are keeping blood pressure and blood sugar in target ranges, because these are the two engines that damage the kidneys.

7.1 Blood pressure control

Good blood pressure control can slow the progression of kidney damage. The latest guideline (KDIGO 2024) suggests a stricter systolic target (for example below 120 mmHg) in some CKD patients, but the appropriate target depends on age, comorbidities and proteinuria and must be set with your doctor. Certain blood pressure medicines with kidney-protective effects (see the medication section) are often used.

7.2 Blood sugar control

If you have diabetes, keeping blood sugar in the target range is important for kidney protection. Good control is reported to slow the onset and progression of proteinuria. However, as kidney function declines, some diabetes medicines may need dose adjustment or change, so drug choice should be discussed with your doctor.

8. Management 2 - diet: sodium, protein, potassium and phosphorus

Diet is a major pillar of CKD care, but recommendations vary greatly with stage and lab results. Below are general principles; actual application must be individualized with a clinical dietitian and doctor.

8.1 Reducing sodium (salt)

A low-sodium diet is recommended across almost all stages. Reducing sodium helps lessen swelling and high blood pressure and lowers proteinuria. A common target is about 5 g of salt (roughly 2,000 mg of sodium) per day. Many people get a lot of sodium from soups and stews, processed foods and salty preserved foods, so drinking less broth and cutting processed foods and eating out is effective.

8.2 Protein - not about cutting it out entirely

The kidneys process protein-metabolism products, so excessive protein can be a burden. But cutting protein out entirely risks malnutrition and muscle loss and is actually harmful. In early stages (1-2) severe restriction is not needed and only excess should be avoided, while in advanced stages high-quality protein may be adjusted to an appropriate amount (a set grams per body weight per guidelines). The right protein amount differs completely by stage, body weight and whether you are on dialysis, so set it with your dietitian and doctor.

8.3 Potassium - individualized by lab value

When kidney function declines, potassium can build up and cause dangerous arrhythmias. In the past potassium was restricted across the board, but current practice favors individualized adjustment guided by blood potassium. People with high potassium adjust high-potassium foods such as bananas, potatoes, tomatoes and oranges, and cooking methods (such as boiling) can lower potassium. Do not restrict potassium on your own; decide with your care team based on blood tests.

8.4 Phosphorus

Accumulated phosphorus weakens bone and raises the risk of vascular calcification. In advanced stages, phosphorus-rich processed foods, colas (phosphate additives) and some dairy are adjusted, and phosphate binders may be used if needed. As with potassium, this is individualized by lab value.

8.5 Fluids

Early on there is usually no need to restrict fluids heavily, but with progression or swelling and reduced urine output, fluid control may be needed. This too varies by stage, so follow your care team's guidance.

9. Management 3 - medication caution and avoiding nephrotoxicity

As the kidneys weaken, so does their ability to process drugs, so medication is a double-edged sword. Some drugs protect the kidneys; others can harm them.

9.1 Kidney-protective medicines (by prescription)

RAAS inhibitors (ACE inhibitors and ARBs), which reduce proteinuria and protect the kidneys, are commonly used, and recently SGLT2 inhibitors are recommended for kidney protection in some patients with proteinuria (with or without diabetes). Statins for cholesterol are also used to lower cardiovascular risk. Doctors prescribe and adjust all of these based on stage and lab results.

9.2 Things that may harm the kidneys (nephrotoxicity)

  • NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory painkillers): long-term or excessive use of these common painkillers can burden the kidneys.
  • Some contrast agents: imaging contrast dye can stress the kidneys, so tell staff in advance if you have CKD.
  • Unverified herbal products, supplements and high-dose vitamins: depending on ingredients, these can be nephrotoxic.
  • Dehydration: severe diarrhea, vomiting or over-diuresis can sharply worsen kidney function.
⚠️ Medication caution. The above is general information and does not recommend or forbid any specific drug. If you have CKD, always tell your doctor and pharmacist about your kidney status before starting any new medicine, including painkillers, supplements and herbal products. Do not stop a prescribed kidney-protective drug (such as a RAAS inhibitor) on your own; if you suspect side effects, consult your care team first.

10. How to slow progression - a summary

Tying together everything so far into kidney-protecting actions yields the following. This combination is the most powerful way to slow progression.

  • Manage blood pressure and blood sugar to target: keep the ranges you set with your doctor.
  • Reduce proteinuria: take prescribed kidney-protective medicines reliably.
  • Low-sodium and individualized diet: cut sodium; adjust protein, potassium and phosphorus by lab values.
  • Quit smoking: smoking raises both kidney damage and cardiovascular risk.
  • Weight and exercise: lose weight if overweight and move regularly within your limits.
  • Avoid nephrotoxicity: be careful with painkillers, contrast dye and unverified supplements; prevent dehydration.
  • Regular monitoring: check eGFR, proteinuria and electrolytes regularly.

11. End stage (stage 5) - dialysis and transplant are last-stage options

If, despite management, kidney function falls very low to end stage (stage 5, kidney failure), kidney-replacement therapy that substitutes for the kidney's role is considered.

  • Hemodialysis: blood is filtered by a machine, usually at a medical facility on a regular schedule.
  • Peritoneal dialysis: a method using the peritoneum that can be done at home.
  • Kidney transplant: transplanting a donated kidney, which can benefit quality of life and survival when conditions match.

Importantly, not every CKD patient reaches dialysis or transplant. With good management in early and middle stages, progression to end stage can often be slowed substantially. Which end-stage treatment to choose is best discussed with your care team in advance, considering your condition, life and values.

12. Common misconceptions and conclusion

  • "No symptoms, so I'm fine": CKD is usually symptomless, and by the time symptoms appear it has often progressed. If you have risk factors, regular testing is the answer.
  • "You must cut out protein entirely": over-restriction is harmful through malnutrition and muscle loss. Set the right amount by stage with a dietitian.
  • "Everyone should reduce potassium and phosphorus": unlike the past, current practice individualizes by blood level. Blanket restriction is not recommended.
  • "Just take a supplement that's good for kidneys": unverified supplements and herbal products can instead be nephrotoxic.
  • "CKD means dialysis soon": early detection and management can greatly slow progression, and many patients are managed without dialysis.

The real goal of CKD care is not to make a lab value look nice for a moment, but to preserve the remaining kidney function as long as possible and reduce cardiovascular events. The key tools are not flashy secrets but things we already know: manage blood pressure and blood sugar to target, cut sodium, individualize your diet, quit smoking, avoid nephrotoxic drugs, and monitor regularly. This ordinary combination is the most powerful. And the one message not to forget is this: read your kidney numbers and stage with your doctor and dietitian, not fragments from the internet.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. What is chronic kidney disease (CKD)?

It is structural damage or a decline in kidney function lasting at least three months. It is usually diagnosed when eGFR is below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, or when a damage marker such as proteinuria persists three months or more. CKD is staged 1 to 5 by GFR and proteinuria, and early stages are usually symptomless. Confirm diagnosis and staging with your doctor.

Q2. What are the most common causes of CKD?

Worldwide, the top cause is diabetes and the second is high blood pressure, both of which slowly damage the kidney's microvessels and filtering units. Other causes include glomerulonephritis, inherited disease such as polycystic kidney disease, certain drugs and toxins, and recurrent infection or obstruction. Controlling diabetes and blood pressure is central to protecting the kidneys.

Q3. What should I watch for in a CKD diet?

Generally, reduce sodium and adjust protein, potassium and phosphorus by stage and lab results. Early on, protein is not severely restricted but excess is avoided; potassium and phosphorus are individualized by blood level rather than restricted across the board. Because diet varies greatly between people, set it with your doctor and dietitian.

Q4. How can I slow CKD progression?

Strictly managing blood pressure and blood sugar to target is most important. Take prescribed kidney-protective medicines (RAAS inhibitors, and SGLT2 inhibitors in some patients), quit smoking, manage weight, exercise, and avoid nephrotoxic drugs and dehydration. Targets and medicines depend on stage and comorbidities, so decide with your doctor.

Q5. Does everyone with CKD eventually need dialysis?

No. Many people are managed stably in early and middle stages and can slow progression greatly. Dialysis and transplant are kidney-replacement therapies considered only at end stage (stage 5), and not everyone reaches them. Early detection and management of blood pressure, blood sugar and diet is the best way to prevent progression.
