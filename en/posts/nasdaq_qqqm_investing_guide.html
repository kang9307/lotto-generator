Investing in the Nasdaq-100 - US-Listed QQQ and QQQM versus Korea-Listed Overseas ETFs

Index character, expense ratios, tax structure and account-level decision rules, set out as of July 2026

July 29, 2026 Finance & Economy

Introduction: which fund is cheaper is the second question

An important scope note before anything else. Every tax figure in this article describes how Korean tax law treats a Korean tax resident. Taxation of the same two funds is completely different depending on where you live: the classification of the income, the available deductions, the withholding rate, the filing calendar and the treatment of losses all change with residence, and tax treaties add another layer. If you are not a Korean resident, treat the Korean numbers here as a worked case study of how income classification drives outcomes, and confirm your own position with the tax authority of your country of residence or a qualified adviser.

With that said. Someone who has decided to invest in the Nasdaq-100 usually starts by comparing products. Is QQQ or QQQM the better vehicle, how large is the expense ratio gap, which one trades more heavily. These are not wrong questions. For a person living in Korea, however, they are the second set of questions. The first is different: do you buy on the US market, or do you buy a fund listed on the Korean market?

Why does that fork matter more? The fee difference is on the order of a few hundredths of a percentage point per year. The two routes, by contrast, are taxed under entirely different regimes. One produces capital gains; the other produces dividend income. When the classification of income changes, it is not only the rate that changes but the deductions, the ability to offset losses, the filing method, whether the amount is folded into aggregate taxation, and even how health insurance contributions are assessed. The result is that the same amount invested in the same index can leave a meaningfully different sum in your hands.

More importantly, neither route wins in every case. When gains are small, one wins. In the middle range, the other wins. When gains become large, the first wins again. Account type adds a further variable: pension savings accounts and individual retirement pensions (IRPs) in Korea cannot hold overseas-listed securities at all, so choosing the account automatically narrows the product set. That is why the conclusion of this article is not a fund name but a statement: your account type and your annual financial income decide the answer.

The article works through that structure in order. It first checks what the Nasdaq-100 actually is, then confirms how QQQ and QQQM differ using provider material, then sets out how Korean tax law treats the two routes using material from the National Tax Service, the Ministry of Government Legislation, the Korea Exchange and the Financial Services Commission. Finally it runs hypothetical numbers to find the points where the advantage flips. Figures that could not be confirmed are not included, and every figure that is included is accompanied by the source used to confirm it.

1. The Nasdaq-100 - checking the technology index label

Most introductions call the Nasdaq-100 a technology index. Judged by outcome that is not badly wrong, but it is not how the rule is written. Invesco's product page defines the index as the 100 largest non-financial companies listed on the Nasdaq. The operative word is not technology. It is non-financial.

Getting this right matters for two reasons. First, banks, insurers and brokerages are structurally absent from this index. They are not merely underweight; the rule keeps them out. Second, plenty of non-technology companies are included. A consumer, communications or healthcare company that is listed on the Nasdaq and large enough will be a constituent. In other words this is not an index that picks a sector, but an index that takes one exchange's largest companies and removes a single sector.

The maintenance rules deserve attention too. According to provider material, the Nasdaq-100 is rebalanced quarterly and reconstituted once a year. There is also a third mechanism: the special rebalance.

  • The index is modified market capitalization-weighted. It is not plain cap weighting; caps are applied on top.
  • Under the rules published by Nasdaq, constituents weighted above 4.5 percent may not collectively exceed 48 percent of the index. Breaching that limit triggers a special rebalance.
  • The real-world example is the special rebalance effective 24 July 2023. Nasdaq announced it on 7 July 2023 and adjusted weights using constituents and shares outstanding as of 3 July 2023.
  • In that action no securities were added or removed. Only weights changed. The combined weight of the seven largest names is reported to have fallen from above 55 percent to roughly 43 percent.
  • The existence of this mechanism also tells you something uncomfortable: the index carries a structural tendency to concentrate in a handful of megacaps. The rule does not eliminate concentration; it only reverses it once a threshold is crossed.
  • For that reason it is hard to argue that holding the Nasdaq-100 alone completes a diversification plan. The combined weight of the top holdings, not the headline number of 100, determines the real concentration.

To summarise, the Nasdaq-100 is a basket of one exchange's large non-financial companies rather than the US market, and within that basket a few names dominate. Reading it as broad US exposure leads to mispricing the risk. The mechanics of ETFs as an instrument are covered separately in the beginner's guide to ETF investing.

2. QQQ and QQQM - two containers for the same index

The two best-known US-listed vehicles for the Nasdaq-100 are QQQ and QQQM. Same manager, same index. So why do both exist? Because they are aimed at different users.

QQQ is the older product, launched on 10 March 1999. Decades of accumulated trading volume and a deep options market make it suited to participants who trade actively or run derivative strategies. QQQM arrived much later, on 13 October 2020, and provider material presents it as the lower-cost version aimed at buy-and-hold investors. In short, the same index has been packaged twice for two different uses.

3. In December 2025, QQQ changed its structure

This item is something that has to be re-checked for 2026. Older comparisons state QQQ's expense ratio as 0.20 percent and therefore describe the gap to QQQM as 0.05 percentage points. That description is no longer accurate.

According to the Invesco press release dated 19 December 2025, QQQ shareholders approved converting the fund from a unit investment trust into an open-end fund, together with a change of governance to a board of trustees. Trading in the converted structure began on Monday 22 December 2025. Alongside the conversion, the total expense ratio fell from 0.20 percent to 0.18 percent.

  • The practical meaning of the conversion is greater operational flexibility. The release notes that the fund gains the ability to reinvest income and to participate in securities lending.
  • Those activities were constrained under the unit investment trust structure, a legacy of the fund's 1999 launch that the conversion was designed to modernise.
  • The release states that the conversion is not a taxable event for QQQ investors. That statement is made from a US tax perspective; for a Korean resident, the Korean rules described below are what govern.
  • The net effect is that the fee gap between QQQ and QQQM narrowed from 0.05 to 0.03 percentage points. The single most cited reason for choosing between the two funds became correspondingly weaker.

The episode illustrates the nature of information in this field. An expense ratio is not a constant. Providers adjust fees in response to competition, and structural events like this one become the occasion. A claim that one fund is a few hundredths of a point cheaper is therefore a value that has to be read together with the date it was checked, and the figures in this article rest on a July 2026 check.

4. The real fork - listed in the US, or listed in Korea

From here the material becomes specific to Korean residents. There are broadly two routes into the Nasdaq-100.

First, buying an ETF listed on a US exchange. You purchase in dollars through your brokerage's overseas trading service. QQQ and QQQM belong here. For Korean tax purposes this is treated as the transfer of overseas stock.

Second, buying an ETF listed on the Korea Exchange that tracks the Nasdaq-100. A Korean manager creates the fund and lists it domestically, and you trade it in won. This is commonly called a Korea-listed overseas ETF. For tax purposes it is income from a domestically established investment trust, that is, dividend income.

The index is the same, but tax law sees the two completely differently. One is a gain from selling a foreign share; the other is a distribution from a domestic fund. That single classification difference produces everything that follows.

For context, on 30 January 2026 the Financial Services Commission published a plan to remove regulatory asymmetries between Korea-listed and overseas-listed ETFs. It identifies investors migrating to overseas-listed products because certain product types are unavailable domestically or face stricter entry requirements, and proposes changes to permitted product ranges and derivatives infrastructure. What that plan addresses is product regulation, not taxation. The tax differences described below are not removed by it.

5. Taxation of overseas-listed ETFs - the capital gains route

A gain from selling a US-listed ETF is capital gains income. The current structure, confirmed against National Tax Service and Ministry of Government Legislation material, is as follows.

  • Who is taxed - National Tax Service guidance states that overseas stock transferred by a resident who has maintained a domicile or place of residence in Korea continuously for five years or more up to the date of transfer is subject to capital gains tax. Unlike domestic listed shares, the tax applies even if you are not a large shareholder.
  • Rate - Under Article 104(1)(12) of the Income Tax Act as summarised by the Ministry of Government Legislation, shares other than small and medium enterprise shares are taxed at 20 percent. Local income tax is levied at 10 percent of the income tax, so the effective burden is 22 percent.
  • Basic deduction - Article 103 of the Income Tax Act provides 2.5 million won per year. The National Tax Service notes that following amendment this deduction is applied on a combined basis across domestic and overseas shares, whereas it previously applied separately to each.
  • Offsetting gains and losses - National Tax Service guidance states that for transfers from 1 January 2020, domestic and overseas share gains and losses may be netted. Within one tax year, losers offset winners and only the net gain is taxed.
  • Filing - There is no interim return; only a final return is required. Under Articles 110(1) and 111(1) of the Income Tax Act, it is filed and paid between 1 and 31 May of the year following the transfer. If the deadline falls on a holiday it moves to the next business day.
  • Distributions - Distributions paid by a US-listed ETF are dividend income. Under the Korea-US tax treaty 15 percent is withheld in the United States, which exceeds the domestic withholding rate on dividend income (14 percent under Article 129(1)(2) of the Income Tax Act), so guidance generally describes no further Korean tax as arising on that basis.
  • Aggregate taxation - Capital gains are taxed separately and are not folded into aggregate income. However large the trading gain, that alone does not make you subject to aggregate financial income taxation. Distributions, being dividend income, do count toward that test.

The point most often missed here is that netting works only inside a single tax year. A large loss this year cannot be deducted from next year's gain, because there is no loss carry-forward provision. When losses and gains fall in different years, the benefit of netting is simply unavailable. The broader mechanics are covered in the capital gains tax guide.

One more point. The moment of currency conversion changes the tax. Under National Tax Service interpretation, the transfer price and the acquisition cost of an overseas asset are each converted at the base rate or arbitrated rate under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act as of the settlement date. If the won weakened in the interim, a position that lost money in dollars can still produce a taxable gain in won. The reverse also holds. Currency is revisited below.

6. Taxation of Korea-listed overseas ETFs - the dividend route

An ETF listed on the Korea Exchange that tracks an overseas index is, in tax terms, a domestic investment trust. Trading gains are therefore treated as dividend income. Based on the Korea Exchange's summary of ETF taxation, the structure is as follows.

  • Trading gains - Domestic equity ETFs are not taxed on trading gains, but ETFs tracking overseas indices, commodities and similar exposures are taxed on a holding-period basis. Withholding applies to the smaller of the realised trading gain and the increase in the taxable base price.
  • Rate - 14 percent dividend income tax plus 1.4 percent local income tax gives 15.4 percent. Because it is withheld at the point of sale, no separate filing is required.
  • Distributions - Distributions are withheld at the same 15.4 percent. Trading gains and distributions are therefore bundled into a single income category.
  • Securities transaction tax - Not levied on ETFs, which differs from selling an individual share.
  • Offsetting gains and losses - Not available. If one holding loses and another gains, full 15.4 percent applies to the gain regardless. This is the sharpest weakness of the route.
  • Aggregate financial income taxation - Because the trading gain is dividend income, it is added directly to the 20 million won annual test covering interest and dividends. Amounts above the threshold are combined with other income at progressive rates, a structure set out in the comprehensive income tax guide.

The concept of the taxable base price increase is unfamiliar at first. In plain terms it asks how much a separately calculated tax reference price has risen. That figure can differ from the actual market price move, and tax attaches to whichever is smaller. Because of this rule, the headline return and the taxable amount need not match. Managers publish the exact base price daily, so any precise calculation has to use that published value.

There is a further consequence. Being classified as dividend income has effects beyond tax. Under National Health Insurance Service guidance, eligibility as a dependant is assessed on combined interest, dividend, business, employment, pension and other income, and where interest and dividend income exceeds 10 million won a year the whole amount is counted. A large gain in a Korea-listed overseas ETF can therefore affect health insurance dependant status. Capital gains are not among the income types listed for that test. Because the criteria also vary with property holdings, individual confirmation with the Service is necessary.

9. What happens when you lose money - the biggest long-run difference

Most tax comparisons calculate only the case where money was made. Over a long holding period, however, the difference that actually compounds is how a loss is treated.

  • Overseas listing - netting works within the year. A 10 million won gain and a 6 million won loss realised in the same tax year leave a net 4 million won, from which the 2.5 million deduction is taken, so only 1.5 million is taxed.
  • Overseas listing - netting stops at the year boundary. With no carry-forward provision, last year's loss cannot reduce this year's gain. Using a loss therefore requires realising the offsetting gain in the same year, which is itself a constraint.
  • Domestic listing - there is no netting at all. Because the income is classified as dividend income, the concept of a loss never enters the calculation. It is entirely possible to be down overall and still be taxed on a specific holding's gain.
  • Inside an ISA, domestic-listed products are netted. Financial Services Commission guidance states that an ISA nets gains and losses inside the account and taxes only the net gain, which patches the weakness of the domestic route at the account level.
  • Neither route makes a loss beneficial. Tax falls; principal does not return. Manufacturing a loss for tax reasons inverts the order of the decision.

The general point is that the more volatile the holdings and the longer the horizon, the more valuable loss netting becomes. As shown earlier, the Nasdaq-100 is dominated by a small number of megacaps and does not move gently. For that kind of asset, whether losses can enter the tax calculation is a variable of a completely different magnitude from 0.03 percentage points of fee.

10. The account changes the answer - taxable, ISA and pension accounts

This is why the account has to be chosen before the product. Account type determines both what may be held and how the result is taxed.

  • Ordinary brokerage account - Can hold both routes, taxed exactly as set out in the table above. It offers the widest choice but no tax benefit.
  • ISA (individual savings account) - Under Article 91-18 of the Restriction of Special Taxation Act, gains and losses are netted inside the account, amounts up to the exempt limit are untaxed, and the excess is taxed separately at 9 percent (9.9 percent including local income tax). The same article sets the exempt limit at 2 million won generally and 4 million won where the qualifying conditions are met, with a total contribution ceiling of 100 million won. Only Korea-listed products are eligible.
  • Why the ISA rate matters - the excess is settled at 9.9 percent instead of flowing into aggregate taxation. That covers both weaknesses of the domestic route at once: no netting and full aggregation.
  • Pension savings account - Securities listed on an overseas exchange cannot be held. Only Korea-listed products are eligible. In exchange, contributions attract a tax credit and gains inside the account are deferred until withdrawal.
  • Individual retirement pension (IRP) - Likewise cannot hold overseas-listed securities, and adds a limit that no more than 70 percent of the balance may sit in risk assets, so equity products cannot fill the whole account.
  • The real benefit of a pension account is deferral. Tax is not taken at each sale but settled as pension income on withdrawal, which grows more valuable the longer the compounding period. Credit limits and withdrawal rates are set out in the pension savings and IRP tax guide.

11. Currency - a much bigger variable than the expense ratio

Buying an overseas-listed product means converting won into dollars. The costs and risks introduced there are of a different order from the expense ratio.

  • Conversion spread - the difference between the published mid rate and the rate you actually receive. It varies widely with preferential treatment and is incurred twice, on the way in and on the way out. Because rates differ by institution and promotion, no specific figure is given here.
  • Direction of the exchange rate - while you hold a dollar asset, a weaker won raises the return measured in won and a stronger won reduces it. The index can rise while the won-denominated result is a loss, if the currency moves further the other way.
  • Currency enters the tax calculation too - under National Tax Service interpretation, the transfer price and acquisition cost of an overseas asset are each converted at the base or arbitrated rate on the settlement date. A position that lost money in dollars can therefore generate a taxable gain in won.
  • Hedged and unhedged versions - Korea-listed products often come in a currency-hedged form marked with (H) at the end of the name. Without the (H), currency movement passes straight through in the unhedged form.
  • Hedging is not free - it carries a cost that moves with the interest rate differential between the two currencies, among other factors. Perfect elimination is not achievable, so tracking error remains. A hedged share class is not automatically the safer choice.
  • Without a currency view - choosing a hedge because you expect a particular currency direction is itself an investment decision. This article does not say which is preferable; it only points out that the structures differ.

To summarise: an unhedged Korea-listed product gives currency exposure without a conversion cost, a hedged Korea-listed product reduces currency exposure at the price of hedging costs, and an overseas-listed product carries both the conversion cost and the currency exposure. Readers interested in dollar assets in their own right may find the guide to dollar investing useful alongside this article.

13. Which fits whom - decision criteria

The list below is not a recommendation of any product. It is what someone who has already decided to invest in the Nasdaq-100 should check when choosing a route. The answers differ by person, so read each against your own situation.

  • Will your realised gains for the year exceed the basic deduction? If not, the overseas listing is simpler because the deduction takes the bill to zero.
  • Is your combined annual interest and dividend income near 20 million won? If so, factor in that domestic-listed trading gains are added straight to that test.
  • Do you run several holdings with mixed results? Then either the overseas listing with its netting, or an ISA with in-account netting, fits structurally.
  • Are you willing to file a return every May? If not, the domestic listing settles by withholding and adds no admin. Missed filings attract penalty tax.
  • Are you also aiming for a contribution tax credit? Then you are using a pension savings account or IRP, which narrows the choice to Korea-listed products automatically.
  • How long is your horizon? With ten years or more to withdrawal, the compounding value of deferral raises the case for a pension account.
  • Does health insurance dependant status apply to you? If you are registered as a dependant of a spouse or parent, rising dividend income can affect that status, so confirm with the Service.
  • Do you want currency exposure, or less of it? Wanting it points to the overseas listing or an unhedged domestic product; wanting less points to a hedged version.
  • How concentrated are you already in one index? The Nasdaq-100 holds 100 large non-financial companies with heavy weights at the top. It is hard to call that a finished diversification plan.
  • Is the amount one you could see fall and still function? No amount of tax optimisation removes the fact that principal can be lost. This may belong at the top of the list rather than the bottom.

The fact that this list does not converge on a single answer is itself the conclusion. For some people a Korea-listed fund inside a pension account fits; for others an overseas-listed fund in an ordinary account fits; and most end up using both for different purposes.

14. Frequently asked questions

Q. Is the Nasdaq-100 a technology index?

More precisely, it is a non-financial index. On the provider's own description it holds the 100 largest non-financial companies listed on the Nasdaq. The rule removes the financial sector rather than selecting technology, which is why consumer, communications and healthcare names appear. The heavy information technology weight is a result, not the purpose of the rule.

Q. Which is better, QQQ or QQQM?

They track the same index, and as of July 2026 the expense ratios are 0.18 percent and 0.15 percent respectively. On the provider's framing, lower fees suit long holding while depth suits frequent trading and options use. For a Korean resident, however, account type and tax structure are far larger variables than 0.03 percentage points.

Q. Which route is better for tax, overseas or domestic listing?

It reverses with the size of the gain. Near the level of the basic deduction the overseas listing wins; in the middle range the lower-rate domestic listing wins; once financial income enters aggregate taxation the overseas listing wins again. Where losses are mixed in, the netting available on the overseas route becomes more valuable still.

Q. Can a pension savings account or IRP buy a US-listed ETF?

No. Neither account may hold securities listed on an overseas exchange directly; only Korea-listed products qualify. Reaching the Nasdaq-100 through a pension account therefore means using a Korea-listed tracker. An IRP additionally caps risk assets at 70 percent of the balance.

Q. Is Korea's financial investment income tax in force?

No, it was abolished. The December 2024 amendment to the Income Tax Act confirmed the repeal, and the stated reason for the 31 December 2025 amendment to the Enforcement Decree of the Securities Transaction Tax Act, published by the Korea Law Information Center, is that following the abolition the reduced securities transaction tax rates are restored from 1 January 2026. Judgements based on older guides will be wrong.

Conclusion - the answer lies in the account and the income, not the product

Compressed into three sentences: first, the Nasdaq-100 is an index of large non-financial companies rather than a technology index, and it is concentrated enough that a rule exists to force its megacap weights back down. The number 100 does not guarantee diversification. Second, the fee gap between QQQ and QQQM narrowed from 0.05 to 0.03 percentage points when QQQ changed structure in December 2025. This is a value that needs re-checking each year. Third, for a Korean resident the decisive variable is not those 0.03 percentage points but the tax structure.

Restating that structure: the overseas listing produces capital gains, so it carries a 2.5 million won deduction and loss netting and stays out of aggregate taxation, but it is taxed at 22 percent and requires a return each May. The domestic listing produces dividend income, so the rate is a lower 15.4 percent and withholding ends the matter, but there is no deduction, no netting, full inclusion in the 20 million won annual test, and a possible effect on health insurance assessment. That is what produces the non-linear conclusion: small gains favour the overseas listing, mid-sized gains the domestic listing, large gains the overseas listing again.

On top of that sits the account layer. Pension savings accounts and IRPs cannot hold overseas-listed products. If you have decided to use the contribution credit and tax deferral, the product debate resolves itself. An ISA nets inside the account and settles the excess at 9.9 percent, patching both weaknesses of the domestic route simultaneously. The practical sequence is therefore purpose, then account, then tax structure, then product, with the fee comparison as the final refinement.

Finally, the character of this field bears repeating. Tax rules and fees keep moving. The financial investment income tax was scheduled and then repealed, securities transaction tax rates were restored in 2026 as a result, and QQQ's expense ratio fell in December 2025. Many comparison articles still online reach conclusions from values that are no longer true. That is why the sources used to confirm each point are listed below.
