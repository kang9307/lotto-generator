The trigger - a proposal from one short post

On October 3, 2026, Simon Willison published a short post titled, in substance, "We're going to need default hard budget caps on pretty much everything", and GeekNews introduced it on October 4. The claim is simple. Pay-by-usage services and APIs should come with a feature that says "after $X per month, cut this thing off and return errors".

The background the source gives for raising this now is agents. Coding agents and personal agents have greatly reduced the friction of spinning up code that does useful things, and that code can do things that cost money (calls to paid APIs, hosted web applications, systems that bill for additional storage and compute).

Three questions are addressed.

  • What exactly does the source claim, and on what basis
  • How do the primary AWS and Google Cloud documents the source links to define the feature
  • What considerations can be read from the two documents when designing or choosing a hard limit

What follows uses only the cases in the source and the policies in the two official documents. Other services' limit features and industry generalities are not in the source, so nothing is asserted about them.

The source claim - reduced to five sentences

The source's claim can be reduced to the following five points. The wording in parentheses reproduces the source's expression.

  1. Soft caps are not enough. A limit of the "send a warning email" kind will not cut it (Soft caps ... will not cut it), and limits must be hard.
  2. The core scene is a warning email arriving at midnight. By the time the email is read, a service that ran away while its owner slept may have consumed several hundred or several thousand dollars more.
  3. There is an objection that businesses may not want hosted applications to start throwing errors, but the author expects that most businesses and individuals would prefer errors to a surprise bill of $10,000 or more.
  4. Hard caps should be the default, and those who want to live dangerously should lift them on an opt-in basis. The proposed wording is "Remove the budget cap. My application will not be shut down if I exceed the configured budget limit, and I will be responsible for subsequent charges."
  5. Agents could help with this. The hope is that they would favor providers with hard budget caps and warn new, inexperienced builders against deploying on uncapped services.

The fifth point shows that this subject hangs not only on human habits but also on the recommendation behaviour of agents. The source wrote it as a wish for an ideal world and did not describe it as an implemented feature.

Why a stop and not an alert - the conditions under which an alert works as control

The source's reason for rejecting soft caps is short. A soft cap of the form "after $X per month, send me a warning email" will not cut it, and no one wants to learn from a warning email that arrived at midnight that, while they slept, the service had consumed several hundred or several thousand more dollars. Putting soft and hard limits side by side on the same criteria gives the following.

CriterionSoft limit (warning)Hard limit (stop)
What the service does at the limitSends a notification. Use continuesCuts use and returns errors
Loss ceilingOnly sends a warning email (the source's definition)"After $X per month, cut it off and return errors" (the source's definition). The Google blog says Spend Caps trigger within minutes of reaching the threshold
When nobody is presentCost accumulates while the alert goes unreadWorks without anyone
Service availabilityMaintainedInterrupted once the limit is reached
The source's assessment"Will not cut it"Should be the default, opt-in to lift

To get a feel for the time gap, take an example calculation that is not in the source. If a service that ran away over eight hours of night had spent $10,000, that is about $1,250 per hour (calculated). The Google blog writes "Traditional billing data can sometimes take hours to reconcile". The figure is an assumption to aid understanding and is not the number of any incident.

The objection that the source raises alongside hard limits is also worth setting down. It is that businesses may not want a hosted application to throw errors because of a budget. The source's answer to the objection is a prediction of preferences: "I expect that most businesses and individuals would prefer errors to a surprise $10,000+ bill". This is a judgment rather than empirical data, and the source presents it as such. That is why an opt-in release is proposed for those who want to take the risk.

In summary, in the source's definitions a soft limit sends a warning email and a hard limit cuts usage off and returns errors. The AWS documentation places 50%, 75% and 90% alerts and a stop at the limit within one feature (see the later sections), so there are implementations where the two devices sit together. What the source rejects is not alerts themselves but treating a soft cap of the form "after $X per month, send me a warning email" as enough.

What the source does not cite - the range of cases

When reading pieces on this subject one often pictures concrete bill-shock incidents, but what the source used as grounds is not an incident citation. What is in the source and what is not are separated here.

  • In the source: statements that the author has heard many stories of people who refuse to use AWS for personal projects - explained as a justified fear that a runaway service might bankrupt them
  • In the source: a statement that the author has heard stories of people who did not anticipate this and ended up seriously burned
  • In the source: hypothetical expressions such as "several hundred (or several thousand) dollars" and "a surprise $10,000+ bill"
  • Not in the source: the name, date or billed amount of any specific service or incident
  • Not in the source: a citation of a case where an agent actually ran away

Accordingly, no account is given here of which service caused what accident. The source's argument lies not in the number of incidents but in a structure: when usage-based billing, no cap and an automated consumer overlap, the loss is not tied to a person's reaction speed.

AWS implementation - what the official documentation says

The source says AWS finally launched spending limits a few weeks ago, and links the September 16, 2026 announcement and the documentation. The announcement's sentences, in translation, are: "When you're ready to upgrade to a paid plan, you can set a monthly spend limit for your project based on your usage patterns. If a project's usage reaches its spend limit, your project is paused for that month." What the documentation (Create a spend limit in AWS Settings) specifies is arranged in the table below.

ItemWhat the documentation says
ScopeA monthly limit per project. Can be set on some projects only, and on up to 10 projects
PrerequisiteA paid plan is required. Only project owners manage it; those with access can view it
What it capsA ceiling on pre-tax cost. Credits are excluded
Minimum limitThe greater of $20 and a conservative estimate of likely spend. The reason is that hitting a limit is a disruptive experience
NotificationsWhen actual cost reaches 50%, 75% and 90% of the limit, or when it is expected to reach the limit within 10 days
When the limit is reachedThe project is paused, all resources stop, and data is preserved
ResumingRaise the limit in settings. Some resources may need a manual restart
If left aloneIf no action is taken within 90 days of the pause, the project data is permanently deleted
Intended useExperimentation, learning and sandboxes. Usable in production only where a brief pause is acceptable
Rollout statusA warning states the new experience is being released to a limited number of customers

The most striking part of the documentation is the optional early controls, which put stages ahead of the hard stop.

  • About 7 days before the limit: stop creating new resources. Implemented with a service control policy that AWS manages, and the documentation warns that with auto-scaling on, new instances may not launch
  • About 5 days before: pause idle resources. EC2, RDS and SageMaker endpoints are targeted, and the criteria are usage metrics over 14 days (for example, EC2 with peak CPU under 5% and network under 5 MB per day)
  • About 4 days before: pause the highest-cost active resources. Selected from five services, EC2, RDS, Lambda, Bedrock and SageMaker, and the documentation says it can prevent a runaway Lambda or an unexpected Bedrock spike. It is opt-in and carries a warning that it can be disruptive

This staged composition reads as a design that puts gradual intervention between the alert (soft) and the stop (hard). Making the alert points concrete by calculation, at the minimum limit of $20 the 50%, 75% and 90% alerts are $10, $15 and $18 respectively.

Finally there is an exception attached to the documentation. To buy a domain with Route 53, the purchase must be within the limit, and only one domain can be bought every 24 hours. If a purchase fails because of the limit, the limit has to be raised first.

There is a point in reading the documentation's definition that is easy to miss. The limit is the baseline that triggers the stop, and how much more cost can accumulate beyond the baseline (the size of the application delay) is not written in the AWS documentation. It is therefore more faithful to the documentation to understand the limit as the point where the stop begins than as an absolute ceiling on cost.

Google Cloud Spend Caps - what the official blog says

The source briefly says Google Cloud launched a similar feature, Spend Caps, in July. What the linked blog (July 29, 2026) says is as follows.

ItemWhat the blog says
What it isA new native feature of Google Cloud Budgets. Sets a monthly financial cap on specific services within a project
When the cap is reachedAutomatically restricts further cost-incurring usage for that service in that project
StagePublic preview. Applied to a single project and service for a fixed monthly period
Supported servicesGemini API, Agent Platform, Cloud Run, Cloud Run Functions (four)
DelayOrdinary billing data can take hours to reconcile, but Spend Caps for AI services trigger within minutes of hitting the threshold
DestructivenessNon-destructive. Data and resources are not deleted, and services outside the budget scope are unaffected
RecoveryThe restriction stays until it is lifted manually with one click in the budget screen
ExceptionFixed commitment fees such as committed use discounts and provisioned throughput continue to be billed
Suggested useTest and development environments

The same blog also introduces early anomaly detection as a stage ahead of the cap. It automatically builds an expected baseline of daily service-level cost from historical data, monitors early signals before costs are fully reported, and, when an anomaly appears, produces a root cause analysis that points to the top three SKUs driving it. This composition also puts alerting through stopping in one flow, so its direction matches AWS's staged composition.

Putting the two implementations side by side - and where the summary diverged

Aligning the two documents' statements on the same criteria gives the following. The differences are more useful for judgment than the commonalities.

  • Unit of scope: AWS covers the whole project, Google an individual service within a project
  • Strength of the stop: AWS halts all resources of the project, Google restricts the additional billed usage of that service
  • Data: both say they do not delete data, but AWS has a 90-day permanent-deletion deadline after the pause and the Google blog states no such deadline
  • Recovery: AWS resumes by raising the limit with some resources needing a manual restart, Google lifts the restriction with one click
  • Exceptions: AWS excludes credits and works on a pre-tax basis, Google keeps billing fixed commitment fees
  • Rollout status: AWS to a limited number of customers, Google in public preview with four supported services

Meanwhile, the GeekNews summary described the Google feature as monthly-granularity only and not reflecting credits, and what was confirmed is this. The monthly (fixed monthly period) scope, four services and application to a single project and single service are in the blog. A statement about credits is not in the Google blog. The statement that credits are excluded belongs to the AWS documentation. Because the two providers' conditions may have been mixed in the summary, credit treatment has to be checked directly in each provider's documentation.

One inference can be read from this difference. A method that halts all resources by project suits a small experiment environment that uses one project per purpose, and a method that restricts only the additional billed usage by service suits an environment that wants to block only the particular AI API an agent calls while keeping the rest alive. This is an inference drawn from the two documents' statements, and neither document says so.

What to consider when designing - seven points readable from the two documents

The considerations below are derived not from generalities but from the statements in the two documents above. The basis is given in parentheses.

  1. What unit to stop at - the scope of impact differs depending on whether it is a whole project or an individual service (AWS per project, Google per service).
  2. How fast to stop - billing data takes time to be reconciled. Google separately says it triggers within minutes for AI services (Google blog).
  3. What remains - whether data is kept and the ceiling on the pause period differ. AWS permanently deletes after 90 days (AWS documentation).
  4. What falls outside the limit - identify charges outside the limit calculation, such as credits, taxes and fixed commitment fees (AWS documentation, Google blog).
  5. How to release it - know the recovery procedure and whether a manual restart is needed before the stop happens (both documents).
  6. Whether there are stages before the stop - gradual intervention is possible, such as alerts, stopping new creation, pausing idle resources and pausing high-cost resources (AWS early controls, Google anomaly detection).
  7. How to set the lower bound of the limit - AWS sets the minimum at the greater of $20 and estimated spend so that normal variation does not trigger a stop (AWS documentation).

The parts that overlap with the source's proposal are default-on and opt-in. How the two documents treat that point is not written in the range confirmed. The AWS documentation describes setting a limit as a user action and does not mention whether limits are on by default. That it should be the default is the source's proposal, not the two providers' current behaviour.

New problems a hard stop creates

A hard limit gives up availability in exchange for stopping a cost runaway. The source introduces this point directly as an objection from businesses, and it is the AWS documentation that states that hitting a limit can be a disruptive experience. The Google blog has no such statement.

  • The source: presents the objection of businesses that do not want hosted applications to throw errors because of a budget
  • The AWS documentation: calls hitting a limit a disruptive experience and explains that this is why a minimum limit exists
  • The AWS documentation: warns that in environments with auto-scaling on, stopping new resource creation can result in new instances not launching
  • The AWS documentation: says to use it in production only where a brief pause is acceptable
  • The AWS documentation: data is deleted if no action is taken within 90 days
  • The Google blog: recommends applying it first to test and development environments

In summary, the AWS documentation says spend limits are designed for experimentation, learning and sandbox workloads, yet also says they can be used in production environments when a brief pause is acceptable. The Google blog recommends applying caps to test or development environments. Whether there is a gap between the source's ambition of "pretty much everything" and the current implementations is not settled by the documents alone, and that judgment is not made here.

Checks that can be run now

To carry the source and the two documents over to your own environment, this order helps.

  1. Check the limit feature of each usage-billed service you use in its current documentation. Services other than the two cases here have no basis in the source, so direct checking is needed.
  2. Tell whether the limit is a warning or a stop. See whether the documentation's sentence ends at a notification or restricts use.
  3. Check the unit of the stop (project, service, account) and the deadline for data handling after the stop.
  4. List the charge items that are not included in the limit (credits, taxes, commitment fees).
  5. If there are paths called by agents or automation, see whether those paths sit under the limit. A path nobody is watching is the source's problem scene.
  6. Rehearse the recovery procedure in advance. Learn the order of raising the limit after a stop and manually restarting some resources.
  7. Put a low limit on test and development environments first. That is the use the two providers' current implementations are most closely matched to.

What matters in these checks is knowing in advance what happens when the limit is reached, more than the limit value itself. A limit that has never been exercised is only configured, so it is safer to confirm in a test environment that it really works and that recovery after a stop succeeds.
