Medical applications of optogenetics remain at the stage of fundamental research and ongoing clinical trials, and they do not replace medical advice for treating any condition or making personal health decisions.

Context and Overview - Why the 2026 Nobel Prize Selected Optogenetics

On October 5, 2026, The Nobel Assembly at Karolinska Institutet announced the award of the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine jointly to Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann, and Georg Nagel. The official citation reads: “for their discoveries concerning light-gated ion channels and optogenetics”. The prize amount of 12 million Swedish kronor (SEK) is shared equally among the three laureates.

Optogenetics is a method that makes it possible to switch on, or off, the activity of individual nerve cells in a living brain. According to the Popular information, an adult human brain has around 90 billion nerve cells, each of which forms thousands of connections with other nerve cells. In many cases the pathway from a nerve cell stretches to a completely different area of the brain, while nerve cells that govern entirely separate physical functions can be closely intermingled. Working out which cells carry which function in such a structure has long been a hard problem.

In the twentieth century, researchers successfully identified which regions of the brain govern various physiological functions. The methods of the time were relatively blunt, however, and could not prove that a specific type of nerve cell directly caused a certain feeling or behavior. The Popular information likens the resulting image of the brain to a blurry photograph with many details missing, and the press release likens it to a sketch map full of question marks.

Francis Crick is presented as one of the researchers who saw this limit early. The Popular information says that, a few decades after the discovery of DNA’s double helix in 1953, Crick began investigating the mechanisms that underlie human consciousness and envisioned ways to activate individual nerve cells in a living brain. Because signals in nerve cells are extremely rapid, he concluded that light would probably be the ideal medium to match that speed. The Scientific background adds that Crick proposed a method in which “all neurons of just one type could be activated or inactivated, leaving the others more or less unaltered”, speculated that “the ideal signal would be light”, and himself admitted that the idea was “rather far-fetched” at the time.

The press release and the Popular information describe optogenetics in four main ways:

  • Discovery of a molecular switch. Peter Hegemann and Georg Nagel discovered a protein, channelrhodopsin, in a single-celled alga. When blue light hits it, a channel opens and charged ions flow into the cell.
  • Conversion into a switch for nerve cells. Karl Deisseroth transformed the protein into a light-controlled switch for nerve cells. In 2005 he triggered nerve signals in rat nerve cells with blue light, and two years later he made it work in the brains of living mice.
  • Establishing causal relationships. With this method, researchers have been able to reveal neural circuits governing specific memories, feelings and behaviors relevant for neurological and psychiatric disorders.
  • Clinical attempts. In clinical medicine, researchers are using the method in attempts to restore sight in people with visual impairment.

The press release states that optogenetics has fundamentally altered our understanding of the brain. Per Svenningsson, Chair of the Nobel Committee for Physiology or Medicine, is quoted as saying: “Optogenetics provides opportunities for mapping the brain in a way that we could once only dream of.”

The official documents list each laureate’s birth year, degrees, position and the institution where the prize-awarded discoveries were made. The press release divides the roles as follows: Hegemann and Nagel discovered a remarkable protein, channelrhodopsin, in a single-celled alga, and Deisseroth transformed that protein into a light-controlled switch for nerve cells.

  • Karl Deisseroth. Born 1971. Ph.D. in 1998 and MD in 2000 from Stanford University, USA. D.H. Chen Professor and Professor of Bioengineering and of Psychiatry and Behavioral Sciences, at the Howard Hughes Medical Institute and Stanford University.
  • Peter Hegemann. Born 1954. Ph.D. in 1984 at the Max Planck Institute for Biochemistry, Martinsried, Germany. Hertie Senior Professor of Neuroscience at Humboldt University of Berlin.
  • Georg Nagel. Born 1953. Ph.D. in 1988 at the University of Frankfurt, Germany. Professor of Molecular Plant Physiology at the Department for Molecular Plant Physiology and Biophysics, Botany I, University of Würzburg.
  • Where the prize-awarded discoveries were made. The press release states that Hegemann’s discoveries were made at the Max Planck Institute for Biochemistry in Martinsried and Nagel’s at the Max Planck Institute for Biophysics in Frankfurt.

Light-Gated Channels in Single-Celled Algae - Hegemann and Nagel Characterize Channelrhodopsin

The starting point of optogenetics was not a brain laboratory but a laboratory asking how a single-celled alga responds to light. According to the Popular information, in the early 1990s Peter Hegemann at the Max Planck Institute for Biochemistry in Martinsried asked a simple question: how can the unicellular alga Chlamydomonas react so rapidly to light? Chlamydomonas is famous for its ability to swim towards light. When researchers stir this green alga into a petri dish and illuminate one side, the green color shifts towards the light source, as the microscopic algae swim towards it. The algae sense the light with an eyespot, a tiny orange dot on the cell’s surface that contains a light-capturing molecule, retinal.

Hegemann measured the electrical signals that arose in the eyespot when it was illuminated, using minuscule electrodes (the 1992 paper by Harz, Nonnengasser and Hegemann). The algae reacted incredibly quickly: just half a millisecond after light reached the eyespot, an electrical impulse occurred. The Popular information compares this with the human eye. In the eye, light triggers a complex chemical chain reaction with many steps, and its final protein, an ion channel, forms an opening in the cell surface through which charged ions flow and create an electrical signal. The entire process takes at least 10 milliseconds, so the response in Chlamydomonas is more than twenty times faster than in our own eyes.

Hegemann could think of only one explanation: the process must be much simpler in Chlamydomonas than in a human eye. In the early 1990s he formulated the hypothesis that a single protein complex both captured light and acted as an ion channel. The Scientific background remarks that this inference shifted the search from signaling pathways to molecular identity. The proposal was met with skepticism, however. Researchers knew of many types of ion channels, but none of them could react to light on their own. Isolating the algal light-sensitive protein was also hard, because once removed from its natural setting the protein became unstable and easily damaged.

A turning point came around the turn of the millennium. The Popular information says that Japanese researchers had mapped the DNA of Chlamydomonas and made the genetic code for thousands of genes available, and that the Hegemann group found among them two genes with significant similarities to known genes for light-capturing proteins. The Scientific background adds that three research groups, led by Peter Hegemann, John Spudich and Takahashi, independently identified sequences encoding proteins homologous to archaeal bacteriorhodopsin in the early 2000s. Hegemann’s laboratory named them channelopsin-1 and channelopsin-2 (Chop1 and Chop2) and hypothesized that they might function as light-driven ion channels. Spudich’s laboratory silenced the genes by RNA interference and showed that their products take part in the photoreceptor currents, and the Takahashi group used immunofluorescence to show that the proteins localize to the eyespot.

The decisive functional proof needed Georg Nagel. Hegemann contacted a colleague whose electrophysiological expertise with ion channels and pumps in heterologous expression systems was exactly what the project needed. According to the Scientific background, Nagel obtained his Ph.D. under Ernst Bamberg at the Max Planck Institute of Biophysics in Frankfurt, where he first met Hegemann. Nagel used egg cells from frogs (the African clawed frog Xenopus laevis) to study proteins. He injected copies of the two genes, provided by Hegemann, separately into different batches of frog eggs, which began to mass-produce the two unknown proteins and placed them in the surrounding membrane. Nagel quickly discovered that the hypothesis formulated by Hegemann a decade earlier was correct: the newly discovered proteins were ion channels that opened when exposed to light. They were named channelrhodopsin-1 and channelrhodopsin-2.

The Scientific background distinguishes the properties of the two proteins. In 2002, Nagel expressed mRNA encoding channelopsin-1, added all-trans-retinal, and made two-electrode voltage-clamp recordings. Green-light illumination induced inward photocurrents carried primarily by protons, which was the first direct evidence of a light-gated ion channel and earned the protein the name channelrhodopsin-1 (ChR1). The paper is titled “Channelrhodopsin-1: a light-gated proton channel in green algae” (Science, 2002). The following year, 2003, the Nagel team expressed channelopsin-2 in frog oocytes and in human embryonic kidney cells (PNAS, 2003). Channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) showed an activation spectrum shifted towards blue compared with ChR1 and operated as a broad, non-selective cation channel. A truncated fragment of just the first 315 amino acids was sufficient to preserve light-gated conductance.

The Popular information gives this account. In Nagel’s characterization, channelrhodopsin-2 reacted exceptionally strongly and rapidly to a pulse of light: a channel opened through the surface of the frog egg cell within 0.2 milliseconds, and positively charged ions flowed through it, producing an electrical signal. Hegemann and Nagel realized that they had made a revolutionary discovery and introduced the gene into embryonic human kidney cells and kidney cells from hamsters. The cells became light-sensitive and produced an electrical signal when exposed to light. In 2003 the two published the results and proposed that channelrhodopsin-2 could be used as a powerful tool to generate electrical impulses in cells using light.

The properties of channelrhodopsin confirmed in the press release and the Scientific background are the following four:

  • Very fast opening. Channelrhodopsin-2 opened within 0.2 milliseconds of a light pulse.
  • Non-selective passage of cations. Channelrhodopsin-2 is a broad, non-selective cation channel that mediates a passive, inward-directed cation flux down the electrochemical gradient.
  • A single protein. The Scientific background describes channelrhodopsin as a single component that is both photoreceptor and ion channel. Unlike pumps, which move ions slowly against their gradients, channels let ions flow rapidly down the gradient, matching the timescale of action potentials.
  • Light sensitivity in other cell types. According to the press release, regardless of which cell the protein was put into, that cell became light sensitive.

Transplantation to Neurons and Living Brains - Deisseroth Breakthroughs in 2005 and 2007

In the 1990s, when Karl Deisseroth started to study medicine, he was considering becoming a neurosurgeon, but part of his training took place at a psychiatric clinic, where he was struck by the patients’ suffering and their need for help. He began to wonder why people with depression find it so difficult to feel joy, and what causes autism or schizophrenic delusions. In parallel with his medical studies he gained a Ph.D. in neuroscience. In the laboratory he examined thin brain slices in petri dishes, but he realized that to understand the diseases he met in the clinic, he needed to study how nerve cells function in a living brain.

Deisseroth was not the only researcher trying to develop methods to control the activity of specific nerve cells in the manner Crick proposed. According to the Popular information, however, many were concerned about wasting time and money on something that appeared to be an unrealistic dream. Deisseroth judged that he had to take the risk of failing, because the treatments he could offer his patients were rarely effective and often produced debilitating side effects.

When Deisseroth started his own research group, it began to search for a protein that could trigger electrical impulses in nerve cells. After hearing about the discovery of channelrhodopsin-2, he wrote to Georg Nagel. The Scientific background describes what followed. With the construct provided by Nagel, he transfected cells differentiated from mammalian central nervous system progenitor cells and, using phosphorylation of the transcription factor CREB as a proxy for neuronal activity, observed that the expressed protein responded to blue light. After optimizing lentiviral gene delivery in hippocampal neurons in culture, a stable, membrane-localized expression was achieved without apparent toxicity.

The Popular information tells the story this way. When the DNA arrived, Deisseroth introduced it into rat nerve cells cultured in petri dishes, worried that the sensitive cells would be harmed by the foreign gene. They began to produce channelrhodopsin-2 with no apparent problems. When the group exposed the nerve cells to blue light, the cells reacted immediately, and the light provoked a nerve signal that could be propagated to other nerve cells. The Scientific background says the recordings showed precise, millisecond-scale action potentials, and adds that Nagel made a fortuitous observation that exogenous retinal was unnecessary. The paper published in 2005 (Boyden, Zhang, Bamberg, Nagel, Deisseroth, Nature Neuroscience) was a decisive milestone, but Deisseroth understood that he had a long journey before reaching his goal of controlling the activity of nerve cells in live animals.

Deisseroth now began numerous collaborations, including with Hegemann and Nagel. With Deisseroth’s laboratory at the forefront, researchers soon found more proteins that can turn nerve cells on and off and that are activated by different wavelengths of light. In 2006 the new method was given the name optogenetics. The Scientific background records that Deisseroth, Guoping Feng, Ania Majewska, Gero Miesenböck, Alice Ting and Marc Schnitzer coined the term in a 2006 review.

One year later, in 2007, Deisseroth successfully activated nerve cells in the brains of living mice. His group introduced the gene for channelrhodopsin-2 into a specific nerve cell type in the motor cortex, the area of the brain that governs movement, and illuminated the cells with a thin optic fiber fed into the brain through a small hole in the skull. This made it possible to control movements of the mouse whiskers. According to the Scientific background, the experiment targeted excitatory neurons of layer 5 of the vibrissal motor cortex, used an optical neural interface combining laser diodes with optical fibers, and required no exogenous retinal even in living animals (Aravanis et al., J Neural Eng, 2007).

The Popular information says that the same year, working with other researchers, Deisseroth used optogenetics to wake sleeping mice in a controlled manner. The group introduced channelrhodopsin-2 into a recently discovered type of nerve cell that they suspected controlled wakefulness, and when they shone light on those cells the mice woke up, confirming the hypothesis. The Scientific background identifies these cells as orexin/hypocretin neurons in the lateral hypothalamus and describes a collaboration between the laboratories of Deisseroth and Luis de Lecea (Adamantidis et al., 2007). Fiber-optic illumination increased the probability of transitions to wakefulness from both slow-wave and rapid-eye-movement sleep, and the latency of these transitions depended on the stimulation frequency.

The Popular information lists the 2012 work as another famous piece from the infancy of optogenetics. Together with Susumu Tonegawa (the 1987 Nobel Prize for discoveries in immunology), Deisseroth successfully activated an engram, the specific pattern of neural pathways that is formed when a memory is created. The researchers studied the brains of mice as they experienced fear and noted which nerve cells appeared to form the memory of the experience. When they later reactivated these cells, the mice showed signs of fear despite not being in danger at the time. The Popular information calls this the first experiment in which researchers could demonstrate exactly which nerve cells are necessary for a specific memory. According to the Scientific background, the study expressed channelrhodopsin-2 only in dentate gyrus neurons of the hippocampus that were active during contextual fear conditioning and then stimulated those cells with light in a neutral context to evoke a fear response (Liu et al., 2012).

The results from Deisseroth’s laboratory in the nervous system can be summarized in time order:

  • Cultured rat nerve cells (2005). Blue light on nerve cells carrying the channelrhodopsin-2 gene provoked a nerve signal that could be propagated to other nerve cells.
  • Whisker control through the mouse motor cortex (2007). An optical fiber was inserted into the brain, and light on motor cortex neurons controlled whisker movement.
  • Waking sleeping mice (2007). After channelrhodopsin-2 was introduced into nerve cells suspected of controlling wakefulness, light made the mice wake up.
  • Reactivating a memory engram (2012). Light that later reactivated the hippocampal cells active during fear memory formation produced fear responses in a situation without danger.

A Paradigm Shift in Neuroscience - Moving from Correlation to Causal Manipulation

The press release states that twentieth-century methods allowed researchers to investigate which areas of the brain affect which functions, but did not allow them to prove causal relationships. The Scientific background makes this concrete. Traditional methods such as electrophysiology, lesion studies, pharmacology and imaging were powerful for establishing correlations between neural activity and perception, movement, emotion and cognition, but they lacked the precision to manipulate specific cell types within intact circuits on the millisecond timescale relevant to neural computation. A causal intervention required methods that were spatially targeted, genetically selective and temporally matched to natural neuronal firing.

The Scientific background also reviews the attempts that preceded channelrhodopsin. Together they gradually clarified the design principles a practical optical actuator needed.

  • Richard Fork, 1971. He exploited the weak endogenous light sensitivity of Aplysia neurons to stimulate them with laser light.
  • Ira Farber and Amiram Grinvald, 1983. They stained the membranes of leech neurons with a fluorescent dye, and exciting the dye with a laser generated action potentials. Intense lasers and exogenous dyes caused phototoxic damage, so these approaches were ultimately abandoned.
  • chARGe from Miesenböck and colleagues, 2002. It was the first genetic sensitization to light of non-photoreceptive neurons, co-expressing three proteins from the Drosophila visual cascade in cultured mammalian neurons. It relied on co-injected factors, and signal relay through intermediates made the timing of action potentials unreliable.
  • Photoswitches from Isacoff, Kramer, Trauner and colleagues, 2004. A synthetic photoisomerizable compound was covalently attached to a genetically engineered potassium channel, giving rapid and reversible control of firing, but the synthetic compound had to be delivered to the tissue from outside.

The Scientific background concludes that these approaches pointed to a single protein that could be delivered genetically, couple photon absorption directly to ionic current, and work with millisecond kinetics. Channelrhodopsin met that requirement. In 2007, Feng Zhang and colleagues in Deisseroth’s laboratory, and separately Xue Han and Edward Boyden, adapted halorhodopsin (NpHR) from the archaeon Natronomonas pharaonis. Expressed in mammalian neurons, NpHR pumped chloride ions into the cell under yellow light, hyperpolarizing the membrane and silencing action potentials. Because its wavelength differs from that of blue-light-activated channelrhodopsin-2, the first dual-color optogenetic experiments became possible, allowing bidirectional control of the same circuit. In 2015, John Spudich’s laboratory discovered naturally occurring light-gated anion channels (GtACR1 and GtACR2), which improved the efficiency of optogenetic silencing.

The Popular information gives examples of the circuits that optogenetics has revealed. Researchers have identified neural circuits that govern the sense of pain, social behaviors, thirst, food consumption, reward and attention, as well as nerve cells responsible for functions ranging from controlling the circadian rhythm to causing a fever when the immune system is activated. In studying a complex behavior such as how mice care for their young, they found that the components of the behavior are controlled by separate neural circuits: one governs how mice gather their young in a nest, another how they groom them.

The method also reaches beyond the brain. Deisseroth has demonstrated that heart rhythm can affect our emotions, since forcing the heart to work harder can reinforce feelings of anxiety, and other researchers have identified specific cells in the gut that can explain why some people would rather eat sugar than sweeteners. The Scientific background offers further cases. Among dopamine neurons of the ventral tegmental area, those receiving input from the laterodorsal tegmentum drove reward, while those receiving input from the lateral habenula drove aversion (Lammel et al., 2012). Activating projections from the basolateral amygdala to the central amygdala reduced anxiety-related behavior (Tye et al., 2011). The Scientific background summarizes that complex emotional states are not governed by brain regions as monolithic units but are assembled from discrete, projection-defined circuits.

The Scientific background summarizes the shift as one from “reading” brain activity to “writing” into it. It also describes chemogenetics (DREADDs, from Bryan Roth’s laboratory in 2007) as a complementary strategy: slower, but able to actuate receptors systemically through peripheral ligand injection, which makes it useful for experiments that need prolonged neuromodulation.

Clinical Medicine and Therapeutic Trials - Vision Restoration and Hopes for Cochlear Implants

The Popular information writes that the hope that knowledge generated with optogenetics will lead to new medical treatments has begun to come to fruition. The method has provided greater understanding of the distinguishing features of psychiatric and neurological disorders such as depression, anxiety, schizophrenia, Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease. It is important that the same text says researchers have taken only the “first steps” towards using optogenetics as a medical treatment. The press release likewise describes clinical use as “attempts” to restore sight in people with visual impairment.

Retinitis pigmentosa is a disease that destroys the eye’s rods and cones, and the Scientific background describes it as a hereditary neurodegenerative disease that leads to progressive loss of photoreceptors. According to the Popular information, ongoing clinical trials attempt to restore vision in people who have become blind due to the disease. When a channelrhodopsin-like protein was inserted in the retina of a blind person, the person regained some vision. Using special glasses emitting light, the person was able to discern and grasp objects on a table.

The Scientific background adds the background to this case. Zhuo-Hua Pan and colleagues showed in 2006, in a mouse model of retinitis pigmentosa, that an adeno-associated viral vector could express channelrhodopsin-2 in retinal ganglion cells and let light signals reach the visual cortex, and they later showed expression in marmoset monkeys (2010) and, with the red-shifted channelrhodopsin Chrimson, in macaque monkeys (2021). Botond Roska and colleagues virally delivered Chrimson to retinal ganglion cells in one blind patient with retinitis pigmentosa (Sahel et al., 2021). Light pulses delivered through engineered goggles produced a prolonged, partial restoration of vision, and the patient could distinguish high-contrast objects on a table. Multichannel electroencephalographic recordings revealed object-related activity in the visual cortex. The document states that several clinical trials using optogenetic approaches to improve vision in patients with retinitis pigmentosa are presently underway.

For cochlear implants, the description stays at the level of hope. The Popular information writes that implants currently stimulate the auditory nerve with electricity, and that if it were instead possible to use optogenetics, the implant may be able to activate the auditory nerve with greater precision. The documents give no details of the research status.

What the documents say about clinical use can be arranged as follows:

  • Target disease. Retinitis pigmentosa destroys the eye’s rods and cones, and clinical trials are ongoing to restore vision in people who have gone blind because of it.
  • Reported case. The Popular information and the Scientific background describe one patient whose retina received a channelrhodopsin-like protein and who could discern and grasp objects on a table with special glasses.
  • Animal-stage evidence. The Scientific background lists work showing expression in retinal cells of mice (2006), marmosets (2010) and macaques (2021) as earlier steps.
  • Cochlear implants. This is at the stage of hoping that optogenetics can activate the auditory nerve with greater precision than electricity.
  • Understanding of disease. Optogenetics has helped researchers understand the features of depression, anxiety, schizophrenia, Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease.

This is the extent of what the Nobel Committee documents say, and they assert nothing about treatment effects or future prospects. Anyone with a vision or hearing problem should consult an ophthalmologist or an ear, nose and throat specialist for diagnosis and treatment.

Discovery Timeline and Research Milestones Compared

The discoveries leading to the 2026 prize run from a question in the early 1990s to a memory experiment in 2012. The table below arranges the key publications and accounts in the Popular information and the records in the Scientific background by year.

Early 1990s (paper in 1992) Peter Hegemann and colleagues (Harz, Nonnengasser, Hegemann) Unicellular green alga (Chlamydomonas) Measured that the eyespot photocurrent arises within 0.5 ms (Phil Trans R Soc B, 1992). The hypothesis that the photoreceptor and ion channel form a single protein complex was formulated in the early 1990s
2002 Georg Nagel, Peter Hegemann and colleagues Frog oocytes (Xenopus oocytes) Channelrhodopsin-1 is a light-gated ion channel producing photocurrents carried primarily by protons under green light (Science, 2002)
2003 Georg Nagel, Peter Hegemann and colleagues Frog oocytes, human embryonic kidney cells, hamster kidney cells Channelrhodopsin-2 is a directly light-gated cation membrane channel that opens within 0.2 ms; light response confirmed in mammalian cells (PNAS, 2003)

Two points in the timeline stand out. First, the Scientific background calls the 2003 discovery of channelrhodopsin-2 the second, arguably more consequential breakthrough, coming the year after channelrhodopsin-1. Second, whisker movement and waking from sleep in 2007 are separate studies from the same year, and the Scientific background cites them as separate papers.

Hegemann’s hypothesis in the early 1990s, the identification of candidate genes around 2000, Nagel’s functional proof in 2002 and 2003, and Deisseroth’s expression in nerve cells in 2005 followed one another. The Popular information writes that after the 2003 publication, a young scientist at Stanford University took the baton.

What Could Not Be Confirmed

  • The total enrollment and statistical efficacy endpoints of optogenetic clinical trials in retinitis pigmentosa. The Popular information describes only the partial recovery of one person, and the Scientific background says only that several trials are underway, so overall success rates and adverse event frequencies could not be confirmed.
  • The status of research applying optogenetics to cochlear implants. The Popular information uses only the words “hopes” and “may be able to”, and it does not state a research stage or whether clinical trials exist.
  • The individual names of the Japanese researchers who made the Chlamydomonas gene sequences available around 2000. The Popular information says only “Japanese researchers”, while the Scientific background cites the database as Asamizu et al. (2000).
  • A difference in how the eyespot photocurrent latency is stated. The Popular information gives half a millisecond, whereas the Scientific background, citing a 1991 paper, says the latency after flash stimulation was only microseconds. The two documents alone do not settle which figure belongs to which measurement conditions.
  • The number of nominations for the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine and the committee’s detailed deliberations. They are not stated in the press release or the Popular information.
