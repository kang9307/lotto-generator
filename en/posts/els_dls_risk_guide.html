ELS & DLS Explained — Structure, Returns, and Principal-Loss Risk

From knock-in barriers to step-down early redemption - what the 2024 Hong Kong H-Index ELS losses reveal about the risks you need to know before you invest

Jul 3, 2026 Finance & Economy
⚠️ Investment notice — This article is for general information only and is not a recommendation to buy or sell any product. Loss of principal is possible; investment decisions and their outcomes are your own responsibility.

Intro: A Coupon Higher Than a Bank Deposit - So Why Did People Lose Money?

If you have seen a product at a bank counter or brokerage app advertising a 6-8% annual coupon, there is a good chance it was an ELS (Equity-Linked Security) or a DLS (Derivative-Linked Security). While term-deposit rates sat around 3-4%, ELS and DLS kept selling on coupons nearly double that. But in 2024, large-scale principal losses on ELS linked to the Hong Kong H-Index became real, and it became widely understood that this product is fundamentally different from a deposit.

ELS and DLS are not inherently bad products. But treating them as "a higher-yield substitute for a deposit" without understanding the structure is dangerous. Based on publicly available information as of July 2026, this article explains how ELS and DLS work, the conditions under which principal loss occurs, what the 2024 Hong Kong H-Index ELS losses revealed about the risk, and what to check before considering one. This article does not recommend any specific financial company or product; it is written purely as general financial education.

1. What Are ELS and DLS - The Difference Is the Underlying Asset

1.1 ELS (Equity-Linked Securities)

ELS stands for Equity-Linked Securities: derivative-linked notes whose return is tied to the price movement of a stock index or an individual stock. Commonly used underlying assets include KOSPI200, the S&P 500, the EuroStoxx 50, and the Hong Kong H-Index (HSCEI), which became the center of controversy in 2024. Most ELS are issued against a basket of two or three indices, and in that case the return is determined by the worst performer among them. In other words, no matter how well the other indices do, if just one falls sharply, the entire product's payoff is dragged down by it.

1.2 DLS (Derivative-Linked Securities)

DLS stands for Derivative-Linked Securities. The underlying asset is not limited to stock indices or individual equities; it can be interest rates, exchange rates, commodities such as gold or oil, or credit risk - a much wider set. The structure (early redemption and the knock-in barrier) is essentially the same as an ELS; only the type of underlying asset differs. There have been past cases of large principal losses on DLS linked to overseas interest rates as well.

1.3 Principal-Protected vs. Principal-at-Risk

Some ELS and DLS do guarantee principal, but the large majority of products sold with high headline coupons are principal-at-risk (not principal-protected). Principal-at-risk products are not covered by deposit insurance, and if the issuing securities firm defaults or the underlying asset falls below a certain level, investors can lose part or all of their principal. Whether the seller adequately explained that the product was principal-at-risk turned out to be the central issue in the mis-selling controversy discussed later.

2. How It Works - Early Redemption and the Knock-In Barrier

2.1 The Step-Down Early Redemption Structure

The most common structure is step-down. On observation dates that typically recur every three or six months, the underlying asset's price is compared to its initial reference level. If it is at or above the set redemption condition (e.g., 95% of the initial price, then 90%, then 85%, then 80%, and so on), the product is redeemed early with the agreed coupon added. The condition steps down like a staircase over time - hence "step-down" - and is designed so the chance of early redemption rises the further along the schedule you go. If the condition is not met, the product is not redeemed and rolls over to the next observation date, repeating this process until maturity.

2.2 What the Knock-In Barrier Is

The knock-in barrier is the threshold that switches on the possibility of a principal loss. If the underlying asset's price falls below a set proportion of its initial reference level even once during the observation period (typically somewhere in the high-40% to 60% range, varying by product), this is described as a knock-in event. A knock-in does not immediately confirm a loss. In many structures, if the underlying asset recovers above the knock-in barrier by maturity, investors still receive principal (or principal plus coupon). The problem arises when the asset fails to recover and stays low through maturity - in that case, the decline is paid out directly as a principal loss.

2.3 The Scenarios at Maturity

In summary, there are broadly three outcomes. First, the underlying asset meets the condition on an observation date and the product redeems early (the most common scenario). Second, early redemption never triggers and no knock-in occurs, so at maturity the investor receives principal plus the agreed return. Third, a knock-in occurs and the underlying asset remains below the barrier at maturity, locking in a principal loss proportional to the decline. The trouble arises when this third scenario is sold under the impression of being a "low-risk product".

3. Behind the Attractive Coupon - Why Principal-at-Risk Is a Trap

The reason ELS and DLS coupons exceed deposit rates is simple: it is a premium paid to the investor in exchange for taking on the risk of the underlying asset's decline. The structure embeds an implicit option sale, and the issuer pays the compensation it collects for that risk transfer in the form of the coupon. Put differently, a higher coupon can signal a lower knock-in barrier or higher volatility in the underlying asset. If you do not grasp that "a higher coupon" itself means "you have taken on more downside risk", it is easy to mistake the product for a simple high-yield instrument. When a loss occurs, its magnitude is proportional to the underlying asset's decline - if the index is cut in half, the principal can be cut roughly in half too - which is a fundamentally different risk character from a deposit.

4. The Actual Risk Seen in the 2024 Hong Kong H-Index ELS Losses

4.1 Overview

The Hong Kong H-Index (HSCEI), which traded near 12,000 in early 2021, had fallen to roughly half that level by the end of 2023. The problem was that a large volume of Hong Kong H-Index ELS sold in the first half of 2021, near the index's peak, matured heavily concentrated in the first half of 2024. According to a domestic capital-markets research institute, the outstanding balance of Hong Kong H-Index ELS was about KRW 19.3 trillion as of November 2023, of which roughly 80% was set to mature in 2024, and more than half in the first half of 2024 alone. As the index reached maturity far below its level at issuance, large-scale principal losses became real.

4.2 The Scale of the Losses

Per reported figures, over the five days from January 8-12, 2024 alone, confirmed losses at five major domestic banks totaled roughly KRW 106.7 billion, with an average loss rate of about 50.7%. Of roughly KRW 210.5 billion in invested principal over that period, only about KRW 103.8 billion was actually returned. Estimates of the total loss scale from the Hong Kong H-Index ELS episode vary, but figures in the trillions of won - as high as roughly KRW 6 trillion - have been cited. Numerous reported cases described individual investors receiving back roughly half of their retirement savings or lump-sum funds.

4.3 What Went Wrong

Financial-regulator inspections found that a significant number of sellers failed to adequately explain the possibility of principal loss, and that high-risk ELS were actively recommended even to elderly or low-risk-tolerance investors. In other words, the core issue was not necessarily a flaw in the product structure itself, but that sales occurred (mis-selling) where the product's risk level did not match the investor's risk profile. This should be read not as evidence that the entire ELS/DLS product category is bad, but as a case study of what can happen when someone takes on risk that does not suit them without adequate explanation.

4.4 Tightened Regulation (2025-2026)

Following this episode, financial regulators have pursued institutional reforms to prevent a recurrence. Starting in January 2026, disclosure requirements for high-complexity financial investment products were tightened - sellers must now state, at the very top of the product disclosure document, which consumer types are unsuitable for the product, the possibility of loss, and past loss cases. Suitability-assessment standards were also tightened to require comprehensive consideration of the mandatory information related to an investor's loss-tolerance capacity, and in the banking sector, sales channels for high-risk ELS and other high-complexity products were restricted to designated hub branches - staffed only by personnel with a set minimum years of experience - rather than general teller windows. Even so, tighter regulation does not eliminate the underlying market risk itself, namely the possibility of a sharp decline in the underlying asset.

5. What Actually Happens After a Knock-In

The most common point of confusion for investors is treating "a knock-in occurred" and "a loss is confirmed" as the same thing. A knock-in only switches on the possibility of a loss; it is not a confirmed loss. Even after a knock-in, if the underlying asset rebounds above the knock-in barrier by maturity, the structure commonly still pays out principal (or principal plus coupon). Conversely, if the underlying asset stays weak or declines further after the knock-in and remains so at maturity, the decline at maturity translates directly into a principal loss. This is why, if you are holding a product that has already had a knock-in event, you need to keep watching the direction of the underlying asset through the remaining term - and you should also know that an early cancellation (redemption before maturity) is valued at the market price at that time, which can lock in a large loss regardless of the knock-in barrier.

6. Who Should Avoid This Product

  • Anyone investing funds that cannot absorb a principal loss (housing deposit funds, living expenses, a lump sum needed shortly before retirement, and similar)
  • Anyone who cannot explain the product structure themselves - the early redemption conditions, the knock-in barrier, and the worst-performer mechanism
  • Anyone who may need the funds before maturity - redeeming an ELS or DLS before maturity can produce a large loss, and these products have low liquidity
  • Anyone who sees "a 6-8% coupon" and treats it purely as a substitute for a deposit
  • Anyone classified as low-risk in their investor-profile assessment who was nonetheless recommended a high-risk product

On the other hand, an investor who allocates only a portion of discretionary funds, who fully understands the product structure, and who can tolerate losing a substantial share of principal in the event of a sharp decline in the underlying asset may find it worth considering. This depends entirely on each individual's financial situation and risk tolerance, and this article does not recommend any specific investment decision.

7. What to Check Before Subscribing

  • What is the underlying asset - how many indices, which ones, and whether it uses a worst-performer structure
  • The knock-in barrier level - what percentage of the initial reference price it is set at, and where the underlying asset's price sits historically at issuance
  • Early redemption conditions and frequency - how often it is observed, and how the condition steps down over time
  • Whether it is principal-protected or principal-at-risk - and whether you clearly understand it is not covered by deposit insurance
  • Maturity and early-cancellation terms - fees and the loss structure on an early redemption
  • The issuer's credit risk - ELS and DLS also carry an unsecured-bond-like exposure to the issuing securities firm's credit
  • Whether your investor-profile assessment matches the product's risk rating - and whether, if it does not, the seller flagged the mismatch to you
  • Whether you were given a concrete, numeric loss scenario - specifically, what percentage of principal could be lost in a worst case

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Are ELS and DLS as safe as a bank deposit?

No. Most are principal-at-risk and are not covered by deposit insurance. If the underlying asset stays below the knock-in barrier at maturity, a principal loss can occur.

Q2. Does a knock-in event automatically mean a loss?

No. A knock-in only opens the possibility of a loss. If the underlying asset recovers above the barrier by maturity, the structure commonly still redeems without loss. If it fails to recover, the decline is locked in as a loss.

Q3. Is early redemption guaranteed?

No. Early redemption only occurs if the set condition is met on an observation date. If not, it is deferred to the next date and may not occur at all until final maturity.

Q4. Which is safer, ELS or DLS?

Neither is inherently safer. The structure (early redemption, knock-in barrier) is essentially identical; only the underlying-asset type differs (equity indices versus interest rates, currencies, commodities, and so on). Safety must be judged case by case, based on the underlying asset's volatility, the knock-in barrier level, and the issuer's creditworthiness.

Q5. Could a large-scale loss event like 2024 happen again?

As of 2026, disclosure obligations, suitability assessment, and sales-channel rules have been tightened, but this is an institutional safeguard against mis-selling - it does not remove the underlying market risk of a sharp decline in the asset. Understanding the product structure and loss possibilities yourself remains the fundamental defense, independent of regulation.

Conclusion

ELS and DLS offer a coupon higher than a deposit, but that coupon is the price investors pay for taking on the risk of the underlying asset's decline. Keep meeting the early redemption condition and the outcome can be attractive; but if a knock-in occurs and the underlying asset fails to recover by maturity, you can lose a substantial share of your principal - and that fact needs to be understood before you decide to subscribe, not discovered afterward. The 2024 Hong Kong H-Index ELS losses were less a flaw in the product structure than the accumulated result of sales where risk and investor profile did not match. Even now, with tighter regulation, the principle is the same: however attractive the coupon, it is safer not to invest in a structure you do not understand.
