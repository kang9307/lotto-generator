You Asked a Short Question and Got Twenty Lines - Where Verbosity Comes From and What Cutting It Costs

Where length gets mistaken for quality, and what you lose when you compress

Aug 23, 2026 Tech & IT

Opening: three lines of answer arrive as twenty

You ask whether a setting needs to be on. Back comes an overview paragraph, a five-item list, three caveats, and a closing summary that repeats what was just said. The answer is in there. You simply have to go find it and pull it out.

Calling this chattiness misses the cause. Verbosity is not temperament, it is the result of length being rewarded over and over during training, and the places where it attaches have been mapped in reasonable detail. Once you know those places, you can also predict what disappears alongside the words when you cut. This piece covers that structure, and the price each shortening method charges.

1. The fact that shortening tools now exist as a separate layer

Two tools of the same character surfaced within days of each other. One passes a conversational model's long output to a local model and hands back a shorter version. The other converts a heightened register into plain sentences. Neither changes the original generation. Both work after the text exists.

The tools matter less than what the pairing indicates.

  • Default output is out of step with the length a meaningful number of users want. A mismatch at the level of personal taste does not get a tool built for it
  • Writing the word brief in the prompt did not settle the matter. If it had, nobody would stand up a separate post-processing layer
  • The fix sits after generation, which also says users cannot reach the generation-side dial

So this is not a complaint about one product. It is a signal about a default that the current generation of language models inherited in common. Rather than shopping for a tool, it is worth asking how that default came to be set where it is.

2. The four structures that make answers long

2.1 The length bias preference learning leaves behind

Aligning a model to human preference usually means collecting comparisons between two answers and training a reward model on them. The trouble is that in this data, length and preference are correlated. People pick the answer that looks like effort, the answer with support attached, the answer with structure, and those answers are generally longer.

The 2023 study on length correlations in RLHF decomposed this across three different settings. Taking apart what actually drove reward gains, the authors found the increases were mostly response length rather than any other feature, and decisively, a purely length-based reward reproduced most of the improvement over supervised fine-tuning. After testing a range of length-countering interventions, they identified reward models as the dominant source. Reward models turn out to be non-robust and easily pulled along by length bias in the preference data.

The same problem appears in methods that train no separate reward model. A 2024 paper on disentangling length from quality in DPO shows significant length exploitation in direct preference optimization as well, and reports win-rate improvements of up to 20 percent on summarization and dialogue datasets once length is controlled. Changing the alignment algorithm does not help, because the bias lives in the data and travels with it.

2.2 Formatting earns points too

Length is not the only lever. Surface formatting such as headers, bold text, and bulleted lists raises preference independently. The style control analysis published by the arena operators in August 2024 added four style variables to a Bradley-Terry regression alongside the model terms: answer token length, number of markdown headers, number of bold elements, and number of list items.

In the results the length coefficient ranged from 0.249 to 0.267, dominating the markdown terms at 0.019 to 0.111. That does not make markdown negligible. The point is that every direction was positive. Break an answer into a list and add subheadings, and the same content scores better. A model trained on that is not being strange when it produces a list nobody requested.

2.3 Risk avoidance adds sentences

This axis is about the objective rather than the data. Saying no, that is not possible is badly wrong when it is wrong. Saying it is generally difficult, though depending on version and configuration it may be possible, so verification is advised is almost never wrong. Every added condition shrinks the surface for being falsified, and an answer that cannot be falsified draws less negative feedback.

So models lean toward enumerating cases instead of committing. Human writers have the same habit, and in areas where a disclaimer belongs it is entirely proper. The net effect, though, is that the sentence count rises while the judgment a reader needs in order to decide goes down.

2.4 Without an instruction you get the average

When a request carries no length specification, what the model can produce is whatever length was common for that kind of question in training. The user may have wanted three lines, but that information was never in the request. And since alignment pushed the median upward, the default you get by saying nothing is longer than the average even was.

A common misunderstanding attaches here. Brief is not a length specification, it is an adjective. Unless you say three sentences, or one paragraph, or one table, the model falls back on its own distribution again.

3. Where verbosity actually becomes a cost

Long is not automatically bad. The cost shows up in four specific places, and a different party pays in each.

Where cost appearsWhat growsWho paysWhen it hurts most
Reading timeTime spent locating the needed sentenceThe userRepetitive work asking the same question dozens of times a day
Token billingOutput tokens, usually priced above inputWhoever makes the API callAutomated pipelines multiplied by user count
Context consumptionPrior answers accumulating in the conversationThe whole sessionLong chats, agent loops, jobs with several documents attached
Signal dilutionRatio of the one line that matters to everything elseThe reader's judgmentWhen a warning or exception sits mid-list

The fourth is the quietest and the most expensive. A safety notice sitting ninth in a twelve-item list exists formally and is absent functionally. How token prices get set and why the savings rarely reach the invoice is covered separately in the piece on inference pricing and where cost is actually decided.

4. Length and accuracy are separate axes

This is where a common intuition breaks. The sense that long means someone did not cut corners often holds for human writing, but in a generative model length is a byproduct of optimization rather than a signal of effort.

The cleanest demonstration sits in the 2023 paper that audited LLM judges with MT-Bench. The authors took 23 answers containing numbered lists, rephrased the items without adding a single new piece of information, and prepended the rephrased set to the original. A five-item answer became a ten-item answer whose first five items were the last five reworded.

  • Two of the three large models used as judges preferred the inflated version 91.3 percent of the time
  • The third held up far better at 8.7 percent, but that is not zero
  • The key detail is that information content was identical. Only length changed, and the verdict moved

The reverse direction holds as well. Short does not mean correct. Strip the conditions from a fact that only holds under conditions, and the sentence is short, readable, and false. Treating length as orthogonal to accuracy is the safer working assumption.

5. What an instruction to be brief actually charges

Which is why conciseness is not free. Phare, a 2025 multilingual safety probe, evaluated 17 current models while varying system instructions to measure hallucination behavior. According to the published analysis, instructions emphasizing brevity degraded factual reliability across most models, and in the worst cases hallucination resistance dropped by 20 percent.

The mechanism is easy to accept. If a question rests on a false premise, correcting it takes explanation. Under a brevity constraint the model has to choose between rebutting the premise and agreeing briefly, and it leans toward obeying the instruction. Compression is not the act of shortening sentences, it is the act of choosing what to drop, and the first things to go are usually these.

  • The conditions under which the conclusion holds, such as version, region, date, and prerequisite
  • Exceptions and counterexamples, everything that would follow the phrase except when
  • Support and sources, the thread by which a reader could check the claim
  • Markers of uncertainty, where an unverified item hardens into a settled one
  • Alternatives, the second option for when the first approach does not work

So a length constraint is a cost dial and an accuracy dial at the same time. Turning both without knowing it means paying later.

6. Four ways to shorten, and what each one costs

Four approaches are in actual use, and their characters differ completely.

MethodHow it shortensWhat it costsWhere it fits
Length specified in the promptGives a volume target at generation timeA strong instruction cuts conditions, exceptions, and support firstRepeated queries with low factual risk
Fixed output formatAssigns slots via table, schema, or field listInformation with no slot vanishes entirelyAutomation with downstream processing
Post-hoc summarizationCompresses an already generated answerCarries the original's errors forward unchangedReading material a human verifies at the end
Trimming with a smaller modelDelegates tone and volume to a cheap modelThe trimmer has less judgment than the originalStyle cleanup that needs no factual judgment

6.1 What post-hoc summarization carries forward

The third row deserves separate attention. Post-hoc summarization looks safe precisely because it leaves the original generation alone, and that is exactly its limit. What the summarizer sees is the produced answer, not the source material.

  • If the original contains a false statement, the summary contains it too. Summarization is not a verification step
  • A may in the original easily becomes an is in the summary. Compression deletes hedges first
  • Without context, the summarizer has no basis for deciding which sentence mattered

The repository notes for the local post-processing tool mentioned earlier say this outright: because it sees only the final messages, it may hallucinate or miss important details. When the people who built it acknowledge a limit and the people using it do not know about it, that is where incidents come from. If you plan to trim with a small model, the piece on what quietly degrades local model quality goes into the settings that matter.

7. How do you measure conciseness

Agreeing to cut is easy. Measuring whether cutting helped is hard, because character counts are measurable and say nothing about whether the result improved. Current approaches split roughly three ways.

  • Length-controlled regression — the 2024 length-controlled AlpacaEval fits a generalized linear model with length difference as a mediator, then predicts preference conditioned on a zero length difference. That correction alone raised the Spearman correlation with arena rankings from 0.94 to 0.98
  • Style-controlled leaderboards — as in the arena analysis above, length and markdown are regressed out before ranking. Models that won by answering at length slide down, and terse but correct ones rise
  • Measuring task success and length together — the 2024 study on concise chain of thought cut average response length by 48.70 percent on a multiple-choice benchmark with negligible change in overall accuracy and a 22.67 percent reduction in per-token cost. On math problems, however, the weaker of the two models tested took a 27.69 percent performance penalty

That last figure is the point of this section. The same compression bills differently depending on task and model. So a report that conciseness worked fine is somebody else's result until you measure it on your own workload. Even a twenty-item evaluation set you ran yourself beats it.

8. There are places that should stay long

Leaving out the other side would make this a one-sided piece. Some places lose value when shortened.

  • Safety notice — medication, legal deadlines, possible financial loss, anything whose omission hurts someone. Judge these by what is missing, not by length
  • Procedural explanation — tasks with ordering and preconditions. Merging steps leaves the reader unable to trace where it went wrong
  • Writing for newcomers — the sentence that is filler to someone who knows is the only entry point for someone who does not
  • Genuinely uncertain territory — answering in one line a question whose answer depends on conditions means that line gets applied to the wrong conditions
  • Documents that become the record — output someone will later have to trace back to its reasoning

The common thread: what is safe to cut is the part that says the same thing again, not the part that carries information. Preambles, closing recaps, and the one-line summary after every item usually belong to the first group. Conditions and exceptions belong to the second.

9. What stays with the writer: verification does not transfer

One thing does not change no matter how you tune length. Fact-checking is a human job. Shortening reduces the number of sentences to check, not the obligation to check them. If anything, a compressed sentence sits there in declarative form with its conditions erased, which means when it is wrong, it is more confidently wrong.

  • Verify figures and quotations against the pre-compression original. Treating the summary as the object of verification removes any chance of catching the original's errors
  • Look for conditions lost in compression, especially whether mostly, generally, and depending on version were deleted
  • Hunt for sentences that turned declarative. Hedges disappearing in summarization is a common pattern
  • Check that source links survived. Cutting length removes footnotes first

How readers on the other side read these signals and adjust trust is handled in the piece on what readers judge and what misjudging costs. For style drift and hedging patterns specific to Korean, the measured study of Korean style conventions is the better fit.

10. A working checklist

It starts with giving length as a value rather than an adjective when you make the request. What to specify and what to leave alone differ by situation.

SituationSpecify thisDo not specify this
Queries needing factual accuracyFormat such as table or items, plus a duty to citeA character cap. Support gets cut first
Repetitive simple queriesSentence count, or a one-line answerA demand to explain every new exception
Automated pipelinesSchema and fields, with free prose disallowedNatural-language volume hints. They do not reproduce
Explanations for newcomersThe reader's assumed prior knowledgeReduced volume. Here length is a feature
  • Instead of brief, write three sentences, one table, or under 200 words, something countable
  • Separately ask for the preamble and closing recap to be dropped. Those two are a large share of the volume
  • Decide whether you want a list or prose. If you do not decide, you get a list
  • Request citations independently of the length instruction. When the two conflict, citations lose
  • If you attach post-hoc summarization, keep the original in a log. Verification happens against the original
  • Run the same twenty questions before and after adding the length instruction and compare accuracy
  • Measure cost with output token counts and quality with task success separately. One metric cannot stand in for both

What could not be confirmed

  • What length-related rewards or post-processing commercial services currently use is not public. The mechanisms here come from published research and published evaluation data, and say nothing about any particular product's internals
  • The length bias studies cited were measured mainly on English data. Whether the effect holds at the same magnitude in other languages could not be confirmed
  • The style coefficients of 0.249 to 0.267 were measured on arena data as of August 2024. Model and user composition have changed since, so current values may differ
  • How much the two tools that prompted this piece actually shorten output, and how much information is lost in the process, has no published measurement

Conclusion: length is a value you set, not a default you accept

Verbosity is not a habit to be scolded out of a model. It is a structure that hardened as correlations in preference data passed through optimization. Length and formatting were rewarded together, risk avoidance multiplied the clauses, and wherever no instruction existed the median of the distribution filled the gap.

So the response has to be design rather than preference. Specify length as a value, measure on your own workload what that specification charges in accuracy, and exempt safety notices and procedural explanations from it. And whichever method you use to shorten, verify against the original. A shortened answer has become easier to read, not verified.
