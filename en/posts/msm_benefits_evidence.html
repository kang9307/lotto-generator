MSM and the Evidence - What the Trials Showed and Why the Pooled Analysis Concluded the Opposite

What this compound one step removed from a solvent actually is, what came back when its proposed mechanism was measured directly in the human body, why the individual trials and the pooled analysis point in opposite directions, and what those trials record about sample size, duration and funding

Introduction: what the words organic sulfur do before you read anything else

On the joint supplement shelf this ingredient usually arrives wrapped in the phrase organic sulfur, or in Korean markets under a coined term that translates roughly as dietary sulfur. Those words do a lot of work quickly. Sulfur calls to mind hot springs and garlic, things filed under good for you. The abbreviation MSM sitting next to it looks like a second, more technical ingredient, though the two are the same substance.

The actual lineage does not run through hot springs. MSM is the oxidised form of a solvent called DMSO, and the supplement on the shelf is mostly manufactured by oxidising that solvent. Coming out of an industrial process is not in itself a mark against a compound. Aspirin came out of a laboratory too. But it is worth starting from the knowledge that the impression the label creates and the real history of the molecule sit some distance apart.

MSM is not an ingredient with no evidence behind it. Several randomised controlled trials have enrolled people with knee osteoarthritis, some with double-blind placebo-controlled designs, and analyses pooling those trials exist. Where MSM parts company with most other supplements is what happens next. The individual trials reported that the scores went down. The analysis that pooled those trials reported that the effect was not clinically meaningful. Two sentences pointing in opposite directions over the same body of data, and half of this article is spent on where that gap comes from.

So this is neither a recommendation nor a warning. The aim is to separate out which claim rests on which tier of evidence, and the decision belongs with you and the clinicians who know your history. How to manage knee symptoms themselves is covered in the complete guide to knee pain and arthritis, and boswellia, which sits on the same shelf, has a completely different mechanism story and regulatory position and is handled separately. If you want other ingredients laid out the same way, the entries on milk thistle, coenzyme Q10 and saw palmetto make useful comparisons.

1. What MSM is - a compound one step removed from a solvent

1.1 Three names for the same substance

Shelves, journals and regulations each use a different name, so at first it looks like three or four separate substances. Sorted out:

  • MSM: short for methylsulfonylmethane. This is what appears most often on product labels.
  • Dimethyl sulfone: the chemical name for the same substance. Ingredient tables and specification documents often use this one.
  • Organic sulfur / dietary sulfur: marketing terms that have stuck in retail. Neither is a scientific term, and neither refers to elemental sulfur or the sulfur compounds in hot springs.
  • MSM crystals / MSM powder: these describe the physical form, not a different substance.

The structure is simple: a single sulfur atom carrying two methyl groups and two oxygens. It is an unusually small and simple molecule for a supplement ingredient, and that simplicity comes back when we discuss absorption.

1.2 The relationship with DMSO - metabolite and oxidation product

The fastest way to understand MSM is to set it beside DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide). DMSO carries one oxygen; MSM carries one more and is the oxidised form. The relationship holds in two directions.

  • Metabolic: when DMSO enters the body, some of it is converted to MSM. MSM is therefore the oxidised metabolite of DMSO.
  • Manufacturing: as the 2017 safety review in Nutrients sets out, commercial MSM is produced by oxidising DMSO with hydrogen peroxide and purifying it by crystallisation or distillation. Material marketed as pharmaceutical grade uses the distillation route.

This lineage matters practically. Search the research literature on MSM and you will find that it is frequently reviewed as a pair with DMSO. The systematic review and meta-analysis discussed below both covered the two substances together, which means reading out the MSM-only share requires opening one more layer of the table.

1.3 It occurs in nature, but not in that amount

MSM genuinely participates in the Earth's sulfur cycle and is present in fruits, vegetables, grains, coffee, tea, beer and milk. The same review puts the natural content at hundredths of a part per million. Humans obtain it through diet and through gut microbes metabolising sulfur-containing compounds such as methionine. The body does not manufacture it independently.

This section in one line: it occurs in nature and you can obtain that amount from nature are different sentences. The quantity in a single capsule differs by orders of magnitude from the trace amounts in food, which is why it is in food, so it is safe does not transfer cleanly to supplement doses.

2. The proposed mechanism, and what happened when it was measured in people

2.1 What happens after it is swallowed is comparatively well mapped

For many supplement ingredients, absorption itself is an open question. MSM is better off in this respect. The pharmacokinetics summarised in the Nutrients review run as follows.

  • It is absorbed rapidly in humans, within one hour. Being a small, simple molecule works in its favour here.
  • It distributes widely through tissues and crosses the blood-brain barrier.
  • The general half-life is estimated at more than 12 hours, and around 7.5 hours within the brain.
  • Between 59 and 79 per cent is excreted in urine on the day of administration, either unchanged or as another sulfur-containing metabolite.

In other words, it arrives. That is a threshold many botanical extract ingredients never cross, and it is one of the few things that can be stated confidently about MSM. Arriving and doing something once it arrives, however, remain separate sentences.

2.2 The test that was only possible with this ingredient

The usual limitation when supplement marketing leads with a mechanism is that the mechanism was seen in a test tube and has never been confirmed in people. The frankincense family is the standard example, and that case is examined in the boswellia article.

MSM is in a different position. Because it is absorbed and its blood levels can be measured, trials measuring mechanism markers directly in humans were actually feasible, and they were actually run. Those results are the single most important piece of evidence for understanding this ingredient.

  • Oxidative stress and muscle damage markers: a 2017 half-marathon trial in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition used 8-OHdG as its primary marker alongside MDA, creatine kinase (CK) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). The race itself raised every marker significantly (p<0.001), but there was no statistically significant difference between the MSM and placebo groups on either oxidative stress or muscle damage.
  • Inflammatory markers: a 2023 Japanese knee trial measured inflammatory markers and cartilage biomarkers alongside symptom scores, and the inflammatory markers did not change. The authors noted as a limitation that baseline inflammation in their participants was low to begin with.
This is the crux: for this ingredient the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant story is not merely an untested hypothesis. It was tested in humans, and the markers did not separate. Both trials were small, so nothing here is settled, but not yet confirmed and checked and found no difference carry different evidential weight. Keeping these two papers in mind makes it much easier to read marketing that leads with an anti-inflammatory mechanism.

4.1 Reading the table across

Look only at the results column and the picture seems broadly positive. Read the adjacent columns on the same row and the sentence changes.

  • The 2006 authors called their study a pilot and wrote that benefit and safety could not be adequately confirmed from a trial of that size.
  • In the 2011 trial, the pain score itself failed to reach significance. What reached it were physical function and the total score, with VAS sitting on the boundary at p=0.05.
  • The authors of that trial listed single-site enrolment, small sample, baseline differences in sex, body mass index and locomotor function scores, and a short intervention period as limitations, and went as far as stating that the improvements were likely not clinically significant.
  • Participants in the 2023 Japanese trial were Kellgren-Lawrence grade 0-1, meaning almost no radiographic findings. That is not data that transfers to someone with established osteoarthritis.
  • On the exercise side, the primary marker showed no difference, and the pain measure that did move failed to reach statistical significance.

5. Where those studies are weak - sample, combinations, duration, funding

Sorting the recurring problems into four strands shows why the evidence for this ingredient wobbles.

5.1 The samples are small

The individual knee trials generally ran between 50 and 120 participants. All three trials in the pooled analysis together came to 326. Small samples cause two problems at once: a real effect can be missed for lack of power, and a chance fluctuation can look like an effect. The exercise recovery trial states in the paper itself that it ended with 22 participants against a planned 32.

5.2 Combination products make the share unattributable

The 2016 Italian trial gave MSM together with boswellic acids. Both arms improved, but there was no MSM-only arm, so that design cannot say which ingredient produced the improvement. The trial was also unblinded, which the authors listed as a limitation, and the comparator was another supplement rather than placebo.

The 2004 Indian trial is a rare case of handling this properly, separating glucosamine alone, MSM alone, the combination and placebo across 12 weeks. The design is right, but 118 participants split four ways leaves roughly 30 per arm.

5.3 The durations are short

The knee trials mostly ran 12 weeks, with one at 60 days. Osteoarthritis progresses over decades. Twelve weeks can serve to observe a change in symptom scores but is short for observing whether the course of the disease changed. That is why no row in the table reports preserved cartilage or slowed progression: no trial looked at those endpoints.

5.4 The funding clusters on the selling side

Read the funding column vertically and a pattern stands out. A manufacturer donated product, a seller covered product and testing costs, and researchers employed by an ingredient company appear as authors. None of this amounts to manipulation, and in fact each paper disclosed the relationship openly, which is to their credit.

Still, it affects the weight when the evidence goes on the scale, and it sets up the contrast that follows. The individual trials producing positive results generally had industry money involved; the pooled analysis producing a negative conclusion was carried out with charitable research funding.

How to read a paper quickly: before the results, look at three boxes - sample size, duration and the conflict-of-interest declaration. Those three set the effective range of the results sentence. Once you have read them, the same sentence reads differently.

6. Safety - adverse effects, interactions, when to be careful

6.1 Reported adverse effects

The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH), part of the US National Institutes of Health, states that MSM can cause allergic reactions, digestive upsets and skin rashes. The 2017 Nutrients safety review summarised that it is well tolerated by most people at doses up to four grams daily with few known and mild side effects. Four grams there describes the range of data the review drew on, not an intake recommendation.

In animal data, no-observed-adverse-effect levels by oral administration were reported in the range of 1.5 to 5 g per kg of body weight. In the United States, one specific ingredient received GRAS (generally recognised as safe) status in 2007. GRAS, however, is a judgement about safety as a food ingredient and is not a recognition of efficacy; the two must be read separately.

6.2 Interactions - the absence of established ones is itself information

No clearly established drug interaction has been identified. The only item the safety review raised concerned chronic use and increased alcohol sensitivity, and the authors stated that the sources were anecdotal reports at the level of internet posts, that no peer-reviewed study has examined it, and that further research is needed.

That said, no reported interaction is not the same as no interaction. Where long-term human data is thin, the matter may not have been confirmed absent so much as never adequately looked for.

6.3 Cases where you should speak to someone first

  • Pregnancy or breastfeeding: no data assessing safety in these periods was identified.
  • Taking several medicines regularly: the absence of established interactions is not a safety confirmation.
  • Surgery scheduled: as a rule, any supplement being taken should be disclosed in advance regardless of type.
  • Joint symptoms not yet diagnosed: a swollen, painful knee has causes other than osteoarthritis, and management diverges completely by cause. Starting a supplement first delays that differentiation.
  • Already on prescription medication: reducing or stopping a prescribed medicine is never a decision to make alone. Whether to add or remove a supplement should be settled with the prescribing doctor or pharmacist.
  • A history of allergy: since NCCIH names allergic reactions explicitly, stop and seek advice if skin or respiratory symptoms appear after starting.

7. Where the evidence is firmer - the boxes to fill first

Because this article covers one supplement ingredient, it can easily obscure the areas where the evidence is far stronger. In knee and hip osteoarthritis, what the 2019 guideline issued jointly by the American College of Rheumatology and the Arthritis Foundation placed in the strongly recommended tier is not a supplement.

  • Exercise: including walking, strengthening, neuromuscular training and aquatic exercise, with no hierarchy placing any one above another. The guideline notes that supervised exercise is associated with better outcomes.
  • Weight loss for those who are overweight: an approach that directly reduces the load carried by the knee and hip.
  • Patient education and self-efficacy and self-management programmes: classified as core treatments.
  • Topical non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs for knee osteoarthritis: among the strong recommendations in the pharmacological category. Whether and how to use them is settled with a clinician.

The same guideline explicitly recommends against a number of items, including bisphosphonates, hydroxychloroquine, methotrexate, platelet-rich plasma and stem cell injections in the knee and hip, tumour necrosis factor inhibitors, and interleukin-1 receptor antagonists.

A judgement about order: if budget and time are limited, filling the strongly recommended boxes first has the higher expected value. Exercise and weight management carry the highest grade of evidence at the lowest cost; supplements carry the lowest grade of evidence at a recurring monthly cost. This is not an argument against taking a supplement, but against putting one in the place of those boxes. Where joint supplements generally sit, glucosamine and chondroitin included, is covered in the knee pain and arthritis guide.

8.2 Regulatory position - and how far it could be verified

Accuracy here requires separating what was confirmed from what was not.

  • Confirmed: in Korea, MSM appears by name in the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety materials on health functional foods and is handled within the framework of the Standards and Specifications for Health Functional Foods. The basis for it circulating as a health functional food ingredient exists.
  • Not confirmed: the exact approved wording of the functional claim, and whether it is classified as a notified or an individually recognised ingredient, could not be verified by opening the primary document. The full text of the relevant notice is distributed only as a compressed download, and the body text on the national law portal did not render. Copying wording from secondary sources would not meet the standard used in this article, so it has been left out.
  • Recognised daily intake figures: the nationally recognised intake figures have deliberately not been reproduced here, because such a number placed in running text reads as a dosing instruction. Every dose in this article is the value a given trial used.

To check it yourself, the primary routes are searching the ingredient name in the health functional food ingredient database run by the Korean food safety portal, or locating the ingredient name in the published full text of the Standards and Specifications for Health Functional Foods. Wording printed on retail pages and blogs frequently differs from the approved wording and is best not relied on.

Internationally the contrast is sharp. In the United States, as noted above, one ingredient holds a safety status as a food ingredient, but that is a safety judgement, not a recognition of efficacy. And NCCIH continues to hold the position that very little research has been done, so it is uncertain whether it helps osteoarthritis symptoms.

9. What could not be confirmed

Gathering the unfilled boxes in one place. Scattered through the text they only make reading harder; collected here, they make clear what to look at next.

  • Funding for the 2006 and 2004 trials: no conflict-of-interest declaration was identifiable within the published abstracts, so those cells in the table were left blank.
  • The Korean approved claim wording and classification: the primary document could not be opened, for the reasons given in 8.2.
  • A recent pooled analysis: no new systematic review or meta-analysis from 2021 onward was found. The pooled analysis in current circulation dates from 2011.
  • Structural endpoints: no trial using imaging measures such as cartilage thickness or joint space as a primary endpoint was identified.
  • Long-term safety: human safety data beyond 12 weeks is thin.
  • Alcohol sensitivity: only anecdotal reports exist, with no peer-reviewed study examining them.
  • The contribution within combinations: apart from the 2004 four-arm trial, no study separating MSM's share when taken with other joint ingredients was identified.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does MSM work for joints?

This is not at a stage where yes or no is an honest answer. What makes MSM unusual is that the individual trials and the pooled analysis point in opposite directions. The 2006 pilot in Osteoarthritis and Cartilage randomised 50 people for 12 weeks and reported significant decreases in WOMAC pain and physical function versus placebo; the 2011 trial in BMC Complementary and Alternative Medicine reported a 14.6 mm difference in WOMAC physical function and 15.0 mm in the total. Yet a meta-analysis published the same year pooled three randomised controlled trials totalling 326 patients and found a visual analogue scale pain reduction of 6.34 mm that was not statistically significant, concluding that current evidence suggests these supplements are not clinically effective in reducing osteoarthritis pain. The authors of the individual trials also noted themselves that the improvements may not have been of a clinically meaningful size.

What exactly is MSM, and how does it differ from DMSO?

MSM stands for methylsulfonylmethane; its chemical name is dimethyl sulfone. It is a simple organosulfur compound with one sulfur atom carrying two methyl groups and two oxygens. The relationship with DMSO is best explained as lineage: DMSO carries one oxygen, MSM one more, making it the oxidised form and the metabolite DMSO becomes in the body. Commercial MSM is made by oxidising DMSO with hydrogen peroxide and purifying by crystallisation or distillation. It also occurs naturally in very small amounts in fruits, vegetables, grains, coffee, tea, beer and milk, and humans obtain it from diet and gut microbial sulfur metabolism. The body does not synthesise it on its own. It is a compound one step removed from a long-established solvent, which says nothing about whether it is good or bad, but is closer to the facts than the story on the shelf.

Why do the studies reach different conclusions?

Four things overlap. Sample sizes are small: the individual knee trials ran roughly 50 to 120 participants, and one exercise trial finished with 22 against a target of 32. Combination products are a problem: the 2016 Italian trial gave MSM with boswellic acids, so the share attributable to each ingredient cannot be separated. Durations are short: mostly 12 weeks, which against the timescale of osteoarthritis can show symptoms but not the course of the disease. And funding: several trials disclose that product was donated or research paid for by a company that sells it, whereas the pooled analysis that reached a negative conclusion was supported by charitable research trusts.

Are there side effects or drugs it should not be taken with?

NCCIH states that MSM can cause allergic reactions, digestive upsets and skin rashes. The 2017 Nutrients safety review summarised that most people tolerate it well at doses up to four grams daily with few known and mild side effects, where four grams describes the range of the data drawn on rather than an intake recommendation. No clearly established drug interaction has been identified. The only item that review raised was anecdotal reporting about chronic use and alcohol sensitivity, and the authors stated that no peer-reviewed study has examined it. So it is neither confirmed safe nor confirmed risky on that point. If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, take medicines regularly, or have surgery scheduled, speak to a doctor or pharmacist before starting.

Is it better taken together with boswellia or glucosamine?

There are not enough trials designed to answer that. The 2016 Italian trial compared a combination of MSM and boswellic acids against glucosamine sulfate and both groups improved, but it was unblinded and had no MSM-only arm, so the contribution of each ingredient cannot be separated. The 2004 Indian trial did include both single-ingredient and combination arms, but with 118 participants split four ways. Rather than supporting combinations, this is better read as not yet tested. Every ingredient added creates another untested combination and makes it harder to identify the cause if an adverse effect appears. For the boswellia evidence see the separate article, and for glucosamine and chondroitin the knee pain guide.

Conclusion

In one sentence, MSM is an ingredient that is hard to judge not because the upper rungs of the evidence ladder are empty, but because there is data up there and it does not support the explanation given below. Absorption is confirmed, it reaches tissues, and several double-blind placebo-controlled trials have actually been run. Not many supplement ingredients have accumulated that much.

The difficulty is that the data points in diverging directions. The individual trials reported that knee function scores went down, while the analysis pooling them reported that the pain reduction was not statistically significant. The anti-inflammatory and antioxidant mechanism was measured directly in humans, and in both trials that measured it the markers did not separate. The positive results generally have seller money attached; the negative conclusion was produced with charitable funding.

Which leads to a practical summary. If the goal is managing knee symptoms, filling the boxes carrying a strong recommendation has the higher expected value: exercise, weight loss where someone is overweight, and self-management education, which cost the least and carry the highest grade of evidence. If you decide to try it anyway, it is better to do so knowing that the decision is not a bet on a confirmed effect but a wager of money and time on a thin stretch of evidence. And that judgement belongs not to this article but to you together with clinicians who know your history and your medicines.

Stated once more: this article is for information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Whether to take any supplement should be decided in consultation with a doctor or pharmacist. Every dose given above is the value used in that trial, not an intake instruction. Nothing here suggests that any supplement can substitute for a prescribed medicine, and adjusting prescribed medication on your own is dangerous. If joint symptoms persist or worsen, seek medical care before adjusting supplements.
