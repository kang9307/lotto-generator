Volume-Rate Bags and Waste Sorting in Korea: What Goes Where

What belongs in the bag and what does not, which rules hold nationwide and which only in your own district - as of August 2026

Aug 11, 2026 Life & Practical

Introduction: When the bag you put out is still there at night

You put a bag outside the door in the morning and it is still there in the evening. Your neighbour's is gone, yours is not, and on closer inspection there is a yellow warning sticker on it. The cause is almost always one of three things. The bag is not from this area, something that should not be in a bag is inside it, or the day or time was wrong. None of these are a failure of effort. They are a question of how far you checked the rules.

Korean waste rules come in two layers. The lower layer is the national principle set by statute. The upper layer is the local rule each city, county or district writes into its own ordinance. That is why an online guide saying 'this goes here' can be wrong in your neighbourhood without being a lie — it was written for somewhere else. So this article separates, from the start, what is the same everywhere from what you must confirm locally.

Scope · This covers household waste in Korea. Business waste, construction waste and medical waste follow separate procedures. Fine amounts reflect the law and the schedules published by local governments as of August 2026, and it is the local authority that decides what is actually imposed. This article contains no advice on avoiding enforcement.

1. The bag is not plastic film, it is a prepaid disposal fee

Think of a volume-rate bag as 'a bag for rubbish' and the system never makes sense. What you pay for is not the plastic; it is the cost of processing the waste, paid in advance. Article 14 of the Wastes Control Act makes household waste the responsibility of the mayor or district head, and provides that the fee be collected by selling volume-rate bags or markers identifying the item as waste. Food waste may be charged by the quantity discharged, which is where per-household weighing devices and payment chips come from.

Two things follow. First, buying the bag is paying the fee, so waste put out without one is waste nobody has paid for. Second, because the money is local revenue, the bag is only valid inside the authority that issued it. The area name printed across the bag is not decoration; the bag is the receipt.

The duty on the person discharging waste sits in Article 15 of the same Act. Residents must handle or reduce their waste in a way that does no harm to the living environment, as prescribed by ordinance, and the concrete method of sorting and storing recyclables is left to ordinance. That single clause explains everything else in this article.

2. Why another area's bag is refused, and where that bends

This is the classic newcomer experience. A box of bags left over from the old neighbourhood goes out in the new one and simply is not collected. The reason is plain, as Korea's policy briefing portal explains: the processing fee went to the old authority while the new one does the collecting. There is no mechanism to settle that between local governments, so the default became refusal.

That default has loosened in recent years. To spare people who have just moved, more authorities now accept outside bags on conditions. There are three patterns.

  • Accepted as they are — put them out with no extra step.
  • Accepted with a verification sticker — collect a sticker at the community service center and attach it, often with a cap on how many bags.
  • Not accepted — you buy local bags and transfer the contents.

Which of the three applies cannot be worked out from someone else's blog post about a different district. The convenient moment is the day you register your new address, since you are at the community service center anyway. The wider sequence of thinning out possessions before a move is covered in The Complete Moving Guide.

The right column is wide for a reason. Some areas incinerate, others landfill, and haulage distance and labour costs differ. Different processing means different costs, and different costs mean different bag prices and different accepted items. So treat any list advertised as 'the national rules' with suspicion. The four principles below are about the only real exception.

4. The four principles: empty, rinse, separate, do not mix

Recycling is not 'putting things in the recycling bin'. It is getting material into a state a sorting facility can actually pick out. Four rules produce that state.

  • Empty — get out whatever is left inside. A PET bottle half full of juice is a contaminant that ruins the recyclables around it.
  • Rinse — wash off food and residue with water. Greasy containers may need a little detergent before they qualify.
  • Separate — pull off labels, caps and pumps made of a different material. Mixed materials get discarded as one lump.
  • Do not mix — sort by type into the designated bin. Bundling everything into one bag and dropping it in the recycling bin is not sorting.

One test runs through all four. If rinsing will not make it clean, it is not recycling, it is the bag. That sentence settles most borderline cases. Oil-soaked paper, plastic tubs with food baked onto them and dirty film get pulled out at the sorting line and burned anyway, contaminating neighbouring material on the way. Better not to put them in.

The duty itself is statutory. Article 7(2) of the Framework Act on Transition to a Circular Economy Society requires everyone to discharge waste in a state that makes circular use as easy as possible. The four principles are that phrase translated into kitchen language.

7. Where food waste ends and ordinary waste begins

Food waste is collected on the assumption that it becomes feed or compost. The test is therefore not 'did this come from food' but 'could livestock eat it, and will it survive the grinder'. Hold onto that and you rarely need the list.

One caveat matters more than the list. The food waste standard is set by local ordinance, so the banned items genuinely differed from district to district. Seoul produced a standard list for food waste separation precisely because the answer used to change within one city. This is the first thing to re-check after a move.

In practice the remaining problem is water. Moisture is weight, weight is fee, and it is also the source of smell and flies. Straining broth off and bagging only the solids makes summer noticeably easier. If you are considering equipment to cut the volume itself, How to Choose a Food Waste Processor covers installation conditions and the regulations involved.

8. Why clear PET bottles are collected on their own

The separate PET slot is a recent addition. After the Ministry's guideline on separate collection of recyclable resources was amended, separate discharge of clear PET bottles began at apartment complexes on 25 December 2020 and was extended to detached houses, low-rise blocks and small commercial premises on 25 December 2021.

The point is grade. Mixed-colour PET becomes low-value filler, whereas clear PET collected on its own becomes high-grade feedstock that can go back into bottles or textile fibre. Hence the specific actions.

  • Empty and rinse — leftover drink contaminates the other bottles.
  • Remove the label — with a fingernail for adhesive types, along the perforation where there is one.
  • Crush it — volume reduction is what makes collection efficient.
  • Cap it — guidance generally says to screw the cap back on to hold the shape and keep the cap in the stream.
  • Keep coloured PET out — brown or green beer and drink bottles do not belong in the clear bin.

Detached-house areas often run a day-of-week system for film and clear PET. That schedule is a local rule and needs rechecking after a move.

9. Bulky waste: report first, sticker second

Furniture, mattresses, bicycles and large cabinets — anything that will not go in a bag — are bulky waste. The skeleton of the procedure is national.

  1. File a discharge report on your local authority's website or app, or at the community service center or call centre.
  2. Select the item and size and pay the fee.
  3. Write the issued number on the item or print and attach the sticker that serves as proof of payment.
  4. Put it out at the place and time you were told.

What varies locally is the fee per item, the application channel, the place and time, and even where the line between bulky and ordinary waste falls. The same three-seat sofa costs different amounts in different districts, so it is more reliable to read the price off the application screen than to look it up in advance.

Two things are out of bounds. Copying or reusing a payment sticker is grounds for refusal and is treated as illegal dumping. So is putting the item out without reporting it. That is where the fines below come in.

10. Appliances do not need a paid sticker

This is the most commonly missed item. Waste electrical appliances are collected free of charge, with no bulky waste fee, because manufacturers and importers carry the cost under extended producer responsibility. Book through the waste appliance pickup reservation system or by phone on 1599-0903.

CategoryTypical itemsQuantity condition
Single itemRefrigerator, washing machine, air conditioner, TV or monitor 31 inches and above, solar panelOne is enough
BulkTVs and monitors 30 inches and under, navigation units, tablets, small and mid-size appliancesFive or more together

From 1 January 2026 the take-back scope widened. Subordinate legislation under the Act on Resource Circulation of Electrical and Electronic Equipment and Vehicles extended the obligation from a list centred on mid and large appliances to substantially all electrical and electronic products, with medical devices posing infection risk and defence items excluded. Clothes dryers, handheld fans and small kitchen appliances are now inside the system. How quickly small items are handled on the ground may still vary by area, so check the item list in the booking system before calling for a pickup.

11. The streams that never go in a bag: medicines, batteries, lamps

These three must stay out of the bag. Before recycling is even a question, there are water pollution and fire risks involved.

11.1 Unused medicines

Flushing leftover medicine sends antibiotic residues into the sewer system. The official guidance on medicine take-back differs by form.

  • Tablets and capsules — discard the carton and hand in only the contents, gathered in a bag.
  • Powders — do not open the sachets; hand them in sealed as they are.
  • Liquids, syrups, ointments — close the cap so nothing leaks and hand in the container as a whole.
  • Collection points — pharmacies, public health centres, district offices and community service centers.
  • Post boxes — used by some local authorities, but liquids, eye drops and ointments must not go in, as leaks damage other mail.
  • Health supplements and quasi-drugs — not medicines, so they go in the volume-rate bag.

11.2 Batteries and fluorescent lamps

Both go to dedicated collection boxes, found at community service centers, apartment management offices, village halls and some convenience stores. Locations vary by area.

  • Take batteries out of the product. Left inside a remote or a toy, they do not get processed.
  • Tape the terminals of lithium cells. Impact or moisture can start a fire. The same goes for power banks.
  • Handle lamps without breaking them. If one has already broken, wrap it in newspaper before putting it in the box.
  • Flat LED panels and fittings with ballasts often fall outside the lamp box and follow the bulky waste or local route.
Lithium batteries fail at the moment of compaction. A power bank or a pair of earbuds dropped into a volume-rate bag can be crushed in the collection truck or at the sorting facility and ignite. This is a repeatedly cited cause of fires at recycling plants. Inconvenient as it is, use the battery or small appliance route.

12. What the fines attach to

The point of listing these is less the money than seeing which act falls under which article, because that organises the rules. There are two branches: dumping in the wrong place (Article 8 of the Wastes Control Act) and discharging by the wrong method (Article 15 of the same Act).

The ceiling is statutory. Violations of both Article 8 and Article 15 carry a fine for negligence of up to one million won under Article 68(3), and the individual amounts above are the Schedule 8 standards of the Enforcement Decree as published by local governments (see Geumcheon-gu and Paju). Reductions may apply for recipients of basic livelihood benefits and people with disabilities, and there is a window to object after notice.

In everyday life the last row catches far more people than the fourth. Mixing recyclables and food waste into one bag, or putting waste out outside the designated day and time, lands there. Knowing your own area's schedule is therefore more useful than knowing the amounts.

13. How this compares with systems elsewhere

Korea's scheme is unusually granular by international standards. Setting it beside other systems makes clear which parts are specifically Korean.

  • United States — kerbside collection from household carts is the norm, and single-stream operation, where recyclables go into one cart and are separated at a materials recovery facility, is widespread. Rules are set city by city or county by county, with no national standard, which is the one thing it shares with Korea.
  • Japan — sorting categories differ by municipality, and many municipalities require designated bags bought at a price. The instinct that 'the rules change when you move' is very close to the Korean experience.
  • China — municipal ordinances run household waste classification, generally built on a four-way split of recyclables, hazardous waste, kitchen waste and other waste, with the labels varying by city.

Three things tend to catch newcomers to Korea. First, the bag itself is the fee, so there is no separate bill and no cart of your own; you buy the right bag at a convenience store or supermarket. Second, kitchen waste is a genuinely separate stream with its own container, chip or weighing device, and putting it in the general bag is a breach rather than a preference. Third, the bag stops working the moment you cross a district boundary, which has no equivalent in a cart-based system.

What the three systems share is that the local rule is always the final authority. Where Korea differs is in loading the cost directly onto the price of the bag, so the person discharging feels it every time. Throw away less and you pay less. That design is exactly why a Korean bag loses its validity outside the area that issued it.

14. A thirty-minute check on moving day

There is not much to confirm in a new neighbourhood. The order is what matters.

  1. Ask while registering your address — where bags are sold, and whether outside bags are honoured and how.
  2. Confirm collection days and times — general, food, recyclables, film and PET often differ.
  3. Confirm the food waste method — dedicated container, payment chip, or per-household weighing device.
  4. Read the food waste exclusion list once — this is where local differences concentrate.
  5. Check whether non-combustible sacks are sold — you will want one for ceramics, glass and planters.
  6. Save the bulky waste channel — the local website or its app.
  7. In an apartment, read the management office notice — complex rules are often more specific than the ordinance.

Get through the first six and you will rarely need to look any of it up again for a year. Skip the check and you arrive at the same information after several rounds of refusal and re-discharge.

What could not be confirmed

  • Bag prices and sizes by locality. There is no single national figure, so none is quoted here. Your local notice is the only source.
  • A nationwide picture of outside-bag acceptance. Full acceptance, conditional acceptance and refusal all coexist and change. Check individually.
  • Bulky waste fees by item. The same item costs different amounts in different areas. The application screen governs.
  • A national food waste exclusion list. None exists, because it is an ordinance matter. The table above is the widely shared common ground.
  • Actual fine impositions and reduction rates. Local discretion applies, so the table cannot be read as what you will be charged.
  • Local implementation of the 2026 appliance take-back expansion. Statutory scope and field operations can move at different speeds.
  • Details of overseas systems. Section 13 describes their character only; individual city rules are out of scope.

Conclusion: a bag is a local rule you can hold

Three lines are worth keeping. First, the volume-rate bag is a receipt for a prepaid fee, so it stops working outside the area that sold it. That is why bags are the first thing to sort out after a move.

Second, the recycling test is whether rinsing makes it clean. Empty, rinse, separate, do not mix holds everywhere in the country, and material that fails the test belongs in the bag rather than the bin. Putting doubtful items in the recycling bin costs the material around them.

Third, almost everything else is local. Food waste exclusions, days and times, whether sacks are sold, bulky waste fees — all of it. So whatever guide you read, including this one, the last check has to be your own district's notice. Material-by-material instructions and regional information sit together on the national separate-discharge portal.

Finally, the four things that never belong in a bag: medicines, batteries and power banks, fluorescent lamps, and appliances. Each has its own destination, and getting it wrong leads to water pollution or fire. Nearly everything else resolves with one sentence: if rinsing will not make it clean, it is the bag.

Frequently asked questions

Q. What if the bag is too full to tie?

A bag stuffed past its size and left untied may not be collected. Fill it only to the point where it can still be tied; using one more bag is faster than repeating the discharge.

Q. Can I put recyclables in the volume-rate bag?

No. Bagging material that could have been sorted can itself count as a discharge-method breach. The reverse — putting non-recyclables into the recycling bin — is the same problem.

Q. Where do duvets and pillows go?

Not the clothing bank. Because of their bulk they are usually handled as bulky waste by report, or by whatever route the area specifies. A small cushion that fits in a bag goes in the bag.

Q. Can broken glass go in the bag?

Not in the glass bin. Wrap it in newspaper and use the volume-rate bag or a non-combustible sack; the point is that whoever lifts it does not get cut. For larger quantities check the local notice first.

Q. Do apartments and detached houses follow different rules?

They can. Apartment complexes keep bins on site, so day restrictions bite less, while detached-house areas often run a day-of-week system for film and clear PET. Guidance can differ by housing type within the same local authority.
