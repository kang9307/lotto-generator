The Complete Mosquito Repellent Buying Guide: Ingredients, Age Limits, and What Actually Works

Comparing DEET, picaridin, and IR3535 by duration and safety, infant and pregnancy guidelines, electric repellent ventilation, and why ultrasonic devices lack evidence

Jul 24, 2026 Life & Practical

Introduction: Know What Actually Differs Before You Buy

Every summer, store shelves fill up with repellent sprays, bracelets, electric vaporizers, mats, and bug zappers. The problem is that active ingredients, concentrations, and age restrictions vary widely between products, making it easy to get confused about what to use, when, and how. Common myths don't help either - the idea that a higher concentration always means stronger protection, or that anything labeled 'natural' must be safe.

This guide, current as of July 2026, draws on public guidance from Korea's Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS), the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency, the US EPA, CDC, and FTC to walk through how repellent ingredients differ, infant and pregnancy usage guidelines, how electric vaporizers and mats work and why ventilation matters, and which products lack solid evidence. Rather than recommending specific brands, the goal is to help you judge products yourself based on ingredients and mechanisms.

1. How Mosquito Repellent Ingredients Differ: DEET, Picaridin, IR3535, and Natural Options

Repellents fall broadly into synthetic and natural (essential-oil-derived) ingredients. The main ingredients approved as quasi-drugs in Korea are DEET, picaridin (icaridin), IR3535, and PMD (para-menthane-3,8-diol); Korea's efficacy standard requires at least 95% repellency lasting a minimum of 2 hours.

1.2 What to Know About Each Ingredient

DEET is the most extensively studied ingredient, offering strong, long-lasting protection, but its plasticizer properties can dissolve or warp plastics, synthetic fabrics, and some painted surfaces - keep it away from watch straps, eyeglass frames, and coated outdoor gear. Picaridin delivers comparable protection to DEET with less odor, less greasy residue, and less material damage, which has made it an increasingly popular DEET alternative internationally. IR3535 is frequently described as a gentler option for pregnant women and infants. Citronella and other essential oils smell familiar but typically last only about 2 hours or less, requiring frequent reapplication; in Korea, citronella-based products have been excluded from quasi-drug approval since 2017 due to insufficient efficacy and safety evidence, meaning many citronella products on shelves today are classified as general goods or cosmetics rather than regulated repellents.

2. Concentration and Duration: Higher Isn't Stronger, It's Just Longer-Lasting

A common myth is that a higher concentration repels mosquitoes more forcefully. In reality, concentration mainly determines how long protection lasts, not how strong it is. As the table above shows, DEET is reported to last about 2 hours at 10%, about 8 hours at 30%, and about 10 hours at 50%, with efficacy plateauing around 50% - going higher doesn't meaningfully extend protection further. Picaridin follows the same pattern, with duration scaling roughly with concentration.

So the right question when shopping isn't "how strong is this," but "how long will I actually be outdoors." A short walk calls for a lower concentration; a full day of camping calls for a higher concentration or a reapplication plan. Reaching for an unnecessarily high concentration for a child adds irritation and safety risk without adding real benefit.

3. Usage Guidelines for Infants and Pregnant Women

3.1 Age-Based Recommendations

Under Korean MFDS guidelines, DEET products at 10% or less are approved from 6 months of age, while 10-30% products are restricted to age 12 and up. Picaridin repellents are prohibited for infants under 6 months. International guidelines (US CDC and EPA) recommend not using products containing oil of lemon eucalyptus (OLE) or PMD on children under 3. Because no approved repellent exists for infants under 6 months, physical barriers such as mosquito nets or stroller netting should be the priority.

3.2 Application Order and Face/Hand Precautions

Never spray repellent directly on a child's face - an adult should apply it to their own hands first, then apply it to the child while avoiding the face. It's safest to avoid the area around the eyes, nose, and mouth, broken skin, and the palms, since young children often put their hands in their mouths. One application typically lasts about 4-5 hours, so avoid reapplying within that window.

Order matters when combining repellent with sunscreen. Apply sunscreen first, let it absorb for about 30 minutes, then apply repellent on top. Research suggests sunscreen can increase skin absorption of repellent ingredients, and using both together can reduce sun protection effectiveness, meaning sunscreen may need to be reapplied more frequently.

3.3 Guidelines for Pregnant Women

Korean guidance generally treats both DEET and picaridin as having low potential for causing birth defects and considers them usable during pregnancy. That said, many sources recommend picaridin or IR3535 as relatively gentler options. Given the sensitivity of pregnancy, prioritizing lower-irritation ingredients and consulting an obstetrician before use is a prudent approach.

4. Electric Vaporizers, Mats, and Liquid Diffusers: How Pyrethroids Work and Why Ventilation Matters

Electric mosquito mats and liquid vaporizers mostly work by heating a pyrethroid-based insecticide and releasing it into the air. Pyrethroids paralyze the mosquito's nervous system and are generally considered low in toxicity to humans, but heavy inhalation can cause sneezing, rhinitis, headache, tinnitus, or nausea, so prolonged use in a sealed room with people present should be avoided.

Mat-type products are reported to release over 80% of their active ingredient into the air within 2 hours, so there's no need to keep them running longer than that. A practical routine is to turn the device on about 2 hours before bedtime to clear mosquitoes, then open a window to ventilate about 30 minutes before sleep. Running these devices all night in a fully sealed room is not recommended.

5. Mosquito Coils and Outdoor Products: Bracelets and Ultrasonic Devices Lack Evidence

5.1 Traditional Mosquito Coils

Burning coils that release pyrethroid compounds through smoke work on a similar principle to electric vaporizers. However, the smoke itself can irritate the respiratory tract, so coils are better suited to well-ventilated outdoor spaces or balconies than enclosed sleeping areas.

5.2 Mosquito Repellent Bracelets and Stickers: Insufficient Evidence

Popular for their portability during camping and outdoor activities, repellent bracelets and stickers are not on Korea's list of approved quasi-drug repellents. The MFDS has stated no bracelet- or sticker-type product has been formally approved as a repellent. Studies have found bracelets reduce mosquito bites by only about 12-28%, compared to up to 82% for DEET-based repellents - a substantial gap. They can serve as a supplementary measure, but shouldn't be trusted as a primary defense.

5.3 Ultrasonic Mosquito Repellers: No Evidence

Devices claiming to repel mosquitoes with ultrasonic or other sound frequencies lack scientific support. The US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) sent warning letters to about 60 manufacturers of ultrasonic pest repellers in 2001 for unsupported advertising claims, concluded in 2002 that there was insufficient evidence ultrasonic devices repel pests, and in 2003 secured agreements from several manufacturers to stop making exaggerated claims. The prevailing scientific view is that mosquitoes have no sensory organ capable of detecting ultrasound, and this conclusion hasn't been overturned since. Relying on an ultrasonic device as your only defense is not recommended.

6. Zappers, Swatters, and Window Screens: Physical Control Methods

Physical methods that don't rely on chemicals are worth combining with repellents. Bug zappers use UV light to attract mosquitoes before trapping or electrocuting them, and work best in spaces that can run continuously, like a living room or balcony, rather than a bedroom where someone is sleeping. Electric swatters are handy for eliminating a visible mosquito instantly but don't address the underlying breeding problem. Window screens remain the most basic and consistently effective method - repair any tears or gaps promptly, and note that the weep holes at the bottom of window frames are a common entry point for mosquitoes, which can be blocked with fine mesh covers or stickers made for that purpose.

7. Eliminating Indoor Mosquitoes: Breeding Sites, Standing Water, and Gaps

Since mosquitoes lay eggs in water and develop as larvae, eliminating standing water is the most fundamental fix. Regularly empty and clean water that collects in plant saucers, empty bottles or cans, drains, AC condenser trays, and neglected buckets, both indoors and outdoors. Breeding sites increase sharply after rain, so check balconies and rooftops for standing water as well. Regularly inspecting window screens and door/window frame gaps and repairing any openings significantly reduces the routes mosquitoes use to enter your home. Combining chemical repellents with this kind of physical management is far more effective than relying on either alone.

8. What to Do After a Mosquito Bite

The first rule is don't scratch, even though it's itchy. Scratching damages skin tissue and triggers more inflammatory response substances, which can actually worsen the itch and raise the risk of secondary infection. A cold compress is a safer choice - it numbs the area and helps suppress the histamine release that causes itching.

See a doctor if a bite swells unusually large, develops a rash or blisters, and stays warm or inflamed for more than 7-10 days, which can indicate an allergic reaction to mosquito saliva (sometimes called skeeter syndrome). If shortness of breath, dizziness, or other signs of a systemic allergic reaction appear, go to an emergency room without delay.

9. Recommended Combinations by Situation: Home, Camping, and Infants

There's no single right product - the right answer is a combination matched to your situation.

At home, a solid baseline is physical management - checking screens and gaps, eliminating standing water - supplemented by an electric vaporizer or bug zapper used only as needed and always with proper ventilation. For camping or extended outdoor exposure, securing longer-lasting protection with roughly 30% DEET or a higher-concentration picaridin, combined with physical barriers like long sleeves and pants, is the more efficient approach. For infants, don't rely on evidence-poor options like bracelets or ultrasonic devices - prioritize a mosquito net for those under 6 months, and for those 6 months and older, applying a small amount of low-concentration picaridin or IR3535 while avoiding the face is currently the best-supported choice.

Conclusion: Understanding Ingredients and Context Makes Choosing Easier

Mosquito repellent products vary widely in ingredients, concentration, and age restrictions, and the market includes products with weak evidence behind them, so buying at random tends to waste money and time. Three things matter most. First, remember that concentration affects duration, not strength, and choose based on how long you'll be exposed. Second, for infants and pregnant women, prioritize age-appropriate, lower-irritation ingredients rather than trusting your safety to evidence-poor products like bracelets or ultrasonic devices. Third, combine chemical repellents with physical management like window screens and eliminating standing water to get real results.

This guide reflects general information based on publicly available sources as of July 2026. Always check the packaging, instructions, and official MFDS or equivalent local health authority guidance for the exact approval status and usage directions of any specific product.
