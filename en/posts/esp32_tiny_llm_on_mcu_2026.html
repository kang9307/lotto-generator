Running a 28.9M-Parameter LLM on an $8 Microcontroller - Where ESP32-S3 Inference Actually Breaks

The distance between putting an LLM on a microcontroller and getting useful work out of it

Jul 28, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: what it really means when an LLM runs on an $8 board

A microcontroller whose parts cost around eight dollars produces text from a 28.9-million-parameter language model at roughly nine tokens per second. No server, no Wi-Fi connection. That sentence is true, and the published repository lets you check the measurement conditions yourself. The problem is that the picture most readers form immediately after reading it - a small board with a microphone that understands you and answers back - does not follow from that fact at all.

This is not a write-up of a fun demo. It is an attempt to establish, with numbers, how far tiny on-device inference is actually useful and exactly where it collapses. So the order of discussion is not built around how impressive it is, but around what was actually measured, and what those measurements do and do not guarantee.

Here is the skeleton of the conclusion up front. What this project demonstrates is that it is possible to engineer transformer inference on a microcontroller (MCU) at a usable speed. What it does not demonstrate is that you can get useful work out of that model. The gap between those two statements is far wider than it looks, and measuring that gap precisely is the point of this article. The most trustworthy thing about the project is that its author states up front, in the repository documentation, that the model cannot answer questions, follow instructions, write code, or know facts.

1. What was actually measured - esp32-ai by the numbers

The project in question is the slvDev/esp32-ai repository on GitHub, released under the MIT licence. What matters here is that the author published measurement conditions and intermediate figures together in RESULTS.md. That is a different level of credibility from a demo that reports benchmark numbers with no conditions attached.

1.1 Pin down the measurement conditions first

Every figure below comes from an ESP32-S3 N16R8 board (16MB flash, 8MB octal SPI PSRAM) with the CPU at 240MHz, PSRAM in OPI mode, flash in QIO mode, Arduino ESP32 core 3.3.10, compiled at -O3, with the output head and activations handled in int8. Change any one of those - especially dropping PSRAM to quad mode or the CPU to 160MHz - and the numbers do not follow along unchanged.

1.2 The core design is a separation of compute and storage

The substantive idea here is the Per-Layer Embeddings (PLE) structure. The repository describes it as an approach used in Google's Gemma family, adapted to MCU constraints, and the gist is simple. Put the parts that need computation and the parts that only need storage into physically different memories.

  • The dense core, which is exercised on every token, is kept small at 559K parameters and lives in SRAM
  • The 25M-parameter lookup table, which is read once per vocabulary entry, is memory-mapped into flash
  • The volume actually read from flash per token is only about 450 bytes
  • The output head alone sits in PSRAM, relieving pressure on SRAM

So in the sentence "28.9 million parameters running on an MCU", 25 million of those 28.9 million - about 86% - are looked up rather than computed. That is not a criticism; it is the best part of the project. The design falls out of a correct problem statement: what an MCU lacks is not arithmetic throughput but memory and bandwidth.

1.3 The bottleneck is bytes, not multiplications

Look at where the 102.9ms per token goes and that claim is confirmed directly.

  • Output head 57.6ms - about 56% of the total. Logits over all 32,768 vocabulary entries must be computed every token, and the weights live in PSRAM
  • Attention 25.6ms - about 25%
  • PLE table lookup 8.5ms - about 8%
  • FFN 6.9ms - about 7%. The most arithmetic-dense part consumes the least time
  • Input embedding 4.4ms - about 4%

The FFN, heaviest in arithmetic, takes 7%; the output head, heaviest in memory traffic, takes 56%. The same repository measured sequential read bandwidth at 240MB/s for SRAM and 60.7MB/s for PSRAM, with flash taking 20.3µs to randomly read a single 512-byte row. Sweeping the 1.64MB output head once at 60.7MB/s works out arithmetically to about 27ms. Reading the measured 57.6ms as that figure plus int8 dequantisation and softmax on top is the natural interpretation.

The practical lesson is clear. Optimising a language model on an MCU is not about reducing multiplications; it is about reducing bytes read. The optimisation history recorded in the repository shows exactly this. It went from 1,757.2ms per token in the initial implementation down to 102.9ms, roughly a 17-fold reduction, and the levers were staged PSRAM loading, int4 unpacking, group scaling, dual-core parallelisation and int8 quantisation - almost all of them about data movement.

2. The ESP32-S3 as a physical ceiling - what 512KB decides

Why that design was necessary becomes obvious once you read the chip specification. Per Espressif's official datasheet, the ESP32-S3 offers the following.

  • CPU: Xtensa LX7 32-bit dual core, up to 240MHz
  • On-chip SRAM: 512KB, shared between instructions and data
  • On-chip ROM: 384KB, holding boot code and core library functions
  • RTC SRAM: 16KB, retained in low-power modes
  • External memory: external flash and PSRAM over SPI / Dual SPI / Quad SPI / Octal SPI / QPI / OPI interfaces
  • Virtual address space: up to 32MB for external memory (0x4200_0000 to 0x43FF_FFFF)
  • PIE: Processor Instruction Extensions - the datasheet lists a vector-style instruction extension section

Two of these are decisive.

2.1 512KB is a non-negotiable wall

Compressed to the most aggressive 4-bit format, 28.9 million parameters still come to about 14.9MB. Less than one twenty-ninth of that fits in 512KB of SRAM. There was therefore no option other than keeping most parameters outside SRAM, and the PLE structure is an answer to that constraint rather than a choice made for raw performance. Put the other way round, unless MCUs with far larger SRAM appear, MCU LLMs will keep taking the shape of a small core with its lookup tables parked outside.

2.2 The 32MB of virtual address space is shared with flash

The ESP-IDF documentation states that the ESP32-S3 can use up to 32MB of virtual addresses for external PSRAM, but that those 32MB are shared with flash instructions and read-only data. Map 32MB of PSRAM and you have that much less room to map flash. Since this project maps a 12MB lookup table into flash, the expansion route of simply adding PSRAM to grow the model runs into that address space fairly quickly.

The same document notes another trap. DMA bandwidth to external RAM is very limited, and in particular once you handle chunks larger than 32KB the cache becomes insufficient and speeds fall back to raw external RAM speed. A 1.64MB output head lands squarely in that condition. The 57.6ms figure seen earlier is thus explained at the level of the chip documentation.

What to read from this table is not a ranking but the difference in character between the classes.

  • Low-power MCUs with only on-chip flash (the nRF52840 class) are not language model territory. This is where dedicated models of tens to hundreds of kilobytes belong, for wake words or anomaly detection
  • Classes that can attach large external flash (the RP2040 class) are the classic case of storage without execution. Capacity and performance are separate axes
  • The ESP32-S3 and RP2350 are the "it is possible" band. From here, whether octal PSRAM is present becomes the practical fork in the road
  • The ESP32-P4 is the first class where the structure itself might change, since in-package PSRAM is 16 to 32MB. Being able to put the lookup table in PSRAM rather than flash reshapes the latency profile
  • At every class you must separately verify the space to hold the weights and the bandwidth to read them every token. Judging on the former alone will always be wrong

4. What 28.9M parameters can and cannot do

Now the most important part. Hardware coping does not mean the model copes.

4.1 Training data sets the ceiling on capability

This model was trained on TinyStories-style data. TinyStories is a synthetic dataset published in 2023 by Eldan and Li at Microsoft Research, made up of roughly two million short stories written with a restricted vocabulary at the level a three- or four-year-old understands. The paper's central claim was that models under ten million parameters can write coherent short stories, and that is genuinely reproduced here.

The same research also recorded the limits. Because the vocabulary and grammar are deliberately simple, the complexity of the behaviour learned from it cannot exceed that simplicity either. Fluent sentences and correct content are different problems, and the latter was never the goal of this dataset.

4.2 Even larger tiny models fail to follow instructions

One reference point is needed. Hugging Face's SmolLM2-135M has roughly 4.7 times the parameters of this model, was trained on two trillion tokens, and supports an 8K context. Even so, its published figures read as follows.

  • GSM8K 1.4% - accuracy on grade-school multi-step arithmetic. Treating this as "cannot solve" is fair
  • IFEval 29.9% - instruction following. It misses most of the detailed conditions in an instruction
  • The model card itself states that generated content may be factually inaccurate or logically inconsistent

In short, a 135M model that is far larger and far better fed still follows instructions less than 30% of the time. Expecting instruction following from a 28.9M-parameter TinyStories model below it has no basis. The real limit here is not "it lacks knowledge" but "it cannot hold a format." Knowledge can be supplied from outside, but a model that cannot keep to an output format cannot even be parsed, and therefore cannot be wired into another system.

4.3 So the real boundary falls here

  • Possible: continuing short story sentences, producing a text stream that is mostly grammatical
  • Possible: demos, exhibitions and teaching material that show this board composing sentences by itself
  • Possible: serving as an engineering study piece for on-device inference pipelines
  • Not possible: question answering - an item the repository documentation explicitly rules out
  • Not possible: instruction following - ask it for JSON and you will not reliably get JSON
  • Not possible: code generation, factual lookup, summarisation, translation
  • Not possible: a control loop that reads sensor values, decides and issues commands - format compliance fails, so the wiring never happens

That last item matters most. A large share of the motivation for putting a language model on an MCU is "control the device in natural language", and that is precisely the use case that fails first at this scale.

5. Memory and quality tradeoffs by quantisation step

Quantisation is the precondition that makes this structure possible at all. Let us look at what is given up and what is gained, in numbers.

A few points need calling out from this table.

  • The sizes in the fp32, fp16 and int8 rows are parameter count multiplied by precision, not figures this project measured. Their quality loss was not measured either
  • 4-bit is not 0.5 bytes per parameter. Each group carries an fp16 scale, so at group-128 it becomes 0.5 + 2/128 ≈ 0.516 bytes
  • Checking that calculation, 28.9M × 0.516 ≈ 14.9MB, which lines up with the shipped file of 14,912,332 bytes. The file size is consistent with the description
  • Going from group-64 to group-128 shrinks the file by roughly 0.4MB but increases quality loss. Reading it as a trade made to fit 16MB flash is the natural interpretation
  • Losses in nats become a perplexity multiplier when exponentiated. +0.063 nats corresponds to about 6.5% worse and +0.089 nats to about 9.3% worse (converted figures)

5.1 One quantisation step cancels a generation of architectural gain

The most interesting number in this project is elsewhere. At a vocabulary of 32,768, the measured perplexity was 12.58 for the baseline structure and 11.41 for the PLE structure. Converted to logs, the gain PLE earned is about +0.098 nats. Yet in the shipping quantisation format the PLE model loses +0.063 to +0.089 nats.

In other words, the gain from changing the architecture and the loss from squeezing to 4 bits are almost the same size. That is why structural improvement and compression ratio should never be boasted about separately in on-device model design. They draw on the same account.

To be fair, though, in the same measurement the baseline structure lost +0.089 to +0.109 nats to quantisation, degrading more than PLE did. The repository therefore reports that PLE retains 121 to 126% of its relative advantage after quantisation. The claim that PLE is a structure more robust to compression is supported by those numbers. It does not mean there is no loss.

One more caveat. The perplexity discussed here is a value within the TinyStories domain. It is not an indicator of general language ability or factual accuracy, and it cannot be compared directly against another model's perplexity.

6. Criteria for when rules beat an LLM

In embedded product planning this is the judgement that actually matters: put in a language model, use a dedicated small model, or handle it with rules.

6.1 A counterexample from the same board

Interestingly, Espressif's own answer is not an LLM. Per the official ESP-SR documentation, offline speech command recognition on the same ESP32-S3 runs on the following resources.

  • MultiNet7: 18KB internal RAM, about 2,920KB PSRAM, average 11ms per frame
  • MultiNet5 Q8: 16KB internal RAM, about 2,310KB PSRAM, average 12ms per frame
  • MultiNet4 Q8: 10.5KB internal RAM, about 1,009KB PSRAM, average 11ms per frame
  • WakeNet9 (2 channels): 16KB internal RAM, about 324KB PSRAM, average 3.0ms per frame
  • Up to 200 command words can be registered, and the documented response latency target is within 500ms

On the same eight-dollar board, what satisfies the real requirement - "turn on the light", "turn on the air conditioner" - is not a 15MB language model but a dedicated model of a few hundred kilobytes to three megabytes. Resources, latency and failure modes all differ by orders of magnitude. There is no reason a language model would solve a 200-command problem better.

6.2 Six decision criteria

QuestionRules or a dedicated model fit whenA language model is worth considering when
Can you enumerate the output space?Commands, states and class labels are finiteThe output genuinely has to be free-form prose
What is the input?Buttons, sensors, a fixed set of utterancesUsers type unpredictable sentences
What moves when it is wrong?Physical actuators or safety-related actionsOnly characters appear on a screen
Can the device verify the result?A schema or range check settles it immediatelyA separate validator exists and failures can be rolled back
What does failure look like?It must go silent or return an errorA plausible wrong answer is tolerable
How often do you update?Field OTA is frequent and bandwidth is narrowUpdates are rare or can be done over a wire

The conclusion from this table is simple. Most MCU products do not fall in the right-hand column. The third row is decisive in particular. A language model does not fail quietly; it fails in a plausible-looking shape. On a device holding a relay closed, that failure mode is far harder to handle than a rule-based failure.

One more distinction is worth drawing. The requirement to output natural language and the requirement to understand natural language are entirely different problems. What most embedded products need is the former, and the former is solved perfectly well - and far more stably - by templates and string tables. When the latter is genuinely required, 28.9 million parameters are not enough. The band between those two is startlingly narrow, and that is the practical information this experiment provides.

7. Power, heat and latency in practice

This axis is invisible in demo videos and immediately problematic in products.

7.1 This is a sustained maximum load

While inference runs, the ESP32-S3 drives both cores at 240MHz and reads octal PSRAM and flash without pause. Producing one short paragraph of 100 tokens means 102.9ms × 100 ≈ 10.3 seconds of continuous full load.

Power design for battery-operated MCU products normally assumes minimising awake time and spending most of the cycle asleep. Language model inference is precisely the opposite load profile. Insert a 10-second full-load window into a design that wakes briefly, reads a sensor and goes back to sleep, and the power budget has to be recalculated.

Exact active-mode current figures are tabulated in section 5.6 of the ESP32-S3 datasheet, but we were not able to confirm the values in that table directly from the primary source while writing this article. We therefore do not quote specific milliamp figures. If you need a number for a design, consult that section of the datasheet itself. The direction, however, is clear: a sleep-oriented design and sustained inference do not sit well together.

7.2 Heat comes back as a performance penalty

  • Sustained full load inside a sealed enclosure raises die temperature, and temperature in turn eats into clock and voltage margin
  • High-speed PSRAM modes are sensitive to heat and timing margin. ESP-IDF notes that a 120MHz setting is available for octal PSRAM, but the faster the setting, the tighter the conditions
  • Benchmark numbers come from an open development board on a desk, and there is no basis for expecting the same numbers from a product inside a case
  • On battery-powered devices, voltage droop and heat arriving together can cause reboots, so the power supply needs margin

7.3 Latency should be judged by the first character

9.5 tok/s is not far off the speed at which a person reads text on a screen. With streaming output it may not feel sluggish at all. But from a product standpoint the metric that matters is not average throughput; it is time to first token and time to the complete response.

  • A screen showing streamed text - 9.5 tok/s may well be sufficient
  • A control flow that parses a complete sentence before acting - even 40 tokens take over 4 seconds. As a button response, that is disqualifying
  • Voice command handling - MultiNet's documented latency target is within 500ms. A different order of magnitude
  • Multitasking - there is little headroom to run other real-time work during the 10 seconds inference occupies

8. What to check first before productising

Between a hobby project and a shipped product sit items that a demo never reveals.

8.1 Updates - the first thing that blocks you

This one falls straight out of the numbers. The deployed model file is 14,912,332 bytes and the partition is 15,597,568 bytes. The headroom is 685,236 bytes, about 4.4%.

  • Using this model on 16MB flash leaves no room to build a rollback-capable A/B partition pair
  • That means there is no copy to fall back on if power is lost mid-OTA. For a commercial product that alone can be disqualifying
  • The alternatives are assuming 32MB-class flash, or keeping the model on external storage and verifying it at boot. Either way, the eight-dollar premise wobbles
  • The cost and failure rate of pushing a 15MB image to thousands of units over narrow bandwidth also has to be budgeted separately

A demo working at eight dollars does not mean a product works at eight dollars. This single item changes the cost structure.

8.2 Safety and misbehaviour

  • Because output is free text, strings that were never enumerated in advance can appear. The downstream parser must be designed on that assumption
  • Never wire model output directly to an actuator. An allow-list validation layer in between is the minimum condition
  • If generated text is shown to users, the UI must indicate that grammatically fluent but factually wrong sentences are possible
  • The device being offline also means you cannot stop it remotely when something goes wrong. A local stop mechanism is required
  • For products aimed at children or vulnerable users, exposing generated text at all should be reconsidered. TinyStories-class models were not built with safety alignment as a goal

8.3 Licensing, reproducibility and maintenance

  • The reference repository is MIT licensed, but the terms of training data and derived models are separate from the code licence. Each must be checked before commercial use
  • You need to be able to reproduce the quantisation pipeline and training code in house. A model you cannot reproduce is a model you cannot update
  • Arduino ESP32 core 3.3.10 and -O3 show that the build environment is itself part of the performance figure. Without pinned toolchain versions the numbers will not reproduce
  • For a product shipping over several years, the pace of change in the tiny-model ecosystem and component end-of-life risk both need factoring in

9. The distance from PCs and phones - not the same "local"

The phrase "running an LLM locally" gets used as if it meant one thing, but change the hardware class and it becomes a different activity.

  • PC class - VRAM decides essentially everything, and models of billions to tens of billions of parameters go into real work. That territory is covered separately in our local LLM running guide, on hardware requirements and GGUF quantisation choices
  • Phone class - attempts to squeeze a 27B-class model down to 3.9GB. The tradeoffs on that axis are laid out in our 27B-class on-device model analysis
  • MCU class - 14.9MB. Against a phone's 3.9GB that is about one 262nd, and in parameters, about one 934th of 27B
  • Using local models on the developer-tooling axis is treated separately in local LLM agent development tools and local LLM proxies

What matters is not the ratio but the qualitative break the ratio creates. Between a PC and a phone you have "the same kind of tool, slightly less clever". Between a phone and an MCU you do not. Below 262 times down, the category of tool itself changes. The phone's model summarises, translates and calls tools. The MCU's model continues a story. Putting these on one axis and saying the gap will soon be closed has no basis, because what sits between them is not time but memory bandwidth, a physical quantity.

So when looking at the future of MCU on-device inference, the thing to watch is not a model-size race but chips like the ESP32-P4 above, where in-package PSRAM capacity and memory bandwidth move up a class. That is what has to change before the structure changes.

Conclusion: what was proven and what was not

To summarise.

  • Proven 1 - transformer inference can run on an MCU at a speed a person can read. The optimisation record from 1,757.2ms to 102.9ms per token, roughly 17-fold, is published
  • Proven 2 - separating compute from storage can work around the 512KB SRAM wall. Routing 25 million of the 28.9 million parameters through a flash lookup table is how
  • Proven 3 - the bottleneck in MCU inference is memory bandwidth, not arithmetic. The breakdown showing 57.6ms of the 102.9ms in the output head is the evidence
  • Not proven 1 - that useful work can be extracted from this model. The repository documentation itself rules out question answering, instruction following, code writing and factual knowledge
  • Not proven 2 - that this approach suits a product. Update headroom of 4.4%, sustained full load, and free-text output are three separate design burdens
  • Not proven 3 - that a language model beats a dedicated model on an MCU. Official figures showing MultiNet7 handling 200 commands in about 2,920KB of PSRAM on the same board point the other way

The value of this project does not lie in the headline that LLMs work on MCUs too. It lies in leaving behind, in measurable form, where it works and where it stops. Because the author wrote down the limits first, readers can avoid setting the wrong expectations and map it onto their own problem. That is exactly the part missing from most on-device AI announcements.

If you are planning an embedded product, three things are applicable today. First, rewrite the requirement by separating "output natural language" from "understand natural language." Most of the time it is the former, and templates finish the job. Second, draw the update path before choosing a model. If there is no flash headroom, that model was never a candidate. Third, always compare against a dedicated small model on the same board. An alternative that differs by orders of magnitude in resources and latency is often already sitting in the official documentation.
