The Occasion: Which Species Is the Heaviest?

A post on the Signore Galilei blog (September 27, 2026), introduced on GeekNews, opens with one question: if the mass of every individual of a species is added up, which species on Earth is the heaviest? The starting point is a familiar saying, that all the ants in the world weigh as much as all humans combined. The post says this is not true. Human mass outweighs ant mass by 5 to 1. It adds that termites might beat humans. Either way the comparison is not fair, because ants and termites are each a collection of thousands of species while humans are a single one.

The answers the post gives are split by category. Among animals, cattle are heaviest at about 650 million tonnes, and among wild animals Antarctic krill come first, with a 2009 estimate of 379 million tonnes. Widening the view to plants, black spruce in the boreal forest stands at more than 11 billion tonnes in Canada alone and Dahurian larch at no less than 8.4 billion tonnes in Russia, each more than ten times the cattle, human or krill figure (calculation). No mass basis is stated for the tree values. The post presents the last two values not as a settled ranking but as candidates drawn from the literature and public statistics.

The question does not close on a single answer. The same object moves by factors of several depending on where the species boundary is drawn, what weight is measured and which range is summed. What follows reads the post’s values along those three axes, in this order.

  • The values for three animal species and the arithmetic behind them
  • Where animals stand among the kingdoms once mass is converted to carbon
  • The point at which harvest and standing stock part ways for cultivated plants
  • How the two boreal tree estimates were built and where they run out

The Structure of the Question: Fix the Species and the Weight First

The phrase “dominant species” hides at least three choices. Because the ranking moves when a choice moves, all three need to be fixed before the numbers are read.

  • Taxonomic unit. The post does not pit a group of thousands of species, such as ants or termites, against a single species. What is compared is one species each: cattle, humans, Antarctic krill, a particular tree.
  • Basis of weight. Most of the post’s values are total mass in tonnes, but the same object measured in carbon mass comes out more than an order of magnitude smaller. Among the cited sources, the krill figure is labeled fresh biomass.
  • Range and date. Krill is a 2009 estimate, sugar cane is the harvest of the single year 2023, and black spruce is a forest statistic for one country, Canada. The range summed and the year differ from value to value.

Ants show clearly that headcount and mass can give different rankings. A 2022 study reported by NPR estimated that there are about 20 quadrillion ants worldwide (1 trillion times 20,000), or about 2.5 million ants per human. The same report says the ants’ total mass is about a fifth of the total human mass. By number the ants lead by a wide margin, while by mass humans lead. The “5 to 1” in the post is that ratio.

Running the same arithmetic on Antarctic krill sharpens the contrast. The post puts one krill at about 2 g. Dividing the total of 379 million tonnes by 2 g gives about 1.9 times 10 to the 14th power, or roughly 190 trillion animals (back-calculation). Compared with 20 quadrillion ants, the ants outnumber krill by about 100 times (calculation). Yet the ants’ total mass is a fifth of the human total, about 70 million tonnes (calculation), which is less than a fifth of the krill’s 379 million tonnes. The side with a hundred times more individuals sits below a fifth on mass. Back-calculating from these values puts a single ant at roughly 3.5 mg.

The effect of how a species is defined on the comparison shows up in the same place. Taking all ants as one lump gives about a fifth of human mass; splitting them by species makes the value for any one species smaller than that. The post adds that termites might beat humans but gives no figure for them.

Animals: How the Cattle, Krill and Human Figures Were Built

The post starts with animals. Humans are near the top for a single species, at about 350 million tonnes. The heaviest wild animal is Antarctic krill, whose 2009 estimate is 379 million tonnes. One krill weighs only about 2 g, but the numbers in the Southern Ocean put krill in a statistical dead heat with humans, and they feed the giant blue whales, the post says. The top of all animals, however, is cattle. Cattle are the largest domesticated animal at about 650 million tonnes, and pigs in second place, at about 140 million tonnes, trail both humans and krill.

How these values were produced is written in the BBC Science Focus answer that the post links in a footnote. The three values are built in different ways.

  • Humans, about 350 million tonnes. The answer states 6.9 billion people averaging 50 kg each. The product is 345 million tonnes (calculation), which the answer rounds to roughly 350 million tonnes.
  • Cattle, over 650 million tonnes. The answer gives 1.3 billion animals at a conservative 500 kg each. The product is exactly 650 million tonnes (calculation).
  • Antarctic krill, 379 million tonnes. This is not a headcount multiplied out but a 2009 global estimate. The answer calls it fresh biomass and adds that unknown aspects of the ecology of this shrimp make the figure hard to be sure of.

The answer also adds a comparison. Blue whales, which eat krill, comprised about 35 million tonnes before whaling and about half a million tonnes in 2001. The krill estimate is about 11 times the pre-whaling blue whale total (calculation).

That the values are products of assumptions affects how the ranking is read. If the assumed average weight behind the human figure of 345 million tonnes moves by 10%, the value becomes 379.5 million tonnes, the same level as the krill figure of 379 million tonnes (calculation). A difference of that size can arise from a small change in the assumptions. It matches the post’s “statistical dead heat” between humans and krill. The gap between cattle and krill, by contrast, is wide. Even if the average cattle weight assumption shifts by 100 kg, the value moves by 130 million tonnes (calculation), which does not overturn the 271 million tonne gap to krill (calculation). The pig figure of 140 million tonnes is 0.4 times the human figure (calculation).

Converted to Carbon Mass: Where Animals Sit Among the Kingdoms

Instead of tonnes of mass, the post introduces a study that compared groups of life by carbon mass. Bar-On, Phillips and Milo (2018, PNAS) tally all the kingdoms of the biosphere at about 550 gigatonnes of carbon (Gt C). The abstract summarizes that plants, at about 450 Gt C, are the dominant kingdom and live mostly on land; animals, at about 2 Gt C, live mostly in the sea; and bacteria (about 70 Gt C) and archaea (about 7 Gt C) are concentrated in deep subsurface environments. The post adds 12 billion tonnes for fungi, 4 billion for protists and 0.2 billion for viruses.

The seven kingdoms add up to about 545.2 billion tonnes, which agrees with the abstract’s roughly 550 billion within rounding (calculation). The post explains what the conversion means. Carbon is only a part of an organism, so the numbers shrink, but it is the more meaningful basis for tracking the flow of food energy through the biosphere. A comparison between 1 pound of watermelon and 1 pound of steak accompanies it. For cattle, 650 million tonnes is about 61 million tonnes of carbon, a ratio of about 9.4% (calculation).

  • The carbon mass of all animals is about 1/225 that of plants (calculation).
  • The carbon mass of one species, cattle, at 61 million tonnes is about 3% of the 2 billion tonnes for all animals (calculation).
  • The post sums up that animals exceed viruses but fall short of protists, archaea, fungi, bacteria and plants.

The post goes one step further. Most bacteria live in aquifers and seafloor sediments, which are hard to study. One extremely dominant bacterial species under the sea might hold the highest biomass on Earth, but the post says the winner is more likely to be a plant. No number is attached to this passage. It is a statement of likelihood, hard to use as grounds for a ranking, and best read only as the premise for the follow-on discussion in which the heaviest species is a plant.

Cultivated Plants: Sugar Cane at 2 Billion Tonnes and What a Harvest Means

Because the top animal was a domesticated one, the post looks at cultivated plants first. Among cereals, maize has the largest annual harvest, at 1.1 billion tonnes, nearly double the mass of cattle (the post’s wording; the calculation gives about 1.7 times). The biggest crop, however, is not a grain but sugar cane. The post says the 2023 harvest topped 2 billion tonnes and links Our World in Data production statistics as its basis. That is about 5.7 times the total human mass of 350 million tonnes (calculation), and the post writes that humanity harvests about 6 times its collective weight in sugar cane every year.

The post points out that sugar cane is a perennial. Its root system persists between harvests, so the average living mass of sugar cane over a year is probably higher than that of an annual crop such as maize. No number accompanies this statement.

  • The annual maize harvest of 1.1 billion tonnes is about 1.7 times the cattle figure (calculation).
  • The sugar cane harvest of more than 2 billion tonnes is about 3.1 times the cattle figure and about 5.7 times the human figure (calculation).
  • The 2 billion tonnes of sugar cane is no more than about a fifth of the black spruce figure of more than 11 billion tonnes (calculation).

One distinction matters in reading these. A harvest is the amount gathered from the fields in a year, while biomass is the amount present at a point in time. The post gives sugar cane as a harvest and explains the possibility that its standing mass exceeds maize by the plant’s perennial habit. The tree values it is compared with, on the other hand, come from forest statistics on the amount currently growing, that is, standing stock. Placing a harvest beside a standing stock carries the caveat that the two are different kinds of quantity.

The Boreal Forest: Black Spruce and Dahurian Larch

Moving to wild plants brings in trees. The heaviest single organism comes from a tree species. It is Pando, a clonal colony of aspen with interconnected roots, which weighs 6,000 tonnes on its own. But the post says aspens are rare. It asks whether a more common tree has more mass as a whole species and looks for the answer in the boreal forest that rings the Arctic. According to the post, in the boreal forest 3 or 4 species can account for more than half of the forest’s total mass, whereas tropical forests can require more than a hundred species to reach the same share.

The 2023 Nature paper that the post links as its basis for the tropical side gives, in its abstract, a value on a different scale. Extrapolating survey data from Africa, Amazonia and Southeast Asia to all closed-canopy tropical forests, it estimates that 1,053 species make up half of about 800 billion trees with trunk diameters of at least 10 cm. This value is by tree count, not by mass. The post’s “more than a hundred species” is a statement about mass, so the two values cannot be swapped as if they shared one scale.

Black spruce (Picea mariana). The post calls this species the most common tree in arctic Canada. According to Canada’s National Forest Inventory, more than 11 billion tonnes of it are growing in Canada alone, and some also grows in the United States. The character of this value falls into four points.

  • Range. It is the value for one country, Canada. The US portion is not in the sum, so the global value is no smaller than this.
  • Source. The post cites a customized report from Canada’s National Forest Inventory.
  • Wording. “More than 11 billion tonnes” is a lower bound, not a point estimate.
  • Comparison. It is about 17 times cattle at 650 million tonnes, about 31 times humans at 350 million tonnes and about 29 times Antarctic krill at 379 million tonnes (calculation). Since it is not known whether the mass bases match, these multiples are for reference only.

Dahurian larch. Russia’s forest statistics are not detailed by species. The post therefore estimates backward from the figure for larches as a whole, in this order.

  • Larches as a whole account for 35.6% of Russia’s forest land.
  • Russia’s total forest amounts to 82.2 billion cubic meters.
  • Multiplying the two gives about 29.3 billion cubic meters of larch (calculation). This assumes the share of area can stand in for the share of volume.
  • Applying the density of larch wood, the post arrives at 16.8 billion tonnes of larch in Russia. Back-calculating from this value gives a density of about 0.57 tonnes per cubic meter (back-calculation).
  • The two main larch species in Russia are the Dahurian larch in the east and the Siberian larch in the west. Dahurian is the more common, so it must be at least 50% of the total. That makes at least 8.4 billion tonnes, and possibly more.

Both candidate values are lower bounds. The black spruce figure of more than 11 billion tonnes counts Canada only, and the Dahurian larch figure of at least 8.4 billion tonnes takes half of all larch as its floor. If Dahurian were assumed to be all of Russia’s larch, the upper end would be 16.8 billion tonnes (calculation). The ranges overlap, so these sources do not settle which comes first. The post itself says only, in its last sentence, that spruce and larch have a massive lead on this metric, and it does not rank the two against each other.

Converting to carbon allows a direct comparison with all animals. The post says living black spruce wood is about 51% carbon, and links the USDA Forest Service woody-tissue carbon database as its basis. Multiplying 11 billion tonnes by 0.51 gives about 5.6 billion tonnes (calculation), 2.8 times the 2 billion tonnes for all animals. The carbon in this one species is about 1.4 times the 4 billion tonnes for protists, about 0.8 times the 7 billion tonnes for archaea and about 1.2% of the 450 billion tonnes for the plant kingdom (calculation). The post sums it up this way: humans may still control the biosphere, but on at least this one metric the spruce and larch trees have a massive lead.

Three things can be read from the same table. First, the side that is larger by mass differs from the side that is larger by number. Ants lead on number but trail humans on mass. Second, the size of one body differs from the size of a whole species. Pando is 6,000 tonnes as a single organism, but the post says aspens are rare and moves on to the boreal forest in search of a tree whose whole species has more mass. Third, the same object lands in entirely different places depending on the unit. The same cattle are 650 million tonnes in tonnes and 61 million tonnes in carbon. A ranking is meaningful only between values in the same unit.

The 3.9 billion tonnes in the black spruce row is 2 billion tonnes divided by 0.51 (calculation). It means the post’s 11 billion tonnes is about 2.8 times that baseline, a ratio that meshes with the post’s phrase “nearly 3 times”. The multiplication has one premise, though. The USDA database defines woody-tissue carbon concentration as the mass of carbon per unit of dry mass. To apply that share to 11 billion tonnes, the 11 billion tonnes would have to be on the same dry-mass basis, and the post does not state the mass basis of this value.

When such a comparison turns up, the items to check come down to four.

  • Mass of what. Look at whether it is fresh weight, dry weight or carbon. The same cattle are 650 million tonnes and also 61 million tonnes.
  • Summed over what range. Look at whether it is one country or the whole planet, one year of harvest or standing stock at a point in time.
  • Whose estimate. Look at whether it comes from published statistics (forest inventory, harvest), a research estimate (krill in 2009) or the author’s own calculation (larch).
  • Lower bound or point estimate. A value carrying “more than” or “at least” cannot be used to fix a ranking.

What Remains Unverified

  • The source data for the black spruce figure of 11 billion tonnes, Canada’s National Forest Inventory customized report, and the Russian forest register values (35.6%, 82.2 billion cubic meters). Both values stand as the blog post states them and were not matched against the primary data.
  • The body and tables of Bar-On et al. (2018). Only the abstract was matched, so 12 billion tonnes for fungi, 4 billion for protists, 0.2 billion for viruses and 61 million tonnes of carbon for cattle are as the blog post states them.
  • The USDA database page that the post links as its basis for the 51% carbon share of black spruce has no species-level value. The 51% figure itself was not matched.
  • Whether the values for black spruce and Dahurian larch are wet weight or dry weight, and whether they share a basis with the cattle and human values.
  • The post does not address whether livestock breeds count as a single species, the specific value for termites or the value for any single bacterial species. The statement that termites might outweigh humans has a linked paper behind it, but its value was not matched.
  • The original paper behind the 2009 krill estimate, and updated 2026 values for humans and cattle. The BBC Science Focus answer uses 6.9 billion people and 1.3 billion cattle as assumptions.
  • Our World in Data production statistics behind the 2023 sugar cane figure of 2 billion tonnes and the maize figure of 1.1 billion tonnes. Both values stand as the blog post states them and were not matched.
  • The body of Table 1 in the 2023 Nature paper. Only the abstract’s by-count value (1,053 species) was matched.
