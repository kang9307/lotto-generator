The Cathedral, the Bazaar, and the Winchester Mystery House - A Third Model of Software Development in the AI Era

Why AI-Generated Codebases Need a Third Metaphor Beyond Cathedral and Bazaar

Jun 5, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: Why Reopen a 1998 Classic

Eric S. Raymond's The Cathedral and the Bazaar, published as an essay in 1997 and collected into a book in 1999, is one of the most cited writings in software history. It contrasts two ways of building software: the Cathedral, where a few craftsmen sculpt carefully in a closed space, and the Bazaar, where a crowd of people add their hands at once in a noisy marketplace. Born from explaining the success of Linux, this metaphor became a philosophical foundation of the open-source movement and defined a generation's development culture.

Yet in 2026 we have a reason to reopen this classic, because the author of code is no longer only human. In an era where AI takes a request and produces a function, a module, sometimes an entire service, the two models of cathedral and bazaar can no longer fully explain what is happening inside our codebases. I believe we need a third model. As its metaphor, I propose a bizarre mansion that really exists in California, the Winchester Mystery House. This article respectfully revisits the original while adding my own perspective for the AI era.

1. The Cathedral - The Aesthetic of Central Planning

Let us revisit the first pillar of the original, the Cathedral model. The cathedral Raymond described is not simply "the bad way." It is, rather, the aesthetic of meticulous planning and authoritative control. Just as a medieval cathedral is built over decades under the consistent vision of a master architect, in this model a few core designers hold the whole structure in their minds and stack code according to that blueprint.

The Cathedral's strengths are clear. There is a coherent vision for the whole, boundaries and responsibilities are well defined, and there is the reassurance that one person (or a few) understands the entire system. Commercial closed software, well-managed internal core systems, and projects with clear RFCs and architecture documents belong here.

The weaknesses are equally clear. It is slow, the core designer becomes a bottleneck, and diverse outside perspectives struggle to enter. Raymond viewed the cathedral critically not because it is wrong but because Linux showed it could be faster and more robust.

2. The Bazaar - The Chaos of Distributed Evolution

The second pillar is the Bazaar. What Raymond found in Linus Torvalds's development of Linux was that, even without a clear master plan, stronger software could emerge from a process in which countless contributors threw in and refined code according to their own needs. He compared the scene to a noisy Middle Eastern marketplace.

The core proposition of the Bazaar model is compressed into one famous sentence:

Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow.

This is "Linus's Law": the more contributors and reviewers there are, the faster defects are found and fixed. The Bazaar model has the strengths of fast iteration, early release, and an influx of diverse perspectives. The entire open-source ecosystem centered on GitHub today is a descendant of this model.

What deserves attention is that the Bazaar is not disorder either. The bazaar has maintainers, merge authority, code review, and norms the community has agreed on. The subject of design has merely been distributed from one person to many; the fact that human intent shapes the structure is identical to the Cathedral. This very commonality is the key to understanding the third model.

3. The Hidden Commonality - Human Intent

Let us pause here and name something the original does not state explicitly. Cathedral and Bazaar look like opposites on the surface: centralized versus distributed, closed versus open, few versus many. But the two models share a decisive premise: behind every line of code there is a human who intended it.

In the Cathedral the master designer, and in the Bazaar each of many contributors, understands (at least within their own field of view) why the code they write is needed and where it fits in the whole. The developer sending a pull request knows what their change is for, and the maintainer judges whether it fits the overall vision. In other words, both models are part of one family in that intent sculpts structure.

So what happens if the author of code holds no intent? AI generates plausible next tokens from statistical patterns. Behind a line of code AI produced, there is no "why." There is only the probability that "in such a context, such code often appears." Right at this point, something that is neither cathedral nor bazaar begins.

4. The Winchester Mystery House - A Metaphor That Really Exists

Before entering the third model, we should know what the Winchester Mystery House actually is. This mansion in San Jose, California, was expanded almost without pause for about 38 years from the late 19th century by Sarah Winchester, heiress to the Winchester rifle fortune. There was no architect overseeing the whole and no finished blueprint. Rooms were simply added endlessly, staircases laid, doors hung.

The result is bizarre. Doors that open onto walls, staircases that run into ceilings, hallways that lead nowhere, doors on the second floor that open to the outside. Each addition may have had its own reason in the moment, but as a whole there is no coherence. Some rooms duplicate the function of others; some passages end in dead ends. The house clearly stands, and people can move through it, but no one understands it as a whole.

This is why the mansion is a perfect metaphor for the AI-era codebase. The Winchester House is the physical incarnation of a structure that is "locally functional but globally undesigned." And that is exactly the shape that grows in our repositories when AI appends code request by request.

5. The Third Model - The Codebase as Winchester House

Now the heart of the matter. I will call the new development style of the AI era the "Winchester House model." Placing its characteristics alongside Cathedral and Bazaar:

Dimension Cathedral Bazaar Winchester House
Designer A few core designers Many human contributors Statistical pattern (AI)
Source of structure Centralized planning Distributed human intent Accumulated local optima
Consistency High (single vision) Medium (held by norms) Low (no global intent)
Speed Slow Fast Very fast
Signature risk Bottleneck, rigidity Diffused direction Architectural-debt explosion

The core of the Winchester House model is the separation of "function" from "structure." For each request, AI produces, at astonishing speed, code that is locally plausible and usually works. Look at a single function or a single endpoint and you find nothing to fault. The problem is that the whole these fragments compose has no vision anyone is responsible for. New rooms keep appearing, but no one asks how those rooms form a single house.

5.1 Why This Is Neither Cathedral Nor Bazaar

Some may ask, "Isn't AI throwing in code just another contributor added to the bazaar?" The decisive difference lies in review and intent. In the bazaar, a contributor is responsible for their change, and a maintainer filters it against the overall vision. In the Winchester model, generation speed overwhelms review capacity, and the generator has no global vision to begin with. Bugs become shallow only with "enough eyeballs," but AI pours out code faster than eyeballs can keep up. This is the moment Linus's Law is inverted.

6. Vibe Coding and Unplanned Proliferation

"Vibe coding," popular since 2025, is the clearest example of the Winchester model. You throw a natural-language "build me a feature like this," accept whatever AI produces if it works, and move on. It is fast, fun, and astonishingly effective at the prototype stage.

The trouble is what happens as this accumulates. Each request appends new code while only loosely referencing what came before. The same feature gets implemented three places in subtly different ways, a similar data model scatters into four variants, and error handling differs module by module. Each piece works, so no one stops. This is how the Winchester House's dead-end staircases get laid, one step at a time.

What must be made clear is that AI itself is not the problem. Just as a hammer does not collapse a house, AI does not ruin a codebase. The problem is the human choice to keep adding without designing. What built Sarah Winchester's mansion was not the tools but the unstopping decision to keep expanding.

7. Architectural Debt - The Cost That Piles Up Unseen

The greatest risk of the Winchester model is architectural debt. If the technical debt people commonly speak of is "messy code," architectural debt is a problem at the level of "wrong structure." The latter is far harder to repay.

Architectural debt is dangerous because it is invisible in the short term.

  • Duplicate abstractions: Several abstractions do the same thing, making it ambiguous where to fix when a change is needed.
  • Inconsistent boundaries: Responsibility is divided unevenly between modules, so fixing one place breaks an unrelated one.
  • Hidden coupling: Parts that look independent on the surface are implicitly entangled, so the ripple of a change cannot be predicted.
  • Incomprehensibility: At some point no one can hold the entire system in their head anymore.

This debt compounds. At first, adding features is fast, but the more the codebase resembles the Winchester House, the more exponentially the cost of changing a single line grows. And past a certain threshold, understanding an existing room takes more time than building a new one. This is the opposite pole of the strategy of deliberately choosing "boring technology" to preserve simplicity, a view I explore further in Choose Boring Technology - The Paradox of the AI Era.

8. Is Linus's Law Still Valid in the AI Era?

Let us re-examine the original's core proposition, "given enough eyes, all bugs are shallow," in the AI era. This proposition stood on two implicit premises. First, the number of eyes (reviewers) looking at code can keep up with the speed of producing it. Second, those eyes understand meaningful context.

AI shakes both premises. First, generation speed overwhelms human review speed. When thousands of lines pour out in a day, gathering "enough eyes" becomes impossible in itself. Second, AI-generated code is more superficially plausible than human-written code, which actually dulls reviewers' vigilance. Far from becoming shallow, bugs hide deeper beneath a plausible surface.

That does not mean Linus's Law has been discarded. If anything its value has grown. In the AI era, "eyes" must be reinterpreted as a multi-layer structure that includes not only human reviewers but automated tests, static analysis, architectural verification, and AI-assisted review. The key is to artificially restore the balance between generation and verification.

9. So What - How to Preserve Intentional Design

Criticism alone is not enough. If you want to build, in the AI era, not a Winchester House but (at least) a well-managed bazaar, what should a developer do? I propose the following five.

  1. Position AI as a worker, not the architect. Architectural boundaries, module responsibilities, and data flow are defined by humans first. AI is entrusted with the implementation "within" those boundaries. People draw the floor plan; AI is the laborer who lays bricks quickly.
  2. Ask "is it understandable" alongside "does it work." The questions of code review must change. Not just whether it passes, but "will this structure still be explainable six months from now."
  3. Turn on tools that enforce boundaries. Block unplanned coupling automatically with module dependency rules, static analysis, and architecture fitness functions. Do not rely on human willpower alone.
  4. Hunt duplication regularly. AI is good at producing duplication. Make periodic refactoring and abstraction consolidation a conscious item on the schedule.
  5. Externalize the global vision into documents. Pull the architecture that lived in one person's head out into ADRs (Architecture Decision Records) and design documents. Explicitly provide the "blueprint" AI will reference the next time it produces code.

One principle runs through all five: do not lose intent as the price of gaining speed. AI makes the expansion infinitely fast, but the question "why place this room here" is still the human's to answer. This theme touches the larger arc of shifting developer identity, a discussion continued in Engineering in the LLM Era.

10. A View from the Korean Development Scene

Mapping this discussion onto the realities of Korean development reveals a few concrete implications.

  • Coupling with the SI and outsourcing structure: In SI projects under heavy deadline pressure, the "just make it work" temptation of AI-generated code is especially strong. To avoid handing a Winchester House to the next team that will maintain it after transfer, architecture documents and consistency criteria must be specified as deliverables from the contracting stage.
  • The startup speed myth: AI is a powerful accelerant for the Korean startup culture of "build fast and break things." But upon entering the scaling stage beyond Series A, architectural debt accumulated early often trips up hiring, onboarding, and expansion.
  • The junior-education problem: In an environment where AI builds all the code, junior developers easily lose the chance to learn "why we design it this way." The capacity for intentional design is no longer cultivated naturally; it becomes a scarce skill organizations must teach deliberately.

The conclusion for the Korean scene is clear. The first investment in AI adoption is not "faster generation" but "the structure that holds the generation." Lay bricks quickly without a floor plan, and you get the most expensive kind of house.

11. Respecting and Extending the Original

Finally, I want to make the relationship with the original explicit. The Winchester House model does not negate Cathedral and Bazaar. It adds a layer on top of them. The question Raymond posed in the 1990s - "who, and how, makes the structure of software?" - is exactly the same question three decades later. The only thing that has changed is that "a non-human generator" has been added to the list of candidate answers.

And that very point makes the original's insight shine brighter. Raymond's core message was not "the bazaar is superior to the cathedral" but that the social and cognitive process of making structure itself determines the quality of software. In the AI era, this message becomes a warning. If you remove human intent from that process, you build neither a cathedral nor a bazaar but a bizarre mansion that expands without end.

12. Conclusion + 3 Things You Can Apply Today

The Cathedral and the Bazaar were two ways humans make the structure of software. The AI era adds a dangerous third possibility: the Winchester House model, where only local optima accumulate without intent. This model is not inherently bad. At the prototyping and exploration stage it is a blessing. But the moment you make it a permanent residence, the most expensive bill of all - architectural debt - arrives.

Three things you can apply today:

  1. Draw the floor plan first. Before you put AI to work on code, have humans define architectural boundaries and module responsibilities on a single page. Expansion without a blueprint is the start of a Winchester House.
  2. Change the question in review. Add "will it still be understandable in six months" to "does it work." The smoother the surface of AI code, the more it deserves suspicion.
  3. Enforce boundaries automatically. With dependency rules and architecture fitness functions, keep the structure from collapsing even in the moments human willpower weakens. Intentional design lasts only when propped up by tools.

The question Raymond posed three decades ago still holds. Who, and how, makes the structure of our software? The AI era's answer is up to us: remain architects holding a floor plan, or become slaves to an expansion that cannot stop. The broader context continues in AI After Software - The Harness Era.
