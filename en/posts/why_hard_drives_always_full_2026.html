Why Hard Drives Are Always Full

Usage grows to match capacity, and why structure beats cleanup

Jul 27, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: Tens of terabytes, and still only 3% free

Bring up storage in any group of technical people and the same confession surfaces almost immediately: I bought plenty of space and it filled up again. A recent developer post on exactly this subject caught my attention for that reason. It opens with a self-audit — 3% free on the root drive, 1% free on a separate 12TB drive, despite tens of terabytes on hand — and adds an informal poll in which roughly half of respondents reported a drive more than 75% full. The sample is far too small to call it statistics, but I would bet that asking ten developers around me produces a similar result.

What makes this interesting is that it is not a technology problem, it is a behavior problem. Cost per gigabyte collapsed over the past two decades, and the volume an individual routinely handles went from tens of gigabytes to tens of terabytes — a three-order-of-magnitude jump. Yet the felt experience of running out of room is identical to what it was back then. Plenty of people would say they see the low disk space warning more often now, not less. When capacity grows a hundredfold and the experience does not budge, the binding constraint is on some other axis.

This article takes that other axis apart. Why adding capacity is not the fix, what genuinely consumes space, how much you lose before you store a single byte, what the 2026 pricing environment looks like, and how to build a retention structure instead of doing another cleanup. The conclusion up front: keeping everything forever is not possible, and if you do not decide what gets discarded first, the next drive fills exactly the same way.

1. Parkinson's Law, storage edition

1.1 The original law and its data corollary

Parkinson's Law started as a satirical essay in The Economist in 1955. The original line is work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion. A week-long deadline takes a week; a month-long deadline takes a month. The idea propagated into many fields, and in computing it settled as Parkinson's Law of Data. As the hacker jargon file phrases it, data expands to fill the space available for storage, with the note that buying more memory encourages more memory-hungry techniques.

The same shape shows up everywhere. Network traffic expands to fill available bandwidth is an old sysadmin proverb, and induced demand in road planning — widen the highway, traffic grows, congestion returns — is structurally identical. The common core is simple: increase supply and demand re-anchors to the new supply. Storage is no exception.

1.2 The real cause is procrastination, not entropy

Push a step further and a sharper explanation emerges. Framing a filling drive as natural disorder is poetic but inaccurate. The actual mechanism is blunt: a full disk is not a problem until it is so full you cannot put more stuff on it. So nobody acts before the threshold, and once the threshold hits, people do the minimum required to free a few gigabytes right now. That minimum changes nothing structural, so the same warning returns within days.

This is not unique to disks. Software optimization waits until the bottleneck hurts, calendars get managed only after double booking, and infrastructure gets funded after the outage. The interesting paradox is that we are taught premature optimization is the root of all evil, yet waiting until the crisis point reliably makes the same problem far more expensive to solve. Storage is simply where the paradox is easiest to see.

2. Why more capacity is not the answer

2.1 When friction disappears, so does judgment

When space is tight you make dozens of small judgments. Do I keep the 4K original or just the export? Will I really replay this game? Can this project folder go? Those judgments accumulate into the state of your disk. Once space is abundant, the judgments stop happening — they carry a cognitive cost and there is no longer any reason to pay it today. The result is that keeping becomes the default and deleting becomes the exception.

The key point is not that people are lazy but that the default flipped. Camera apps keep original and edit by default, game launchers never suggest uninstalling anything, and package managers do not tell you to clear the cache. The whole ecosystem is designed to retain. Unless the user explicitly authors a rule pushing the other way, the outcome only ever moves in one direction.

2.2 What actually happens when you go from 4TB to 20TB

Multiply capacity by five and a recognizable pattern follows.

  • Video retention policy changes. Originals that used to be deleted after editing now stay forever, so a single shoot deposits tens of gigabytes permanently.
  • Games never get uninstalled. Several big titles sit resident at roughly 100GB each because reinstalling is mildly annoying.
  • Backups accumulate without limit. With no retention window, snapshots and full backups pile up indefinitely.
  • VM and container images are never pruned. The phrase I might need this later quietly becomes hundreds of gigabytes.
  • Duplication goes unchecked. The same file exists in Downloads, on the Desktop, in a project folder and in a synced cloud folder.
  • New hobbies appear. Local AI model weights, RAW photo libraries and lossless audio — things you would not have attempted before — start arriving.

That last item matters most. Adding capacity does not merely give existing data more elbow room, it activates entirely new consumption patterns. One local language model checkpoint is tens of gigabytes; keep several variants and you are into the hundreds. That is why filling 20TB takes far less time than intuition suggests.

3. You lose before you start: labeled vs usable capacity

Before discussing behavior, account for the arithmetic loss. You receive less than the label the moment you buy a drive. Manufacturers state 1TB as one trillion bytes in decimal, while Windows and several other systems convert with binary multiples of 1024. Divide a trillion by 1024 three times and you land near 931, and the accurate unit for that number is GiB, not GB. It is a units mismatch, not a defect.

LabelActual bytes (decimal)OS display (binary)Approx. after format and reserves
500GB500,000,000,000about 465GiBabout 455–462GiB
1TB1,000,000,000,000about 931GiBabout 910–925GiB
2TB2,000,000,000,000about 1,862GiBabout 1,820–1,850GiB
4TB4,000,000,000,000about 3,725GiBabout 3,640–3,700GiB
12TB12,000,000,000,000about 10,914GiBabout 10,700–10,850GiB

The last column is what you actually feel, accounting for filesystem metadata, journals, recovery partitions and reserved system areas. It varies with filesystem type, block size and vendor imaging, so treat it as an approximation. A few more items belong in the same ledger.

  • SSD over-provisioning — the controller hides a slice of flash for wear leveling and garbage collection. A few percent is common even on consumer drives.
  • Block waste on small files — a 1KiB file occupies 4KiB on a 4KiB-block filesystem. With hundreds of thousands of small files in a source tree or cache folder, that is not noise.
  • Headroom for performance — SSD write performance can degrade when free space gets extremely low, and filesystems fragment badly. In practice, keeping 10–20% permanently free is a sane baseline rule.
  • Snapshots and trash — data you believe is deleted often persists in snapshots, the recycle bin or version history, still occupying blocks.

So a 4TB drive gives you roughly 3.6TiB usable, and if you honor a 15% headroom rule the practical working space is around 3TiB. Budget for that 25% gap between label and reality from the beginning.

4. What eats the space: a measurable breakdown

The claim that a drive filled up somehow is almost never true. On most drives the top five items account for more than 80% of usage. The table below lists typical sizes commonly encountered as of 2026. Individual variance is enormous, so measure your own system — but this is enough to decide where to look first.

4.1 Split regenerable from irreplaceable

The most important column is not size but cleanup difficulty. Data divides into exactly two kinds.

  • Regenerable — games, package caches, container images, build artifacts, public model weights, anything a streaming service will serve again. Deleting costs time, not data.
  • Irreplaceable — photos and video you shot, documents you wrote, family records, personal project source, material you cannot obtain again. Deleting is final.

Cleanup always starts on the regenerable side. And on most drives regenerable data is more than half of the total. On a developer laptop the ratio climbs further: clearing container caches, dependency folders and build output alone routinely recovers tens of gigabytes.

4.2 Why development machines fill fastest

Developer machines fill quickly because of a specific structural habit: the same data is stored many times over. Ten projects with independent dependency trees mean ten copies of the same library. Container images that cannot share base layers duplicate in the same way. It is common to see a trivial service ship as a container image over 1GB, and reports of the same image dropping below 100MB after optimization are equally common. That gap shows up not only on disk but in deploy time and network cost.

Practical habits worth adopting: use a package manager that shares a content-addressed store, periodically empty dependency folders for dormant projects, and cap the container build cache. All three are easily reversible, which makes them low-risk.

5. The 2026 variable: storage has stopped getting cheaper

Everything above rested on a hidden assumption — capacity keeps getting cheaper. For roughly two decades that held, which is why just buy more was a reasonable response. The 2026 situation challenges it.

As researched in July 2026, market analyses and reporting converge on a common picture. AI datacenter buildouts absorbed NAND flash at enormous scale, and memory makers shifted capacity toward higher-margin high bandwidth memory, tightening conventional NAND supply. Contract prices for NAND and SSDs jumped by large double-digit percentages on a quarterly basis in early 2026, and as SSD supply tightened, substitution demand pushed HDD prices up as well. Enterprise-class capacity drives saw especially steep moves, and consumer NVMe pricing sits far above the bargain levels of a couple of years ago.

A note on prices. Storage pricing moves sharply not only quarter to quarter but week to week. The above summarizes research conducted in July 2026 and guarantees no specific retail price. Always check actual pricing and stock at the moment you buy.

Three practical implications for an individual follow.

  • The cost of just buy more went up. Upgrading used to be cheaper than curating; when the price environment shifts, that calculation shifts with it.
  • Keeping existing hardware healthy matters more. When replacement is expensive, monitoring drive health and maintaining redundant backups gains weight.
  • Free cloud headroom is not immune. When underlying cost rises, free tiers and plan structures can be adjusted too.

Balance is warranted, though. Industry forecasts generally place supply normalization somewhere between late 2026 and 2027, and forecasts like these miss frequently. So neither stockpile now nor wait, it will crash soon rests on solid ground. The variable an individual actually controls is not price but the growth rate of their own data.

6. A workable diagnostic procedure

The mandatory first step is measurement. Deleting by intuition leaves the large blocks untouched while risking the dangerous files. Follow this order.

  1. See the whole picture — total capacity, free space and free percentage for every drive.
  2. Analyze the tree — sort top-level folders by size and identify the top five. Most answers appear right here.
  3. Classify — decide whether each item found is regenerable or irreplaceable.
  4. Reversible actions first — move and archive before you delete; deletion comes last.

6.1 Tooling by operating system, conceptually

  • Windows — the storage page in Settings gives a rough category breakdown. For detailed tree analysis, visualization tools in the WizTree or TreeSize family are convenient. Use the built-in cleanup feature for temporary files rather than manual deletion.
  • macOS — storage management in System Settings lists categories and large files. Check the photo library and Time Machine local snapshots separately.
  • Linux — ncdu in a terminal and a graphical disk usage analyzer on the desktop are the standard pair. If you run containers, inspect container storage on its own.
  • NAS — most vendors ship a storage analyzer in the admin UI. Always review snapshot consumption alongside file data.

6.2 Safe, read-only commands

The commands below delete nothing. They exist purely to establish the current state.

# Linux / macOS - free space per filesystem
df -h

# Folders directly under home, sorted by size (Linux)
du -h --max-depth=1 ~ | sort -h | tail -20

# macOS uses a different flag spelling
du -h -d 1 ~ | sort -h | tail -20

# Space used by containers (read-only summary)
docker system df
docker system df -v

PowerShell on Windows offers equivalent inspection.

# Drive usage and free space
Get-PSDrive C

# Sort folders under home by size (read-only, may take a while)
Get-ChildItem $HOME -Directory | ForEach-Object {
    $size = (Get-ChildItem $_.FullName -Recurse -File -ErrorAction SilentlyContinue |
             Measure-Object Length -Sum).Sum
    [PSCustomObject]@{ Folder = $_.Name; GB = [math]::Round($size / 1GB, 2) }
} | Sort-Object GB -Descending

Warning about deletion. This article deliberately provides no delete commands. Pasting a deletion command found online without understanding it is a leading cause of data loss, and it is frequently irreversible. Follow these rules instead.

  • Confirm at least one backup on a separate physical device before deleting anything.
  • Do not touch system folders whose role you do not understand, or recovery partitions.
  • A local delete inside a cloud sync folder can remove the remote original. Verify the client behavior first.
  • When uncertain, move instead of delete, then remove after a few days without issues.
  • For application caches, prefer the official cleanup feature each application provides.

7. Structure over cleanup: designing storage tiers

A single big cleanup buys a few weeks. The durable answer is to split data by access frequency into tiers and apply a different rule to each. It is a familiar idea in enterprise storage and it transfers cleanly to a personal setup.

TierContentsMediaRule
HotActive work, last three monthsInternal SSDKeep 20% free; move to warm as soon as work completes
WarmThis year's material, occasionally reopened projectsLarge HDD or NASReassess for cold every quarter
ColdRecords you must keep, or sentimental archivesExternal HDD, NAS archive volumeCompress and archive; maintain an index only
OffsiteEvery piece of irreplaceable dataCloud or physically separated deviceVerify regularly; restore tests are mandatory
EphemeralCaches, build artifacts, container imagesInternal SSDSet a cap; purge automatically on a schedule

The tier most often missing is ephemeral. The moment caches and build output are treated as data worth keeping, they colonize the hot tier. Conversely, offsite must exist regardless of any capacity concern, because it is the last line of defense when a cleanup goes wrong.

7.1 Building automatic cleanup rules

Cleanup that depends on willpower fails. Encode the rules into the system instead. These work well in practice.

  • Downloads folder expiry — move files untouched for 30 days into a staging folder, and review again 30 days later. Two stages rather than immediate deletion keeps it safe.
  • Backup retention windows — state generation counts explicitly, such as seven daily, four weekly and six monthly. Without a policy, backups outgrow the source.
  • Container and build cache caps — set a maximum cache size so old entries are evicted automatically.
  • Monthly top-item review — a ten-minute habit of checking only the top five folders catches most runaway growth early.
  • Free-space alerts — trigger a notification when free space drops below 20%. Acting before the threshold is the whole point.
  • Project-close checklist — make emptying the dependency folder and moving to archive an explicit step when a project ends.

7.2 Are compression and deduplication silver bullets?

Modern filesystems such as ZFS and Btrfs offer transparent compression and deduplication. The practical judgment here is clear.

  • Compression is usually worth enabling. On compressible data such as text, logs, source code and database files, savings are substantial, and because less physical data is read and written, throughput can actually improve. A fast algorithm as the default with a higher-ratio algorithm reserved for archival volumes is a sound combination.
  • It is pointless on already-compressed data. On a volume dominated by photos, video and archives, the ratio hovers near 1 and you burn CPU for nothing.
  • Deduplication is not recommended for most personal setups. The dedup table must live in memory, and the frequently cited rule of thumb is several gigabytes of RAM per terabyte of data. At tens of terabytes the requirement becomes unrealistic. If you know duplicates exist, file-level cleanup or an offline dedup tool delivers far better value.

In short, consider compression by default and deduplication only for special workloads. Neither is a license to skip the question of what you are willing to discard.

8. Cloud plans, external drives and NAS: the real math

8.1 What a cloud subscription actually buys

Consumer cloud storage at the 2TB tier clusters within a fairly narrow monthly band across the major providers, and exact figures move with promotions and policy changes — check the official page at signup rather than trusting a number in an article.

The amount matters less than the structural character of the choice. A subscription converts a hardware failure risk and an offsite backup problem into a recurring fee, which is often a good trade. In exchange you accept a dependency — access ends when the subscription does — and pricing that steps up as data grows. Past the 2TB tier, consumer plan options thin out quickly, which is a real constraint for anyone with a video or RAW photo habit.

8.2 External drive or NAS?

Judge with these criteria rather than by capacity alone.

  • An external drive fits when you are in the low terabytes, archival storage is the goal, and simultaneous access from multiple devices is unnecessary. Lowest upfront cost.
  • A NAS fits when several devices share the same material, automated backup and versioning matter, and the data must survive a single drive failure.
  • Most people need both. A NAS as primary storage plus an external drive kept offline as backup is the most robust practical combination.

One common misconception. RAID is not a backup. It survives drive failure but does nothing about accidental deletion, ransomware, fire or flooding. A backup must live separately and physically apart. The often-quoted 3-2-1 rule — three copies, two media types, one offsite — remains a solid baseline.

9. Artificial constraints as a counterintuitive fix

The most interesting proposal in the original post was to impose a constraint smaller than the resources you actually have. Deliberately developing against a low-powered small computer forces attention to efficiency, and the resulting software runs faster everywhere. Underneath sits a sharp observation: resources and capability often permit the same outcome, just at a different cost.

The idea transfers directly to storage. Concrete forms worth trying:

  • Set a virtual ceiling — treat a 4TB drive as a 3TB drive and use 3TB as the cleanup trigger. You act far earlier than the real threshold.
  • Use quotas — enforce per-folder limits with filesystem or NAS quota features so the system, not your willpower, holds the line.
  • Trade space for space — require freeing 100GB before adding 100GB. Slightly extreme, but it definitively caps growth rate.
  • Cap copy counts — decide in advance how many copies of the same material may exist.

The point is to move the threshold forward so you meet the problem while it is still cheap to solve. At 99% full you have neither the mental slack to decide nor the scratch space to move things around. At 75% you still have options.

10. Five mistakes people repeat

  • Hunting only for large files — a large-file search misses the mass created by hundreds of thousands of small ones. Folder-level tree analysis comes first.
  • Forgetting trash and snapshots — if free space does not grow after deleting, the data is usually still sitting in one of these.
  • Cleaning a cloud sync folder locally — the remote original can vanish with it. Verify the client's behavior before proceeding.
  • Starting a big cleanup with no backup — the most common and most damaging mistake. Verifying a backup first is not optional.
  • Treating symptoms instead of causes — freeing the urgent 10GB and stopping means repeating the exercise next week. Find what keeps growing and put a rule on it.

Conclusion: keeping everything forever is not possible

To summarize: hard drives are always full not because storage is scarce but because we operate inside a structure where retention is the default and deletion is the exception, and we make no decisions until we hit the threshold. Add capacity and behavior re-anchors to it, so a few months later you are back in the same place. On top of that, the market conditions of July 2026 have raised the price of the just buy more response itself.

So invert the order. Measure first, classify regenerable versus irreplaceable, build tiers by access frequency, and let rules rather than willpower maintain them. Compression generally helps, deduplication is overkill for most personal setups, and no technology answers the question of what you are willing to lose.

One last conclusion, uncomfortable and important. Storing everything forever is not achievable. Storage carries ongoing cost, media have finite life, and management effort grows faster than linearly with volume. So before ordering the next drive, settle one thing: what are you willing to discard? Without that criterion, 20TB or 100TB converges on the same 3% free.
