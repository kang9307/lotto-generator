What a Mouse-First Interface Leaves Out - Making a GUI Fully Keyboard Operable

Two basic requirements, reach and escape, the places where focus disappears, and what automated checks cannot judge

Aug 30, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: losing the mouse happens more often than you think

Say your wrist hurts and you cannot use a mouse for a few days. Open the work application you use every day and try to get through it with the Tab key alone. Most people stall around halfway. Focus never lands on certain buttons, some regions have no way out, and nothing on screen tells you where focus currently is. That screen was built with care and passed review. It just did so on the assumption that a mouse exists.

The topic came around again recently in developer circles. One developer argued that a GUI should be fully operable from the keyboard, pushing back on the common belief that terminal interfaces are inherently better for keyboard users. The claim was that GUIs are not incapable, only deprioritised by the people building them, citing GNOME's interface guidelines stating that every action should also be possible with the keyboard, along with the shortcut work he had put into his own application. That piece also concedes that a mouse is better, and sometimes required, for certain tasks.

What follows is the next question. What exactly does a mouse-first screen leave out? The gaps land in predictable places, and most of them are about state rather than features. Terminals are a different problem and are not covered here; the argument that a text grid has nowhere to put structure in the first place is in what it means for a terminal UI to have no structure. This article is the opposite case: why a screen that already has somewhere to put structure still ends up unusable from the keyboard.

1. Who actually uses a keyboard only

Without widening the frame first, this topic narrows immediately. Four categories produce keyboard-only operation, and permanent conditions are one of them.

  • Permanent conditions — tremor or limited range of motion that makes small targets hard to hit accurately, or operating by sound rather than sight. Screen readers themselves run on top of keyboard interaction, so when keyboard operation breaks, that route breaks with it.
  • Temporary conditions — a wrist or arm injury, a cast, a recovery period after surgery. Days or weeks only, but identical while they last.
  • Situational conditions — holding a child in one arm, travelling with no surface for a mouse, presenting while sharing a screen, a remote desktop where the cursor lags, a wireless mouse with a dead battery.
  • Expertise — anyone crossing the same screen hundreds of times a day. Repetitive form entry, admin screens that shuttle between list and detail, developer tooling. For them the round trip of hand leaving the keyboard and returning is itself the cost.

Compress those four into a line and you get this. Keyboard operation arises from the conditions of a moment, not from an attribute of the user. Which is why the statistic that almost none of our users are like that answers nothing. Statistics count the permanent category only; the other three are already mixed into the sample while remaining invisible to any survey.

Expertise shares its interests exactly with the other three. What someone fast wants and what someone without a mouse needs are structurally the same thing, and in organisations where accessibility gets no budget, the identical work passes under the name of productivity.

2. Two requirements come first - reach and escape

Before layering on any clever shortcuts, only two questions matter. Can you get there, and can you get out.

The first is reach. Focus has to land on every point where something happens. The most common leak is things that look like buttons and are not.

<div class="btn" onclick="save()">Save</div>      <!-- Tab never reaches it -->
<button type="button" onclick="save()">Save</button>  <!-- reach, activation, role included -->

The two look identical on screen and behave identically under a mouse. The difference surfaces only on a keyboard. That is why the defect stays quiet: the people building and the people reviewing both use a mouse, so no amount of staring at the screen reveals it.

The second is escape, and this one is far more severe. When focus enters a region with no key that takes it out, that is a focus trap. Failing to reach costs one feature; failing to escape costs the entire screen from that point on. If Tab circles the same spot and Shift+Tab will not back out, the only remaining option is closing the window, and whatever was being typed goes with it.

Escape comes first in any review. An unreachable button can be fixed later, and users route around it in the meantime. Someone who got stuck generally does not come back.

Traps also form in predictable places.

  • Embedded third-party widgets and inline frames
  • Hand-rolled overlays and dropdowns
  • Code that forcibly pulls focus back whenever it leaves
  • Regions that swallow key events wholesale while handling scrolling themselves. Stopping propagation for the sake of a feature ends up blocking the exit for focus

3. An invisible focus indicator may as well not exist

A mouse has a cursor crossing the screen. A keyboard does not, and the focus indicator stands in for it, which makes the indicator the cursor itself rather than decoration. Without it, pressing Enter means not knowing what is about to be pressed, and near a delete or payment control people simply stop.

The habit of removing focus indicators is well established, and the reasons are understandable. The browser default outline clashes with the design, and reviewers point out that it lingers after a mouse click and looks untidy. The problem is that the fix chosen at that point was to remove the indicator entirely.

/* no indicator after a mouse click, indicator kept for keyboard movement */
:focus-visible { outline: 3px solid #1a73e8; outline-offset: 2px; }

Beyond outright removal there are two more routes to the same outcome, and in practice they show up more often.

  • Low-contrast indicators — an outline close in colour to the background exists in code and not to the eye. On a screen where the background differs inside and outside a card, a single colour guarantees that one side disappears.
  • Obscured indicators — a sticky header, a bottom consent bar, a drawer sliding in from the side. Focus moved behind it while nothing visibly happened, then reappears abruptly after a few more presses. What was passed in between is unknowable.
  • Indicators pushed off screen — horizontally scrolling lists or tab strips where focus crosses into a region that is not visible and scrolling does not follow.

All three produce the same experience as the first. And in all three the indicator exists in the code, so static checks rarely catch them.

4. When order and layout disagree

Keyboard movement has an order. Document order decides it; styling decides where things appear. They are two different things, so they can disagree. When they do, users can no longer predict where the next press will take them while looking at the screen. A mouse has no such problem at all, because it uses coordinates rather than sequence.

What layout-altering styles cause

Properties that reorder items, grid placement that assigns cells directly, and settings that reverse direction all change visual order only. Movement order stays put. Responsive layouts tangle this further: there is one document, so if the visual order differs between wide and narrow viewports, at least one of them is guaranteed to disagree.

Add the habit of setting positive tab index values and it worsens. Any value above zero pulls that element ahead of the document's natural order. A single number added to fix one component ends up redefining movement order for the whole page.

Repeated regions and skipping

The other face of the ordering problem is repetition. With thirty menu items in the header, a keyboard user passes thirty stops before reaching the content, and does it again on every page. Mouse users never pay that cost once, which makes it hard for builders to feel.

  • Skip links — placed at the very start of the document and sending focus straight to the content. They are usually hidden and revealed on focus, and when the reveal styling is missing, focus arrives while the screen shows nothing, which makes the link useless.
  • Landmarks — marking header, content, sidebar and footer as document structure lets assistive technology users jump by region. Build everything from generic containers and that method disappears.
  • Heading hierarchy — headings placed in order are themselves a table of contents and a navigation method. Scrambling the levels to control font size breaks that too.
  • Collapsed regions — content collapsed out of sight but still present in tab order sends users through a long stretch of nothing.

5. Why hand-built widgets are risky

Native elements give behaviour away for free. A single button element already carries tab order membership, activation on Enter and Space, a focus indicator, disabled-state handling, and a role exposed to assistive technology. Nobody implemented any of it; it is simply there.

Rebuild the same look from a generic container and all of that vanishes, and every key behaviour has to be implemented from scratch. What happens in practice is roughly this. A click handler goes on, a role attribute goes on, and a tabindex goes on. That much passes the checker, but there is no Enter handler. Focus arrives, the indicator shows, and nothing presses.

Composite widgets demand more. Below is a summary of the key behaviour the W3C ARIA Authoring Practices Guide asks of each pattern.

WidgetWhat a native element providesKey behaviour to implement when built by hand
ButtonTab reach · Enter/Space activation · disabled handlingBoth Enter and Space. Space must also suppress page scrolling
Checkbox · radioSelection state exposed · arrow movement within a groupSpace to toggle. Within a group, arrow keys move and only one member stays in tab order
Tab listNothing (no equivalent native element)Left and Right arrows move to previous and next, wrapping from the last to the first. Up and Down for vertical lists. Home and End for the ends
SliderArrow steps · Home/End · value exposedArrow keys move one step, Home sets the first allowed value, End the last. Page Up and Page Down move in larger increments

The table produces its own rule of thumb. Do not rebuild what a native element already does. If the appearance is wrong, changing the styling is almost always cheaper than changing the element.

6. Modals and popovers - go in, come back, and do not leak

Overlaid surfaces break more often than anything else in keyboard operation. There are three requirements and usually only one is met.

  • Focus must move inside when it opens. Opening without moving focus leaves users tabbing through the layer behind, unaware that anything appeared. The ARIA Authoring Practices Guide states that when a dialog opens, focus moves to an element inside the dialog, with the choice of element depending on content and destructive actions avoided.
  • Focus must return when it closes. Close without restoring focus and it drops to the top of the document, so the next Tab starts at the top of the page and the user loses their position entirely. The same guidance says focus generally returns to the element that invoked the dialog.
  • Focus must not leak backwards while open. If Tab does not wrap from the last item to the first, focus crosses into the layer behind. A common mistake is hiding the background from assistive technology without also removing it from tab order, so it vanishes for sound and persists for focus.

One addition: focus alone must not change the screen. If arrowing through a list navigates or submits at every item, keyboard users lose the ability to choose at all.

7. The conflicts that come with adding shortcuts

Once keyboard operation is taken seriously, shortcuts are the tempting next step. A different problem starts there. Keys are finite and already owned.

  • Keys the browser uses — tab switching, address bar, find, reload, zoom. Overlap an application feature onto those and the user loses one of the two.
  • Keys the operating system uses — window switching, screen lock, capture. Free on one platform does not mean free on another.
  • Keys assistive technology uses — the most frequently forgotten. Screen readers use single character keys as their own commands in reading mode, for moving to headings or tables. Intercept the same letter and that user loses a means of navigation.
  • Users mid-typing — single character shortcuts fire while text is being entered into a field. Typing a name and triggering a save is the usual shape of it.

The standard has a specific answer here. Success Criterion 2.1.4 Character Key Shortcuts (Level A) in WCAG 2.2 requires that a shortcut made only of letter, punctuation, number or symbol characters offers at least one of turning it off, remapping it, or activating it only on focus. All three take the form of giving the user control rather than removing the shortcut.

8. What automated checks miss, and what people have to do

This is the heart of it. Keyboard problems are the category least likely to be caught automatically.

The W3C WAI guidance on selecting evaluation tools says so directly. Tools cannot check all accessibility aspects automatically and human judgement is required, and evaluation tools cannot determine accessibility, they can only assist in doing so. A warning that tools sometimes produce false or misleading results comes attached.

What tools do well is clear: contrast ratio calculations, missing alternative text, label and input association, duplicate identifiers — anything a rule can settle. The core of keyboard operation mostly falls outside that.

  • Is focus actually visible — a tool can see that an outline property exists. But all three routes above, the low-contrast indicator, the obscured indicator and the one pushed off screen, are states in which the property exists. Overlap after rendering and real contrast sit beyond static rules.
  • Does the order make sense — a tool can enumerate the order, but whether that order preserves meaning is not a decidable question. The criterion itself asks for an order that preserves meaning and operability, and preservation of meaning is a human judgement.
  • Does anything trap focus — traps appear only during interaction. Scanning document structure will not reproduce them, and if a screen takes several steps to reach, the tool never gets there.
  • Does the widget actually press — a role and a tabindex pass rule checks, but without an Enter handler nothing happens. This is the most common shape of a failure that passed.

Tools are a floor, not a pass mark. Fixing what they find is necessary, but finishing that list does not produce a keyboard-operable screen.

Put the mouse away and go through it once

The only substitute is doing it, and all it takes is moving the mouse off the desk. On a laptop, disable the trackpad too, because observation ends the moment a hand drifts over. One representative flow is enough.

Log what you find as functional defects. Filed as accessibility items they become a separate workstream, and separate workstreams tend to slip a quarter. The save button does not press is simply a bug.

9. What the standard asks for

Most of what is above already exists as success criteria in the W3C Web Content Accessibility Guidelines. Below are the criteria directly tied to keyboard operation, as confirmed in the WCAG 2.2 document.

Number · nameLevelSubstance
2.1.1 KeyboardAAll functionality must be operable through a keyboard interface without requiring specific timings for individual keystrokes, except where the function depends on the path of movement rather than endpoints
2.1.2 No Keyboard TrapAIf focus can be moved to a component with a keyboard, it must be possible to move focus away using only a keyboard. If standard methods do not work, the user must be told how
2.1.4 Character Key ShortcutsAShortcuts made only of letter, punctuation, number or symbol characters must offer at least one of turn off, remap, or active only on focus
2.4.1 Bypass BlocksAA mechanism must be available to bypass blocks of content repeated on multiple pages
2.4.3 Focus OrderAWhere sequential navigation affects meaning or operation, components must receive focus in an order that preserves meaning and operability
2.4.7 Focus VisibleAAAny keyboard operable interface must have a mode of operation where the focus indicator is visible
2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum)AAWhen a component receives keyboard focus, it must not be entirely hidden by author-created content
3.2.1 On FocusAReceiving focus alone must not initiate a change of context

Read in order, the table maps onto the sections above. Reach is 2.1.1, escape is 2.1.2, the focus indicator is 2.4.7 and 2.4.11, ordering is 2.4.3, repeated regions are 2.4.1, shortcuts are 2.1.4. Six of them are Level A, which is worth noting: A is the most basic tier in this scheme, and most of keyboard operation sits there.

One thing has to be stated plainly. Which standard is mandatory for whom, and to what extent, varies by jurisdiction and by the service in question. This article covers technical requirements only; legal obligation has to be confirmed against the current rules of the relevant jurisdiction. The technical side, though, points at broadly the same items regardless of jurisdiction, so the work is already identified before those rules are checked.

What could not be confirmed

  • The scope and subjects of legal obligation by jurisdiction. National and sector-specific rules were not compared directly.
  • Figures for what percentage of problems automated checkers catch. Vendor-published ratios exist, but no neutral source with a disclosed method and sample was confirmed, so none is quoted.
  • Trends in the number of keyboard accessibility disputes or remediation demands. No source with an identifiable surveying body and year was confirmed.
  • A comparison of keyboard support across non-web desktop frameworks. The items here are framed for the web, and native toolkits provide a different range of default behaviour.

Conclusion: a mouse is a shortcut, not the only route

What a mouse-first screen leaves out is state, not features. Where you are now, where you go next, how you get out of here. A mouse substitutes a single coordinate for all three, which lets builders forget they are managing state at all. A keyboard makes the screen carry that management.

So the order of work runs from the bottom. Remove the traps first, make focus visible, align movement order with the screen, and cut a path through the repeated regions. That is most of it, and shortcuts come after. Reverse the order and you get a screen with elegant shortcuts that cannot be used with Tab.

None of this benefits one group alone. The person with an injured wrist, the person presenting, and the person crossing the same screen hundreds of times a day need exactly the same thing. A mouse is a convenient shortcut. There is no reason for it to be the only route to the screen.
