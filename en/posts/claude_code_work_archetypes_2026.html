Five Archetypes of Workers in the AI Age — Insights from the Creator of Claude Code

Prototyper · Builder · Sweeper · Grower · Maintainer — A new language for understanding how people work, not just what title they hold

2026-07-01 Trends & Culture

Introduction: When Job Titles Stop Describing How People Work

As of June 2026, AI tools have multiplied the speed at which engineers write code by several times. Something curious follows: the traditional boundaries between engineering, product management, design, and data science are dissolving. A person's job title increasingly fails to explain what they actually do each day. Saying "I'm a backend engineer" tells you almost nothing about the kinds of problems they spend their energy on.

Into this context, Boris Cherny — the creator of Claude Code — offered a useful frame. Observing his own team, he identified five recurring patterns in how people actually work: Prototyper, Builder, Sweeper, Grower, and Maintainer. These aren't job functions; they're patterns of work. A designer might be a Prototyper. An engineer might be a Maintainer. A PM might be a Sweeper. The archetype crosses the org chart.

This article introduces that frame but doesn't stop there. It examines why these distinctions feel more urgent in 2026, what the genuine strengths and traps of each archetype are, and how AI tools are reshaping the value each one provides.

1. The Five Archetypes — What Each One Does

Let's anchor on Cherny's original definitions before building on them. His key premise: "These roles are not really tied to job function — across Anthropic, some designers match category 1, some 2, some 3; same for engineers, PM, DS." They describe patterns of work, not titles.

1.1 Prototyper — The Possibility Explorer

The Prototyper "comes up with brand new ideas; churns out many ideas, most of which don't ship." They invest energy in exploration itself rather than completion. AI tools have dramatically amplified the Prototyper's output. Where validating a single idea once took days, it now takes hours.

Strength: Covers the possibility space quickly. Comfortable with failure, so they see directions others don't attempt. They inject energy into teams during early, pre-traction stages when the right direction isn't yet known.

Trap: Prone to a pattern of not carrying good ideas through to completion. AI's acceleration of prototyping deepens this trap — when ideas come too easily, the discipline of committing to one weakens. A team over-indexed on Prototypers creates a graveyard of unused experiments.

1.2 Builder — The Finisher

The Builder "quickly turns a prototype or idea into production-grade product or infrastructure." They think about scalability, reliability, and maintainability as they build. A Builder knows what "done" means — not just working, but built well enough that others can take it over without it falling apart.

Strength: The most directly amplified by AI coding tools. Code generation speed goes up, but judgment about what to build and how to structure it remains the Builder's domain. As AI handles more boilerplate, Builders can ship more in less time.

Trap: Can fall into building without questioning whether the thing should be built at all. Also, if AI-generated code isn't reviewed carefully, the Builder can ironically become the fastest producer of technical debt.

1.3 Sweeper — The Simplifier

The Sweeper "cleans up the UI, simplifies the code and system, unships features, optimizes performance." They create value by removing, not adding. Of all five archetypes, Sweeper is the most distinctive. The idea of actively "unshipping" features — deliberately removing them — is rarely celebrated but often badly needed.

Strength: As AI accelerates code generation, complexity debt accumulates faster. Sweepers manage this debt. Teams with strong Sweepers maintain codebases that remain understandable over time, which directly sustains team velocity.

Trap: Sweeper contributions are invisible in most organizations. A PR that adds a feature gets noticed; a PR that deletes 500 lines while preserving behavior doesn't. In an era of AI-generated code overproduction, the Sweeper's role becomes more critical while recognition remains scarce.

1.4 Grower — The Market Tuner

The Grower "takes a product that has been built and iterates on it to improve Product-Market Fit." They're sensitive to user data, market signals, and competitive shifts. Their question isn't "how well is this built?" but "how well is this landing with the market?"

Strength: Saves teams from the trap of building technically excellent products that nobody uses. Can communicate in users' language and orient the team's priorities toward real market signals. With AI enabling faster experimentation, the Grower's judgment directs experiments toward meaningful outcomes.

Trap: Can over-respond to short-term signals at the cost of long-term direction. Grower-heavy teams sometimes keep adding features because "users seem to like it," piling on complexity in the process.

1.5 Maintainer — The Stability Keeper

The Maintainer "owns a mature system to make it secure, reliable, fast, and efficient as it scales." Stability is their value. A system that doesn't go down is their definition of success. Often the quietest person on the team — until something breaks, at which point they're the most urgently needed.

Strength: Without Maintainers, technical debt and security vulnerabilities accumulate silently in mature systems. No AI tool fully replaces the deep contextual knowledge a Maintainer builds about a system's dependencies, history, and failure modes.

Trap: Can drift toward resisting change. "It's working fine, don't touch it" is the Maintainer instinct — a useful brake, but it can also block necessary modernization. Embracing AI tools, upgrading architecture, and accepting controlled disruption requires Maintainers to sit with discomfort.

2. How AI Tools Reshape Each Archetype

Cherny's frame wasn't a simple taxonomy exercise. Its context matters: engineering, product, design, and data science are melting into a new kind of role, and these archetypes try to capture what actually happens within that fusion.

AI tools affect each archetype differently. Prototypers can explore more possibilities per day. Builders offload implementation repetition and focus on design judgment. Sweepers face a new burden: managing complexity that AI generation produces at scale. Growers can run more experiments with faster data analysis. Maintainers can automate repetitive operations but must scrutinize AI-generated code for subtle vulnerabilities.

The critical shift: one person can credibly span more archetypes than before. When AI lifts implementation weight, a Prototyper can also carry Builder responsibilities to a meaningful degree. But the more AI speeds up execution, the more judgment becomes the defining human contribution. Deciding what to build, what to simplify, and what to preserve — these decisions don't get automated.

3. Archetype Mix Across the Product Lifecycle

One of Cherny's clearest insights is that the right archetype mix changes as a product matures. Early exploration stages lean on Prototyper plus Builder plus Sweeper — rapid experimentation matters most. Once traction appears, Builder, Sweeper, and Grower become central. Mature systems keep Sweeper, Grower, and Maintainer at the core while borrowing Builder skills as needed.

This lifecycle lens matters because one of the most common team mistakes is deploying the wrong archetype energy at the wrong product stage. A team of Prototypers on a mature product keeps adding unnecessary features while the codebase decays. A team of Maintainers on an early-stage product builds nothing. When structuring or assessing a team, the first question should be: what stage is this product at?

4. Identifying and Developing Your Archetype

The most practical question: which archetype are you, and how do you develop the ones you're weak in?

The most honest diagnostic is to notice when your energy rises. If you feel excited exploring new ideas, Prototyper tendency is strong. If you feel satisfied completing a polished, production-ready thing, you lean Builder. If cleaning up messy code brings real reward, you're likely Sweeper. If user signals energize you to iterate, Grower. If system stability brings relief, Maintainer.

As Cherny notes, most people span two or three archetypes. That's a feature. The ability to shift between modes when the situation demands it is genuinely valuable. But knowing your dominant archetype has practical uses: it clarifies where you contribute most, and it surfaces what your team might be missing.

4.1 Building Weaker Archetypes

A Builder who wants to develop Grower instincts should deliberately spend time on user interviews and data analysis — not as a PM exercise, but as a first-person investigation. A Prototyper wanting Builder habits should practice carrying a single idea through to a "done" standard repeatedly. A Maintainer developing Sweeper sensibility might start by committing to a regular practice of simplification PRs on frequently-touched code.

AI tools can accelerate this development. Prototypers use AI to validate ideas faster. Builders use AI to offload boilerplate and focus on architecture. Sweepers use AI to analyze code quickly and identify refactoring directions. The caveat: AI doesn't provide direction. What to simplify, what to build, what to preserve — those judgments remain human.

5. Why This Framework Matters Now

Cherny's frame is more than a taxonomy. It implicitly asks a bigger question: in an era where AI dissolves job function boundaries, how should organizations understand and place people?

Traditional title-based org design says "hire two backend engineers, one PM." But when AI tools blur those boundaries, two "engineers" can have fundamentally different working styles and contributions. The archetype frame provides a new language for that diversity.

For hiring, it opens a more precise question: not "we need a senior engineer" but "we need Sweeper energy right now — someone who brings order to complexity." For team assessment, naming a missing archetype is more actionable than vague dissatisfaction with team dynamics. For career design, knowing your archetype helps you identify which team and product stage will let you contribute most.

Conclusion — Know Your Pattern, Not Just Your Title

As of 2026, AI tools are changing how work gets done faster than organizations are updating how they describe roles. Boris Cherny's framework offers a practical corrective: understand people by how they work when energy is high, not just what their job title says.

None of the five archetypes is inherently superior. A Maintainer isn't less valuable than a Prototyper. What matters is which archetype a team needs at its current product stage, and whether the people in place match that need. The most actionable questions are: which archetypes are represented on your team, which are missing, and where do you personally contribute most?

AI tools accelerate every archetype's speed. But the judgment calls that define each archetype — what ideas to pursue, what to build, what to simplify, which market signals to follow, what to keep stable — those remain stubbornly human. In the AI age, that judgment is the work that matters most.
