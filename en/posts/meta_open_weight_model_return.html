Getting the weights is not getting the source - how to actually judge an open AI model

What separates open from closed is not the announcement. It is the license text and the operating bill.

Intro: what a downloadable file does not tell you

If you are weighing whether to host a model in-house, most of the information reaching you right now is organised along the wrong axis. There is no shortage of coverage on which model scored what and which company opened which thing. But the thing that blocks or clears an adoption is not the score. It is the few lines of contract attached to the weight file.

On 10 August 2026 Meta swung back toward open model releases. The company said it would publish weights including its most capable model, and introduced a new family aimed at running on laptops. The same announcement carried arguments directed at rivals who keep their models closed. As news, that is the whole story. For anyone doing the work, the real questions start after that. Can we use the model that was opened, how far can we use it, and what do we take on in exchange?

The answers live in the license file and the operating budget, not in the announcement. And they differ company by company. The same model can be immediately adoptable at one firm and a negotiation item at another. That is why one release produces conflicting conclusions.

This article is not a spec sheet for any individual model. It covers what the open-versus-closed framing actually divides, where licenses draw their lines, and which organisations should pick which side given those lines. Individual releases are covered separately in GLM 5.2, Kanana-2 lightweight models and LongCat-2.0, and the regulatory fight over openness itself is in the open weight regulation debate.

1. What is settled and what is not

Separate the facts first. Mixing what is confirmed with what is still unknown is how adoption decisions go wrong.

1.1 What reporting confirms

  • On 10 August 2026 Meta stated a policy of publishing weights, including its most capable model
  • A separate open family aimed at laptop-class hardware was announced alongside it
  • The announcement took the form of a roughly 6,500-word essay arguing that concentrating the technology in a few companies is itself a risk
  • The line about distributing superintelligence rather than centralising it was widely quoted
  • It also argued that US labs face more constraints on training data while overseas competitors face fewer
  • Reporting from late 2025 described a prior move toward a closed, revenue-oriented direction

1.2 What is not yet documented

Almost everything an adoption decision actually needs sits in this list.

  • The full license text — the contract terms decide adoptability outright
  • Parameter counts and benchmarks — not confirmed as of the announcement
  • Actual weight publication dates — stating a policy and shipping files can be different days
  • Whether data information and training code are released — the deciding items for the open source test below

That is why this article names no model specs. What is settled is a direction; the conditions arrive with the documents. Until then there is exactly one thing you can decide in advance: which conditions would clear your company and which would block it.

2. Where open source and open weights diverge

These two get used interchangeably in conversation, but they sit at different layers by definition. What matters is exactly where that gap bites in practice.

2.1 The three things OSI requires

The Open Source Initiative's Open Source AI Definition spells out what must be released for the four freedoms to be real. Those freedoms are to use the system for any purpose and without having to ask for permission, to study how it works and inspect its components, to modify it for any purpose including changing its output, and to share it for others to use with or without modifications. To exercise them, the definition names three components.

  • Data Information — sufficiently detailed information about the training data so that a skilled person can build a substantially equivalent system
  • Code — the complete source code used to train and run the system, representing the full specification of how data was processed and filtered and how training was done
  • Parameters — the model weights and other configuration settings

Most open releases hand over only the third. The consequence is concrete: you can audit the product but not the ingredients. When a model answers a particular class of question strangely, weights let you observe the behaviour but not trace the cause back to the training data. Bias audits, copyright dispute responses and regulatory filings all hit the same wall.

2.2 The common mistake - does a permissive license make it open source?

No, and this is where sources contradict each other most often. Shipping weights under Apache 2.0 does not satisfy OSAID if the training code and data information are missing. The definition asks what you released, not what the license is called.

The practical conclusion, though, runs in the opposite direction. Weights under a permissive license are the easiest to adopt even when they fail the definition. Commercial use, redistribution and derivative release carry almost no extra conditions, so legal review is short. Meanwhile a model that comes closer to the definition by publishing data and code can still be harder to adopt if its usage restrictions are dense.

So track two axes separately. One is the depth of openness (what you received), the other is freedom of use (what you may do). Collapse them into the single word open and your evaluation is misaligned from the first step.

3. What actually changes by license type

Model releases in circulation sort into roughly four types. Compared on the same criteria, here is what each permits and blocks.

Two things to read out of that table. First, the heaviest legal review is not the closed option but the middle ground of vendor community licenses. Pure permissive terms are short and closed APIs are standard boilerplate, but a license a vendor drafted itself has to be matched clause by clause against your own circumstances. Published models really do arrive with structures where running your own service is fine but providing to third parties requires a separate agreement; one worked example at clause level is in the Kanana-2 license breakdown.

Second, OSAID compliance correlates weakly with ease of adoption. The definition asks about reproducibility and auditability; adoption difficulty asks about usage restrictions. Different questions, different answers.

4. The five lines that block adoption

If you are reviewing a model license for the first time, find these five items before reading the whole document. Nearly every blocked adoption traces back to one of them.

4.1 Is there a threshold on commercial use

Some licenses push you into a separate negotiation once user count or revenue crosses a line. It is invisible while the company is small and binds after it grows, so read it against your three-year position, not today's.

4.2 Third-party provision or internal use

This is where most companies get caught. Calling a model inside your own service and selling a product with the model embedded are frequently treated as different acts. Systems integrators, packaged software vendors and device makers all split here. The boundary is not always crisp either. If your own service exposes an API and customers call it, is that internal use or reselling model access? There are genuinely undecidable stretches in the text.

4.3 What redistribution requires

  • Include a copy of the license
  • Mark modified files as changed
  • Preserve copyright and attribution notices
  • Pass the same obligations to recipients

Notice obligations survive even in permissive licenses. Free does not mean obligation-free.

4.4 Derivative naming rules

Some licenses require a specific prefix or phrase in the name of fine-tuned outputs. It looks trivial and it lands directly on your product name and marketing copy. Irrelevant if the model stays internal; a trademark conversation if you plan to publish derivatives.

4.5 May you train other models on the outputs

The distillation clause. Quietest item, largest blast radius. Building a small model from a large model's outputs has been standard practice for a long time, and a clause closing that path changes the entire cost-optimisation design. Closed API terms of service commonly carry equivalent language.

5. Four things openness actually buys you

Measuring the value of open models by benchmark score usually produces the wrong conclusion. Closed models still lead in certain bands, and where that gap currently sits is covered in the open versus closed capability gap. The value of openness is elsewhere.

5.1 On-premise deployment

With weights in hand you can run on your own hardware. The key point is that this is not a performance argument. For organisations whose data cannot leave the perimeter, a closed API is not a benchmark competitor, it is simply absent from the candidate list. Situations where no comparison table is needed do exist.

5.2 Inference without data egress

When inference terminates inside your network, there is less to write into processing agreements, less need to establish a transfer basis, and less vendor retention policy to review. The real gain is that the number of items you must explain in a security review goes down.

5.3 Fine-tuning

  • Internal terminology, document formats and business rules can be moved inside the model
  • Long instructions no longer ride along on every call, which lowers token cost
  • On narrow tasks this creates bands where a small model beats a large one
  • With an adapter-swap approach you can maintain several task-specific variants at once

Note that permission to fine-tune and permission to distribute the derivative are separate clauses. Internal use only, with distribution subject to negotiation, is a real pattern.

5.4 Cost predictability

This is the most underrated item. Closed API cost scales with call volume at a unit price the vendor sets. Self-hosted cost is bound to hardware and headcount as a fixed line that barely moves when volume rises. At low volume the API is dramatically cheaper; past some crossover point the ordering flips.

Variance matters more than the average. For anyone building a budget, the deciding number is what the worst month costs, not the mean. Self-hosting has a ceiling enforced physically by hardware, and it removes external variables like price increases and model deprecation. The flip side is that the same ceiling becomes a queue when traffic spikes.

6. The three invoices openness sends

Any article recommending open models without this section is half-written. The weights are free; the operation is not.

Item Closed API Open, self-hosted When it surfaces
Availability Vendor SLA Your on-call. Incident staffing required The first overnight outage
Version management Vendor updates and deprecates You decide, and you verify When a security patch lands
Safety filtering Included Built by you. Base weights lack much of it When you open it to external users
Abuse response Vendor policy and blocking Your policy, your logs When prompt injection attempts arrive
Liability Terms allocate it somehow Usually as-is. Distributor disclaimers are standard When a bad output reaches a customer
Capability drift Vendor improves it It stands still. Upgrades are migrations Six months later, next to a competitor

6.1 Safety tooling is not a bundled feature

Published weights generally carry safety alignment, but service-grade defence sits outside the model. Input filtering, output checking, rate limiting, logging and audit trails, and a reporting workflow are all yours to build. With a closed API that layer was folded into the price and therefore invisible. For an internal tool the burden is small; the moment external users are involved, that layer is the service quality.

6.2 Where liability lands

Open model licenses generally disclaim warranties and exclude liability. In other words problems caused by outputs land on the operator, not the distributor. Closed APIs do not take on much liability either, but there is at least a counterparty and a written allocation. For an organisation that has to produce compliance documentation, that difference changes the shape of the paperwork.

6.3 Capability freezes at adoption time

With a closed API, vendor improvements lift your service for free. Self-hosting does not work that way. Your model stays where it is until you run a migration. That is both a weakness and a strength, because it also means behaviour does not change under you overnight. Anyone who has operated a pipeline that depends on output formatting knows what that stability is worth.

7. What open weights mean under regulation

This is where openness stops being a preference and becomes a feasibility question for a large set of organisations.

7.1 When data cannot leave, there is no comparison table

Finance, public sector, healthcare and defence commonly operate under internal rules or external requirements that forbid sending certain data to outside systems. In a network-segregated environment it gets simpler still. An external API does not enter the candidate pool regardless of how good it is. For these organisations open models are not an alternative, they are the only path.

The order of evaluation inverts as a result. Most teams measure capability, then cost, then decide on deployment. These teams have deployment fixed first and measure capability only within it. Comparison material written without knowing that ordering is mostly unusable to them.

7.2 The EU AI Act draws its line at obligations, not at openness

Article 53 of the EU AI Act imposes four obligations on providers of general-purpose AI models: keep technical documentation, give downstream providers information on capabilities and limitations, put in place a policy to comply with EU copyright law including the Article 4(3) reservations of Directive 2019/790, and publish a sufficiently detailed summary about the content used for training. The text waives only the first two for models released under a free and open-source licence that allows access, usage, modification and distribution, with parameters and architecture information publicly available. The copyright policy and the training content summary stay. And the article states that this exception shall not apply to general-purpose AI models with systemic risks.

The design principle is worth naming: openness earns a bounded set of waivers, not an exemption from the regime. Anyone building a compliance narrative around we published the weights, therefore we are outside scope should read the last sentence of that article first.

7.3 Language and tokenizer efficiency is a separate axis

A model that leads on English benchmarks does not automatically lead on your language. Tokenizer efficiency in particular never shows up in a score while it directly changes monthly cost and effective context headroom. Running 100 of your own documents through each candidate's tokenizer and counting tokens tells you more about the adoption decision than reading a leaderboard.

7.4 How other jurisdictions handle it

China draws a different line. The Interim Measures for the Management of Generative AI Services took effect on 15 August 2023. Article 2 limits scope to services providing generated content to the public within China, and states that enterprises, research and educational institutions that develop or apply the technology without providing services to the domestic public fall outside the measures. Article 17 requires a security assessment and algorithm filing for services with public-opinion attributes or social mobilisation capacity. The dividing line is public service provision, not openness. Japan runs on soft law: the AI Business Operator Guidelines from the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications and METI reached version 1.0 in April 2024 and version 1.2 in March 2026, and are explicitly non-binding.

8. Putting the incentive structures side by side

There is no reason to take either camp's arguments at face value. Rather than judging claims by the speaker's sincerity, map what is profitable from each position and you can decide how much to discount which argument.

Position Where revenue comes from Structurally favourable conclusion Where the argument is strongest
API-first model vendors Token pricing and subscriptions Keep model access inside their infrastructure The real difficulty of safety control and abuse response
Open-releasing platforms Ads, devices, platforms - outside the model Commoditise the model layer Dependency and pricing risk from concentration
Cloud providers Compute and managed services Either way, traffic runs on their platform The point that operating burden is real
Regulators Not applicable A structure where responsibility can be attributed The need for a remedy path after an incident
Adopting organisations Their actual business Low switching cost They belong to neither camp

Here is how to read it. Both camps' arguments point in the same direction as their own business models. That does not invalidate the arguments. The claim that safety control is hard and the claim that concentration is dangerous both deserve examination on the merits. It means the strength of a claim should be measured by the evidence offered, not by the confidence of the person offering it.

And the practically important part is that the adopting organisation is on neither side. Whether open or closed prevails, what you need is a structure that survives either outcome. Code deeply optimised for self-hosted serving cannot go back to a closed API, and a pipeline built around one API's response format cannot move to self-hosting. Lock-in is a design problem, not a camp problem.

9. The decision - which side, and when

Treated as a binary, this question usually yields the wrong answer. What settles in practice is a segment-by-segment mix.

9.1 Conditions favouring open self-hosting

  • Data cannot leave the perimeter — this one condition settles it
  • Monthly call volume is high enough that unit price dominates total cost
  • The task is narrow and repetitive, so fine-tuning pays off
  • Latency governs usability and the network round trip has to go
  • A pipeline depends on output formats that must not shift unannounced
  • GPUs and operations staff already exist or are firmly budgeted

9.2 Conditions favouring a closed API

  • A small number of tasks need frontier capability and they are the product
  • Call volume is still small or hard to forecast
  • There is no operations headcount and no room to build an on-call rota
  • You want safety filtering and abuse response handled externally
  • Product direction shifts often and models get swapped frequently
  • You are still validating and it is the wrong time to create fixed cost

9.3 What the mix looks like in practice

The arrangement that most often settles looks like this.

  1. High-volume structured work runs on an in-house open model: classification, tagging, extraction, normalisation, first-pass filtering
  2. The hard minority of requests escalates to a closed API, often landing somewhere around 5 to 15 percent of calls
  3. Sensitive data paths are separated entirely so that external calls are physically impossible
  4. The model call site stays behind an interface so either direction remains available

The value of this arrangement is less about cost saving and more about negotiating position. The mere fact that you can move is your defence against price increases and deprecations.

9.4 Five things to check today

  1. Pull the full license for each candidate and match it against the five lines in section 4
  2. Get a one-line answer from legal on whether your business form counts as third-party provision
  3. Run 100 of your own documents through each candidate tokenizer and count tokens
  4. Cost out current monthly volume and a 3x scenario for both API and self-hosting
  5. Build a 50-question evaluation set from your own data. It is an asset you reuse on every model change

10. What could not be verified

Items where the evidence was not sufficient, collected in one place rather than hedged throughout.

  • Full license text for the models covered by the announcement — terms unconfirmed, so they are not given a row in the table
  • Parameter counts, benchmarks and exact weight publication dates — not confirmed as of the announcement
  • Whether data information and training code are released — the deciding items for OSAID, still unknown
  • How Korean regulation treats open weights — implementing documents were not reviewed directly, so only foreign regimes are cited
  • Individual provisions on openness in Japan's AI Business Operator Guidelines — version history and the non-binding soft law character were confirmed, but clause-level treatment of open weights was not checked
  • The escalation ratio in mixed deployments — the 5 to 15 percent figure is a common design starting point, not a measured industry average

Conclusion: openness is a bundle of conditions, not a state

Open versus closed gets discussed as a matter of camps, but from the adopting side it is not a camp, it is a bundle of conditions. What did you receive, what may you do with it, what do you take on in exchange. Because that combination lands differently at every company, the same model is the right answer at one firm and the wrong one at another.

So when a new model release crosses your feed, the first file to open is the license, not the benchmark table. Capability can be changed later; contract terms cannot. Finishing capability validation and then getting blocked by legal spends time and credibility at once.

Two things you can do today. First, check whether your current model call site sits behind an interface. If it does not, you cannot move no matter which camp wins. Second, build a 50-question evaluation set from your own data. With that file you can choose by your own criteria instead of someone else's benchmark. However the open-versus-closed picture moves from here, organisations holding those two things receive that movement as a choice, and everyone else receives it as a notification.
