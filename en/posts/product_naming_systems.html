Introduction: when you suddenly need thousands of names a year

With five products you do not need naming rules. Argue for an hour in a meeting and pick the one you like. The problem is that this method collapses abruptly at some point, and it always collapses the same way. New names start colliding with old ones, the word used in the warehouse drifts from the word on the product page, and support has to ask three times before anyone knows which item is being discussed.

The story that IKEA puts Swedish place names on sofas and men's names on bookshelves gets told as brand folklore. But the background the company states on its own help page is not sentimental. The founder had a hard time remembering numbers, so products got names, and the same page says the company creates roughly 2,000 to 3,000 new products every year. At that scale, naming is not creative work. It is a process.

This article is about what changes when you have a system for naming, what types of system exist, and where each of them breaks. The range runs from consumer product names to software version numbers and codenames. They look like distant worlds, but the problem underneath is the same one.

The four jobs a name actually does

When choosing a name, most people ask whether it is a good name. But a name does four separate jobs, and the four demand different things. That means a single scale cannot decide it.

  • Identification — two different objects must not answer to the same word. If that is all you need, a serial number is the cheapest option.
  • Memory and speech — it has to be memorable, sayable over the phone, and audible shouted across a warehouse. Serial numbers collapse right here.
  • Search — the string a customer types when they come back has to belong to you. This is where common nouns pay.
  • Inventory and ordering — the layer logistics, accounting and order systems handle. Here it is codes that work, not names.

Trying to satisfy all four with a single string is where most naming accidents begin. The design that actually works splits the layers. Leave the identifier the machines use alone, and put a human-facing name on top of it. The recollection that the founder could not remember numbers does not mean the numbers went away. It means a human layer was added above them.

Miss that distinction and two mistakes follow. One is pushing specification data into the human-facing name until it reads like XT-4200-B-KR. The other is running inventory on names alone with no code system, so the day a name changes you lose your history. Names can change; codes must not. Put both in the same string and one of them gets sacrificed.

What the IKEA rules actually tell you

What the published IKEA help page says is shorter and more concrete than you would expect. Most of it is not taste. It is conditions for filtering candidates mechanically.

  • Each category has its own dictionary. The examples the page gives are Swedish place names for sofas, men's names for bookshelves, and words drawn from animals and nature for children's products.
  • The name must be a real word and between 4 and 12 letters long.
  • It should preferably contain one of the letters Å, Ä or Ö.
  • It has to feel good to say out loud.
  • It must never be an existing trademark or a surname.

The line worth staring at is the last one. The ones above it are style; that one is a collision check. The company also states that every possible name is checked for undesirable meanings in other languages and for political or religious associations. Half the rulebook is about which names are good and the other half is about which names cause accidents.

The 4 to 12 letter limit has a reason too. Short strings collide with other meanings easily and are more likely to be already claimed as trademarks. Long ones are hard to say and get truncated on shelf labels and in web listings. That band is less aesthetics than a decision about the operable range. The preference for Å, Ä and Ö reads the same way: those letters are uncommon outside Swedish, which by itself lowers the odds of colliding with someone else's mark.

Copying the rules verbatim would be pointless, though. What transfers is not Swedish geography but the structure: fix a dictionary per category, write the format conditions as numbers, and put the collision check inside the process. All three are available today to a company with ten products.

Four types of naming system

Naming in practice splits four ways. The split partly overlaps with the distinctiveness spectrum in trademark law. US trademark practice sorts marks into generic, descriptive, suggestive, arbitrary and fanciful, with protection strengthening toward the end. The two classifications are not identical, but the direction is: the more a name describes the product, the weaker it is, and the more meaningless, the stronger.

Real companies mix all four: an arbitrary brand, a series product line, a numeric generation, descriptive options. That combination works fine as long as the layers stay separate. The trouble starts when two systems share one layer. If some models in a line carry names and others carry numbers, there is no way to read the ranking.

Series naming starts with the dictionary

The point of series naming is not choosing a name but building the list you choose from. Place names, constellations, Greek gods, whatever: the size of that list is the number of products the system can carry. A company shipping thousands a year needs a list in the tens of thousands, which is why categories that effectively never run out get picked.

Fixing a dictionary buys three things. Duplicate checking becomes mechanical (compare the list against usage history and you are done), the category itself carries product line information, and because the rule is explicit the decision can be delegated to whoever is doing the work.

The cost is equally clear. The words on that list are not words you made. They already mean something, they mean something else in another language, and somebody may hold them as a trademark. That is why the IKEA rules contain the line about existing trademarks and surnames. A large dictionary means many candidates, not safe ones.

When a dictionary starts running dry the symptoms come first. Candidate meetings get longer, strained variants appear, and someone proposes reusing a discontinued name. That last one is the dangerous one: a revived name comes with old reviews, old support tickets and old inventory data attached. How much room is left is a number you can count, so it belongs on your dashboard.

The two ways numbering collapses

Numbering looks like the cheapest and most precise system. Order comes for free and it never runs out. And yet it collapses in two ways.

The first is skipping. When a digit expresses only order there is no reason to skip one. The problem is that in the market the digit sits next to a competitor's digit. At that moment the number stops being order and becomes a signal of new versus old, and signals invite adjustment.

  • Samsung skipped 6 in the Galaxy Note line in 2016 and shipped the 7. It was reported as a decision to match the Galaxy S7 of the same year so the device would not look a generation behind.
  • Microsoft shipped 10 after Windows 8. The widely circulated explanation is legacy code that identified old releases by the leading characters of a version string, but that is not the reason the company put forward publicly. Its stated grounds were branding and the scale of the platform shift.
  • For the Xbox 360, a former head of the business later recalled in an interview that avoiding the look of being a generation behind the PlayStation 3 was part of the thinking.
  • PHP put the next major version to a vote and settled on 7 after the abandoned 6 effort, because that name already lived in books and documentation and would cause confusion.

The four reasons are entirely different: aligning a sibling line, a brand shift, competitive perception, a collision with an old name. The result is the same. People keep looking for the number that is missing. Articles asking what happened to it stay in search for years, and the company repeats the explanation every time. The cost of a skip is not paid once; it keeps being paid.

The second is stacking several axes onto one digit. Put generation, tier and size in the same number and the reader cannot tell which axis moved. Numbering also has an irreversible property: you can never lower a number once raised, and because bigger reads as better you cannot introduce a smaller one either. That is why, when a line's numbers fall out of step with a rival's, skipping is the only way to resynchronize.

The test is simple. A number used as a marketing signal gets skipped; a number used as a promise does not. The institutional form of the second one is semantic versioning, which the next section covers.

Names break when they cross languages

That a name reads differently in another language is well known. What is less known is where it breaks. Accidents happen in slang and pronunciation, not in the dictionary entry, and a dictionary lookup cannot see that layer.

A few cases are confirmed in public reporting. Mitsubishi's four-wheel-drive model was sold as Montero in Spain and most of the Americas and as Shogun in the United Kingdom, because the original name reads as vulgar slang in Spanish. Honda prepared a small car as the Fitta, confirmed the word is obscene in Nordic languages, and launched it in 2001 as Jazz in Europe and Asia and Fit in the United States. In both cases the company caught it in advance. They are consumed as cautionary tales, but they are actually records of a check working.

Conversely, the most famous failure story of all is not true. The tale that an American car sold badly in Spanish-speaking markets because its name reads as does not go has circulated for decades, but according to the fact-checking outlet Snopes the car sold well in Mexico and Venezuela, with Venezuelan figures exceeding the company's expectations. The two phrases are pronounced differently, and the manufacturer knew the meaning and kept the name anyway. The habit naming work actually requires is checking a case before citing it.

Language problems do not only come from obscenity. Android dropping dessert names from its public version labels at 10 is the example. The reasons Google gave were that L and R are not distinguishable when spoken in some languages, and that some desserts are unfamiliar in parts of the world or not even considered desserts there. A name with no offensive meaning still breaks if it simply does not land. What happened next is the more interesting part: the public label switched to numbers while the internal codenames kept the dessert tradition. The layer people say internally and the layer that goes outside were separated.

The cost of a name nobody can search

Once search became the default route to a product, names picked up one more condition: can you own that string? A pleasant common noun does badly on this test. You have to share the results page with the original meaning, and the more common the meaning the smaller your share.

The Go programming language is the case people cite. The official name is Go, yet search in practice runs on golang, an alias that came from the old golang.org domain and stuck because a common English word is hard to look up. The name ended up being two names. That is less a failure than the classic price of choosing a plain word. If the community builds you an alias you are lucky; if not, you simply lose the traffic.

  • Common noun — understood immediately. In exchange it competes with other meanings in search and is hard to protect as a mark.
  • An existing word used arbitrarily — a real word unrelated to the product. Easy to say while still reasonably distinctive.
  • An invented word — best in search. In exchange you have to spend separately to make the word known.

The check is easy. Search the candidate as written and look at what fills the first page. If a strong owner is already there, the cost of taking that spot is the real price of the name.

Software names: versions and codenames

Software attaches three layers of name to the same thing, and the three promise different things. Projects that keep the layers apart have fewer naming problems; projects that blur them explain themselves again at every release.

LayerWhat it promisesTypical formWatch out for
Version number Compatibility. Machines read it MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH Break the rule and the number carries no information at all
Codename Identifies a target in development. People say it A series dictionary, alphabetical If it leaks it competes with the official name
Release name Announces a generation. The market reads it Year, generation number, brand Marketing pressure lands here, and so does skipping

Semantic versioning is the institutional form of the version number layer. Raise MAJOR when compatibility breaks, MINOR when you add functionality compatibly, PATCH for compatible fixes. The stated purpose of the spec is avoiding dependency hell. Specify dependencies too tightly and you fall into version lock, where upgrading one thing forces new releases of everything attached to it; too loosely and you fall into version promiscuity, assuming compatibility with versions that do not exist yet.

The test from the previous section reappears here. A semantic version number is not a signal but a contract, and a contract cannot be skipped. Release-name numbers, by contrast, are signals the market reads, so skipping keeps happening there. Two layers moving differently is not a contradiction but the normal case; the trouble comes when one digit is asked to do both jobs.

The codename layer has textbook series examples. Debian takes release names from characters in a Pixar feature, a convention that began because the person then leading the project worked at Pixar, with the first codenamed release in 1996. Ubuntu pairs an adjective with an animal in alliteration and walks the alphabet. Both have large dictionaries, encode order in the rule so the next name is predictable, and stay memorable enough to say out loud. Commit message conventions and log level conventions are working on the same problem.

Rules a small team can write today

A team with ten products does not need to imitate a system built for thousands. But a few decisions that are expensive to reverse are worth making now.

  1. Split the layers first. Separate the code machines use from the name people say, and declare that the code never changes.
  2. Fix a dictionary per category. Decide what kind of words each product line draws on, put the candidate list in a file, and count it to see how many years it covers.
  3. Write the format conditions as numbers. Length range, allowed characters, forbidden patterns. The less judgment a rule needs, the longer it survives.
  4. Put collision checks in the process. Internal duplicates, trademark search, domains and account handles, and a native speaker of every target market reading it aloud. Skip one and that is where the accident lands.
  5. Leave room to grow. Design five categories even if you have three product lines, and if you use numbers, pick exactly one axis for the digit to carry.

Trademark is a separate axis

Everything above is operations; trademark is its own axis. Two things are worth knowing. First, the more plainly a name describes the product the harder it is to defend legally, and coined words are the strongest, which points the same direction as the type table above. Second, include trademark search when narrowing candidates, but leave registrability to a professional. The same string produces different outcomes depending on the class of goods designated, and similarity is not judged by comparing strings. Filtering out obvious collisions through search is the part you can do yourself.

What I could not verify

Beyond what appears on the published IKEA help page, the company's naming rules could not be confirmed. Which dictionaries apply to other categories has been covered by various outlets, but only the three the company states directly are cited here. Internal figures such as dictionary size and candidate rejection rates are not published.

The legacy version-string check widely cited as the reason Windows 8 was followed by 10 is not the company's official explanation. It spread from developer communities, and the reasons the company gave publicly were branding and the platform shift. The Xbox 360 naming background likewise rests on reporting that quotes a retrospective interview, not on a company document.

No material was found measuring what number skipping actually did to sales or awareness. What the companies stated was intent, not outcome. The axis on which a name changes judgment itself is handled separately in Disease Names Change What Patients Choose.

Conclusion

Treat naming as creative work and it becomes a bottleneck the moment the catalog grows. Treat it as a system and the bottleneck disappears, leaving a handful of design decisions: which dictionary, what format conditions, which collisions to check, what the digit carries. Settle those four and naming meetings get short and the decision gets delegated.

None of the four types is the right answer, but there is an order for choosing. Split the layers first. If you do not separate machine codes from human names, every remaining decision becomes a compromise. Then pick one thing to buy at the human layer. Immediate comprehension points to descriptive; owning search and trademark points to arbitrary; producing many names consistently points to series. Numbers are safer as an axis laid on top of a name than as a replacement for one.

One last judgment about digits. A number used as a signal will eventually be skipped; a number used as a promise has no reason to be. Which one you are building gets decided the moment you put a number on your first product.
