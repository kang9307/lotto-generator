Stalking Response Guide: From Reporting to Evidence and Legal Action

Definitions, immediate safety steps, evidence, Korea's Stalking Punishment Act, and digital stalking defense in one guide

2026-07-20 Law & Legal
⚠️ Legal information notice — This article is for general information only and is not legal advice. Consult a lawyer for your specific case. Statutes described here are those of the Republic of Korea and may change.

Introduction: You Don't Have to Face Stalking Alone

If you are in immediate physical danger, stop reading and call your local emergency number right now (112 in Korea). If you cannot speak safely, use a text-based emergency reporting option where available. Your safety comes first.

Stalking rarely feels like a single event. It can start small - one extra text, one uncomfortable glance - and slowly take over daily life. Many victims second-guess themselves, wondering if reporting it would be an overreaction, or if they're just being sensitive. If someone's behavior makes you feel afraid or unsafe, that alone is reason enough to act.

This guide walks through what stalking is, how to secure your immediate safety, how to collect evidence that can hold up legally, how Korea's Stalking Punishment Act handles reporting and protective measures, how to defend against digital stalking, and where to find support - current as of July 2026. Whatever is happening, it is not your fault.

1. What Is Stalking - Definitions, Types, and Warning Signs

1.1 The Legal Definition

In Korea, the Act on the Punishment of Stalking Crimes (commonly called the Stalking Punishment Act) has been in effect since October 21, 2021. It defines stalking behavior as acts committed against a person's will, without justifiable reason, that cause fear or anxiety, including:

  • Approaching, following, or blocking someone's path
  • Waiting near or watching someone's home, workplace, or school
  • Repeatedly sending messages, calls, or images through phone, text, email, or social media
  • Delivering or leaving unwanted items directly or through a third party
  • Damaging property near a person's residence

When these acts occur persistently or repeatedly, they legally constitute a stalking crime - a single, isolated incident is treated differently from an ongoing pattern.

1.2 Types of Stalking Behavior

Stalking is not limited to physical following. It commonly appears as:

  • Physical stalking: waiting outside your home, workplace, or commute route; following; unwanted approaches
  • Communication-based stalking: repeated contact from new numbers or accounts after being blocked; unwanted gifts or letters
  • Digital and online stalking: monitoring social media, tracking location, attempting to access accounts
  • Third-party stalking: pressuring friends or family for information about your whereabouts

1.3 Warning Signs the Situation Is Escalating

Watch for a sudden increase in contact frequency, continued contact after you have clearly said no, the person knowing private details you never shared, or threatening language. If any of these appear, don't wait - move directly to the safety steps in the next section.

2. Securing Immediate Safety

2.1 If It's an Emergency, Call Now

If you face a physical threat or the person is nearby, don't hesitate - call your local emergency line immediately (112 in Korea). If speaking isn't safe, use a text-based emergency reporting option where available. Police can respond on-site with immediate protective steps, including stopping the behavior and connecting you to counseling or shelter services.

2.2 Build Your Own Safety Plan

Even without an active emergency, preparing in advance speeds up your response later. Vary your routes and routines, and know the exits and safe spots at places you frequent. Keep your phone charged, and save emergency contacts - family, police, a hotline - as speed-dial numbers. Review the security of your home and workplace, including locks, lighting, and entry points.

2.3 Tell People You Trust

Keeping stalking secret only delays help. Let family, close friends, or a manager at work know what's happening so they can watch for warning signs and support you in a crisis. Ask someone to accompany you on routine trips when possible, and build the habit of immediately telling someone nearby if a stranger approaches. Asking for help is not weakness - it is one of the most effective ways to protect yourself.

3. Collecting Evidence for Later

3.1 What and How to Record

Because stalking cases hinge on proving a pattern, documenting incidents as they happen matters. Keep a log noting the date, time, location, what happened, and any witnesses. Memory fades, but records don't. A notes app or a physical notebook both work - consistency and factual detail are what count.

3.2 The Legal Boundaries of Evidence Collection

Only legally obtained evidence holds up in court. Recording a call or conversation you are personally part of is legal in Korea and can be used as evidence. However, accessing someone's phone or social media accounts without authorization, secretly recording a conversation you are not part of, or planting a GPS tracker or hidden camera is itself a crime. Never attempt this to get evidence, since it can turn you into the one facing charges.

3.3 Screenshots, Call Logs, and Witnesses

When capturing messages, make sure the date, time, and the other person's account name or profile are visible in the screenshot. Keep original texts, DMs, emails, and missed-call logs rather than deleting them. Photograph unwanted gifts, threatening notes, or damaged property, and keep the physical items if possible. If CCTV or dashcam footage exists near your home or workplace, request it before it's overwritten, and collect contact information and statements from any witnesses. A clear record of you saying stop contacting me is especially valuable, since it helps prove the contact was against your will.

4. Legal Response - Centered on Korea's Stalking Punishment Act

4.1 How to Report

In Korea, you can report stalking by calling 112 (police), visiting a local police station, or contacting the Women's Emergency Hotline 1366, available 24/7. If speaking isn't safe, use text-based emergency reporting. After a report, your statement and any evidence you've gathered are used to open a case, and protective measures - described below - can be applied in stages.

4.2 Emergency, Urgent, and Provisional Measures

Korea's protective system has three tiers.

  • Emergency measures: taken on the spot by responding officers - stopping the behavior, issuing a warning about future punishment, notifying the offender to cease contact, or referring the victim to counseling or shelter services.
  • Urgent emergency measures: if repeat behavior is likely and prevention is urgent, police can independently order, for up to one month, a no-approach zone within 100 meters of the victim's or family's residence, and a ban on contact via telecommunication.
  • Provisional measures: ordered by a court at a prosecutor's request, combining any of a written warning, a 100-meter no-approach order, a telecommunication contact ban, an electronic location-tracking device, or detention at a police holding facility or detention center. No-approach and tracking-device orders last up to three months and can be extended twice, for three months each; detention is capped at one month.

Violating these measures carries its own penalties: violating a no-approach or telecommunication ban under a provisional measure can mean up to 2 years in prison or a fine of up to 20 million won, and violating an urgent emergency measure can mean up to 1 year in prison or a fine of up to 10 million won.

4.3 Penalties and the End of the No-Prosecution-Without-Complaint Rule

A stalking crime itself is punishable by up to 3 years in prison or a fine of up to 30 million won, rising to up to 5 years or a fine of up to 50 million won if a weapon or dangerous item was carried or used. A key amendment took effect on July 11, 2023: Korea abolished the rule that let a case be dropped simply because the victim withdrew their complaint or said they didn't want punishment. Now, prosecutors can continue investigating and prosecuting based on the severity of the case and the public interest - a change meant to stop offenders from pressuring victims into withdrawing complaints. Separately, a new victim protection order system, letting victims petition courts directly for a no-approach order, is scheduled to take effect in April 2027.

4.4 General Principles for Readers Outside Korea

If you live outside Korea, the specific laws and terminology differ, but the basic framework is similar. In an emergency, call your local emergency number (911 in the US, 112 across the EU, 110 in Japan, 110 in mainland China). Most countries offer some form of restraining or protective order for stalking and harassment. It's worth looking up your local domestic violence or stalking hotline and legal aid organization in advance, so you know exactly who to call if you need help.

5. Defending Against Digital Stalking

5.1 Warning Signs and Checks

Unusually fast battery drain, a sudden spike in data usage, or an unfamiliar app you don't remember installing can all be signs of location-tracking software, sometimes called stalkerware. Review your phone's location-sharing settings and any family-sharing apps, and check your account login history for unfamiliar devices. If you find something suspicious, it's often safer to document it with screenshots and consult police or a specialist rather than deleting it immediately, since it may serve as evidence.

5.2 Strengthening Account and Device Security

Change passwords on your important accounts and turn on two-factor authentication (2FA). Check your active sessions and connected devices, and log out anything unfamiliar immediately. Set social media profiles to private, and turn off real-time location sharing and photo GPS metadata. If the person may know your account recovery details, such as your phone number or backup email, update those too.

5.3 Reporting Online Harassment and Cyberstalking

Repeated harassment through social media or messaging apps, spreading false information, or distributing photos and videos without consent can each be a separate crime on their own. Use the reporting and blocking tools on the platform, and for serious cases, contact a cybercrime unit or your local police and hotline. As noted above, avoid trying to fight back by accessing the other person's accounts without authorization, since that risks making you the one facing charges.

6. Support Organizations and Counseling

Key resources in Korea
112 (police emergency) · Women's Emergency Hotline 1366 (24/7 counseling and emergency protection) · Safe-Dream, safe182.go.kr (stalking and violence support information) · Smart Citizen Report app (text-based emergency reporting)

6.1 Key Resources in Korea

The Women's Emergency Hotline 1366 connects nationwide, 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, offering counseling, crisis intervention, and referrals to emergency shelters. Counseling can be anonymous, and cases can be connected to legal, medical, or investigative support as needed. Emergency housing, up to 30 days, and temporary housing, up to 6 months, are available at no cost. Regional stalking and dating-violence victim support centers, along with the Korea Legal Aid Corporation for free legal counseling, are additional resources worth using.

6.2 General Guidance for Readers Outside Korea

If you're reading this from outside Korea, take time now to look up and save your country's domestic violence and stalking hotline, a local police non-emergency line, and a nearby legal aid organization. Many countries also offer dedicated stalking helplines or online counseling channels, and embassies or consulates can sometimes point citizens abroad toward local support resources.

7. Psychological Recovery and Support From Others

Stalking leaves more than a physical threat - it often causes ongoing anxiety, sleep problems, and withdrawal from relationships. These reactions are normal, not a sign of weakness. Don't hesitate to reach out to a counseling service or therapist. Hotlines like 1366 and regional victim support centers can connect you to free psychological counseling.

If someone you know is going through this, the most helpful thing you can do is believe them without question and ask what kind of support they actually want, rather than questioning why they didn't speak up sooner. Recovery isn't a straight line - it moves forward and back. Take the time and support you need, at your own pace.

Conclusion: Three Things You Can Do Today

Responding to stalking isn't a single action - it's an ongoing process of staying safe, documenting what happens, pursuing legal protection, and recovering emotionally. Here are three steps you can start today.

First, if it's an emergency, call 112 in Korea or your local equivalent without hesitation; if not, start building your own safety plan. Second, begin logging incidents with dates and times, and gather evidence through legal means only. Third, don't carry this alone - reach out to a hotline like 1366, your local police, or someone you trust. Korea's protective laws keep strengthening, and more channels for help keep opening up. You are not alone.
