What was measured - 350,000 drives and a quarterly tally

Backblaze publishes failure statistics every quarter for the hard drives it uses to store data in its data centers. According to the Q2 2026 post, it monitored 359,101 drives during the period. After removing 3,881 boot drives and drives that did not meet the inclusion criteria, 354,415 drives were analyzed. Leaving boot drives out shapes the character of the statistics: they describe drives that store customer data, not drives that hold a server operating system.

The central indicator is the annualized failure rate (AFR). It converts the failures during the quarter into a one-year rate using the time the drives were running, expressed as drive days. The quarterly table lists, per model, the drive count, average age in months, drive days, failures and AFR, and the lifetime table lists the same items accumulated since deployment. The conditions for a model to appear are also fixed: the quarterly table requires more than 100 drives and more than 10,000 drive days, the annual view 250 and 50,000, and the lifetime table 500 and 100,000.

This piece focuses on how to read the headline that the quarterly AFR rose from 1.24% to 1.73%. To give the conclusion first, the number makes sense less as a signal that drives as a whole got worse than when it is broken down to see how much a few models with small samples or high ages move the average. The source itself explains the rise at the level of individual models.

  • Period: April 1 to June 30, 2026. Published September 29, 2026.
  • 359,101 drives monitored, 354,415 analyzed after exclusions. The 3,881 boot drives are left out of the tally.
  • The quarterly table requires more than 100 drives and more than 10,000 drive days for a model to be listed.
  • The source says the same data can be downloaded from Backblaze Hard Drive Test Data page.

The first thing to look at in the table is the arithmetic of the totals line. Divide 1,498 failures by 31,553,350 drive days and multiply by 365 and the result is 1.7328...%, which matches the 1.73% in the table. The 1.73% is therefore not a separate estimate but a value that comes from two totals inside the table. Knowing this structure lets the headline change be split along two paths: the numerator, failures, rose, or the composition of the denominator, drive days, tilted toward models with higher failure rates. The source does not present that decomposition numerically; it attaches only model-level explanations.

How AFR is calculated - failures and drive days

One has to know what AFR counts in order to read 1.73% at the right size. The AFR in the table is failures divided by drive days, multiplied by 365. A drive day is a cumulative unit that adds 1 whenever one drive runs for a day. If 1,000 drives run for a year that is about 365,000 drive days, and with 17 failures in that period the AFR is about 1.7% (an arithmetic illustration). So an AFR of 1.73% is the average conversion of about 1.73 failures per 100 drives per year. It is not a life expectancy forecast for any individual drive.

This calculation has two properties. First, because the denominator is drive days, newly deployed drives contribute only for the time they ran. The 9,620 drives that are six months old (Toshiba MG11ACA24TE, average 5.8 months) contributed 824,903 drive days in the quarter, about 2.6% of the quarter total of 31,553,350 (arithmetic). Second, the quarterly AFR moves with the model mix that ran during that quarter. Even if the failure rate of each model stays unchanged, the overall AFR rises when the share of higher-rate models grows. Because of these two properties, reading the quarterly AFR as a trend in drive quality requires looking at the change in model mix as well.

  • AFR equals failures divided by drive days times 365. The 1.73% can be reproduced from the table totals.
  • Drive days accumulate by multiplying running time by the number of drives, so drives deployed at different times contribute differently.
  • The quarterly AFR is an average over that quarter model mix, not a lifespan forecast for individual drives.
  • Even for the same model the AFR wobbles from quarter to quarter because drive counts and ages change.

The three models are the HGST HUH721212ALN604 (12TB) at 7.63%, the Seagate ST10000NM0086 (10TB) at 9.33% and the Seagate ST14000NM0138 (14TB) at 8.26%. When the sample is small - how far one failure moves the number

What the source means by a low drive count can be worked out concretely from the table values. AFR is failures divided by drive days times 365, so one additional failure raises AFR by 365 divided by drive days times 100 percentage points. The heart of the sample problem is that this increment is larger the fewer drive days there are. The table below computes that increment for five models in the quarterly table; it is arithmetic from the drive days in the table, not a value the source presents.

The first row of the table is the most dramatic. A model with 14,221 drive days and one failure is calculated at an AFR of 2.57%, which is higher than the quarterly overall 1.73%. Had that model had no failure its AFR would be 0%, and with two failures about 5.1% (arithmetic). One failure makes a difference of 2.57 percentage points. By contrast, for a model with 4,146,339 drive days the same single failure moves the rate only 0.009 percentage points. It fits this structure that the source calls its zero-failure models an honor roll and, in its explanation of the lifetime table, adds that most of those models fell short of the drive count criterion and could not appear in it. The source does not compute or present statistics such as a confidence interval; it stops at pointing out that the drive count is small.

For the two Seagate outliers the failures are 22 and 25, so the effect of a single failure above is 0.33 to 0.42 percentage points and not large. The AFR is nevertheless high, in the 8 to 9% range, because the drive days, 86,092 and 110,410, are only around 0.3% of the total (31,553,350), so a couple of dozen failures show up directly as a large ratio. The difference also shows that a small sample does not automatically mean a product of chance. Twenty-two to twenty-five failures is not a small number, and the possibility that the model failure rate really was high cannot be ruled out. The source likewise describes these models as having pretty clear reasons for high failure rates, and does not assert that the variation is temporary.

  • The amount one failure moves AFR is 365 divided by drive days times 100 percentage points (arithmetic).
  • A model with 14,221 drive days: one failure equals about 2.57 percentage points.
  • A model with 4,146,339 drive days: one failure equals about 0.009 percentage points.
  • Comparing AFRs lines up properly only when the drive days are of a similar scale.
  • The source does not compute a confidence interval and only points out that the drive count is small.

Age is only part of the explanation - what the table shows

The source cites age as a reason for high-AFR models. Open the table and it becomes clearer that age is only part of the explanation. Three models in the quarterly table have an average age above 100 months. The Seagate ST8000DM002 (114.8 months) has an AFR of 2.46%, the Seagate ST8000NM0055 (104.7 months) 3.44%, and the Seagate ST10000NM0086 (101.7 months) 9.33%. All three are well past eight years, yet their AFRs are scattered from 2.46% to 9.33%. Being old does not by itself fix the AFR.

There is a mirror image too. Among models with a young average age, there are cases in the table where the AFR stands out. The Seagate ST24000NM002H has an average age of 12.2 months and an AFR of 3.3%, and its lifetime AFR is 3.24%. Other models in the same capacity class, the Toshiba MG11ACA24TE (5.8 months) and the WDC WUH722626ALE6L4 (26TB, 3.4 months), are at 0.58% and 0.95%. Such differences between models are not explained by age. The source attaches no separate explanation to this, so the reason is not asserted here.

The table tells three things. Age is a common companion of high AFR but not a sufficient condition. Average age is an average within a model, and within the same model the deployment dates of individual drives can differ. And the AFR of a young model takes time to settle in the lifetime table because its running period is short. A one-line summary such as AFR rose because of many old drives therefore carries only part of the information in the source table.

  • The quarterly AFRs of the three models with average age above 100 months are scattered at 2.46%, 3.44% and 9.33%.
  • The Seagate ST24000NM002H, with an average age of 12.2 months, has a quarterly AFR of 3.3%, and the source attaches no separate explanation.
  • The difference from other young models (0.58% at 5.8 months, 0.95% at 3.4 months) is not explained by age alone.
  • The models for which the source cites age as the reason are two: HGST HUH721212ALN604 and Seagate ST10000NM0086.

The number of models above 3.0% - the text and the table

The source writes that seven models that are not outliers still had an AFR above 3.0%, and continues with 10 out of 31 drives having an AFR higher than 3.0%. It adds that a casual look at the last few quarters shows this distribution skewing toward the higher end, which will raise the average. It also says it has not done a full analysis of how rare such a skew is. This is the most general source sentence explaining the headline rise, so it is carried over as written, but counting the rows in the quarterly table gives different numbers.

The quarterly table consists of 31 model rows, and eleven rows have an AFR above 3.0%. Three of those eleven are the outliers (7.63%, 9.33%, 8.26%), and the remaining eight are the non-outliers (3.34%, 3.44%, 4.17%, 3.17%, 3.47%, 3.3%, 3.66%, 4.07%). The text seven and 10 and the table counts eight and eleven differ by one each. The source does not say which is the basis, so this piece takes the table rows as the basis and records the difference under what could not be verified.

Without the outliers - the average and the lifetime AFR

How much the three outliers move the average can be computed from the table totals. Subtract the 227 failures (180 plus 22 plus 25) of the three models from the 1,498 total, and subtract their 1,057,799 drive days (861,297 plus 86,092 plus 110,410) from the 31,553,350 total, and 1,271 failures and 30,495,551 drive days remain. Computing AFR from these gives 1.52% (arithmetic). Even without the three models, then, the quarterly AFR is 1.52%, higher than the 1.24% of Q1. This means the rise is not entirely due to the three models.

This calculation is a decomposition for reading, not a claim that the models may be set aside. The source treats outliers as a separate item not to adjust the average but to explain which models stand out and why. Doing such a decomposition helps gauge whether the headline 1.73% is the result of everything getting worse together or of the upper part of the distribution becoming thicker. The calculation above points closer to the latter. The share contributed by the three outliers is 0.21 percentage points (1.73 minus 1.52, arithmetic), while the rest of the rise comes from the distribution of models that are not outliers.

The lifetime AFR of 1.41% is an indicator that cushions these swings. The lifetime table accumulates failures and drive days since deployment. Total drive days are 558,148,607 with 21,609 failures, and this quarter drive days of 31,553,350 are only 5.7% of that (arithmetic). That is why, even when the quarterly AFR moves from 1.24% to 1.73%, the lifetime AFR rises only slightly. The source calls the lifetime AFR consistent while adding that it ticked up slightly, which fits this structure. The division of roles is that the quarterly value swings with one quarter composition and the lifetime value shows the long-run level.

  • Excluding the three outliers: 1,271 failures, 30,495,551 drive days, AFR 1.52% (arithmetic).
  • The three outliers add about 0.21 percentage points to the quarterly AFR (arithmetic).
  • Even without the three models the figure is higher than the 1.24% of Q1, so the rise is not the effect of those three alone.
  • Of the 558,148,607 drive days in the lifetime table, this quarter share is about 5.7% (arithmetic).
  • The quarterly AFR is sensitive to composition and the lifetime AFR moves slowly. The two values play different roles.

Cautions when carrying this over to your own storage decisions

Here is read-only general advice for readers who want to bring this table to a purchase or replacement decision. It is not a recommendation to buy or avoid any product, and it is not for building a ranking of manufacturers. The table is an aggregate per model, and even within one manufacturer the AFR is scattered from 0% to the 9% range depending on the model. It is safer to read manufacturer names only as labels for identifying models.

First, environments differ. These statistics come from Backblaze operating conditions. The source states that boot drives were excluded and that the subject is drives used to store data. Conditions such as temperature, vibration, write patterns and power cycles are not included in the source tables, and if your environment differs the failure rate of the same model can differ too. Second, the sample size has to be substituted with your own scale. In an environment with a few dozen drives, one or two failures in a model are enough to make the AFR equal any row of this table. Section 5 above shows that size.

Third, separate quarterly from lifetime. Because the swings in quarterly AFR are strongly affected by model mix, rather than changing a replacement plan on one quarter rise, look at whether the same model quarterly value continues over several quarters and what its gap to the lifetime AFR is. Fourth, the raw data is public. The source says the full dataset used for the quarterly tables and charts can be downloaded free from the Hard Drive Test Data page, with conditions attached to its use. Opening the row for a model of interest and reading its drive days and failures directly is more accurate than quoting a summary sentence.

  • The table is a per-model aggregate and not a basis for ranking manufacturers. Even within one manufacturer, AFR by model differs widely.
  • First compare the Backblaze conditions (drives for data storage, boot drives excluded) with your own environment.
  • Calculate the weight of one failure with your own drive count. The fewer the drives, the more the AFR swings.
  • Quarterly values are sensitive to composition, so look at several quarters together with the lifetime AFR.
  • The raw data is public and free, so you can see the drive days and failures of a model of interest directly.
  • How larger capacities raise the amount of data to move at the next replacement cycle is covered from the capacity planning angle in Why hard drives are always full.

What could not be verified

  • The body-text basis for the Q1 AFR of 1.24%. The Q2 post presents the quarter-by-quarter trend as a chart and does not state it in a body sentence. The 1.24% was taken from a summary of the Q1 report and could not be checked directly against the chart values in the Q2 post.
  • The arithmetic of the analyzed count. Subtracting 3,881 boot drives and 705 drives that missed the criteria from 359,101 monitored gives 354,515, while the source writes 354,415. The difference is 100, and the source does not say whether it is a typo or a difference in how exclusions were counted.
  • The number of models above 3.0%. The source text (seven beyond the outliers, 10 of 31) and the count from the quarterly table (8, 11) differ by one each. The table rows were used as the basis.
  • A quantitative decomposition of the AFR rise. The source gives only model-level reasons and does not separate the shares of more failures and of a changed model mix. The 1.52% calculation here is an arithmetic decomposition from the table.
  • The source explanation for the Seagate ST24000NM002H (average 12.2 months, AFR 3.3%). There is no separate statement.
  • Confidence intervals for small samples. The source does not compute or present them, and the one-failure sensitivity here is an illustration computed from drive days in the table.
  • Details of the Backblaze operating environment (temperature, vibration, write patterns). They are not in the source tables.
  • The individual link of the GeekNews discussion post. It could not be identified by search, so only the site front page link is given.
