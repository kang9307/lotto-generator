I Think I Have LLM Burnout - The New Fatigue of AI Coding Tools

Why productivity keeps climbing while developers keep getting more tired - on the paradox of delegated judgment and supervisory labor

Jul 11, 2026 Trends & Culture

Introduction: Why "Burnout" Got Reattached to a New Word

As of July 2026, a strange new phrase has been circulating in one corner of the developer community: "LLM burnout." Burnout is normally a concept tied to overwork or repetitive emotional labor, so why has it been reattached to AI coding tools right now? The trigger was an essay a developer named Alec Scollon posted to his own blog. It hit the front page of Hacker News and sparked heavy discussion on GeekNews (hada), giving language to a feeling many developers hadn't been able to name. Scollon admits that a year of daily use of tools like Claude Code, ChatGPT, and Gemini genuinely made him more productive - and yet, over the last few months, he's started dreading the act of reading AI output. His line, "I don't see myself stopping," is telling precisely because it's a paradox: he doesn't want to quit, but he's exhausted. That contradiction is the heart of the phenomenon. Working from the premise that many of us have felt something similar in recent months, this piece tries to take the phenomenon apart piece by piece - what this new fatigue actually is, why it happens, and what individuals and teams can do about it.

1. What LLM Burnout Actually Is

1.1 The Cognitive Load of the Prompt-Verify-Review Loop

A developer who uses AI coding tools daily lives inside a particular loop: describe the desired outcome in a prompt, check whether the result is correct once it arrives, and re-explain what's wrong so it can be fixed. At first glance this looks easier, since typing volume drops - but what's actually being spent isn't your fingers, it's your judgment. The cognitive load of writing code tends to follow familiar, procedural patterns. The cognitive load of verifying code is an evaluative task that requires a fresh judgment call every single time. These draw on different mental resources, and the second one burns out much faster.

1.2 Supervisory Fatigue - From Builder to Inspector

Multiple studies and field reports converge on the same point: the nature of the work has shifted from "building directly" to "supervising." That shift looks easier on the surface, but it's actually a kind of labor that never lets you fully relax. When you write code yourself, you get a natural breather at the point where you're stuck. When you're supervising AI output, you can never predict exactly when the model will confidently hand you a wrong answer, so there is no safe moment to lower your guard. Keep that tension up all day and you end up feeling drained even though you never did anything physically demanding.

1.3 The Breaking of Flow

Programming flow arrives when the goal is clear, feedback is immediate, and your skill matches the difficulty of the task. That rhythm keeps getting interrupted - while you wait for the AI to finish a task, and again while you review the result and re-issue instructions. Attention research has repeatedly shown that it takes a meaningful amount of time to return to full focus after any single interruption. Conversational work with an AI structurally bakes this kind of interruption into the process itself.

2. Why It Happens - Structural Causes

2.1 Productivity Pressure and the Disappearance of Natural Speed Limits

In the past, physical constraints - typing speed, compile time, the time it took to look something up - acted as natural buffers. AI tools have removed much of that friction. The problem is that the time saved rarely turns into rest; more often, organizations absorb it as an expectation of even more output. Once "faster" becomes the new baseline speed, you end up in a permanent sprint with no built-in buffer.

2.2 An AI That's Always On

When collaborating with another person, you naturally moderate how often and how intensely you ask things of them, out of respect for their time, mood, and context. An AI never tires, answers around the clock, and never complains no matter how many times you ask again. That limitless availability is convenient, but it also removes a natural brake. In situations where a human collaborator would have signaled "that's enough for now," the AI just keeps producing the next answer. The responsibility to stop is left entirely on your own shoulders.

2.3 The Paradox of Delegated Judgment

The whole point of delegating code-writing to AI is to reduce cognitive burden. But the responsibility for judging whether the delegated output can be trusted is never actually delegated - if anything, it can get heavier, because that judgment has to be made fresh every time. When you write code yourself, you already know what you did and why. When you're reviewing AI output, you first have to reconstruct why it was written that way at all. You delegated the work to make things easier, and a new kind of labor - verification - moves right in to fill the space. That's the paradox.

2.4 The Trust-Verify Cycle and Decision Fatigue

Early on, developers check every piece of AI output carefully. Over time, as experience accumulates that the AI is usually right, verification naturally loosens - a pattern known as automation complacency. The problem is that the mistakes an AI makes after it has earned trust tend to be the more dangerous ones. Conversely, staying deliberately vigilant to guard against that risk accumulates its own decision fatigue. As the number of outputs to review in a day grows, attention degrades toward the end of the list - not because of weak willpower, but because that's simply how human cognition works under load. This repeated recalibration between trust and vigilance is the core mechanism of LLM burnout.

3. The Loss of Joy When Coding Becomes Supervision

3.1 The Conditions for Flow, and Where They Disappear

Psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi identified the conditions for flow: a clear goal, immediate feedback, and a match between your skill and the difficulty of the challenge. When a developer solves a problem directly, these three conditions arise naturally. But when AI resolves the hard part first, the "appropriately difficult challenge" that actually produces flow disappears along with it. The output arrives faster, but the flow and sense of accomplishment that used to come from the process vanish with it.

3.2 From "Builder" to "Inspector"

Many developers chose this field for the joy of solving problems themselves - wrestling with a design, breaking through a blocker, the satisfaction of the moment something finally works. That was the reward. But once the center of gravity shifts from "building it yourself" to "checking what AI built," that reward structure starts to wobble. Reviewing and approving are necessary, but they don't provide flow or a sense of accomplishment on their own. If this identity shift goes unrecognized while fatigue keeps accumulating, people end up looking for the cause of that fatigue in the wrong place.

4. Individual Responses

4.1 Set Boundaries - Decide for Yourself When to Use AI

Not every task needs an AI in the loop. It makes sense to lean hard on AI for repetitive, low-stakes boilerplate and familiar refactoring where verification is cheap, and to handle work that involves important design judgment or costly mistakes yourself. Deciding this in advance as a rule, rather than improvising each time, reduces ongoing decision fatigue by itself.

4.2 Keep Hand-Coding Alive - Protecting Time for Flow

It helps to deliberately reserve part of the day for writing code by hand, with no AI involved. That can look like giving up some productivity, but it's actually protecting a resource that doesn't show up cleanly in any metric: flow and the sense of accomplishment. If that resource runs dry over the long term, productivity itself eventually collapses along with it.

4.3 A Review Budget and Deliberate Rest

Capping how much AI output you review in a day is another useful lever. Rather than reviewing output nonstop all day, inserting deliberate breaks between review sessions helps prevent the drop in review quality that tends to show up later in the day. If you notice yourself skimming reviews instead of actually reading them, treat that as a signal to stop for a moment.

5. Implications for Teams and Organizations

5.1 Don't Just Demand More Speed

Immediately refilling saved time with more work raises productivity metrics in the short term, but over the long run it spreads the exact fatigue described in this piece across the whole organization. The starting point is not automatically converting AI-enabled slack into higher output, and not forcing AI usage rates into individual performance metrics.

5.2 Separate Generation from Evaluation, and Manage Total Review Volume

Rather than having one person constantly toggle between generating code and evaluating it, separating the two phases in time reduces the cost of cognitive switching. Managers should also acknowledge that there is a realistic ceiling on how much AI output one person can review in a day, and build into team process the fact that the risk of missed defects grows as review fatigue accumulates.

Conclusion: Sustainable Developer Wellbeing in the AI Era

The benefits AI coding tools provide are real. They ease the burden of repetitive work, help people get up to speed faster in unfamiliar territory, and make more possible than before. This piece isn't arguing that you shouldn't use AI tools. It's arguing that sustainable use starts with recognizing that the benefit isn't free - it demands a new kind of labor, supervision, in return. LLM burnout isn't a failure of willpower; it's a structural phenomenon that naturally follows from a change in what the tool actually does to your job. Individuals can respond by setting boundaries and protecting time for flow; teams can respond by refusing to automatically refill saved time with more work. As of July 2026, this conversation has only just begun. Simply having a name for it is already a first step toward better answers.
