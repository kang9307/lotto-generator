The Complete Air Conditioner Buying Guide — Types, Sizing, and Electricity Cost

From comparing wall-mounted, standing, and system units to sizing calculations, inverter vs fixed-speed, and installation cost traps — a July 2026 buying guide

2026-07-24 Life & Practical

Introduction: What Should You Actually Base an AC Purchase On

As summer approaches, every appliance store and shopping site is flooded with terms like "inverter," "grade 1 efficiency," "room size rating," and "bladeless cooling." Choosing the right unit for your home means weighing room size, usage pattern, and budget all at once, and it is easy to keep putting off the decision until you get caught in the peak-season installation rush.

Based on general practical information current as of July 2026, this guide walks through the differences between AC types and their installation requirements, how to calculate the right capacity for your floor area, key specs like inverter technology and energy efficiency ratings, electricity cost and tiered-rate calculations, common pitfalls in installation cost and outdoor unit placement, noise and airflow considerations, seasonal maintenance, and selection criteria by housing type. It does not recommend specific brands or models; the goal is to give you a framework you can apply yourself. Exact prices, power consumption figures, and specifications vary by product and over time, so always check the manufacturer's current official specifications before purchasing.

1. Comparing Types: Wall-Mounted, Standing, System, Window, and Portable

Air conditioners fall into roughly five categories based on installation method and use case. Even with identical cooling performance, satisfaction can drop sharply if the type doesn't match your installation constraints and lifestyle.

1.1 Wall-Mounted Units

The most common form: an indoor unit mounted on the wall, connected through a wall opening to piping and an outdoor unit. It takes up no floor space, making it a good fit for small rooms or studios, and tends to run quieter than standing units, though its cooling range (how far cool air reaches) is generally narrower.

1.2 Standing Units

A larger floor-standing indoor unit that generally covers a wider cooling range and more power than wall-mounted units, making it suited to larger spaces like living rooms. It does take up floor space, however, and many users report it runs louder due to its larger fan.

1.3 System (Ceiling-Embedded) Air Conditioners

A single outdoor unit connects to multiple indoor units (commonly up to around nine, depending on the product) embedded in the ceiling throughout a home. This allows independent cooling of the living room and each bedroom with no visible indoor unit for a cleaner interior look, but it requires ceiling construction work, so it's typically decided during new construction or a renovation, and it usually carries the highest upfront cost.

1.4 Window Units

Mounted directly in a sliding window opening with no wall drilling required, making it a good fit for rental housing or any space where restoring the original condition matters. There's no separate outdoor unit, which simplifies installation, but keep in mind that noise and heat are both generated from the single unit itself.

1.5 Portable Units

A wheeled unit that vents exhaust through a duct run out a window gap, requiring no outdoor unit or piping work at all. It's useful as a stopgap if you move frequently or missed the installation window, but for the same rated cooling capacity it's generally considered less efficient than wall-mounted or standing units, so it's safer to treat it as a supplementary option rather than a primary cooling solution.

2. Sizing: Calculating Cooling Capacity for Your Floor Area

AC capacity is often described casually in terms of "room size," but the actual spec is expressed as cooling capacity (kW or BTU). Choosing by room-size label alone can mismatch your actual conditions, so it helps to understand the underlying calculation.

2.1 Basic Calculation

A commonly cited rule of thumb estimates required cooling capacity as floor area (square meters) × 400 to 700 watts. For example, a roughly 33-square-meter space would call for somewhere between about 1.3 and 2.3 kW under this range. Because the range itself is wide and multipliers vary between sources, the final decision should be based on the manufacturer's stated cooling capacity for your specific floor area.

2.3 Adjustment Factors: Insulation, Floor Level, Sun Exposure, Occupancy

The table above assumes standard conditions, so it's safer to size up a level if any of the following apply.

  • Insulation: Older buildings, poorly insulated units, or rooms along an exterior wall lose and gain more heat, favoring a larger capacity.
  • Floor level and sun exposure: Top-floor units or rooms facing west or south with prolonged direct sunlight heat up more and warrant sizing up.
  • Occupancy: More occupants means more body heat and activity-related heat load, so multi-person households should calculate with some margin.
  • Kitchen adjacency and window area: Spaces next to a kitchen or with large, non-double-glazed windows gain more heat and may need an adjustment.

An undersized unit will run continuously without ever reaching the target temperature, driving up both electricity cost and component wear, while an oversized unit adds unnecessary upfront cost and can cause discomfort from overly aggressive cooling — matching capacity to actual conditions is the key.

3. Key Specs: Inverter, Energy Rating, and Add-On Features

3.1 Inverter vs Fixed-Speed

Fixed-speed (constant-speed) units run the compressor motor at a set speed until the target temperature is reached, then shut off, restarting at full power once the temperature rises again — an on-off cycle. Inverter units continuously vary the compressor's rotation speed to match conditions, running at low speed to hold the temperature steady even after reaching the target. Inverter units are generally reported to be more efficient for long, continuous use, while frequent on-off cycling can make them relatively less efficient due to the power draw of restarting. Some sources suggest fixed-speed units aren't meaningfully worse when used briefly and intermittently, so understanding your own usage pattern is the starting point for choosing between the two.

3.2 Energy Efficiency Rating (CSPF)

An air conditioner's energy efficiency rating is graded from 1 to 5 based on cooling capacity divided by power consumption (CSPF, Cooling Seasonal Performance Factor), with a rating closer to 1 meaning lower power draw for the same cooling capacity. One study cited monthly average consumption for a 2,300W-class wall-mounted unit at about 33.7 kWh for a grade-1 product versus about 52.2 kWh for a grade-5 product, while other sources cite a roughly 30 to 45 percent difference between grades. Figures vary by product and methodology, but the direction is consistent: a higher rating means larger long-term electricity savings. If you plan to run the unit for long hours all summer, it's worth considering a higher-rated model even at a higher upfront price.

3.3 Reading Cooling Capacity Against Power Consumption

Spec sheets list both cooling capacity (kW) and power consumption (W) together. For the same cooling capacity, lower power consumption means better efficiency, and this ratio is the basis of the CSPF used to derive the energy rating. Rather than simply choosing "the unit with the highest cooling capacity," it's more advantageous long-term to choose "the unit with the lowest power consumption for the capacity you actually need."

3.4 Dehumidifying and Air-Purifying Add-Ons

Many recent air conditioners add a dehumidify mode and an air-purifying filter. An AC's dehumidify mode operates based on a target temperature, so it may not lower humidity to your target level as reliably as a dedicated dehumidifier would if humidity control is your top priority. Air-purifying features similarly tend to have more limited filter capacity and circulation performance than a dedicated air purifier. It's reasonable to treat these add-ons as "nice to have" conveniences and prioritize core performance — cooling capacity and efficiency — in your decision.

4. Electricity Cost: Tiered Rates and Savings Principles

4.1 The Cost Calculation Formula

Daily usage (kWh) = wattage ÷ 1000 × hours of daily use
Monthly usage (kWh) = daily usage × days used
Monthly cost = monthly usage (kWh) × electricity rate

The key point is that air conditioner electricity cost isn't determined by usage alone; in many tiered residential rate systems, the per-unit rate itself changes depending on which tier your household's total monthly usage falls into.

4.2 Summer Tiered Rate Brackets

Residential electricity rates in Korea rise in tiers as usage increases. In the general period, the tiers are up to 200 kWh, 201-400 kWh, and above 400 kWh, but during the summer period (July 1 to August 31), these thresholds are eased to up to 300 kWh, 301-450 kWh, and above 450 kWh.

It's not a small, gradual increase — crossing a tier threshold is a step-function jump where both the base charge and per-unit rate rise at once, so whether your AC use pushes your household into the next tier is what really drives the bill you feel.

4.3 How Inverters Save Energy, and Matching Pattern to Type

Because inverter units can hold temperature at low speed, their power draw curve flattens out during continuous use, whereas fixed-speed units must restart at full output every time they cycle back on. One source notes that cycling a fixed-speed unit on and off at roughly two-hour intervals can cut electricity cost by about 70 percent compared to running it continuously for 12 hours — which, read the other way, means leaving a fixed-speed unit running continuously for long stretches is inefficient. Inverter units, by contrast, are generally reported to be more efficient when left running continuously to hold a temperature rather than switched on and off frequently for short bursts. In short: fixed-speed favors "short and intense," inverter favors "long and steady."

4.4 The 26°C Recommendation

South Korea's government and its Korea Energy Agency recommend keeping indoor cooling temperature around 26°C (about 79°F) in summer, based on keeping the gap between indoor and outdoor temperature within about 5°C to reduce physical strain while also cutting power consumption. A 1983 study by the Korea Institute of Energy Research found a comfortable indoor temperature range of 21.2 to 26.5°C, making the 26°C recommendation close to a balance point between comfort and energy savings.

5. Installation Cost, Piping, and Outdoor Unit Placement Traps

5.1 Base Installation Fees

Commonly cited base installation fees in the Korean market are roughly 100,000-150,000 won for wall-mounted units, 150,000-200,000 won for standing units, 250,000-350,000 won for a wall-mounted plus standing 2-in-1 set, and 50,000-100,000 won for window units (figures will differ in other markets). Portable units typically require no separate installation fee since they need no outdoor unit or piping work.

5.2 Add-On Costs — What Quotes Commonly Miss

Base installation fees typically cover only about 3 to 5 meters of piping and a standard outdoor-unit mount; anything beyond that is usually billed as an add-on.

If the outdoor unit placement is awkward or the piping run is long, these items can stack up to add a substantial amount on top of the unit price. It helps to ask ahead of time what add-on items an installer commonly flags on-site, especially when buying at the lowest online price.

5.3 Outdoor Unit Placement Checkpoints

Since the outdoor unit's job is to expel heat, it needs good airflow around it to maintain efficiency and lifespan. Insufficient clearance from a wall or a cramped, enclosed spot can cause expelled heat to be drawn back in, reducing cooling efficiency and shortening the unit's life. Prolonged direct sunlight on the unit is another efficiency drag. Longer piping runs also mean more refrigerant loss and higher cost, so keeping the distance between indoor and outdoor units as short as possible is generally favorable. In multi-unit housing, also check any safety regulations or building management approval required for outdoor unit brackets in advance.

6. Noise, Airflow Direction, and Bladeless Cooling

Indoor unit noise is generally reported to be higher for standing units than wall-mounted units, so it's worth checking for a low-noise (sleep) mode and the decibel (dB) rating in the spec sheet. Outdoor unit noise can become a source of dispute with neighbors if placed near a window, so factor that into the placement decision as well. Left-right and up-down auto-swing airflow affects how evenly cool air spreads through the room. Many premium models now feature bladeless (draft-free) cooling, which diffuses cool air through many small vents rather than a direct stream, and this is reported to help reduce the discomfort and potential cold-related strain from direct airflow during sleep or long cooling sessions.

7. Maintenance: Filters, Mold, Refrigerant, and Seasonal Checkups

7.1 Filter Cleaning

Dust buildup on the filter reduces cooling efficiency and increases noise. During peak summer use, it's generally recommended to check and clean the filter about every two weeks.

7.2 Preventing Mold and Odor

Condensation inside the indoor unit during cooling creates an environment where mold can easily take hold. Running the fan-only (internal dry) mode for a period after use helps dry out the interior and is generally helpful for preventing mold and odor.

7.3 Refrigerant Checks

Under normal use, refrigerant in a sealed system shouldn't decrease on its own, so if cooling performance drops despite a clean filter, suspect a refrigerant leak or piping issue and have it inspected by a professional.

7.4 Pre- and Post-Season Checkups

Before summer begins (roughly May-June), it's a good idea to clean the filter and run a short test cycle to confirm the unit cools properly. After summer ends (roughly September-October), running the internal-dry mode to clear out moisture and covering the unit to keep dust out will help it perform better next season.

8. Selection Checklist by Floor Area and Housing Type

Priorities shift depending on your housing situation.

  • Studio apartment (single occupant): A window unit with minimal installation constraints, or a wall-mounted unit sized for a small room, is generally sufficient.
  • Two-room apartment/small home (two or fewer occupants): A wall-mounted unit in the bedroom paired with a small standing unit in the living room works well, or a wall-mounted unit alone may be enough if space is tight.
  • Apartment (larger household): A standing unit in the living room combined with wall-mounted units in each bedroom is common; if you're doing new construction or a renovation, a ceiling-embedded system AC is also worth considering.
  • Short-term rental: If restoring the original condition matters, a window or portable unit that requires no wall drilling is advantageous.
  • New construction/planned renovation: If considering a system AC, plan the piping and electrical work together early in the interior design phase to reduce add-on costs later.

Finally, run through this checklist before you buy.

  • Did you calculate capacity factoring in floor area and adjustment factors (insulation, sun exposure, occupancy)?
  • Did you choose inverter vs fixed-speed based on your actual usage pattern (long continuous use vs intermittent use)?
  • Did you check the energy efficiency rating?
  • Did you identify likely add-on costs beyond the base installation fee (extended piping, drilling, removal, etc.) in advance?
  • Did you check the outdoor unit's airflow clearance and any building management approval needed?
  • Did you check the noise decibel rating and availability of bladeless/low-noise modes?
  • Do you actually need add-ons like dehumidify or air-purify modes?
  • Did you check the warranty and after-sales service policy?

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q. What size air conditioner do I need?

A common rule of thumb estimates required capacity as floor area × 400-700 watts, though multipliers vary by source, so the final call should rest on the manufacturer's stated capacity for your floor area. Size up a level if insulation is poor, sun exposure is heavy, or occupancy is high.

Q. Should I choose inverter or fixed-speed?

Inverter is generally reported to be more efficient for long, continuous daily use, while fixed-speed may not be meaningfully worse for short, intermittent use. Fixed-speed units tend to have a lower upfront price.

Q. How much does it cost to run per month?

Multiply wattage by hours of use for consumption, then by your rate, but whether you cross the summer tier thresholds (up to 300 / 301-450 / above 450 kWh) is what really drives the felt cost. Consumption can differ by roughly 30-55 percent between the highest and lowest efficiency ratings depending on the product.

Q. How much does installation cost?

Base fees run about 100,000-150,000 won for wall-mounted and 150,000-200,000 won for standing units, but stacked add-ons like extended piping, wall drilling, an outdoor bracket, and removal of an old unit can add a substantial amount on top of the unit price.

Q. What's the ideal temperature setting?

The government and Korea Energy Agency recommend around 26°C, based on keeping the indoor-outdoor gap within about 5°C. Setting it too low increases both electricity cost and physical strain.

Conclusion

Working through just three things in order — type, capacity, and key specs — can cut your odds of a bad purchase substantially. Choose a type that matches your housing and installation constraints, calculate capacity with your floor area and adjustment factors in mind, then check inverter vs fixed-speed against your usage pattern along with the energy efficiency rating. Add in awareness of hidden installation cost items, outdoor unit placement, pre- and post-season filter and mold maintenance, and the 26°C habit, and you'll get an efficient, comfortable summer out of it.

Note: The power consumption, pricing, and electricity cost figures in this article are general reference values and will vary by product, market, and electricity rate. Always confirm the manufacturer's current official specifications before purchasing.
