Who wrote it, and where — Tao's blog, Avigad's text

On October 5, 2026, a post titled "The Future of Mathematics" appeared on What's new, the blog of the mathematician Terence Tao. The name of the blog's owner carries enough weight that the piece is easily passed along as Tao's own statement on the future of mathematics. The bracketed note at the top of the post says otherwise. It is a guest post by Jeremy Avigad, and the note adds that the text was initially written in a different file format and converted using AI. The "T." at the end of the note is the signature of the blog's owner.

Authorship is not a side detail here. Every first-person sentence in the post belongs to Avigad, and when the post speaks of "blogs like this one," the blog it means is Tao's. Read with the wrong author in mind, the subject of nearly every sentence changes.

Avigad's home page introduces him as a member of the Department of Philosophy and the Department of Mathematical Sciences at Carnegie Mellon University, and as director of the Hoskinson Center for Formal Mathematics and of the Institute for Computer-Aided Reasoning in Mathematics. Under research interests it has a single line: "Formal methods and AI for mathematics." The post is therefore written by someone who works directly on formal methods and AI for mathematics, and who addresses the mathematical community as "we."

The occasion for the post is stated in its first paragraph. Avigad had attended a meeting of science and technology startups supported by Convergent Research. As the post explains it, Convergent Research oversees the Lean FRO, and the Lean FRO is a nonprofit that develops the Lean theorem prover. When he introduced himself as a mathematician, many participants were eager to talk about the impact of recent events in AI on mathematics and about reactions in the mathematics community. They were surprised, he writes, to hear that he finds the tone of the community responses on blogs like Tao's and Proofs and Prompts "generally positive and encouraging." That assessment comes with a qualifier attached: everyone recognizes that fundamental aspects of day-to-day professional life are bound to change. Proofs and Prompts describes itself on its front page as a communal blog for mathematicians' thoughts on how AI is changing what it means to do mathematics.

The starting point — what the narrow view lost and what the broad view keeps

The post opens by separating two views of what mathematicians do. The narrow view is the one encapsulated in daily workflows: try to solve problems, and when the hard problems are too hard, make up easier approximations, solve them, and then vary the parameters. Avigad writes that this practice has been disrupted by the events of the last few months, and he immediately limits the sense in which he means it: "in the sense that the kinds of results that would have, a year ago, made for perfectly respectable publications can now easily be generated with the help of AI."

Two phrases in that limitation are doing work: "the kinds of results" and "with the help of AI." The sentence is a long way from saying that every result now appears on its own. Even so, the post says, the disruption leaves two worries behind.

  • What it will mean to do mathematics going forward.
  • How to train and support the next generation of mathematicians to do "whatever that is."

The broad view comes from the history of mathematics. What has remained stable despite centuries of change, the post says, is that mathematics is a culture of rigorous reasoning and communication, one that provides language and abstractions that let people think and communicate more reliably and efficiently. "Surely such reasoning is still important, even in the age of AI."

There is also a passage on the fact that many mathematicians find the subject beautiful. Aesthetic pleasure, the post argues, does not diminish practical utility but is explained by it. The ground offered is an expectation on the author's part: he expects that the reason doing mathematics feels so good is that "it is the exercise of capacities that are so fundamental to our survival as a species that they are wired into our DNA." The conclusion is closed with a conditional as well. If that expectation is right, mathematical thought is not going away any time soon.

Three epigraphs stand before the body of the post. They set the temperature at which the text asks to be read before any argument begins.

  • A sentence by Howard Stein, saying that mathematics underwent in the nineteenth century a transformation so profound that it is not too much to call it "a second birth of the subject."
  • Mark Twain: "The report of my death was an exaggeration."
  • "May you live in interesting times." The attribution reads only "(traditional)".

Two things that came apart — solving problems and gaining understanding

The last paragraph of the introduction is the axis of the whole post. The sentence reads: "The challenge is that solving the kinds of problems we have been solving for decades becomes decoupled from the goal of enhancing our mathematical understanding when we let AI do the work."

Unpacked, the point runs as follows. Until now, solving a problem brought understanding along with it, so counting solved problems could stand in for counting progress in understanding. To say the two are decoupled is to say that this proxy relationship no longer holds. Drop the conditional clause in the middle of the sentence, though, and it becomes a different claim. The condition the post names for the decoupling is not the existence of AI but "when we let AI do the work." This reading in terms of a proxy is an interpretation from outside the post.

That is why the question changes. In the post's words, the question "isn't whether we still need mathematics, but rather how to pursue mathematical understanding in the age of AI." Avigad then says he will provide "three general answers." The fact that the author himself attached the word "general" matters again later on.

Before moving to the three answers, it helps to lay out on one page the activities the post treats as distinct. The rows of the table below contain only categories that appear in the post, and the two right-hand columns condense the post's own sentences.

What stands out in the table is the difference between rows where the middle column is filled and rows where it is empty. Two rows are empty: choosing problems and grading solutions, and the culture of rigorous reasoning and communication. For these two the post writes down nothing that AI changes, only what stays with mathematicians. The three answers are built on that asymmetry. This reading in terms of an asymmetry is an interpretation from outside the post.

First answer: solve harder problems

The first answer starts from two observations about today's foundation models.

  • They have seen the entire mathematical literature. That is the post's own wording.
  • They are tireless. A swarm of agents can make its way to a solution by trying countless variations.

Avigad takes these two features to explain one phenomenon: the AI-generated solutions to open problems seen so far "all have a similar character." In the post's characterization, "they are problems that AI could solve by cobbling together available techniques." A parenthetical note credits Matthew Ballard for this characterization and for the analysis that follows.

The sentences after that are calibrated with some care.

  • Probably not. Asked whether every interesting mathematical question can be answered that way, the post replies "Probably not." The register is that of an estimate.
  • Even solvable questions may have better solutions. Questions that can be answered that way "may have more interesting and satisfying solutions that invoke novel ideas and insights."
  • The near future is left open. "Perhaps, in the near future, AI systems will be able to come up with such insights, but, at the very least, let's recognize that we are not there yet."
  • The conclusion is anticipation. In any case, the post says, there are still hard questions to be solved and ambitious research programs to pursue, and the possibility of making progress on them with the help of AI "is exciting."

The sentence that comes right after "we are not there yet" describes the way reinforcement learning grades actions. According to the post, reinforcement-learning training regimes have systems chain conventional moves and learn, from a history of failures and successes, which ones are most promising in a given state. The fact that the value of an action is graded solely in terms of the success of a final trajectory "breeds superhuman cleverness but may miss creativity and higher-level strategizing." Here too the verb is "may miss," not "misses."

Shortening this answer to "AI cannot solve hard problems" points it in the wrong direction. The final sentence of the section places progress on hard problems alongside help from AI. What the first answer recommends is not keeping a distance from AI but raising the level of the problems beyond what recombining techniques can reach. The separate issue that scores and solved-problem counts cannot tell kinds of ability apart is handled, from the side of experiment design, in the perturbation tests that tell reasoning apart from recall.

Second answer: think bigger thoughts — why Riemann's lecture is the example

The second answer begins with a counterfactual. The present situation would be much more depressing, the post says, if mathematics were a matter of ticking off problems imposed by aliens, an endless sequence of exercises and exams. The good news is that it is not: "when we do mathematics, we get to choose the problems, grade the solutions, and favor the ones we like best." The ones who decide what is interesting, which questions to pursue, and why, are the mathematicians.

The example Avigad chooses is Riemann. After the sentence that the things most admired in mathematics often seem to come out of nowhere, the post gives the following account.

  • In 1853 the young Bernhard Riemann submitted three potential topics to his advisor, Carl Friedrich Gauss, to choose from for his Habilitationsvortrag, a lecture he was required to give to secure a teaching position at the University of Göttingen.
  • Gauss reportedly chose the topic for which Riemann was least prepared, to see what he would make of it.
  • The resulting lecture, "On the Hypotheses Which Lie at the Foundations of Geometry," was published posthumously in 1868, and the post says it revolutionized the field.
  • The lecture distinguished a space's metric properties from its topological properties and introduced the general notion of a manifold, though the latter did not receive a fully rigorous treatment until the twentieth century.
  • The focus on intrinsic properties of a space, those determined by the metric and independent of embedding in a larger space, was key to Einstein's theory of general relativity decades later.
  • Riemannian geometry has had applications Riemann himself could never have imagined, from robotics and medical imaging to statistical analysis.

Next comes Felix Klein's recollection, quoted from William Ewald's sourcebook From Kant to Hilbert. Klein says that the publication of Riemann's lecture occurred just when he was beginning to occupy himself independently with mathematical problems, and then writes: "Much seemed to us dark and difficult to understand, and yet of unfathomable depth, where the modern mathematician, who has already absorbed all these things into his mode of thought from the outset, only admires the clarity and fecundity of the exposition."

The reason the quotation is there lies in the two sentences that follow it. What seemed dark and mysterious to the young Klein is now part of the canon, something foundation models have absorbed and internalized through their training, just as young mathematicians do. And Avigad borrows a point from Ballard once more: no reinforcement-learning setup could have evaluated Riemann's decisions. Two reasons are given. The benefits are diffuse and hard to track, and the time horizon is much too long.

The post names three more developments of which the same can be said: Galois' focus on groups of permutations in the study of solvability of algebraic equations, Poincaré's qualitative studies of dynamical systems, and Grothendieck's far-reaching conceptual innovations. Then it raises the question a reader is likely to ask and sets it aside. Will AI eventually be able to make advances like these? The post's reply: "That's not even the right question to ask."

The reason has to do with where value is attached. Being told that some AI agent is spinning out theorems that are highly interesting to it and to other AI agents "does nothing for us." The post continues: "We should care about AI only insofar as the results are interesting and important to us, and, at the end of the day, it's up to us to decide what that means." The paragraph closes with the sentence "The values we assign to mathematical developments are embedded in our history and culture."

There is an analogy as well. Imagine planning a trip to go backpacking in the Alaskan wilderness for exercise and recreation. You might be happy to let AI help book the flight, but not to let AI take the hike for you and send you pictures. In mathematics, the post says, what is at stake is not recreation but "agency over our reasoning and deliberation." "Whether or not AI can think, it can't think for us." The paragraph ends by saying that with AI there is even more to explore and no shortage of avenues for discovery.

The weight of the second answer does not rest on a forecast of capability. Its work is to move the question from "can AI do it" to "to whom are the results interesting and important." That reading is an interpretation that goes beyond the post. What specifically goes missing when only the conclusion of a proof arrives is treated, from the side of the artifact, in The Answer Is Correct but Unusable.

Third answer: try new things — what should count as mathematics

The first two answers were about using AI to help with things mathematicians already did. In the third, Avigad turns. The technologies themselves raise new questions, he writes, and there is a lot to learn about how to use them effectively. He notes that he has argued in another essay that mathematicians should be actively involved in understanding how the technologies work and in coming up with novel and creative ways to use them to do new mathematics. That essay, he says, is now scheduled to appear in the Notices of the AMS.

The ground offered is precedent. The section's second paragraph opens with "AI changing what it means to do mathematics is not without precedent." A list follows.

  • The use of algebraic methods to solve geometric problems in the seventeenth century. The post says it was a new technology and that not everyone liked it.
  • Infinitesimals, later in that century.
  • Algebraic structures in the nineteenth century.
  • Set-theoretic abstraction and structural language in the early twentieth century.
  • Numerical and symbolic computation, more recently.

The post sums these up by saying they "were all alien and disconcerting when they were new." A proposal about evaluation follows at this point. Those who invest time and energy in getting proof assistants and neural networks to help discover new mathematics should be viewed "as doing mathematics proper, rather than dismissing them as mere technicians." The next sentence is phrased as a possibility: in the age of AI, developing symbolic automation or training a neural network "can be no less a contribution to mathematics than manually chaining inferences to prove a theorem."

The opposing argument also appears in the post. Avigad writes that he has heard arguments that as the job market contracts, mathematicians should turn inward to preserve traditional mathematical skills. His own position is the reverse. He believes, he writes, that engaging with new technologies and learning how to use them to improve the ability to reason and discover new mathematics will keep the discipline strong. The section closes with the sentence "Expanding our view of mathematics is the best way to expand the profession and keep it relevant."

Of the three answers, the third sits closest to institutions. What counts as a contribution to mathematics is a question that translates directly into criteria for hiring and publication. The guest post frames it only as a matter of recognition; the linked essay describes the hiring side explicitly. What a proof assistant actually checks and what it does not guarantee is covered in Formalization Is Not Proof.

What the linked essay adds — scope and how work gets evaluated

The essay the guest post points to is "What is mathematics now, and what should it be?", submitted to arXiv on August 24, 2026. It is listed under cs.AI and math.HO, and its abstract is two sentences long: advances in neural theorem provers have been impressive, but the successes obscure a broader vision of what AI can do for mathematics and how mathematicians can engage with AI, and the essay advances a more expansive and optimistic point of view. The text has six sections.

What the third answer of the guest post refers to becomes concrete in the way this essay defines its scope. Avigad takes the phrase "AI for mathematics" to encompass all of the following.

  • The formalization and digitization of mathematics. The development of proof assistants, carefully curated libraries, automated reasoning tools, and new means of interaction and collaboration.
  • Symbolic AI. The use of SAT solvers, SMT solvers, first- and higher-order provers, constraint solvers, and symbolic optimization techniques.
  • Machine learning beyond large language models. Reinforcement learning and neural networks as means of detecting patterns in mathematical data, discovering new phenomena, finding mathematical objects of interest, computing solutions to partial differential equations, and homing in on the parameters where interesting behavior occurs.

The essay attaches a qualifier of fairness here: "To be fair, other applications of AI in mathematics have had nowhere near the successes we have seen with neural provers." In other words the author states up front that the call to widen the scope is not a report of results already achieved.

On evaluation the essay is more direct than the guest post. For most of those who learned the new technologies, it says, engaging with them "has been tantamount to abandoning mathematics." The reason given is that "publications in computer science count for nothing on job applications." And: "In the eyes of the mathematical establishment, whatever they are doing, it is not mathematics."

The essay also limits the size of its own demand.

  • It states explicitly that it is not claiming every mathematician has to become an expert in machine learning and formal methods.
  • It does hold that it will be important for future mathematicians to have "basic competence with these tools," and compares this to the way PhD programs expect competence in algebra, analysis, geometry, topology and other areas.
  • It says that if mathematics is to succeed in the new age, it is not enough for mathematicians "to be mere consumers of AI."
  • It grants that "Conservatism is one of mathematics' superpowers" while adding that the flexibility to adapt to social, political, cultural, and technological changes is also important.

And there is a sentence about ordering: "The question of how to get there is secondary; the first step is to reach a clearer consensus on what we want mathematics to be." The three answers of the guest post can be read as the author's own opening proposal toward that consensus. That is an interpretation drawn from reading the two texts together.

Three messages to the next generation

The first paragraph under the last subheading is one in which the author assesses his own text. He writes that his "rosy outlook" on the future and his "glib advice" to solve harder problems, think bigger thoughts, and try new things "will not provide much comfort to students and early-career researchers, who feel the ground shifting beneath their feet." He adds that his words are not meant to diminish the challenges ahead or to suggest easy responses to the disruption.

The post also says where concrete measures are being worked out. Mathematics departments, community leaders, educators, professional societies, and journal boards "are holding emergency meetings all over," and are doing their best to make concrete recommendations and take appropriate action. The paragraph ends: "We have our work cut out for us."

Despite that uncertainty, the post says, there are some clear messages that can be sent to the next generation, and it gives three.

  1. We stand with you. Nothing is more important, the post says, than the health and strength of the discipline and ensuring that the next generation can thrive. Alongside this stands a sentence of humility: the experience and expertise of the established generation are limited, and some of the things it thought it knew in the past are no longer valid.
  2. Mathematics is as important as it ever was, and the next generation is needed. "AI must not replace our collective ability to reason and deliberate, and mathematics remains a core capacity for doing so."
  3. The next few years will be extremely interesting. The community is at a new frontier where its communal norms, values, and expectations are beginning to break down, and it is up to everyone to figure out what should replace them.

The post ends with the author's confidence that future historians will see this moment as the start of a new era of mathematics and will weigh the consequences of present actions and decisions, and with the sentence that mathematics "has never been for the faint of heart."

Sorting what the three messages contain from what they leave out makes the character of the post clear. What they contain is attitude and direction. Measures such as numbers of positions, the wording of evaluation criteria, or changes to curricula are absent, and the post stops at listing the bodies that are producing them. This fits with the author calling his own advice "general answers" and going as far as to label it "glib."

What the community has to redefine — proposals, grounds, open points

Taken together, the guest post and the linked essay yield seven items that the community has to settle anew once solving and understanding have come apart. Read across, the table shows a consistent shape. The proposals are directions; the grounds are precedents from the history of mathematics and an observation about the character of current AI solutions; and the open points fall almost entirely under "who, by what procedure, against what criterion." Agreeing with the direction still leaves the separate job of turning it into institutional language.

The structure is not unfamiliar to readers outside mathematics. Any field in which the cost of producing output has dropped quickly can hold the same questions up against its own work. The five points below are an interpretation that goes beyond the post.

  • The unit being counted. Are the amount of output and the growth of understanding being counted with the same metric? A metric designed when the two moved together keeps reporting the same number after they have come apart.
  • The scope of what is handed over. In the post, the decoupling happens when the work is handed over. Has it been decided, task by task, how much is delegated and where direct work begins?
  • Who assigns value. Does the authority to decide which problems matter still rest with people, or have the problems that are easy to solve taken the place of the problems that matter?
  • The people who build tools. Is building tools and automation evaluated as the main job, or treated as a side task?
  • The entry path. If making up and solving easier approximations was the training of early-career people, what fills that training once the exercise has lost its value?

How the bottleneck moved to understanding once generation became cheap on the software side is covered in Understanding Is the New Bottleneck.

How strongly the post asserts things — the hedges summaries tend to drop

A text carrying a title as large as "The Future of Mathematics" tends to gain force as it circulates in one-line summaries. The post itself lowers its level of assertion in several places. The table below collects those places. The table does not point in one direction only. Drop the hedges and the post becomes simpler toward optimism and toward pessimism alike. The post views the future of the field brightly and, within the same text, acknowledges that this outlook will not provide much comfort to early-career researchers. Carry over only one of the two and it becomes a different text.

Objections in the comment section — read them apart from the post

Seven comments are attached to the post, all dated October 5, the day it went up. Comments are not the words of the author or of the blog's owner; they are the opinions of the individual commenters. Those that push directly against the post's argument are nevertheless useful for reading it. What follows are claims made in comments, not claims made in the post.

  • An objection about the unit of evaluation. One comment argues that what the mathematical community actually values is publications, not understanding. When the commenter's own papers were published, the comment says, what mattered was whether they were clear, correct and new, not whether the author understood them.
  • A question about definition. Another comment asks what the precise mathematical definition of the word "understanding" is.
  • An objection to the Alaska analogy. One comment holds that the analogy does not fit, because in the way academic credit is assigned what counts is results and publications, not the journey. The same comment recasts the analogy as taking an AI robot on the hike instead of a human companion, and says that this removes the forging of new communities.
  • An objection about the incentive to explore. In reply to the post's sentence that with AI there is even more to explore, the same comment says there is even less incentive to explore.
  • A view in another direction. There is also a comment that sees no problem if computer science and mathematics were to merge back together, and that calls this a time of opportunity for some graduate students in mathematics.

The first objection points at the same spot as the "Open point" column in the table of proposals above. Even if the pursuit of understanding is set as the goal, if the unit the community assigns value to is the publication, what closes the gap between the two? The linked essay's remark that publications in computer science do not count on job applications is an observation at the same level. Connecting the comment and the essay, however, is an interpretation that goes beyond the post, and the commenter and the author do not arrive at the same conclusion.

What could not be confirmed

  • The issue and timing of publication in the Notices of the AMS. The guest post says only "now scheduled to appear," and no notice from the journal was confirmed.
  • The date, place and attendance of the Convergent Research meeting. The post gives no details.
  • The text or talk in which Matthew Ballard's analysis appears. The guest post relays his characterization with thanks and gives no source link.
  • The specific cases meant by "the events of the last few months." No names of models, companies or problems appear in the body of the guest post.
  • The historical source for the story that Gauss chose the topic Riemann was least prepared for. The post itself writes it at the level of "reportedly."
  • The original source of Klein's recollection and its location within From Kant to Hilbert. The book itself was not opened.
  • The context around the epigraph sentence in Howard Stein's paper.
  • Data supporting the premise that the job market is contracting. The guest post says only that such arguments have been heard.
  • Whether converting the manuscript using AI introduced any differences between the original manuscript and the posted version. The note reports only that a conversion took place.
  • Replies by the author or the blog's owner to the comments. The number and content of comments change over time.
