International Driving Permit - Getting One Is Not the Same as It Being Accepted

Geneva and Vienna - how two separate conventions split recognition, and the order to check things before you fly

2026-07-29 Life & Practical

Introduction: why 「one permit, drive anywhere」 is wrong

Search for an international driving permit before a trip and you will land on the same conclusion everywhere. Bring a passport photo and your licence, and it is done in a day. That part is true. Obtaining the document is easier than anything else described in this article. The trouble starts afterwards, at a rental counter in another country, when someone looks at the paperwork and says this is not valid here. At that moment half of the preparation becomes worthless.

This happens for a structural reason, not because travellers are careless. An international driving permit is not a single global standard. It is issued under an international convention, and there is more than one convention. The Convention on Road Traffic concluded at Geneva in 1949 and the Convention on Road Traffic concluded at Vienna in 1968 are separate treaties. They have different lists of contracting parties, and each prescribes its own model of international driving permit in its annexes.

So this article does not simply list an application procedure. Official agency pages already do that well, and copying them is only half of the help. What this article separates is three layers. First the legal layer of the conventions, second the administrative layer of national recognition, third the contractual layer of what the rental company demands. These are decided in three different places, and failing any one of them keeps you out of the driver's seat.

Scope note: this article describes the international driving permit issued in the Republic of Korea. If your licence was issued elsewhere, your issuing authority, fee, validity and application route will be different, and you should read your own country's motoring authority instead. What transfers across borders is the structure in sections 2, 7, 8 and 9, not the Korean procedure in section 4. Facts here were confirmed on 29 July 2026 from the Korea Road Traffic Authority, the Korean police civil affairs portal, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs overseas safety travel site, Korean official legal information services and the United Nations treaty database. Figures that could not be confirmed, and country-by-country recognition lists, were deliberately left out.

This article is general information researched as of July 2026. Treaty membership, application procedures, fees, and the scope and duration of recognition in each country can change without notice. Figures are given with the date they were confirmed; changes after that date are not reflected here.

Before departure, always re-confirm with the Korean National Police Agency, the Korea Road Traffic Authority, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, and the official sources of your destination country. Treatment can differ by residence status, length of stay, and even by province or state within the same country.

Driving on documents that are not valid in that jurisdiction may be treated as driving without a licence and prosecuted under local law, and it can also weaken your position on an insurance claim. If anything is uncertain, not driving is the safest choice.

1. What the permit actually is - a certifying document, not a licence

One misunderstanding has to go first. An international driving permit does not grant a new driving privilege. It is closer to a document that certifies the domestic licence you already hold in an internationally readable form. Once that character is understood, most of the rules that follow explain themselves.

The Korean legal basis sits in the Road Traffic Act. Article 96 is titled driving of motor vehicles on an international driving permit or a mutually recognised foreign driving licence, and provides that a person who obtained such a permit from a competent foreign authority under a convention, agreement or arrangement may drive for one year from the date of entry into the country. The article lists four bases.

  • The Convention on Road Traffic concluded at Geneva in 1949 - the protagonist of this article, and the convention Korea is a party to.
  • The Convention on Road Traffic concluded at Vienna in 1968 - a separate treaty from Geneva, discussed in detail below.
  • Conventions, agreements or arrangements between Korea and a foreign state that mutually recognise international driving permits - bilateral arrangements outside the conventions.
  • Conventions, agreements or arrangements that mutually recognise the driving licences issued by the other state - here the licence itself is recognised, not the international permit.

The same article carries a restriction that matters in practice. An international driving permit may not be used to drive a commercial vehicle under the passenger transport or freight transport business statutes. There is an exception: driving a vehicle rented from a licensed vehicle rental business is allowed. A traveller renting a car and driving it personally falls inside that exception. Carrying passengers for a fare is an entirely different matter.

Vehicle categories are limited too: only the categories recorded on the permit itself. If your domestic licence covers ordinary cars only, the permit will show the corresponding range and nothing more. Anyone planning to drive a camper, a large van or a motorcycle abroad must check that range before departure. The same category confusion that appears in The Complete Used Car Buying Guide repeats itself across borders.

In short, the permit is an accessory document attached to a domestic licence. If the domestic licence is suspended or revoked, the permit loses its foundation. Korean official guidance states that when an application is made during a suspension period, the permit issued is valid for one year beginning the day after the suspension ends - a rule that only makes sense once you see the document as an accessory.

2. The core point - there are two conventions

This is why the article exists. There is no single international driving permit. More precisely, two conventions govern the document, and each prescribes a different model in its own annexes. The two documents differ in appearance, in how particulars are entered, and above all in which countries will accept them.

The reason is historical. A convention on road traffic was concluded at Geneva in 1949, and a more developed convention was concluded at Vienna in 1968. But not every state moved to the newer one. Some stayed with Geneva, some moved to Vienna, some joined both, and some joined neither. The result is a world map drawn as two overlapping zones of recognition.

The table below reflects the status pages of each convention in the United Nations treaty database, opened directly on 29 July 2026. These numbers move. The durable takeaway is not the count but the fact that the two treaties are separate.

The row on the status of the Republic of Korea is the single most important line in this article. In treaty practice signing and acceding are different acts. A signature indicates agreement with the aim of the treaty; becoming a contracting party requires a separate act of ratification or accession. In the participant table for the Vienna Convention, the Republic of Korea shows only the signature date of 29 December 1969, with the ratification and accession column empty.

That is how 「one permit, drive anywhere」 collapses. The document in your hand belongs to the Geneva family. If your destination is a party to Vienna but not to Geneva, then unless that state extends a separate courtesy, your document does not connect to any legal basis there. Not because it is fake, but because that country never joined the treaty that would make it readable.

3. Korea's status, and the trap of assuming recognition is reciprocal

The Korea Road Traffic Authority states plainly that Korea is a Geneva Convention country, which matches the accession date of 14 June 1971 in the United Nations treaty database. So far so clear. But the same guidance carries another sentence that creates confusion: since 1 January 2002 Korea also permits driving inside Korea on a valid international permit issued by a Vienna Convention country.

Reading that as so Korea joined Vienna as well gets the direction backwards. That sentence governs foreigners coming into Korea. It means the Korean government decided, in domestic law, to accept Vienna-model permits; it does not mean Korea became a contracting party. Article 96 of the Road Traffic Act indeed lists Geneva and Vienna side by side, but that article is about how to treat people arriving here.

  • Inbound - Korea accepts both Geneva-model and Vienna-model permits, allowing driving for one year from the date of entry. Permits issued in Hong Kong and Macau are also listed as recognised.
  • Outbound - when a Korean driver drives abroad, that country's rules apply. Our generosity gives the other state no reason to be generous back.
  • Conclusion - recognition is not automatically reciprocal. Read the destination country's notice, not your own country's.
  • Derived trap - online summaries often claim Korea joined both conventions. That reading appears to come from copying the domestic-law rule above into the treaty column. The primary source is the United Nations treaty database.

One more distinction. The one year in the Road Traffic Act runs from the date of entry, not the date of issue. The validity of the permit itself (one year from issue) and the period during which a given country lets you drive are two separate clocks. Ministry of Foreign Affairs guidance likewise notes that the permit is valid for one year from issue but that the driving period allowed may differ according to residence status. That is usually where long-stay residents stop being treated like tourists.

The item most often overlooked is the photo specification. Passport standard covers background, head size and how recently it was taken. Turning up with an old photo or an ordinary ID photo and being sent to reshoot on the spot is common. If a new passport is on the to-do list anyway, printing spare photos saves a second trip.

5. What must be carried together - the permit alone is not enough

This section is short and prevents more real damage than any other. Do not drive on the international permit alone. The Korea Road Traffic Authority states this in strong terms: in most foreign countries, including the United States, driving on an international permit without also carrying the Korean licence and passport may be punished as driving without a licence, and it instructs drivers to carry the permit, the domestic licence and the passport together.

  • The three are a set - the permit certifies a domestic licence, so without the licence there is nothing left to certify.
  • Originals, not copies - a copy is fine when applying, but the original must be carried when driving. A photo on a phone is not a substitute.
  • Matching romanisation - Korean official legal guidance warns that the document may be treated as ineffective when the romanised name spelling and the signature do not match. Compare passport and permit at the issuing counter.
  • Domestic licence validity - a licence suspended for a missed aptitude test takes the permit down with it. Check renewal deadlines before departure.
  • Split where you keep them - all three in one wallet means losing all three at once. Separating passport and licence documents changes how fast you recover.
  • On your person, not in the car - checks usually happen outside the vehicle, and after a collision you may not be able to reach the car at all.

One thing worth adding. What to do after a collision is not fundamentally different abroad, but doing it in another language and another procedure slows every step. Reading the basic order set out in Car Accident: Complete Guide on What to Do - secure safety, report, record, contact your insurer - once before departure makes the decisions faster when they have to be made quickly.

6. The English-language licence is not an international driving permit

The second common misunderstanding. Korea issues a domestic licence with English particulars printed on it, and many people treat that as equivalent to an international driving permit. They are entirely different instruments. An official interpretation by the Korean National Police Agency, published on the national law information portal, answers that the two are separate systems and that each may have to be obtained depending on the destination.

ItemInternational driving permit (IDP)English-language licence (bilingual)
NatureA separate document based on a conventionThe domestic licence itself, with English particulars
BasisInternational conventions such as Geneva 1949Not an agreement but a unilateral courtesy by the other state
RecognitionMainly convention parties, varying by country69 countries, 109 regions as of April 2026 (ministry guidance)
Permitted periodOne year from issue (may vary with residence status)Varies by country, mostly around three months
Carried withOriginal domestic licence plus passportPassport (the bilingual card is the domestic licence)
Form of issueA separate booklet bearing a passport photoReplacement of the existing card with a bilingual version
Where not recognisedNot applicable; this is the primary instrumentKorean licence plus an international permit plus passport required

The row to study is basis. Ministry of Foreign Affairs guidance describes the English-language licence as a unilateral benefit rather than reciprocity or an agreement: the other state allows short-term driving to a holder of the Korean bilingual licence and a passport without demanding a certified translation. Because it is a courtesy rather than a treaty, a single policy decision by that state can end it. That is the structural reason not to rely on the bilingual licence alone.

Korea Road Traffic Authority guidance on the bilingual licence carries the same warning. The permitted driving period differs by country and is mostly limited to around three months, and in countries that do not recognise it, the Korean licence, an international driving permit and a passport are required. It also advises that additional documents may be needed depending on the country, so confirm with that country's embassy before departure.

  • Short trip, recognised country - the bilingual licence and a passport may be enough, provided you confirmed recognition before leaving.
  • Short trip, not recognised - you need the international permit, and still must carry the domestic licence and passport with it.
  • Long stay or residence - both documents are temporary by design. Eventually you convert to, or obtain, a local licence.
  • Carrying both - it costs more time and money, but for a multi-country itinerary it is the safest combination.
  • Timing the bilingual licence - align it with an aptitude test, renewal or reissue so no extra visit is needed. Fees differ by issue type, so check the official page.

7. Outside the conventions - what is needed when the permit does not work

Some countries are party to neither convention, and some are parties that attach their own extra requirements. In those cases the permit alone does not settle it. This is precisely why the article carries no country list. Such a list goes stale for at least three reasons: treaty membership changes, national operating policy changes, and treatment can differ between provinces or states inside one country.

Remembering where to check lasts far longer. These four cover almost every case.

  • The Ministry of Foreign Affairs overseas safety travel site - country and region information alongside the mutual recognition of driving licences page, including the current bilingual-licence recognition figures.
  • The destination country's embassy in Korea, or the Korean embassy there - ministry guidance itself says to confirm through the Korean embassy or that country's embassy in Korea. This is the most reliable primary check.
  • Korea Road Traffic Authority guidance - for the side you control: issuance requirements, validity and documents to carry.
  • The destination country's own transport or licensing authority page - the final decision belongs to that state. If an English page exists, it is the most accurate source available.

When the convention route is closed, what remains is mutual recognition of driving licences. Ministry guidance describes it as a system letting a holder of a Korean licence exchange it for that country's licence without taking written or practical tests in recognising countries. It is excessive for a two-week holiday, but for someone staying several months and driving continuously it is effectively the only proper route. Cost planning for longer stays such as study or secondment is covered separately in Complete Guide to Study Abroad Costs.

One caveat. Exchange procedures differ by country and usually presuppose proof of residence or a residence status. A scenario where someone enters on a tourist visa and immediately applies for an exchange generally does not work. So the correct order is to settle residence status first, then investigate the exchange.

8. The rental counter blocks you by contract, not by law

The third layer. You cleared the convention, you confirmed national recognition, and you still get stopped at the counter. What blocked you there is not law but that company's rental conditions. A company may choose whom it lends vehicles to, and those criteria are normally set above the legal minimum.

Naming specific companies would be pointless. Conditions differ between operators, and within one operator they differ by country, branch and vehicle class. So remember them as items to verify before booking, not as brand names.

  • Minimum age - the legal driving age and the rental age are different numbers. Surcharges for young drivers are common.
  • Licence-holding period - many operators require the licence to have been held for a minimum period. Newly licensed drivers should check this first.
  • Payment method - a credit card in the driver's own name is frequently required. Debit cards or a card in someone else's name may be refused.
  • Security deposit - typically a hold placed on the card. An insufficient limit stops the rental on the spot.
  • Insurance and excess - the excess on the included cover, and which damages are excluded, are rarely visible on the headline rate.
  • Additional drivers - an unregistered driver at the wheel can void cover after a collision. Register anyone who will share the driving.
  • Cross-border and area limits - clauses may prohibit taking the vehicle into another country or into certain regions.
  • Documents demanded - some counters want only the permit, some check the domestic licence too, some ask for a separate translation.
  • Return conditions - fuel policy, one-way drop-off fees and late-return charges are the classic sources of later disputes.

Insurance is where layers overlap most. The cover sold by the rental company, the benefit attached to the payment card, and a separately purchased travel policy handle different risks on different terms. Duplicating the overlap wastes money; ignoring the gap shows up only after a crash. Cover structure and how to check for duplication are set out in Complete Guide to Travel Insurance, and for comparison with domestic motor cover concepts see Complete Car Insurance Guide.

9. The order to check before departure - fix it at five steps

Turning all of the above into a sequence gives the following. Keeping the order stops later steps from overturning earlier ones. Book the rental car first and check the licence question afterwards, and you will be paying cancellation fees while rebuilding the plan.

[Step 1] Fix the destinations
         └ list transit and neighbouring countries too (crossing a border?)

[Step 2] Which convention, and how is your licence treated there?
         └ check: foreign ministry travel site, embassy, destination authority
         └ outcome splits three ways: IDP needed / bilingual licence enough / neither works

[Step 3] Are extra documents required?
         └ translation, notarisation, proof of residence per country
         └ for a long stay, look into licence exchange (mutual recognition)

[Step 4] Rental conditions      <- a gate separate from the law
         └ minimum age / licence-holding period / credit card in own name
         └ deposit limit / additional driver registration / cross-border permission

[Step 5] Insurance design
         └ excess and exclusions on the rental cover
         └ card benefits and travel policy: overlap and gaps

# Suggested timing

Night before  : final check of three originals (permit, licence, passport)

The important part is that step 2 splits into three outcomes. Which of the three you land on changes both the paperwork and the remaining schedule completely. And when the answer is neither works, the final piece of advice in this article is not to force it. Public transport, a car with a driver, or letting a companion drive are all available.

10. The four places people actually get stopped

Failures rarely come from exotic edge cases. They repeat in the same four places.

Failure pointWhat happensCausePrevention before departure
Expired permitRefused at the counter, or a problem at a roadside checkThe permit runs one year from issue; last year's copy was reusedRead the issue date. Confirm validity through the last day of travel
Original not carriedMay be treated as driving without a licenceOnly the permit was packed; domestic licence and passport left behindCheck the three as one bundle. Photos and copies are not substitutes
Convention mismatchThe document is genuine but that country does not accept itDestination sits in the other convention zone or outside bothDo not skip step 2. Include transit and neighbouring countries

All four share one trait: they are ten-minute problems before departure and nearly unsolvable once abroad. An international driving permit is not something you can have reissued overseas, because its basis is a domestic licence and its issuing authority sits at home.

A hypothetical illustrates the point. Suppose a traveller prepared a permit for country A and then decides on impulse to drive across into neighbouring country B. Two questions arise at once: does B accept the document, and does the rental contract allow crossing the border. The first is a question of law, the second of contract, and failing either produces the same outcome. That is why this article insists on separating the three layers.

Conclusion: issuance is only the beginning

Getting an international driving permit does not take long. Confirming whether that document works where you are going takes longer, and varies by person. The whole point of this article is to spend that time before departure rather than at the counter.

  • There are two conventions - Geneva 1949 and Vienna 1968 are separate treaties with separate party lists. Per the United Nations treaty database, Korea is a party to Geneva (acceded 1971-06-14) and shows only a signature on Vienna (1969-12-29), with no ratification or accession.
  • Recognition is not reciprocal - Korea accepting Vienna-model permits inbound is a separate decision from any foreign state accepting Korean documents.
  • The bilingual licence is not an IDP - it is a unilateral courtesy rather than an agreement, with limited countries and limited duration.
  • The permit alone is not enough - permit, domestic licence and passport, all three as originals, form one set.
  • Clearing the law still leaves the contract - age, card and insurance conditions at the rental desk are a gate of their own.

A closing note on method. Leaving out the country-by-country list was a judgement, not laziness. Such tables are most dangerous when they look most authoritative, because they encourage readers to skip the verification step. Places to check were given instead. Lists go stale; procedures do not. Every figure here carries the date it was confirmed, and what happened after that date is something each reader has to open an official page to find out.

To repeat: this article is general information researched as of July 2026. Treaty membership, application procedures, fees and the scope of recognition in each country can change.

Before departure, always re-confirm with the Korean National Police Agency, the Korea Road Traffic Authority, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and your destination country's official sources. Rental conditions and insurance cover must be read in each company's own terms.

Driving without valid entitlement may be prosecuted as driving without a licence under local law. If verification is not complete, not driving is the safest choice.
