Tinnitus (Ringing in the Ears) Complete Guide - Causes, Types, Tests, Treatment and Management

From noise exposure to pulsatile tinnitus — telling the causes apart, and knowing which warning signs need a doctor's visit

Introduction: What Is Tinnitus, the Sound You Hear in a Silent Room

"There's a ringing in my ear that just won't stop." Tinnitus is the perception of sound in the ear or head when no external sound actually exists. The U.S. National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders (NIDCD) notes that roughly 10 to 25 percent of adults experience some degree of tinnitus, and Mayo Clinic similarly describes it as affecting about 15 to 20 percent of people, particularly older adults. Most cases are mild and temporary, but for some people it persists long enough to affect sleep, concentration, and mood.

Tinnitus is usually not a standalone disease in itself — it is more often a symptom produced by something else, such as hearing loss, noise exposure, or a vascular issue. That is why it helps to look past the sound itself and ask what might be generating it. This guide covers what tinnitus is, its types, common causes, how it is diagnosed, treatment and management options, everyday habits that help, and the warning signs that call for prompt medical care.

1. Types of Tinnitus - Subjective, Objective, and Pulsatile

Tinnitus is generally classified by how the sound is produced and who can hear it. Identifying the type is the first step toward narrowing down the cause and choosing the right tests.

1.1 Subjective vs. Objective Tinnitus

Tinnitus is broadly divided into subjective and objective types. Subjective tinnitus, audible only to the person experiencing it, accounts for the vast majority of cases and is thought to arise somewhere along the auditory nervous system's signal processing, which the brain then interprets as sound. Objective tinnitus is rare and can actually be heard by an examiner, for example with a stethoscope, because it stems from a real physical sound inside the body such as blood flow or muscle contractions.

1.2 Pulsatile Tinnitus

Pulsatile tinnitus is a rhythmic "whooshing" or "thumping" sound that beats in time with the heart. It often results from the sound of blood flow in vessels of the neck or head being transmitted to the ear. Causes can include high blood pressure, atherosclerosis (narrowed arteries), vascular malformations, and, rarely, vascular tumors called paragangliomas (glomus tumors). Because of this range of causes, pulsatile tinnitus more often warrants a thorough medical workup than other types.

2. Common Causes - Why You Hear Sound That Isn't There

2.1 Noise Exposure and Hearing Loss

Hearing loss is the factor most closely tied to tinnitus. According to Mayo Clinic, age-related hearing loss, prolonged noise exposure, ear injury, and circulatory system problems are among the leading causes. Repeated exposure to loud sound can damage the delicate structures of the inner ear that detect sound; as the signal from the affected frequency range weakens, the brain appears to fill that gap with a phantom tinnitus signal.

2.2 Earwax, Medications, and Eustachian Tube or Middle Ear Problems

A buildup of earwax can put pressure on the eardrum and trigger tinnitus — a common cause that a simple ear exam can identify and resolve. Certain medications (ototoxic drugs) can also cause or worsen tinnitus, so if tinnitus began after starting a new medication, it is worth discussing with the prescribing doctor. Eustachian tube dysfunction, middle ear infections, changes in the tiny bones of the ear (such as otosclerosis), and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) problems can also present alongside tinnitus.

2.3 Stress and Vascular or Systemic Conditions

Stress and fatigue do not directly cause tinnitus, but they commonly make existing tinnitus feel louder and more distressing. Systemic conditions that may seem unrelated to the ears — cardiovascular disease, allergies, anemia, and thyroid disorders — have also been linked to tinnitus.

3. Related Symptoms and the Link to Hearing Loss

Tinnitus frequently occurs together with hearing loss. A substantial share of people with tinnitus show some degree of hearing loss on testing, which is why tinnitus is rarely assessed in isolation — accompanying symptoms such as hearing loss, dizziness (vertigo), or a feeling of ear fullness are checked as part of the diagnostic picture. Tinnitus limited to one ear, tinnitus with a sudden onset, and tinnitus accompanied by dizziness deserve particularly close attention.

4. Diagnosis - What Doctors Check

4.1 Hearing Test (Pure-Tone Audiometry)

The American Academy of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery (AAO-HNS) clinical practice guideline recommends a hearing test (pure-tone audiometry) as the standard evaluation for tinnitus patients. Before testing, an otoscopic exam checks for earwax or eardrum abnormalities, since even a small amount of earwax against the eardrum can create a mass effect that produces high-frequency hearing loss and tinnitus. Air- and bone-conduction thresholds are then measured to determine whether hearing loss is present and what type it is (conductive or sensorineural).

4.2 Tinnitus Matching and Additional Evaluation

A tinnitus matching test may be used to characterize the pitch and loudness of the perceived sound. Clinicians may also screen for anxiety or depression, and when tinnitus is pulsatile, one-sided, or a neurological problem is suspected, imaging such as MRI, MRA, or CT may be ordered.

5. Treatment and Management Options

There is no single medication that cures tinnitus. The approach generally follows two tracks: when an underlying cause is identified — earwax, a medication, an untreated condition — that cause is addressed first; when no clear cause is found or tinnitus has become chronic, management strategies that make the sound less bothersome are used alongside.

5.1 Sound Therapy and Tinnitus Retraining Therapy (TRT)

Sound therapy uses white noise or background sound to reduce the contrast between tinnitus and silence, making the tinnitus feel less prominent. Tinnitus Retraining Therapy (TRT) combines sound therapy with professional counseling — education and behavioral techniques — and, per Mayo Clinic, is typically carried out over about 12 to 18 months, aiming to gradually retrain the brain to filter out the tinnitus signal.

5.2 Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and Counseling

Cognitive behavioral therapy addresses how a person reacts to and focuses on the sound rather than the sound itself. While it does not eliminate tinnitus, it is reported to help reduce the anxiety, sleep disruption, and distress that tinnitus can cause.

5.3 Hearing Aids and Treating Underlying Conditions

When noise-induced or age-related hearing loss is also present, hearing aids can help ease tinnitus by amplifying external sound, which reduces the contrast with the tinnitus and lightens the effort of listening. When an underlying condition such as high blood pressure or thyroid disease is identified, treating that condition is the starting point for managing the tinnitus. There is still no medication dedicated specifically to tinnitus, though a doctor may consider supportive medication when sleep or emotional difficulties are significant.

6. Everyday Habits - Living With Tinnitus

6.1 Managing Noise Exposure

Repeated exposure to loud sound — construction sites, concerts, high-volume earphones — can worsen both tinnitus and hearing loss. In noisy environments, wearing earplugs or noise-blocking earmuffs and keeping earphone volume at a reasonable level is advisable.

6.2 Caffeine, Stress, and Sleep

Some people report that caffeine, alcohol, and nicotine make their tinnitus feel louder, though individual variation is considerable, so it is practical to track your own reactions rather than assume a universal rule. Managing stress and keeping a regular sleep schedule will not eliminate tinnitus itself, but they are known to help reduce the subjective distress it causes. If falling asleep in a silent room is difficult, playing low-volume white noise can help.

7. Warning Signs - When to Seek Care Immediately

Do not delay an ENT evaluation if any of the following occur.

  • Pulsatile tinnitus that beats in time with your heart, especially if it starts suddenly
  • Tinnitus that occurs in only one ear
  • Sudden tinnitus with a rapid drop in hearing (possible sudden sensorineural hearing loss, which needs emergency care)
  • Tinnitus accompanied by severe headache, visual changes, dizziness, or altered consciousness
⚠️ Warning Signs - Seek Care Immediately. According to medical institutions such as Penn Medicine, sudden-onset pulsatile tinnitus accompanied by severe headache, visual disturbances (blurred or double vision), or altered consciousness can signal a vascular emergency and should never wait. Do not attempt to self-diagnose.

8. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. Can tinnitus be cured completely?

It depends on the cause. When the cause is clearly reversible — earwax, a specific medication — tinnitus can resolve once addressed. Chronic tinnitus is often managed rather than eliminated, using sound therapy, TRT, or CBT. No single medication cures tinnitus.

Q2. Is tinnitus a sign of a serious illness?

Most tinnitus is a benign symptom linked to noise exposure or hearing loss. However, pulsatile, one-sided, or sudden-hearing-loss-associated tinnitus can, in rare cases, point to a vascular problem or tumor and needs medical evaluation.

Q3. How long does Tinnitus Retraining Therapy (TRT) take?

TRT combines sound therapy with professional counseling and typically runs about 12 to 18 months, aiming to retrain the brain to filter out the tinnitus signal over time.

Q4. Can caffeine or stress make tinnitus worse?

Some people report improvement or worsening, but responses vary widely by individual. Tracking your own symptom changes is more useful than assuming a fixed rule.

Q5. Does tinnitus always come with hearing loss?

They are commonly linked but not always together. Some people with entirely normal hearing test results still experience tinnitus.

9. Conclusion

Tinnitus is a multifaceted symptom that is rarely explained by a single cause. The first steps are identifying the type — subjective, objective, or pulsatile — and reviewing possible causes such as hearing loss, noise exposure, earwax, medications, or systemic conditions, followed by a hearing test and any additional evaluation needed. Treatment often combines correcting an underlying cause with management approaches such as sound therapy, TRT, CBT, and hearing aids, while managing everyday noise exposure, stress, and sleep also helps ease symptoms. Above all, tinnitus that is pulsatile, one-sided, or accompanied by a sudden drop in hearing should never be put off — see an ENT specialist promptly.
