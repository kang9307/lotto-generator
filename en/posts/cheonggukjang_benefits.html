Cheonggukjang is a traditional Korean fermented soybean food made with a short fermentation. It is often described as "good for" many things, but it is rarely clear whether a claim comes from human studies or only from animal and cell experiments. This article follows a 2021 review (Int J Mol Sci 22(11):5746) and sorts ten commonly cited benefits into those with human studies and those at the animal or laboratory stage. Nothing here says that cheonggukjang treats or prevents disease.

One point first. Cheonggukjang is an ordinary food, not a functional ingredient recognized by Korea's food regulator (MFDS) as a health functional food. It does not appear on the regulator's list of recognized functional ingredients for women's menopausal health (Food Safety Korea), either. Isoflavones are handled under separate ingredient standards, and those standards cannot simply be transferred to a bowl of cheonggukjang.

What Is Cheonggukjang?

Cheonggukjang is a traditional Korean fermented food made by fermenting cooked soybeans, traditionally wrapped in rice straw. Fermentation is short, 2-3 days, and gives it a strong aroma and a sticky, stringy texture. The bacterium Bacillus subtilis plays a central role.

History and Cultural Background

Cheonggukjang has long been a traditional way of getting soy protein in Korean cooking. It was commonly made in winter and is used in stews and other dishes. This article could not confirm an exact date of origin.

The 10 Claimed Benefits, Read by Level of Evidence

The "level of evidence" below follows the study types the review describes. Even human studies are often small, or feed a fixed amount of cheonggukjang powder, so they cannot be carried over directly to an ordinary meal.

1. Digestion - evidence: not confirmed in this article

It is often said that fermentation microbes and enzymes can influence the gut environment. This article could not confirm human studies showing improvement in indigestion, constipation or diarrhea, so it does not present this as an effect.

2. Blood pressure - evidence: animal experiment

The blood-pressure result the review describes is from spontaneously hypertensive rats fed a soy protein hydrolysate, whose systolic pressure fell. The earlier claim that hypertensive patients lowered systolic pressure by 5-10 mmHg over 8 weeks could not be confirmed in the source and was deleted. If you need blood-pressure control, discuss treatment and overall diet with your clinician.

3. Cholesterol - evidence: small human studies

The review describes a study in adults aged 25-60 in which the group eating Bacillus-fermented cheonggukjang had lower LDL and non-HDL cholesterol. A study of adults with mild hyperglycemia who ate 20 g a day for 8 weeks is also reported to show lower fasting glucose and LDL. The earlier "total cholesterol -8%, LDL -12%" and "HDL increase" could not be confirmed and were deleted.

4. Immunity - evidence: human evidence not confirmed

The human study the review describes, in healthy Korean men aged 21-29, concerns better isoflavone absorption (bioavailability) after cheonggukjang; results on immune markers were not found. Statements such as "raises NK cell activity" could not be sourced and were removed.

5. Antioxidant activity - evidence: compound analysis and experiments

That it contains antioxidant compounds such as isoflavones is a statement about composition. The earlier "2-3 times higher than plain soybeans" could not be confirmed and was deleted, and there is no confirmed evidence that these compounds slow aging in people.

6. Bone health - evidence: animal and cell experiments (no human trials)

The review has no human trials on bone, only mouse, rat and cell experiments. That vitamin K2 takes part in bone metabolism is general nutrition knowledge, but this article could not confirm that eating cheonggukjang prevents osteoporosis or slows bone-density loss. The postmenopausal bone-density sentence was deleted.

7. Weight management - evidence: small human studies

Human studies described in the review include overweight or obese adults who ate 26 g of freeze-dried cheonggukjang powder for 12 weeks and had lower plasma apoB, and a 12-week 35 g study in which women had lower body-fat ratio and waist circumference and lower hs-CRP. These fed a fixed amount of powder, so they do not mean that eating cheonggukjang makes you lose weight.

8. Anticancer - evidence: cell and animal experiments (no human trials)

The review has no human trials, only cell and animal experiments. The earlier sentence about helping prevent breast, prostate and colorectal cancer was deleted. There is no evidence that cheonggukjang prevents or treats cancer, and this article does not claim it.

9. Skin - evidence: small human studies

The review describes a study of middle-aged Korean women who ate cheonggukjang, with less melanin and fewer red spots, and changes in sebum and moisture indicators and in skin pH. "Fewer wrinkles and better elasticity" could not be confirmed and was deleted.

10. Inflammation - evidence: part small human study, part experiments

In the 35 g, 12-week study above, the inflammation marker hs-CRP was reported to fall. This article could not confirm that symptoms of inflammatory diseases such as arthritis improve, so that claim was deleted.

Things to Keep in Mind

These come before any talk of benefits.

  1. Warfarin users (most important): cheonggukjang is a fermented soy food rich in vitamin K. Vitamin K affects the action of anticoagulants such as warfarin, so if you take one, talk to a doctor or pharmacist first and keep your intake consistent (US NIH Office of Dietary Supplements advice). The review mentions clotting-enzyme (thrombolytic) activity measured in experiments; that does not mean eating it dissolves clots, and it has not been shown in people.
  2. Sodium: this article could not confirm the sodium figure for cheonggukjang itself, but salt and soy sauce added to stews raise intake easily. The Korean food regulator (then KFDA) advised staying under 5 g of salt (2,000 mg sodium) a day, in line with the WHO. If you have hypertension or kidney disease, limit the broth and talk to your clinician.
  3. Purines: people with gout are advised to limit foods high in purines (KDCA). This article could not confirm cheonggukjang's purine content, so decide the amount with your doctor.
  4. Soy allergy: people with soy allergies should avoid cheonggukjang.

How to Include Cheonggukjang in Your Diet

Common ways to eat it. In every case taste before adding salt or soy sauce, and take less of the broth.

  1. Cheonggukjang jjigae (stew): the most traditional way, simmered with tofu and vegetables.
  2. Cheonggukjang bibimbap: rice topped with cheonggukjang and vegetables, mixed before eating.
  3. Cheonggukjang fried rice: stir-fried with rice and vegetables.
  4. Cheonggukjang and doenjang soup: uses both pastes for a soup. Sodium can climb, so season lightly.
  5. Cheonggukjang spread: sometimes suggested as a spread on bread or crackers.

Conclusion

Cheonggukjang is a traditional fermented soy food. Human studies offer small results on cholesterol, body fat, skin and inflammation markers, but talk of blood pressure, bones and cancer is mostly at the animal or cell stage. This article does not say that cheonggukjang prevents or treats disease.

Enjoy it as one option in a healthy diet, but if you take warfarin or another anticoagulant, or have gout, hypertension or a soy allergy, talk to a doctor, pharmacist or other professional first.
