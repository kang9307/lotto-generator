How to Read Your Blood Test Results - A Complete Guide to Key Values & Normal Ranges

CBC, Liver, Kidney, Lipid, Glucose, and Thyroid Values Explained

2026-07-09 Health

Introduction: The Direction of the Number Matters More Than the Number Itself

A few days after your annual health checkup, a report arrives by mail or app. Across several pages, unfamiliar abbreviations and numbers sit next to a small "normal range." Some values fall inside the range; others are flagged with an arrow or asterisk just outside it. At that point, most people do one of two things: ignore the whole thing and file it away, or type the item name into a search engine and read about the worst-case scenario first.

This article is for the middle ground. It explains what the items that most often appear on a report - the complete blood count (CBC), liver function, kidney function, lipids, glucose, and thyroid - actually show, what the phrase "normal range" really means, and how to respond reasonably when a single number falls outside it, based on publicly available health-authority material as of July 2026. One thing up front: this is not a diagnostic tool. It will not, and cannot, declare "you have condition X" based on a specific number. The goal is to help you have a more informed conversation with your doctor, instead of vague anxiety or careless dismissal when you open the report.

1. What "Normal Range" Actually Means - Before You Read the Numbers

Next to every item on a report is usually a reference range, commonly called the normal range. But this range is not an absolute standard.

1.1 Ranges Differ Between Labs

Even for the same test, reference ranges are set slightly differently from hospital to hospital and lab to lab depending on the equipment, reagents, and measurement method used. One lab might list the upper limit for a liver enzyme (AST) as 35, while another lists 40. So when reading your report, use the reference range printed on your own report rather than a number found online.

1.2 Differences by Age, Sex, and Condition

A reference range is usually derived statistically from measuring a large number of healthy adults, representing the band where most of them fall. By definition, some healthy people fall outside it. What counts as normal can also shift with age, sex, pregnancy, muscle mass, and ethnic background. Hemoglobin, for example, has different normal ranges for men and women, and the threshold is often lowered during pregnancy.

1.3 Out of Range Does Not Automatically Mean Disease

A value slightly outside the range does not automatically mean you have a disease. Your condition before the test, meals, hydration, recent exercise, or stress can all shift a value temporarily. Conversely, being inside the range does not mean everything is perfect either. A report should be treated as material for an overall interpretation, not a standalone verdict - the final call belongs to a doctor weighing your history, symptoms, and other tests together.

2. Complete Blood Count (CBC) - Red Cells, White Cells, Platelets, Hemoglobin

The complete blood count (CBC) is one of the most basic tests, counting the cellular components of your blood.

2.1 Red Blood Cells and Hemoglobin - Oxygen Carriers

Red blood cells (RBCs) carry oxygen from the lungs to the rest of the body, and hemoglobin (Hb) is the iron-containing protein inside those cells that binds oxygen. Many references cite roughly 13-18 g/dL for adult men and roughly 12-16 g/dL for adult women (non-pregnant) as a general reference range, though this also varies by lab. Low hemoglobin often raises the possibility of what is commonly called anemia, but the cause can range from iron deficiency to chronic disease to bleeding, so identifying the cause comes first.

2.2 White Blood Cells (WBC) - The Immune Cells

White blood cells fight infection and inflammation. A commonly cited adult reference range is roughly 4,000-11,000 cells per microliter. A high count may point to infection, inflammation, or a stress response, while a low count may point to reduced bone marrow function or the effect of certain medications - but the WBC count alone cannot identify the cause; the differential (neutrophils, lymphocytes, etc.) and symptoms need to be considered together.

2.3 Platelets - The Cells Behind Clotting

Platelets clump together to help stop bleeding when it occurs. A commonly cited adult reference range is roughly 150,000-400,000 per microliter. Platelet counts that are too low may be associated with bruising or a bleeding tendency, while counts that are too high may be associated with clotting risk, and either may call for further testing.

2.4 Other Indices - MCV, RDW, and More

Reports often also include detailed indices such as the average size of a red blood cell (MCV) or how uniform red cell sizes are (RDW). These are supporting indices that help classify the "type" of anemia, and they matter more as part of the overall combination than as isolated numbers.

3. Liver Function Tests - AST, ALT, and GGT

Liver function tests measure enzyme levels that indirectly reflect liver cell damage or bile duct problems.

3.1 AST and ALT - Signals of Liver Cell Damage

AST (aspartate aminotransferase) and ALT (alanine aminotransferase) are enzymes normally found inside liver cells that leak into the blood when those cells are damaged. Ranges vary by lab, but many references cite roughly 10-40 IU/L as a general adult reference range. ALT is relatively more liver-specific, while AST can also come from muscle and other tissue, so the two are usually read together. Elevated values can be linked to fatty liver, alcohol use, certain medications, or viral hepatitis, among other causes, so identifying the cause comes first.

3.2 GGT (Gamma-Glutamyl Transferase) - A Marker of Bile Ducts and Alcohol

GGT is an enzyme known to be sensitive to bile duct obstruction and alcohol intake. Many references cite a general range of roughly 10-50 U/L, though thresholds sometimes differ by sex. It can rise with ongoing alcohol use or certain medications, and an elevated result is sometimes used as a prompt to review lifestyle habits.

3.3 Why a Single Number Cannot Confirm a Diagnosis

An elevated liver enzyme does not automatically mean a specific liver disease. Because the appropriate response depends entirely on the underlying cause, the usual next step after a confirmed rise is further history-taking and testing (ultrasound, viral markers, and so on) to narrow down the cause.

4. Kidney Function Tests - Creatinine and eGFR

Kidney function is assessed using creatinine, a waste product, and eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate), which is calculated from it.

4.1 Creatinine - A Waste Product of Muscle Metabolism

Creatinine is a waste product generated as muscles use energy, and the kidneys filter it out into urine. When kidney function declines, unfiltered creatinine builds up in the blood and the level rises. However, creatinine is strongly influenced by muscle mass, so someone with more muscle may show a somewhat higher value even with healthy kidneys.

4.2 eGFR - How Well the Kidneys Filter Blood

eGFR is an estimate of how much blood the kidneys filter per minute, calculated from the creatinine level along with age, sex, and other factors (unit: mL/min/1.73m²). Generally, 90 or above is considered normal or near-normal function, and 60-89 is described as mildly reduced but not necessarily indicative of disease if there is no other evidence of kidney damage. A result persistently below 60 for three months or more prompts evaluation for possible chronic kidney disease, and the lower the number goes (for example, 15 or below), the more severely reduced kidney function is considered to be. These bands are general reference points, and the final assessment is made by a doctor combining repeat measurements with other tests, such as for protein in the urine. For more on managing chronic kidney disease, see our Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) Complete Guide.

4.3 Trend Over Time Matters More Than a Single Value

Because eGFR can be affected by same-day conditions such as dehydration or muscle mass changes, a trend across several measurements matters far more than a single result. If your result is borderline, discussing a retest timeline with your doctor is more useful than worrying immediately.

5. Lipid Panel - Total Cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and Triglycerides

The lipid panel is a widely used test that measures the fats in your blood to help gauge cardiovascular risk.

5.1 Total Cholesterol and LDL - "Bad" Cholesterol

Total cholesterol is commonly cited as desirable below 200 mg/dL. LDL cholesterol, often called "bad" cholesterol, can build up in artery walls and is linked to atherosclerosis; many references cite below 100 mg/dL as an appropriate level for the general population without cardiovascular disease, while targets are set much lower (for example, below 70) for those who already have cardiovascular disease. In other words, what counts as a "good" LDL number depends on your individual cardiovascular risk. For practical steps, see our guide to lowering LDL cholesterol naturally.

5.2 HDL - "Good" Cholesterol

HDL cholesterol is called "good" cholesterol because of its role in carrying cholesterol from blood vessels back to the liver. Many references cite above 40 mg/dL for men and above 50 mg/dL for women as desirable, and unlike most other markers, a higher HDL is generally considered favorable rather than a low one.

5.3 Triglycerides

Triglycerides are commonly classified as normal below 150 mg/dL, borderline-high at 150-199, high at 200-499, and very high at 500 or above. Triglycerides are especially sensitive to recent meals and alcohol, so whether the test was done fasting is an important factor in interpreting the result.

5.4 The Whole Combination and Overall Risk Matter More Than One Number

Lipid values matter more when read as the full combination of total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and triglycerides, together with other risk factors such as smoking, blood pressure, and diabetes, rather than any single item in isolation. Since target values are also set individually based on cardiovascular risk, it is more accurate to assess overall risk with a doctor than to feel reassured or worried based solely on the "normal range" flag on the report.

6. Glucose Tests - Fasting Glucose and HbA1c

Glucose-related tests are widely used to screen for diabetes, and they look at blood sugar from different angles.

6.1 Fasting Glucose - A Snapshot in Time

Fasting glucose is measured after at least eight hours without food, and a widely used classification is under 100 mg/dL as normal, 100-125 as prediabetes, and 126 or above in the diabetes range. Because whether you actually fasted, what you ate the day before, and your condition on the day can all shift the number, a single value is not treated as conclusive on its own.

6.2 HbA1c - A Roughly Two- to Three-Month Average

HbA1c reflects the proportion of hemoglobin in red blood cells that has glucose attached, representing an average blood sugar level over roughly the past two to three months. A widely used classification is under 5.7% as normal, 5.7-6.4% as prediabetes, and 6.5% or above in the diabetes range. It has the advantage of not requiring fasting and being less affected by day-to-day fluctuations, but the result can be skewed by certain blood disorders, including some forms of anemia. If your result is borderline, see our complete guide to prediabetes and blood sugar control.

6.3 Why Both Tests Are Considered Together

Fasting glucose and HbA1c do not always move together - fasting glucose may be normal while HbA1c is borderline, or the reverse. Because of this, doctors weigh both alongside symptoms, family history, and weight changes, and a borderline result is usually confirmed with a retest to check the trend.

7. Thyroid Function Tests - Focusing on TSH

The thyroid is an organ that secretes hormones regulating metabolism, and health checkups commonly use TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) as the first-line marker.

7.1 What Is TSH?

TSH is a signaling hormone released by the pituitary gland that regulates thyroid hormone production. When thyroid hormone is insufficient, the pituitary releases more TSH, raising the level; when thyroid hormone is in excess, TSH tends to be suppressed and the level falls. Many references cite roughly 0.4-4.5 mIU/L as a general reference range, though this too varies by lab and assay method.

7.2 When the Value Is Borderline

A TSH slightly outside the reference range does not automatically mean thyroid disease - pregnancy, certain medications, and your condition at the time of testing can also play a role. When TSH alone is not conclusive, it is common to check additional tests such as free T4 and free T3 alongside symptoms like fatigue, weight change, or pulse changes. For more detail, see our complete guide to hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism.

8. Preparing for the Test - Why and How Long to Fast

How accurately your results can be interpreted also depends heavily on preparation before the test.

8.1 Why Fasting Is Needed

Tests that include glucose and lipids, particularly triglycerides, can show a temporary spike right after eating, making accurate interpretation difficult. Because of this, 8-12 hours of fasting before the test is commonly advised, during which anything other than plain water - including sweetened water, coffee, and juice - is generally best avoided.

8.2 What to Watch For While Fasting

Plain water is usually allowed, but flavored or sweetened water, gum, and candy can also affect results and are best avoided. Whether to stop your regular medications before the test should always be confirmed with your care team in advance - stopping on your own is not recommended. Strenuous exercise or alcohol on the day of the test can also affect certain values (such as liver enzymes or muscle-related markers), so it is best to avoid them.

8.3 Some Tests Do Not Require Fasting

Not every blood test requires fasting. For example, HbA1c and thyroid function tests (TSH) are often described as not strictly requiring it. However, in a health checkup where multiple tests are done at once, fasting instructions typically cover the whole panel, so following the pre-test guidance from the specific clinic or center you booked is the most accurate approach.

9. When You Need a Retest or Further Workup

Here are general guidelines for when a signal on your report warrants moving to the next step.

9.1 When a Borderline Value Repeats

If a single test shows a borderline result, the usual approach is a retest after some period (weeks to months) to check the direction. A value that keeps moving the same way (for example, gradually rising) is treated as a much stronger signal than a single borderline reading.

9.2 When Multiple Related Items Are Out of Range Together

When several related items are out of range at the same time - for example, liver function and lipids, or glucose and lipids - the possibility of an underlying condition is considered more actively than when only one item is out of range on its own.

9.3 When Symptoms Are Present

If abnormal values are accompanied by symptoms such as fatigue, weight change, swelling, jaundice, or a bleeding tendency, it is safer to seek care sooner regardless of whether the report technically flags a value as out of range.

9.4 Bringing Your Report to the Doctor

When discussing a retest or further workup, bringing past checkup results to show the trend is very helpful. Asking "How has this value changed over the past few years, and what else should I check now?" tends to get a more useful answer than simply asking "Is this number normal?"

Conclusion - Your Report Is the Start of a Conversation

The numbers on a health checkup report are not a final verdict - they are the starting point for a conversation with your doctor. From the oxygen-carrying, immune, and clotting picture shown by the CBC, to the burden on liver cells shown by liver function tests, the filtering capacity shown by kidney function, the metabolic and cardiovascular risk shown by lipids and glucose, and the metabolic regulation shown by the thyroid - each item opens a different window onto the body. What matters is not reacting to a single number in isolation, but watching which direction it is moving and how it combines with the other items.

As of July 2026, this principle still holds. Normal ranges differ by testing facility, a single abnormal value does not automatically mean disease, and the final interpretation always belongs to a medical professional. We hope this article helps you face your next report with fewer vague worries or false reassurance, and better questions ready for your doctor.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. My result is slightly outside the normal range - do I have a disease?

Not necessarily. Reference ranges are statistical intervals derived from measuring many healthy people, so by definition some healthy people fall outside them. Your condition on the day, recent meals, and the timing of the test can also shift a value temporarily. If a value keeps trending the same direction or you have symptoms, discuss the cause with a doctor.

Q2. Why do normal ranges differ between hospitals or labs?

Different labs use different equipment, reagents, and measurement methods, so reference ranges vary slightly between facilities. Use the range printed on your own report rather than a generic number found online, and keep in mind that normal ranges differ by testing facility.

Q3. If my liver enzymes are a little high, does that mean my liver is damaged?

An elevated number alone cannot confirm a specific condition. Causes range from fatty liver and alcohol use to recent medications or strenuous exercise. When a rise is confirmed, further history-taking and testing typically follow to narrow down the cause. See a doctor rather than self-diagnosing.

Q4. How long should I fast before a blood test?

Tests involving glucose and lipids (especially triglycerides) commonly require 8-12 hours of fasting, avoiding anything but plain water. Some tests do not require fasting, so follow the specific instructions from the clinic or center where you booked your checkup.

Q5. How soon should I get a retest?

There is no single rule; timing depends on which value is out of range and by how much. Borderline results are often rechecked a few weeks to months later, but the exact timing and any additional tests should be decided by your doctor based on your individual situation.
