How to Read a Badminton Racket Spec Sheet - What 3U, G5 and Pounds Actually Mean

Weight code, grip size, balance, shaft flex and tension. And where beginners spend money in the wrong place

2026-09-01 Life & Practical

The first visit to a racket wall usually goes the same way. A cheap racket and one that costs ten times as much hang two hooks apart, and the sticker on each says something like 3U G5. The shop assistant asks what weight and what balance you want, and there is nothing to answer from. So most people end up choosing on price, or on colour.

This piece is about reading that sticker. It works through what each line of a spec sheet actually measures, and what difference that number makes on court. Every figure here comes either from a manufacturer product page or from the Laws of Badminton.

Figures here were checked in September 2026 against manufacturer product pages and the Laws of Badminton. Where model names appear, they are there to show how a spec is written, not as a recommendation of any product. Anything said about injury is general, and pain is worth having looked at properly.

The five lines on a spec sheet

A racket spec sheet is five lines long at most. Weight, grip size, balance, shaft flex, recommended string tension. The first two are printed as codes by the manufacturer, and the rest are written out as words or left off entirely. The cheaper the racket, the more often those last three are simply missing.

There is one more, printed in small type on the side of the shaft: the recommended tension range. That is not marketing copy but the band the manufacturer says the frame will take, and it varies a good deal by model.

  • Weight - a grade code with a number in front of U, such as 3U, 4U or 5U
  • Grip size - G4, G5, G6. A grade for handle thickness
  • Balance - head-heavy, even, head-light. Where the centre of mass sits
  • Shaft flex - from flexible through to extra stiff. How readily the shaft bends
  • Recommended tension - a range in pounds. The band the manufacturer says the frame will take
  • Materials - what the frame and the shaft are each made of, usually at the bottom of the table

Weight - the bigger the number in front of U, the lighter

U is a weight grade code, and this is where nearly everyone trips once. The bigger the number, the lighter the racket. A 3U is heavier than a 4U, and 5U is lighter still. It runs against everyday intuition, which is how people end up asking for the wrong thing in a shop and walking out with a racket they did not want.

The published figures make the spacing clear. Yonex prints an average weight next to the grade on each product page, and those come out at 88g for a 3U model, 83g for a 4U and 78g for a 5U. That is 5g per grade.

CodePublished averageHow it feels in the handWhere it usually sits
3U88gSolid. The weight itself pulls the swing pathSingles played on power, or anyone who likes some heft
4U83gNeither light nor heavyThe grade most often on the wall. Singles and doubles alike
5U78gTurns on the wrist aloneDoubles with fast front-to-back exchanges, or to spare the wrist
2U and heavierRarely publishedDistinctly heavyHard to find in an ordinary shop

Five grams sounds like nothing until you remember the whole racket is in the 80g range. That is about six per cent, and because a racket rotates about the handle, where that mass sits changes the feel by more than the raw number suggests.

The published weight is the racket alone. Strings, the original grip, an overgrip and a dampener all go on top of it. What you actually lift is heavier than the label.

Why lighter is not always better

The standard advice to start light is only half right. The force delivered to the shuttle comes from racket mass and swing speed together. If one is small the other has to grow to reach the same result. Pick up a light racket before the swing is built, and the long clear from your back court to theirs will not get there.

And then what happens. It does not carry, so you hit harder. Arm and shoulder tighten, and a tight arm makes a rigid swing. That is the path by which a light racket can build the habit of muscling the shot.

The other direction exists too. A heavy racket makes an already late racket later. If the face does not reach the shuttle in time, there is no chance for the mass to do anything at all. So in practice the decision splits like this.

  • The swing is there but the shuttle does not reach the back - the racket may be on the light side
  • The racket arrives late to the shuttle - it may be on the heavy side
  • Neither, and you are not finding the middle of the face - this is not a weight problem. The racket is not yet the variable
  • The wrist or elbow aches afterwards - look at shaft flex and tension before weight

The claim that a heavier racket teaches a better swing shape is common in club conversation. It is widely repeated, but I could not confirm any basis for it. What can be said with confidence is that weight changes how much you feel, and no further.

Grip size - G gets thinner as the number rises too

G runs in the same direction. The bigger the number, the thinner the handle. Since U and G share the rule, there is only one thing to remember: on a badminton racket, a bigger number means a smaller thing.

Product pages show that the sizes offered differ by model. Entry ranges come in G4 and G5, premium ranges in G5 and G6. So the thickness you want is only available if that model offers it, and the constraint bites harder at the cheap end.

Only one direction is reversible. Making a thin handle thicker is a matter of wrapping an overgrip. Making a thick handle thinner means stripping the original grip, and once stripped it is hard to put back. When in doubt, start thin.

If you want a number to work with, use the overgrip. The published spec for the Yonex overgrip is 25mm wide, 1,200mm long and 0.6mm thick. Wrap that thickness evenly around a handle and the circumference grows by roughly 3.8mm. Overgrips are wrapped with an overlap, though, so the real increase can be more than that.

  • If the fingertips reach the palm with room to spare, the handle is on the thin side
  • If the fingers splay and the hand will not close, it is on the thick side
  • Too thick and the wrist turns less, which can blunt the end of the swing
  • Too thin and you squeeze it for fear of losing it, and squeezing tires the forearm fast
  • If you sweat a lot you will end up double-wrapping. That adds thickness too

Balance - what attacking and defensive actually point at

Balance is where the centre of mass sits, given as the distance from the butt of the handle. The longer that distance, the more mass sits toward the head. Two rackets of identical weight can take quite different effort to swing, in the same way that a stick with a weight on the end is harder to whip than a bare one.

TypeCentre of massWhat happens in the swingWhat it costs
Head-heavyToward the headMore momentum for the same swing. Carries to the backHeavy to turn. Front-to-back changes and fast defence lag
EvenNear the middleA blend of both. No extremesNot the best at either end
Head-lightToward the handleChanges racket direction quickly. Good at the net and in defenceThe same swing carries less distance

So what are attacking and defensive in the product copy. The manufacturer's own material gives it away. In its racket selector Yonex splits its ranges into power, speed and accuracy, and then places each model on a two-axis grid. One axis runs head-heavy to head-light, the other hard feel to soft feel.

Which means attacking is usually another name for head-heavy plus a stiff shaft, and defensive another name for head-light plus a softer one. Not two separate properties, just one grid described in different words. The same document also marks a skill level against every model: basic skills, developing skills, enhanced skills. That labelling is a good deal more honest than the sales copy.

Shaft flex - the one that has to match your swing

The shaft bends back during the swing and springs forward just before impact. Power arrives when that return coincides with the moment the shuttle meets the strings. Choosing a flex is therefore not a matter of taste but a matter of timing.

A fast swing bends the shaft further and it snaps back that much harder. Give that player a soft shaft and it bends too far, returns too late, and the direction wanders. A slow swing on a stiff shaft has the opposite problem: the shaft barely bends at all. Nothing comes back from it, so the arm does all the work, and the impact goes into the arm as well. That is the combination where the elbow or shoulder can take a load.

LabelHow much it bendsSwing it suitsWhat goes wrong when it does not
Flexible rangeBends a lotSlower swings. The starting stageAs the swing speeds up, direction wanders
MediumIn betweenA swing that is still formingBoth properties can feel half-present
StiffBends lessFast swings, with control as the priorityA slow swing gets nothing loaded into the shot
Extra stiffBarely bendsA finished swingImpact goes straight into the arm

Put a manufacturer's own pages side by side and it shows immediately. On Yonex product pages, two entry-range models are both listed as Hi-Flex, at the bending end. The top attacking model is Extra Stiff. That single line is most of what people mean when they say a premium racket does not suit a beginner.

One caution. Flex labels are a relative scale inside a brand. Two companies using the same word give no guarantee of the same stiffness, and I could not find a standard test specification for it.

String tension - pounds, and the racket's own ceiling

Tension is the force the string is pulled to, written in pounds. This is where beginners most often hear a wrong number, along the lines of 24 pounds is standard. Tension ceilings differ by racket, and manufacturers publish a different one per model.

Line up the values from Yonex product pages and the spread is obvious. An entry model with an aluminium frame is listed at 16 to 20 pounds in 3U. Carbon entry models come in at 20 to 27 and 20 to 28 pounds. The top carbon model is 20 to 28 pounds in 4U and 21 to 29 pounds in 3U. Which means putting 24 pounds on the aluminium entry racket is outside the range the maker printed.

CategoryFrame materialPublished weightShaft labelRecommended tension
Entry aluminium modelAluminium (graphite shaft)3U, avg 88gNot listed16 - 20 lbs
Entry carbon model AGraphite5U, avg 78gHi-Flex20 - 27 lbs
Entry carbon model BGraphite4U, avg 83gHi-Flex20 - 28 lbs
Top carbon modelHigh-modulus graphite composite3U, avg 88gExtra Stiff21 - 29 lbs

Why a lower tension helps early on is simple enough. A looser bed stretches further on contact and briefly holds the shuttle before releasing it. Much of the rebound comes from the string rather than the arm, so the same distance costs less effort. And because the whole face stretches, shots that land off centre still travel.

High tension is the reverse. The string stretches less, the shuttle stays on the face for a shorter time, and it therefore goes where it was aimed. In exchange the string is not supplying the power, so swing speed has to, and the forgiving area shrinks so a mis-hit drops away sharply. Raising tension is a trade: control bought with forgiveness.

The warranty is worth a look too. The Yonex USA warranty covers defects in materials and workmanship for a year, and lists string breakage and improper restringing among the exclusions, citing deviation from the official stringing pattern as an example of the latter. No tension figure appears as a condition. The same company publishes its stringing patterns hole by hole per model, which is the other half of that point. It is why a racket should not go to just anyone for stringing.

Tension drops even when nobody plays

This one is not on the spec sheet and matters more than most of what is. The string maker Ashaway states that a racket loses roughly ten per cent of its tension the day after it is strung. That is with the racket standing untouched. The cause is creep, the string slowly elongating at a molecular level.

The important part is that it does not reverse. The same source explains that re-tightening a loosened bed does not restore the original resilience. So 25 pounds is the value at the moment of stringing. A month later that racket is not at 25 pounds.

The same maker has also set out why strings break. The most common cause is stretching the string past its elastic limit, and the break tends to happen on the central main strings near the head, from hits outside the sweet spot. So frequent breakage may be less about bad string than a signal that the contact point is wandering. The same material notes that a cracked grommet, the plastic ring in the hole the string passes through, will present an edge that cuts into the string.

  • The first few days after stringing are the fastest-dropping stretch. The feel straight off the machine is a poor baseline
  • The rule of thumb of restringing as many times a year as you play per week is widely repeated, but I could not find a source for it in manufacturer or federation material
  • The usable signal is that the impact sound goes dull. Sound is sensitive to tension change
  • The second is that the same swing carries less. Shots to the back show it first
  • The third is a grid that stays skewed. You push it straight and it slips back within a rally
  • One broken string ends that racket's day. It is the first reason people end up with a spare

Materials - where aluminium and carbon part company

The material split you meet most often on entry rackets is aluminium against carbon, that is graphite. The difference shows up in the tension ceiling before it shows up in feel. From the table above, the aluminium frame tops out at 20 pounds and the carbon ones at 27 to 29. The frames withstand different loads, so the band you can string within is different.

What is interesting is that it is not about weight. That aluminium model is a 3U at an average 88g, the same grade as the 3U of the top carbon model. The difference is what that same weight can be spent on. Carbon reaches a given stiffness with less mass, which is how 4U and 5U grades exist at all, and it leaves budget to distribute toward head or handle to design a balance.

So what does the price actually buy. As the number rises, this is what changes.

  • Frame material - aluminium to graphite, then to high-modulus composites
  • Tension ceiling - the band widens as the frame takes more
  • Number of options - weight and grip combinations go from one to several
  • How narrow the design is - it is optimised for a particular swing, and suits you less if that is not yours
  • What does not change - length and string bed size. The Laws cap those

That last line is the point. A premium racket is not a racket that fits better but one that fits more narrowly. For someone inside that band it is clearly better, and for someone outside it, worse than a cheap one.

What the Laws fix, and what they leave open

Not one of the things covered so far is set by the Laws. Weight, balance, flex and tension are all at the manufacturer's discretion. U and G are trade notation, not an international standard, which is why they can differ slightly between companies.

What the Laws do fix is size and attachments. Law 4 limits the racket frame to 680mm in overall length and 230mm in overall width. The stringed area may not exceed 280mm in length and 220mm in width, and where the stringing extends into the throat that extension may not exceed 35mm in width, with the overall stringed length not exceeding 330mm. Law 4.3 bars attached objects and protrusions other than those used solely to limit wear or vibration or to distribute weight, and bars any device that would materially change the shape of the racket. That first exception is what grip tape and dampeners rely on.

The shuttle is specified much more tightly. Law 2 sets the following for a feathered shuttle.

There is a speed test as well. Struck with a full underhand stroke from beyond the back boundary line, a shuttle of correct speed lands not less than 530mm and not more than 990mm short of the other back boundary line. The speed number on the tube exists to get a shuttle through that test.

Three places beginners spend money badly

The first is a lot of money on a first racket. As seen above, premium rackets are frequently head-heavy plus extra stiff, and that is a design that assumes a fast swing already exists. It does not help, and it passes impact straight into the arm. On top of that, a broken string ends the session, and with only one racket there is no spare. Past a certain point two reasonable rackets beat one expensive one.

The second is not counting the consumables. A racket is bought once, shuttles and strings keep going out the door. Feather shuttles are consumable; nylon shuttles last far longer but fly and feel differently. What matters is not the unit price but working out the yearly total in advance.

shuttles per week   = sessions per week x shuttles per session
shuttle cost / year = shuttles per week x 52 x (tube price ÷ 12)
string cost / year  = restrings per year x (string + labour)
first-year outlay   = racket + court shoes + the two lines above + grip tape

Run that once and the same conclusion usually falls out. The racket is a smaller share of the first-year total than expected. Which brings us to the third place, the shoes. They are the item most often postponed and the one that gains least from being postponed.

Why putting off shoes is the most expensive choice

Most of what the body does in badminton is not hitting but stopping and pushing off again. Two steps forward and a hard stop, a push sideways and back, a retreat and a spring forward.

Running shoes are not built around that. To absorb forward motion they carry a thick heel stack and a rounded sole. When weight loads sideways the foot can slide inside the shoe or the shoe itself can roll, and an ankle can turn over in that situation.

An indoor court shoe goes the other way. It sits low and flat so the sole stays against the floor, and the sides carry structure that holds the foot when it loads laterally. The outsole is non-marking gum rubber: it grips an indoor floor without leaving streaks on it, and many indoor halls make that a condition of entry.

  • Shoes are hard to borrow. Rackets are not. That asymmetry sets the order
  • Non-marking soles are tied directly to hall rules. Check the rules where you plan to play before buying
  • They hold the width and the instep, so the size can differ from your usual. Better tried on
  • If you wear thick sports socks, try them on wearing those
  • A worn outsole grips less. If you are slipping, suspect the shoes first
  • The order is fixed: shoes, racket, strings, then a second racket

Racket lifespan and care

A racket is not something that wears out. Most of them end in an accident. Clashing with a partner's racket in doubles is the most common, then the floor or the net post. Half of care is the habit of not hitting things, and the other half is the list below.

  • Cut the rest of the strings when one goes. One broken strand removes the tension on that axis and leaves an asymmetric load on the frame. Stringers uniformly advise cutting them. That said, I could not find a manufacturer source documenting a frame actually deformed by it
  • Look at the grommets. A cracked plastic ring in a string hole will present an edge that cuts a fresh string. Ask for them to be checked when you hand the racket in
  • Keep it out of heat. Exposure to excessive heat is listed among the Yonex warranty exclusions. A car boot in summer falls under that
  • Treat the grip as a consumable. A slippery grip makes you squeeze harder, and squeezing stiffens the swing
  • Look under the paint. Hairline cracks start beneath the finish and are easy to miss. If the sound changed after a clash, it is worth suspecting

If you are considering a used racket, the checklist is exactly the one above. The problem is that most of it cannot be checked from a photograph. What makes used rackets hard to recommend to a beginner is not the price but that impossibility.

When you do not need to buy at all

Everything so far assumed a purchase. Sometimes the assumption is the thing to revisit. Most clubs and community programmes keep loan rackets, and those will carry you through the months of learning basic swings and footwork perfectly well. Three or four weeks on a borrowed racket gathers the information a decision needs, and what to check in that time is not the racket but your own state.

  • Did you actually turn up four times or more in four weeks. If not, the racket is not the problem
  • When you picked up someone else's racket, could you feel a difference. If not, the racket is not yet the variable
  • Are you mostly finding the middle of the face. If not, changing tension or weight lands in the same place
  • Does the loan racket feel heavy or light to you. This is the best single clue to the grade of a first racket
  • Do you already have shoes. If not, they come before the racket

The point at which a racket limits you is usually clear. It is when the shot you want is in your head and the racket will not follow. Before that, any racket produces much the same outcome; after it, you can say for yourself what is missing. A racket bought in that state fits far better.

Summary

There is one rule to hold on to. Both U and G get smaller as the number gets bigger. 3U averages 88g, 4U 83g and 5U 78g, and a higher G is a thinner handle. Those two alone will keep the wrong object out of your bag.

The other three are entangled. Balance and shaft flex are handled in manufacturer material as two axes of one grid, and attacking and defensive are names pinned on that grid. Tension has a per-racket ceiling, and on some rackets 24 pounds is outside it entirely. That tension starts falling the day after stringing and does not come back.

The spending order is settled independently of any spec. Shoes first; a racket can wait a few weeks on a loaner; and the largest line in the first year is usually the consumables. Stand at the wall knowing those three and the string on the sticker becomes, for the first time, information you can choose with.

What I could not verify

  • The gram boundaries of the U grades could not be confirmed on a manufacturer page. Notation such as 3U being 85.0 to 89.9g is widely used in trade material, but what I confirmed directly is the published per-model averages of 88g, 83g and 78g
  • I could not find actual grip circumferences in manufacturer material. Sources disagree, giving G4 as 86mm in one place and 3.25 inches in another. What this piece uses is the direction, higher number thinner, and the published overgrip thickness of 0.6mm
  • The boundaries of balance categories, that is from how many millimetres a racket counts as head-heavy, appeared only in retail material. Manufacturer product pages often did not list a balance point as a figure at all
  • I could not find a standard test specification behind the shaft flex labels. Whether one brand's labels are comparable with another's is unverified
  • Whether the published weight is measured unstrung was not stated on the product pages. It is generally taken that way, but I could not confirm the wording
  • The claim that exceeding the tension ceiling voids the warranty is commonly heard, but no tension figure appeared as a condition on the warranty page I checked. What appears there are the exclusions for string breakage and improper restringing
  • I could not trace the restringing rule of thumb to any manufacturer or federation source
  • Systematic reviews of badminton injury sites exist, but I could not reach the abstract text, so no figure such as an ankle injury share appears here
  • No published retail price or games-per-shuttle figure was available from a maker or federation, so the method of calculation is given instead of amounts
  • The federation's own PDF of the Laws refused access, so the clauses were checked against a reproduction of the full text. Clause numbers follow that reproduction
