Jalapeño Project Overview and the Meaning of the 20-Month Timeline

On September 14, 2026, IEEE Spectrum published a report by Matthew S. Smith detailing how OpenAI utilized internal large language models to design Jalapeño, its artificial intelligence inference accelerator. The original article carried the subtitle: 「AI drastically shortened its design time; it will only get faster」. The text notes that when the first chips came back from the foundry in May (「When the first chips came back from the foundry in May」), the team pointed its internal AI models at designing software to run benchmarks.

Regarding the development calendar, the original report documents that 「Jalapeño moved from first architecture concept to first silicon in under 20 months」. This indicates that less than twenty months elapsed between the initial architectural concept and the arrival of the first physical silicon. Furthermore, the report states that 「Only nine months separated the first RTL…from tape-out, when the finished design goes to manufacturing」. Only nine months elapsed from the initial writing of Register-Transfer Level code to tape-out delivery for fab manufacturing.

Regarding team staffing, the source states that 「the group that designed Jalapeño averaged fewer than 100 people over the course of the project」. The engineering group designing Jalapeño averaged fewer than one hundred people across the project lifespan. The report explains that this development was not executed by OpenAI alone, but progressed through a structured division of labor with Broadcom.

Division of Labor Between OpenAI and Broadcom: Boundaries of Front End and Back End

The foundational framework of the Jalapeño initiative reported by IEEE Spectrum resides in the division of responsibilities between OpenAI and Broadcom. The original report defines OpenAI's role as 「end-to-end system design including the inference accelerator, the memory hierarchy, and networking」. OpenAI assumed architectural responsibility across the inference accelerator, the memory hierarchy, and system networking.

Regarding scope boundaries, the text records that the bulk of OpenAI's work concentrated on the 「front end」, spanning early architectural concept development to RTL generation and verification. Broadcom's remit is defined as 「physical design from the gates onward」, encompassing physical design and backend fabrication follow-through.

  • OpenAI dedicated scope: The source states this covered 「end-to-end system design including the inference accelerator, the memory hierarchy, and networking」.
  • Front-end activities: The text notes that OpenAI's work focused primarily on the 「front end」, from concept inception through RTL writing and verification.
  • Broadcom dedicated scope: The source defines Broadcom's role as 「physical design from the gates onward」, handling post-gate physical design and manufacturing delivery.
  • Tooling accessibility gap: According to reporting, Broadcom engineers lacked access to OpenAI's internal LLMs and utilized commercial off-the-shelf models.
  • Character of collaboration: The source records that OpenAI executed the front end while Broadcom executed the backend, without detailing contract clauses or physical process recipes.

This article interprets these 20-month, 9-month, and sub-100-person metrics as applying specifically to OpenAI's scope—system design and the front end—while bearing in mind that Broadcom handled the backend. The source does not disclose the staffing numbers or timeline for Broadcom's contribution.

High-Level Synthesis Tool XLS and Software-Centric Hardware Design

Regarding the engineering workflow, the source identifies the XLS toolchain as a central component. The report introduces XLS as 「an open-source high-level synthesis chain of tools originally developed at Google」. XLS originated as an open-source high-level synthesis toolchain created by Google.

Under the workflow documented in the article, engineers authored hardware specifications in DSLX or C++, and XLS synthesized the logic into Verilog. OpenAI Member of Technical Staff Chris Leary remarked in the interview: 「XLS in some ways looks like software, so it got that benefit」.

  • Nature of XLS: The text describes it as an open-source high-level synthesis chain of tools originally developed at Google (「an open-source high-level synthesis chain of tools originally developed at Google」).
  • Input languages and synthesis: Engineers write in DSLX or C++, and XLS compiles the code into synthesizable Verilog.
  • Leary's explanation: Chris Leary stated in the interview that 「XLS in some ways looks like software, so it got that benefit」.
  • Kahng's appraisal: Professor Andrew Kahng remarked that 「the decision to use AI to accelerate high-level synthesis, such as XLS, makes sense」 and noted that it 「has legs going into the future」.
  • Omitted by the source: The exact parts of the XLS code generated by LLMs and the specific generation methods are not detailed in the source.

This article interprets that, given the source's emphasis on the software-like nature of XLS, the familiar syntax structure may have aided LLM code generation. The source itself does not explain this causal mechanism.

DeepSeek MLA Kernel Benchmark: 40 Hours and 88.94 Percent Utilization

The report states that when the first chips returned from the foundry in May (「When the first chips came back from the foundry in May」), the team directed internal AI models to design software to execute benchmarks. The specific case examined in this phase was DeepSeek's multi-head latent attention (MLA) kernel benchmark.

Regarding the quantitative benchmark outcome, the text records: 「On DeepSeek's multi-head latent attention kernel benchmark, performance climbed from 0.31 percent of the theoretical ceiling (set by the chip's compute and memory bandwidth) to 88.94 percent in roughly 40 hours.」 Utilization climbed from 0.31 percent of the theoretical ceiling determined by compute and memory bandwidth to 88.94 percent in approximately 40 hours.

The source outlines the lineage of language models utilized across development:

  • Early development models: The team utilized reasoning models such as OpenAI o3 (「models like OpenAI's o3」), released in April 2025.
  • Late development models: The team deployed precursors to GPT-6 Astra (「precursors to GPT-6 Astra」), released on September 3, 2026.
  • Internal fine-tuned models: The team concurrently deployed non-public models fine-tuned specifically for semiconductor engineering (「internal LLMs fine-tuned for chip design that are not available to the public」).
  • Ho's statement: Richard Ho, Vice President of Hardware, did not disclose internal model architectural details but stated: 「It's safe to say that Astra and following models will be very good at chip design」.
  • Omitted by the source: The specific iteration methodology and prompt structures across the 40-hour window are not disclosed in the text.

This article interprets this figure as representing hardware utilization relative to a theoretical ceiling on a specific benchmark; whether it generalizes across other workloads is not addressed by the source.

Limits of Autonomous Design and Human Oversight in Engineering

In the original interview, OpenAI Vice President of Hardware Richard Ho stated regarding the engineering role:

「The models are giving superpowers to our engineers」

「Our engineers are still driving the work. They're still the final arbiter of what's going on」

The source notes that Ho does not view complete automation as feasible. Regarding academic reaction, the text reports that Professor Andrew Kahng of UC San Diego noted OpenAI's design speed, remarking that it is 「likely best in class today」. Additionally, discussions raised across GeekNews and Hacker News were reflected in technical commentary:

  • Hacker News community critique: Commentators described the media analysis as superficial (「superficial analysis」) and argued that Broadcom's undisclosed backend contributions were substantial.
  • Intellectual property concerns: Community members raised concerns that utilizing external AI models for commercial chip design could expose proprietary intellectual property.
  • Toolchain access disparity: Broadcom engineers reportedly lacked access to OpenAI's internal models and relied only on commercial public models.
  • Source position on automation: Ho affirmed that engineers continue to drive the work as final arbiters (「final arbiter」) and stated that full automation is not considered feasible.
  • Future directions: Ho noted opportunities for further AI integration in verification and physical design, and Leary added that the team now has tools to manipulate and view waveforms.

This article interprets the report as illustrating a division of labor where a small team utilized AI tools for system architecture and the front end while Broadcom executed the backend, rather than a fully autonomous design.
