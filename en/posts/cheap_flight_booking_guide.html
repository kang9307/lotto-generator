Why Airfares Keep Changing - What You Can Control and Which Tips We Could Not Verify

How booking classes work, how to compare on total cost, and eight popular claims for which we found no primary source

2026-08-01 Life / Practical

Opening: the same flight, a different price than yesterday

You bookmarked a flight last night. Same date, same time, same flight number. This morning it is forty thousand won more. You open a fresh browser window, switch to private mode, and load it again. The price has not moved. At this point most people split into two camps. Either they are certain their search history did it, or they decide today was unlucky and pay.

The point is not that both reactions are wrong. The point is that neither of them is a verified explanation. And holding on to an unverified explanation is what makes people miss the parts they could actually control.

This is not an article about finding cheap flights. There are already more of those than anyone needs, and most of them are the same unverified claims copied from one page to the next. This article does one thing: look first at how the price is formed, and then, on top of that structure, separate what is controllable from what is folklore.

The conclusion, stated up front, lives on the boring side. What actually saves money is comparing on total cost and reading the conditions, not pressing the buy button on a Tuesday afternoon.

How this article was researched. Every figure or standard stated below comes from a primary government or public-agency document we opened ourselves. Where we could not open a document, we did not reproduce its contents, and section 9 lists exactly what we failed to confirm. The same standard was applied to the popular money-saving tips, which is why the table in section 3 records, line by line, whether a primary source was found.

The regulatory material is specific to South Korea. Everything about cancellation fees, aviation consumer protection and dispute resolution assumes a Korean consumer buying in South Korea, and the sources cited are Korean government documents. Readers elsewhere should treat the structural parts as general and the legal parts as jurisdiction-bound. This article also does not recommend or criticise any particular airline, booking site or payment card. Making that kind of call would require opening every document this article failed to open.

1. Why the price keeps moving - they sell products, not seats

One misconception has to go first. An airline is not selling you a seat. You do end up sitting in a physical seat, of course, but the unit that is actually sold is not the seat. It is a product defined by a set of conditions.

Within a single economy cabin, the inventory is carved into several products. The industry calls these booking classes, usually identified by a single letter, and each one carries a bundle of conditions known as a fare rule. The bundle typically covers things like this.

  • Whether changes are allowed, and at what fee. Some products cannot be changed at all, some allow it for a fee, and some only ask for the fare difference
  • Whether refunds are allowed, and on what fee scale. Some products are non-refundable, which means buying cheap is really a bet that your dates will never move
  • Whether checked baggage is included, and how many pieces. Some products carry no baggage allowance at all in the displayed price
  • Whether seats can be selected in advance. Free on some products, paid on others, and on some not available until airport check-in
  • The mileage accrual rate. The same distance flown can earn different amounts, and some products earn nothing at all
  • Stay conditions. Some fares require a minimum stay or forbid exceeding a maximum one

With that in place, the opening scene reads differently. The difference between yesterday and today is most likely not that a price rose, but that the cheaper product that was still available yesterday sold out and only the next one is left. This is why airfares move in discrete steps rather than along a smooth curve.

1.1 Which is why it cannot be forecast

How many seats go to each class, and when that allocation is revised, is an internal demand judgement. And that judgement is not published. That single fact defuses most of the money-saving folklore in circulation.

  • For the claim that buying N days out is cheapest to hold, allocation policy would have to be similar across all routes, and there is no reason it would be
  • Even on one route, the mix of leisure and business demand shifts between peak and off-peak, and the allocation shifts with it
  • Observing that a price moved tells you nothing about why. It could be because you searched again, or because someone else bought the last cheap seat in the meantime. A consumer has no way to tell those apart

Given that structure, the remaining option is clear. Stop trying to predict the next move and instead identify precisely what product is on your screen. That part is verifiable.

It is worth adding that the fact of airfares being sold as several products with different conditions shows up in regulatory documents too. The Korea Fair Trade Commission announcement of 28 September 2016 and the Korea Consumer Agency press release of 21 November 2016, both cited later, are built around the categories of discounted tickets and fees that vary with when you cancel. Neither distinction could exist if a ticket were a single item with a single price.

On headline fare alone, A is 60,000 cheaper. Add only the committed costs and A is 124,000 more expensive. Price in the chance that plans change and it is more than 200,000 more expensive. This reversal is not an exotic case. It is the natural result of headline fares excluding different things.

2.2 The expected-cost line

The second-to-last row may look unfamiliar. It is the probability that your plans change multiplied by the fee. There is a simple reason to include it.

  • The price of a non-refundable fare is not its headline. If the dates are certain the headline is right, but if they are not, you also bought a risk of losing the whole amount
  • You do not need an accurate probability. Distinguishing zero from non-zero already changes the answer. If a work trip is unconfirmed, if you travel with a child, or if a companion has not fixed their dates, p is not zero
  • Conversely, if p is close to zero the non-refundable fare really is cheaper. This calculation is not an argument against cheap tickets. It is a tool for deciding when a cheap ticket is genuinely cheap
  • By the same logic, trip-cancellation coverage in travel insurance works by lowering p. Its scope and conditions are covered separately in the travel insurance guide

3. Testing the folklore - stating whether a primary source was found

This is the most important section of the article. For each of the widely circulated airfare tips, we checked whether published material from governments, public agencies or regulators contains a primary source supporting it. The results are reported exactly as found.

Widely circulated claimPrimary source foundWhat was confirmed instead

Buying on a particular weekday or hour is cheaperNot foundNothing

Eight out of eight, not found. Reading that result correctly matters.

  • Not found is not a proof of falsehood. A source could exist outside the scope of this research, and commercial pricing policy carries no publication duty in the first place
  • Equally, it is not a proof of truth. And in the consumer situation those two are practically the same. Advice you cannot verify gives you no basis for deciding whether to follow it
  • So this article neither tells you to do these eight things nor tells you not to. It simply declines to call them methods of saving money

3.1 Why personal experience cannot settle it

Someone saying they opened a private window and the fare dropped is probably describing a real experience. The problem is that the experience does not discriminate between two different explanations.

  • Explanation 1. Search history was reflected in the price, and clearing it restored the original price
  • Explanation 2. Booking class inventory changed in the interval. Private mode played no part at all
  • The two produce the same observation. Watching the number fall tells you nothing about which one happened
  • Separating them requires observations at the same moment, under the same conditions, with a control, repeated. A handful of personal encounters satisfies none of those
  • On top of that, people remember only the times it lined up. The day the private window changed nothing is not a story, so it never gets passed on

The practical conclusion is this. If an action costs nothing, there is no harm in doing it. Opening a private window takes three seconds. The harm begins when it is labelled a saving technique, because attention then leaves the verifiable savings, total-cost comparison and reading the conditions. Time and attention are finite, and where you spend them determines what you actually pay.

4. Connections versus direct flights - why one booking reference matters

Connecting itineraries are often cheaper than direct ones. But price is not the only thing being compared here, because a connection means buying time cost and risk along with the ticket.

4.1 Putting the time cost into numbers

  • Layover time is itself a cost. If the round trip adds eight hours, it is worth converting that into days of leave
  • Hours spent in an airport cost money in food, multiplied by the number of travellers
  • An overnight connection costs sleep and often takes away the first day after arrival
  • Depending on the transit country you may need a visa or an advance travel authorisation. That is both a cost and a failure risk, and the conditions must be checked with the relevant embassy and foreign ministry guidance

4.2 One booking reference, or several tickets

This is the core of the section. Two itineraries that look identical on screen are completely different products depending on whether they were issued as a single booking or assembled by the passenger from two separate ones.

  • Issued as one booking. The airline treats the whole itinerary as a single contract of carriage. If the first leg runs late and the second is missed, there is a defined scope of airline responsibility for rebooking. Baggage is frequently checked through to the final destination
  • Two separately bought tickets. From the second airline point of view, a delay on the first leg is the passenger own circumstance. Miss it and you may have to buy a new ticket. Baggage normally has to be collected at the transit point and checked in again
  • Unusually cheap combinations on comparison sites are sometimes the second kind. Look for a separate-ticket disclosure or equivalent wording on the screen
  • The way to confirm it is to look at the issued e-ticket and check whether there is one booking reference and how many ticket numbers there are
  • Also check whether the transit requires clearing immigration. If it does, the connection time you need rises sharply

Airports publish minimum connection times, and they differ by airline and terminal combination. Consequences arising from a self-assembled connection shorter than that are generally treated as the passenger responsibility. By contrast, a connection offered inside a single booking was designed by the airline, however tight it looks.

Limits of this section. The specific conditions for through-checked baggage and for responsibility across legs vary with each airline conditions of carriage and with inter-airline agreements. This article did not open any individual airline conditions of carriage, nor the relevant published material from the international air transport association, so it goes only as far as saying where to look and states no specific condition as fact. On a real itinerary, read the connection and baggage clauses in that airline conditions of carriage before ticketing.

5. Cancellation and change rules - where they live and what to read

Cancellation terms are not written in one place. Three layers overlap, and they can disagree with one another. Without knowing that structure, a situation where the airline says a refund is due and the agency says it is not leaves you with nothing to argue about.

LayerWhat it containsWhere to read itWhat happens if you miss it
1. Airline conditions of carriageGeneral terms of the carriage contract: the skeleton for cancellation, changes, baggage and delay handlingThe terms page on the airline siteYou end up hearing case-by-case answers without knowing the baseline
2. The fare rule for your fareConditions applying only to the product you bought: refundability, fee bands, baggage inclusionFinal booking screen, fare rule summary on the e-ticketThe most commonly missed layer. It differs product by product within the same airline
3. The seller own termsHandling fee and accepted service hours where an agency or reseller was involvedSeller terms of use, order confirmation emailIt is charged on top of the airline fee, and cancellation may only be accepted through the seller

5.1 What is confirmed under South Korean rules

From here the content rests on primary sources we actually opened. There are two.

  • Korea Fair Trade Commission, announced 28 September 2016. Seven domestic airlines had terms charging a flat cancellation fee regardless of the period between the cancellation date and the departure date on international tickets, and those terms were corrected. The reasoning was that such a fee constitutes liquidated damages under civil law, and that the clause was void under the standard terms act because it imposed an unfairly excessive damages obligation on the customer the longer the interval between cancellation and departure
  • The correction itself. Cancellations 91 days or more before departure are refunded in full, and the window from 90 days before departure up to the departure date is divided into four to seven bands with the rate rising as departure approaches. Systems were to be updated by 31 December 2016, with the change effective from 1 January 2017
  • Korea Consumer Agency, Consumer Dispute Mediation Committee, press release of 21 November 2016. A consumer who bought three Incheon to Hong Kong return tickets for 665,400 KRW in February 2016 cancelled 66 days before departure, and the airline charged 80,000 KRW per person. The committee applied the corrected terms retroactively and decided on a refund of 23,000 KRW per adult and 25,000 KRW per child.
  • The same release summarises discounted tickets as being cancellable without a cancellation fee 91 days or more before the departure date, with the corrected per-airline fee applying within 90 days

Mind the reference date. Both items above are measures from 2016 and 2017. Airlines may have revised their terms since, and the number of bands and the rates differ between airlines. So do not memorise this as full refund guaranteed at 91 days. What these sources actually give you is the reasoning standard, that a flat fee unrelated to timing can be legally problematic, plus the fact that a real refund decision was issued on that basis. The actual bands and rates for your own ticket must be read in that ticket fare rule.

5.2 What to keep before you pay

Checking the rules and being able to prove you checked are different things. If a dispute arises, you need the second one.

  • Screenshot the fare rule summary and the cancellation fee notice on the final screen and store them with the payment record
  • If a reseller was involved, screenshot the seller cancellation fee notice separately. It can differ from the airline rule
  • When the e-ticket arrives, confirm the fare rule summary, baggage allowance, booking reference and ticket numbers
  • When cancelling, use a channel that records the time of your request, such as a web cancellation form or email. A phone call alone is hard to evidence
  • Because the fee bands are split by date, a single day can move you into a different band. If there is no reason to delay a cancellation, the structure rewards not delaying

When a contract clause becomes void, and what procedure to follow in a dispute with a business, are covered in more depth in unfair terms and voidance and consumer dispute resolution.

6. Overbooking, delays and cancellations - what you can ask for

This section does not state compensation amounts. The reason comes first.

South Korea has a Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport notice known in English as the Aviation Traffic User Protection Standards. Searching the national law information portal confirms it as Ministry notice number 2024-519, effective 27 September 2024. However, this article could not open the body of the notice. Reproducing clauses and figures we have not read would, at that moment, turn this article into bad guidance for the next reader. So we give the path to check instead of the numbers.

6.1 The path to check

  • Look up the Aviation Traffic User Protection Standards on the national law information portal and read the clauses themselves. Check the effective date alongside, since amendments may have changed the text
  • Check the aviation section of the Consumer Dispute Resolution Standards issued by the Korea Fair Trade Commission. These standards are understood to be advisory in character, and this article could not open the aviation schedule of that notice
  • Find the delay, cancellation and overbooking clauses in the conditions of carriage of the airline you flew. That is where your actual contractual relationship sits
  • If it is not resolved, use the counselling service and dispute mediation process of the Korea Consumer Agency. The decision cited in section 5 came out of that route

6.2 What to do at the airport itself

Whatever standard ends up applying, without a record there is nothing to support your claim. This part holds regardless of which rules apply.

  • Get the reason and the times in writing. Ask for a delay or cancellation certificate and check what reason was recorded. Weather versus maintenance can change everything that follows
  • Log the notification times. Keep a photo or message showing when the delay was announced and when an alternative was offered
  • Record what alternative was offered. What you were offered and what you declined becomes the point in dispute later
  • Keep every receipt for extra spending: hotel, meals, transport, and any replacement ticket
  • If you were denied boarding because of overbooking, get that fact and the remedy offered confirmed in writing. Verbal promises leave no trace
  • If you hold travel insurance, separately check whether it covers delays and cancellations. Airline remedies and insurance claims are distinct processes

These items have something in common. All of them can only be done at the airport, and none can be manufactured once you are home. Which is why remembering these six lines helps more than memorising any standard.

7. Mileage - only what is confirmed

Mileage complicates total-cost comparison, because its value is not fixed. This section describes the structure only and states nothing definitive about any particular programme.

7.1 On the accrual side

  • Accrual is usually calculated from segment distance and booking class. The same distance earns different amounts depending on the product
  • Some fares earn nothing at all. Cheaper products are more likely to be in that group, so if you were counting on accrual, confirm it before paying
  • If you want accrual in your total-cost figure, be conservative about it. As shown next, the value is not fixed

7.2 On the redemption side

  • Award tickets normally draw on a separately allocated seat inventory. Having enough miles does not mean a seat exists on your date
  • Even with an award ticket, taxes and fuel surcharges are often payable in cash. It is therefore not a free ticket
  • Consequently a comparison that converts miles to cash at a fixed rate does not hold. The exchange rate depends on timing and seat availability
  • Some programmes set an expiry period. The length and the way miles lapse differ by programme and must be read in that programme terms. This article did not open any individual programme terms, so it states no figures
  • Household pooling, transfer and partner redemption are likewise programme-specific conditions, and for the same reason nothing is asserted here

In short, mileage is better treated as a secondary line in the total-cost comparison. Choosing a more expensive product because of an accrual difference only makes sense when you can fix the value of that accrual. Usually you cannot.

9. What this article could not confirm

Writing unconfirmed material as if it were confirmed would make this article the same as the ones criticised in section 3. The limits are stated plainly.

  • The body of the Aviation Traffic User Protection Standards. We confirmed only the notice number, Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport notice 2024-519, and the effective date of 27 September 2024. The contents and amounts in the overbooking, delay and cancellation clauses were not confirmed, so they are not stated in the text
  • The aviation schedule of the Consumer Dispute Resolution Standards. We mention only that the notice exists and what role it plays. The clauses and figures in the schedule were not opened or confirmed
  • Conditions of carriage, fare rules and baggage rules of individual airlines. Not one was opened. The condition lists in sections 1 and 2 are a list of items to check, not the actual conditions of any airline
  • Published material from the international air transport association. We tried to confirm the rules on through-checked baggage and cross-leg responsibility in section 4 and could not open it, which is why that section gives directions to check rather than answers
  • Terms of individual mileage programmes. Expiry periods, lapse mechanics, pooling and transfer conditions were all unconfirmed, and no figures are given
  • All eight claims in section 3. Not one primary source was found, and the table says so
  • Amendment history since 2016. How each airline revised its terms after the correction cited in section 5 was not checked, which is why the reference date is stated explicitly

The unconfirmed items share a trait. Nearly all of them are questions about how the other side has actually set its rules. And in principle those answers are printed on the screen and in the documents of the very ticket you are about to buy. That is why reading the conditions on your own screen is faster and more accurate than reading someone else summary.

Conclusion: the boring side is the side that saves

To summarise. Airfares come from a structure in which differently conditioned products sell out in sequence, rather than one seat rising and falling in price. That allocation is not published, so prediction does not get off the ground.

We looked for primary sources behind eight widely circulated money-saving tips and found none. That does not make them false, but advice you cannot verify gives you no basis for following it either.

What is verifiable, on the other hand, is clear. Compare on total cost rather than headline fare, and read the four conditions, baggage, seats, changes and refunds, before you pay. As the invented example in section 2 showed, a product with a headline 60,000 lower can finish 200,000 higher on the total. That reversal is arithmetic, not a trick.

There is also something confirmed on the regulatory side. A flat cancellation fee unrelated to timing has been found to be a clause imposing an unfairly excessive damages obligation on the customer and was corrected, and a real refund decision followed on that basis. Material like this is not for memorising. Knowing where to go when you need it is enough.

One thing you can do right now. Next time you look at a fare, check the included checked baggage allowance before you write down the headline price. That single line flips the conclusion of a comparison more often than people expect. And it has nothing to do with which day of the week you logged in.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the fare I saw yesterday higher today?

Most likely the price of the same seat did not rise. The cheaper product sold out and only the next one is left. Allocation policy is not published, so the next move cannot be predicted. What you can do is confirm the conditions of the product now on your screen.

Does private browsing actually work?

Within the scope of this research, no primary source was found. The observed behaviour is equally explained by inventory depletion, and personal experience cannot separate the two. It costs nothing so use it if you like, but treating it as a saving method draws attention away from total-cost comparison, which is verifiable.

How much difference do baggage fees make?

The fee is multiplied by segments and by travellers, so it is the item that widens the total-cost gap fastest. In the invented example in section 2 it added 160,000 for two adults on a return trip, and that alone reversed a 60,000 headline difference.

Where do I go with a cancellation fee dispute?

First read all three layers: the airline conditions of carriage, the fare rule for that fare, and the seller own terms. If it is unresolved, the Korea Consumer Agency offers counselling and a dispute mediation process. The 2016 decision cited in section 5 came from that route.

Why are there no delay compensation amounts in this article?

Because we could not open the body of the relevant notice. Writing an amount we have not read would be bad guidance. Instead, section 6.1 gives the path to check and section 6.2 lists the records to secure at the airport.

## 4. What splits a connecting itinerary

| Aspect | Issued as one booking | Two separate tickets assembled |
|---|---|---|
| Missing leg 2 after a leg 1 delay | Defined airline responsibility | **Treated as the passenger own circumstance** |
| Baggage | Often checked through to destination | Collect and re-check at transit |
| How to confirm | Booking references and ticket numbers on the e-ticket | Same |

Note: specifics live in the **airline conditions of carriage**. This file lists only what to check.

## 5. Cancellation rules live in three layers

1. **Airline conditions of carriage** — general contract terms
2. **The fare rule for your fare** — applies only to your product (most often missed)
3. **Seller own terms** — agency fees can be charged **on top of** the airline fee

Confirmed South Korean primary sources (2016 to 2017 position):
- KFTC 2016-09-28 — flat cancellation fees unrelated to timing found **void under the standard terms act**, corrected to **full refund at 91 days or more before departure** plus **four to seven bands** within 90 days, effective 2017-01-01
- Korea Consumer Agency mediation committee 2016-11-21 — cancellation 66 days out, refund of 23,000 KRW per adult and 25,000 KRW per child ordered
- Note: **current bands and rates must be re-checked in each airline own terms**

## 7. Mileage — assert nothing

- Accrual rate varies **by booking class**, and some fares earn nothing
- Award seats come from **separate inventory**, and taxes or surcharges are often cash
- Therefore **a fixed cash conversion for comparison does not hold**
- Expiry, pooling and transfer conditions must be read in **that programme terms**

## 8. Do not

- Compare headline fares against each other — they exclude different things
- Pay without reading the refund rule — a fluid schedule changes the real price
- Pay without screenshots — a dispute leaves you with nothing to show
- Mistake separately issued tickets for one itinerary — miss the leg and you rebuy
- Spend time on unverified tricks — filling the total-cost table pays better
- Judge the rules from someone else summary — your own fare rule is the only basis
