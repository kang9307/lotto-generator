One build, several rulebooks

Suppose you run a single app with a subscription attached. A few years ago the payment flow had one shape. Call the in-app purchase API, verify the receipt on your server, update the subscription state. That flow was the same whether the app opened in Tokyo, Seoul or Berlin. The revenue split was the same, and the items flagged in review were broadly the same too.

That is no longer true. The same binary opened under a European Union account and under a Korean account can show a different set of payment options. The share a developer keeps differs. Where the app may legally be published differs. The code did not fork; the rules the code stands on forked.

The changes Apple announced for the European Union in August 2026 widen that split by one more notch. But this article is not really about that announcement. The announcement is one cross-section of a longer movement, and the part that matters is that an app running on different rules in different places has stopped being an exception and is becoming the default. What that state leaves in a developer's release process, and on a user's screen, is the body of this piece.

Confirmed facts: what, from when, and where

First, only the scope that can be confirmed from public documents. The sources are Apple's newsroom post and its developer support documentation, and the effective date is October 1, 2026.

  • The scope is the European Union. The developer documentation states that Web Distribution is available only in the EU. Terms in other regions are not altered by this announcement.
  • The per-install Core Technology Fee is eliminated and replaced by a 5 percent Core Technology Commission on digital transactions in apps distributed outside the App Store.
  • Inside the App Store, rates now split three ways by how payment is processed: Apple In-App Purchase, alternative payment processing inside the app, and out-of-app sales through an external link.
  • The Store Services commission on external links applies only to sales within seven days of the link tap, per the documentation.
  • Developers must keep their chosen payment options for 12 months after selecting them.
  • Eligibility for alternative marketplaces and Web Distribution has widened. The requirement to have a legal entity established in the EU is gone; meeting one alternative criterion suffices, such as being publicly traded, having venture funding from an established firm, completing an audit by a licensed accountant, or holding government, educational or nonprofit status.
  • Notarization remains for apps distributed outside the App Store. The documentation describes it as a baseline review covering accuracy, functionality, safety, security and privacy.
  • Rules for minors are stated as well. Kids category apps cannot include purchase links, users under 18 hit a parental gate for alternative payments and links, and users under 13 cannot access web transaction links.

Rates fall into two tiers depending on program participation. The lower tier covers Small Business Program, Mini Apps Partner Program and Video Partner Program participants, plus auto-renewing subscriptions after their first year.

The documented threshold is under EUR 10 million in global revenue over the trailing twelve months, together with under EUR 1 million in lifetime revenue from download and subscription fees on the EU marketplace app.

How we got here: four regions, four instruments

This did not arrive out of nowhere. It is one point in a stack of legal instruments that accumulated region by region. Stated purely as a matter of record, here is what each region required and on what basis.

European Union: ex ante regulation

The Digital Markets Act, Regulation (EU) 2022/1925, designates certain large platforms as gatekeepers and imposes obligations in advance. The European Commission designated six gatekeepers on September 6, 2023 - Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, ByteDance, Meta and Microsoft - with a compliance deadline of March 6, 2024. On April 23, 2025 the Commission found Apple's steering practices in breach of Article 5(4) and imposed a fine of EUR 500 million, alongside EUR 200 million for Meta. Apple has disclosed that it appealed the decision.

United States: remedies through litigation

The United States has no ex ante statute of the DMA type; rules emerge from orders in individual cases. In the Epic Games and Apple matter, orders concerning external purchase links and compliance with them have been contested over several years, and proceedings continued after a Ninth Circuit ruling in December 2025. This is a live matter and no conclusion is asserted here. On the Google Play side, US-facing policies changed in stages under a separate order. The dates set out in Google's developer support documentation are October 29, 2025 for external links and alternative billing, December 9, 2025 for the related programs, June 22, 2026 for the start of providing app listings to third-party Android app stores, July 22, 2026 for implementation, and October 1, 2026 for reporting and service fee requirements.

Japan: designation followed by obligations

The Mobile Software Competition Act took effect on December 18, 2025. The Japan Fair Trade Commission designated Apple and Google in March 2025. Apple announced iOS changes for Japan on December 17, 2025, opening alternative marketplaces, alternative payment processing and external web transaction links together. The rates announced for Japan are numerically different from the EU ones.

Korea and the United Kingdom: separate tracks

Korea chose to write the conduct into a prohibited-practices list: a 2021 amendment to the Telecommunications Business Act covers compelling app developers to use a specific payment method, among other conduct (covered in detail in the Korea section below). The United Kingdom, under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, saw the Competition and Markets Authority designate Apple's and Google's mobile platforms as having Strategic Market Status on October 22, 2025, with specific conduct requirements set through a separate process.

Where the single-store premise gave way

Read Table 2 down the column and a commonality appears. Five jurisdictions use different legal traditions and different procedures, yet they reach for broadly the same two points: the singularity of the distribution path and the singularity of the payment path.

Those two were originally one design. Apps enter through one door, transactions inside them are processed one way, and review, settlement and refunds therefore run coherently inside one system. From a user's side the benefit was concrete: you never have to work out where a subscription is cancelled, and there is one place to ask when something goes wrong.

Once regulation moves to untie that bundle, coherence stops being a property of the design and becomes a variable each region decides separately. What matters here is that the direction each region asks for differs in the details. The EU required opening as far as alternative marketplaces and web distribution. The US orders reached mainly external links, alternative billing, and third-party store distribution inside Play. Japan asked designated firms for comparable opening on its own timetable and terms.

The outcome is therefore not a binary of open and closed, but several doors opened in different shapes. One is open only for payments, another for distribution as well, another on a different date. A single app now stands in front of all of them at once.

Developers I: payment paths and fees become a function of region

The first place this is felt is operations rather than code. When payment paths multiply, everything attached to each path multiplies with them.

  • Receipt verification routes - a platform in-app purchase receipt and a self-hosted gateway's transaction record differ in format and in how they are verified. The server has to trust both, correctly.
  • Refunds and disputes - platform refunds are processed by the platform and notified to the developer; self-processed payments are handled directly. Two desks now touch the same subscription state.
  • Subscription state reconciliation - renewal, cancellation and grace states arrive from two sources and must merge into one account state. Drift here means a paying user losing access.
  • Tax treatment - value added tax works differently when the platform is the seller of record and when the developer is. Regional registration duties follow.
  • Reporting duties - using external links or alternative billing can attach an obligation to report transactions and downloads. Google's developer documentation states such reporting begins for US program participants on October 1, 2026.

The fee side is the more tedious arithmetic. Table 1's rates are determined by the combination of region x path x program participation. Identical revenue nets differently depending on which combination it passed through. And because the EU terms require holding a payment option choice for 12 months, that choice is not a quarterly experiment but an annual decision.

What to settle first in practice is not the rate card but the shape of your own revenue. One usable test: if you already have an organization that can absorb payment processing costs, fraud prevention and refund operations, and your EU revenue share is material, the rate gap on alternative payment paths may survive as real margin. If instead you have handed payment operations wholesale to the platform and your EU share is small, the cost of maintaining two paths at once is likely to exceed the rate gap. Either way the calculation starts from what your organization can operate, not from the percentages.

Developers II: review criteria and distribution channels split

Payments are not the only thing splitting. The route an app takes to reach a device now differs by region.

In the EU, alternative marketplaces and Web Distribution join the App Store as options. The widened eligibility matters in practice because dropping the EU-established-entity requirement gives developers headquartered outside the EU a way to qualify. That said, notarization still attaches to distribution outside the store. Review does not disappear; two reviews of different character coexist.

The United States has a different texture. Per Google's developer documentation, app listings from developers serving US users begin being provided to third-party Android app stores on June 22, 2026, and developers may opt out until July 22. The axis that splits here is not the distribution channel itself but whether you opt out of listing exposure. The same phrase, third-party store, points to different procedures in the EU and the US.

The right-hand column of Table 3 is close to this article's practical conclusion. The cost of regional divergence accumulates mostly around the code, not inside it. Adding a few conditional branches is easy. What is hard is testing every state combination those branches produce, confirming refunds behave correctly in each, and reconciling the accounting. As distribution channels multiply you also meet, at the same spot, the problem of rebuilding fixed references and verification chains for every distribution path.

Users: where you live decides what you can choose

Change on the user side arrives later than on the developer side, and reaches wider.

Start with what grows. A user in the EU can encounter, inside the same app, a path that tells them about a cheaper price outside and lets them go there to pay. Distribution routes that bypass the store are open as well. Japan has had a comparable set of paths open under its own rules since December 2025. More choice also means a channel through which price competition can enter the app itself.

Fragmentation arrives with it. When two users of the same service sit on different payment paths, the following diverge.

  • Where subscriptions are managed - one cancels in device settings, the other on the service's website.
  • Refund desk - one route asks the platform, the other asks the developer directly.
  • Changing a payment method - a routine card swap follows a different procedure.
  • Where purchase history lives - a record once gathered under one account scatters across places.
  • Device migration - restore procedures differ by path when moving to a new device.
  • Protections for minors - the position and scope of age gates follow each region's rules.

Situations where a user crosses regions, through travel or relocation, are the trickiest corner of this split. When account region, the issuing country of a payment method and the actual connection location diverge, which rules apply depends on regional terms, and this article did not secure enough primary evidence to state that definitively for all four regions. The real conditions have to be read in each platform's region-specific terms.

The case for opening, in its strongest form

This is not a matter where one side is plainly right. Putting each case in its most persuasive form is more useful for actual judgment. The opening side first.

  • The size of the transaction cost - a double-digit percentage on digital goods is not a common distribution margin across most industries. If that level persists without competition, regulators took that as reason enough to examine the state of competition.
  • Freedom to inform - the anti-steering conduct at issue in the EU decision restricted developers from telling users that a cheaper option exists. If price information cannot travel, price competition cannot start.
  • A single entry route - in a market with effectively two operating systems, if each has one door in, the rules of that door become the rules of the industry.
  • Asymmetry in rule changes - developers must adjust their business to platform policy changes, with limited leverage in the other direction. Ex ante regulation is an attempt to correct that asymmetry institutionally.
  • Room to experiment - multiple distribution routes create space to try different review standards, different revenue models and different content policies.

The case for keeping control, in its strongest form

The other side has substance too. Reducing it to resistance to change means missing problems that genuinely occur.

  • The defensive value of one checkpoint - when every app passes one gate, there is a clear place to filter malware and fraudulent apps. More routes mean the character and strength of that gate varies by route.
  • More payment handlers, more exposure - when the number of parties touching payment data rises from one to several, the surface for leaks rises with it. Users cannot easily assess each party's security posture in advance.
  • Where fraud incentives move - once links that lead out of an app become a normal feature, screens imitating that flow also look normal. Users lose cues for telling genuine from fake.
  • Consistency of redress - the user benefit of refunds and disputes living in one place is real. Once scattered, the first burden is working out where to go.
  • Effectiveness of protections for minors - age verification and spending limits are easier to apply consistently on one path. The parental gates and link restrictions written into these EU changes can be read as conditions addressing exactly this point.
  • The accumulated cost of regional exceptions - when each region layers on different requirements, the cost is absorbed somewhere. The point has been raised that smaller developers may be more exposed to that burden than large ones.

Put the two lists side by side and it becomes clear that each side is worried about a different failure mode. One sees the loss that comes from absent competition; the other sees the loss that comes from dispersed control. Both have been observed in practice, and which is larger depends on the market, the period and the measurement. So this article takes neither side.

The Korean track: what the official record shows

Korea has its own axis. Only what is confirmable from official sources is stated here, and it matters for readers outside Korea because it is one of the earliest instruments of this kind.

  • The amended Telecommunications Business Act was promulgated and took effect on September 14, 2021. The Korea Communications Commission issued a press release the same day announcing its entry into force.
  • The newly added prohibited conduct sits in Article 50, paragraph 1, items 9 through 11: an app market operator unfairly using its transactional position to compel a specific payment method, unfairly delaying review, and unfairly deleting mobile content from the app market.
  • The specific types and criteria were set by Enforcement Decree, whose amendment passed the Cabinet in 2022.
  • On the enforcement side, reporting indicates that procedural steps such as notice of corrective measures have been taken, but the existence and content of any final disposition could not be confirmed from primary sources for this article. No conclusion about an ongoing procedure is asserted here.

One more practical fact for developers based in Korea or selling into it: the EU changes do not apply in Korea. Apple's documentation limits the scope to the European Union. A studio building in Korea and selling globally therefore has to check Korean conditions and EU, US and Japanese conditions separately. Headquarters location does not determine the terms; the location of the user does.

What could not be confirmed

Items where evidence could not be secured are gathered here. Anything absent from the tables and the body usually has its reason in this list.

  • The precise publication timestamp of Apple's announcement. That the newsroom post is dated August 2026 and that the effective date is October 1 were confirmed; the finer timestamp was not pinned down.
  • The primary text of any European Commission statement on this announcement. Several reports describe a Commission response about monitoring implementation, but the Commission's own posting was not opened as a primary source, so no Commission assessment is quoted in the body.
  • The current stage and final outcome of the Korean enforcement procedure, for the reason given above.
  • The future hearing schedule and outcome of the US Apple matter, which is ongoing.
  • The final content and commencement date of the conduct requirements the UK Competition and Markets Authority will set.
  • Which rules apply when an EU user travels outside the EU. No mention of this was found in Apple's newsroom post.
  • How rates in each region may be adjusted in future. That is prediction, so no figures are given.

What to watch: fixed dates and open questions

Mixing settled items with unsettled ones clouds judgment. Here they are separated.

Dates confirmed in documentation

  • October 1, 2026 - Apple's unified EU business terms take effect. The documentation states that the existing Alternative Terms Addendum and the External Purchase Link Entitlement Addendum are phased out at this point.
  • October 1, 2026 - reporting of transactions and downloads, and service fee requirements, begin for US program participants on Google Play.
  • July 22, 2026 - implementation date for the third-party app store measures on Google Play in the US, the same day as the developer opt-out deadline.
  • December 18, 2025 - Japan's Mobile Software Competition Act took effect; already past, and its conditions are in force.

Not yet settled or not confirmed

  • What final form the commission on external links takes in the United States.
  • The specific content and commencement of UK conduct requirements.
  • Whether EU terms spread to other regions, or whether regional differences widen further.
  • How many alternative marketplaces actually form. Widening eligibility and a market coming into existence are different things.
  • Which payment path developers actually choose. The EU's 12-month hold could delay observable signals of those first choices.

Conclusion: region is now a build variable

To summarize: from October 1, 2026, Apple's app terms change in the EU, comprising the removal of the per-install fee in favor of a 5 percent commission, a realignment of rates by path, and widened eligibility for alternative distribution. The scope is the European Union. That is the whole of what this announcement confirms in documentation.

A longer-lived fact sits alongside it. Where an app opens now determines which rules it runs on, and that state has settled in. The EU, the US, Japan, Korea and the UK are each reaching the same two points with different legal tools, and neither their requirements nor their timings are aligned with one another. There is no confirmable basis today for expecting near-term convergence.

So the thing to hold in practice is not the rate card. Rates change. What does not change is the structure: region is now a variable hanging off distribution, settlement and review. Writing which region and which path a release goes out on as an explicit line in the release checklist is cheaper than rereading the documentation from scratch every time an announcement lands. Users have one thing to remember too. If payment screens inside an app start looking different from before, the app may not have changed - the rules of the region it stands in may have.
