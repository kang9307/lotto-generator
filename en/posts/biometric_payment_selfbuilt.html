What Paying With a Fingerprint Actually Commits You To - Sensitive Data, Thresholds and No Undo

The statutes and the arithmetic that attach to one payment terminal, and why neither shrinks when the system is small

Sep 3, 2026 Tech & IT

Putting a finger on a reader and having the purchase complete needs no explanation. No card, no wallet, no phone. And yet behind that moment sits a kind of exposure that simply does not exist when a card or a phone is used. Cards get reissued and passwords get changed, but a finger does not change.

The prompt for this piece is a single case posted to a Korean developer community, but the subject here is not that case. It is the design decision itself - paying with a fingerprint. There is nothing to praise and nothing to scold in the person who built it. What is worth establishing is which requirements attach the moment that design is chosen, regardless of scale, and why those requirements look the way they do.

This is general information written against the law of the Republic of Korea, not legal advice on any particular matter. Outcomes in individual cases turn on their specific facts, and the bodies that decide are the Personal Information Protection Commission and the courts. Anyone actually operating such a system should confirm the position with a lawyer, the Korea Legal Aid Corporation, or the Commission (privacy complaint centre, 118). Provisions cited are as of September 2026.

The prompt, and what this piece will not do

In early September 2026 a write-up circulated in a Korean developer community. In an environment where phones could not be used, the existing scan-to-pay flow stopped working, so the author built a fingerprint payment terminal and put it into a school store. What the write-up actually establishes is limited. It says that minutiae are extracted and stored rather than the raw fingerprint image, that the transmission path is encrypted, and that the Personal Information Protection Commission's biometric guideline along with the deletion and security provisions of the Personal Information Protection Act were consulted. Authentication took 5.8 seconds in the early version against a 2-second target, and in one month 3,199 of 7,454 payments, roughly 43 per cent, went through the fingerprint path.

Reactions to write-ups like this usually split into impressive and reckless. Both are judgements about a person, and neither leaves much behind. Change the question and something remains. The moment fingerprints are chosen as a payment credential, what attaches automatically, independent of scale or of who built it?

So this piece does not fill in implementation details beyond what the source establishes. It does not identify the author or the school, and it does not rule on whether the design met any requirement. That kind of ruling belongs to a body with authority and the full record. It also does not cover forgery or circumvention techniques. It notes only that attempts to spoof biometric systems have been documented, and that presentation attack detection is therefore an item on the checklist.

A fingerprint cannot be changed

Every judgement starts here. A leaked password gets changed. A compromised card number gets reissued, and a stolen certificate gets revoked and replaced. What these share is that compromise and recovery sit on the same timeline. When something goes wrong, you abandon that credential and move to a new one.

A fingerprint has no such path. There are ten fingers, and an exposed print is never recovered. The biometric protection guideline the Personal Information Protection Commission issued in September 2021 lists uniqueness and immutability as the first characteristic of biometric recognition data, adding that 「once leaked, the harm is difficult to repair」. The sentence that follows introduces the guideline's first principle, proportionality: weigh the benefit of adoption against the risk of infringement and decide whether to adopt at all.

Proportionality sits first for a clear reason. Encryption and access control apply after the decision to collect fingerprints has already been made. Proportionality is the only principle that leaves not collecting on the table.

  • Password leak - change it. The harm stops at the moment of replacement
  • Card leak - reissue. The old number dies
  • Lost phone - remote lock and a new line are available
  • Fingerprint leak - there is structurally no response. Turning off fingerprint authentication in that one system is the whole of it, and if the same finger is enrolled elsewhere, that reach does not extend there

Where the law places a fingerprint

South Korean privacy law does not treat a fingerprint as ordinary personal data. Article 18(3) of the Enforcement Decree defines as sensitive information 「information on an individual's physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics that is generated through specific technical means for the purpose of identifying that individual」. All three pieces are needed: a physical characteristic, a purpose of identifying a specific individual, and generation through technical means.

The last piece matters most. The guideline sets this out in layers. A photograph or a voice file simply kept on file is ordinary personal data. Once it is technically processed for authentication or classification it becomes biometric data, and the subset processed for authenticating or identifying a specific individual is biometric recognition data. That in turn splits into the raw data captured by the sensor and the feature data derived by extracting minutiae, and the guideline states that the feature data falls under Article 18(3) as sensitive information.

That last row is the fork. Article 2(5) of the Act defines a personal information controller as 「a public institution, corporation, organisation, individual or other entity that processes personal information directly or through another person in order to operate a personal information file for business purposes」. Individuals are included. The guideline says a home door lock and similar private installations fall outside, which inverted means that a payment system taking money for goods, operating many people's fingerprints for a business purpose, can make its builder a controller even where that builder is an individual. The size of the organisation does not discount the requirements.

So Article 23(1) attaches. Its structure is unusual: the default is prohibition, with two exceptions layered on top. Either the data subject was told the matters in Article 15(2) and gave consent separately from consent to the processing of other personal data, or a statute requires or permits the processing of sensitive information. Article 23(2) then nails down that a controller processing sensitive information must take the security measures required by Article 29.

Minutiae instead of the image, and the hash misconception

The first choice any fingerprint system makes is not to keep the raw image. Minutiae such as ridge endings and bifurcations are extracted into a set of coordinates and angles, and the image is discarded. The guideline points the same way. Once feature data exists, the raw data has served its collection purpose and should be destroyed without delay; where consent or a statutory basis allows it to be kept, it should be stored separately from other personal data. The guideline even says the design default should be to delete the raw capture as soon as the feature data is generated.

That choice genuinely reduces risk. The trouble is the sentence that usually follows: minutiae are like a hash, so they cannot be reversed and a leak does not matter. That does not hold, and the reason is physics rather than law.

Press the same finger on the same sensor twice and you do not get the same value. Angle, pressure, humidity, contamination and sensor condition differ every time. So biometric authentication does not check equality; it measures similarity. It computes a score between the stored value and the fresh reading and compares it against a threshold. Bury that under a one-way hash the way a password is stored and the computation becomes impossible, because a hash returns a completely different value if a single bit changes.

The wording of the rules mirrors that structure exactly. Article 7(1) of the Commission's notice on standards for securing personal data requires that authentication data including passwords and biometric recognition data be encrypted with a secure algorithm when stored or transmitted, and its proviso singles out passwords alone for one-way encryption so that they cannot be decrypted. Article 7(2)7 lists a user's biometric recognition data among the items that must be stored encrypted. No irreversibility requirement attaches to fingerprints, because none can.

  • A password only needs an exact match check, so one-way storage works
  • A fingerprint needs a measure of how similar, so something comparable has to remain
  • Protecting feature data therefore means wrapping it in encryption and access control, not making it irreversible
  • When the guideline tells manufacturers to design so that 「the raw data is not easily reconstructed from the feature data」, it is asking for higher reconstruction cost, not perfect irreversibility
  • Feature data is therefore sensitive information in its own right. Deleting the raw image does not change that conclusion

Keeping it on the device versus keeping it on a server

There is a reason phone fingerprint unlock became broadly acceptable: the fingerprint never reaches a server. Apple's Touch ID documentation says the node map is stored in an encrypted format readable only by the Secure Enclave and without any identity information, and that this data never leaves the device, is not sent to Apple and is not included in device backups. The same holds for Face ID. What the service provider receives is not a fingerprint but a match result, which is why a breach of the provider's servers does not breach anyone's fingerprints.

The guideline recommends this direction explicitly. The tenth item in its list of protective measures is 「process biometric recognition data on the capture terminal」, on the reasoning that server-side processing widens the blast radius into a mass leak if an incident occurs. For the secure area on the device it points to trusted execution environments and trusted platform modules, Android's KeyStore and TrustZone, and the iOS Keychain.

Yet this item is marked recommended rather than mandatory, and there is a reason. Following it requires an isolated storage area on the terminal, a matching engine running inside it, and a signing scheme that prevents the result from being forged. A phone receives all three at the chip and operating system level; something assembled from a sensor and a microcontroller does not. So self-built systems tend to push matching to a server - often not because it is easier but because it is the only door that is open.

The cost is clear. Server-side matching concentrates feature data in one place, and that store becomes a single point of failure. That is why the guideline recommends a privacy impact assessment where biometric data is transmitted to a server and processed in bulk. Under Article 33 of the Act the assessment is mandatory for public institutions, and Article 35(1) of the Enforcement Decree sets the trigger at a personal information file involving sensitive information or unique identifiers for 50,000 or more data subjects. A private operator under that size is not obliged, but not obliged and not risky are different statements.

The threshold as a scale, and the arithmetic of more people

Biometric authentication is not a matter of right and wrong answers. There is a similarity score and a threshold, and where the threshold sits trades one error against another: false accepts, where someone else is taken for you, against false rejects, where you are turned away. Raise the threshold and false accepts fall while false rejects rise; lower it and the reverse happens. No threshold drives both to zero. In the field that trade shows up as pressure. Frequent false rejects lengthen the queue, and a request arrives to lower the threshold. The request arrives in the language of convenience, but what actually moves is the security level, and that adjustment appears nowhere on any screen.

There is a reference line to work from. Revision 4 of the United States National Institute of Standards and Technology's authentication guideline, SP 800-63B, requires a false match rate of one in 10,000 or better across all demographic groups, limits consecutive failures to five (ten where presentation attack detection is implemented), and requires that on reaching the limit the system disable biometric authentication and offer another factor. It also says biometrics must not be used as a single factor but as part of multi-factor authentication alongside a physical authenticator, and requires an authenticated protected channel between sensor and verifier. This is guidance for United States federal agencies rather than Korean law, but it is a reference line with a number attached. One consumer figure gives a feel for scale: Apple states that the probability of a random person unlocking a device with Face ID is less than one in 1,000,000, a figure that presumes dedicated sensors, isolated hardware and a one-to-one comparison against a single enrolled user.

Change the premise and the arithmetic changes. The guideline distinguishes authentication from identification. The former compares submitted biometric data against a stored record to confirm the person, one to one; the latter compares against many records in a database to pick one individual out of a group, one to N. Placing a finger and having payment complete is the latter. If the user does not first present an identifier, the system has to compare against everyone enrolled, and one attempt becomes N comparisons. However small the one-to-one false accept probability, running that trial N times raises the chance of at least one wrong match accordingly.

  • One-to-one authentication - the user presents an identifier first and one comparison is made. Tap a card, then place a finger
  • One-to-N identification - just a finger. Maximum convenience, and the comparison count equals the enrolled population
  • As N grows, false accepts increase structurally. A setting that was fine for ten people failing at several hundred is not a sign the algorithm got worse
  • Compensating means raising the threshold, and raising it increases false rejects, so users whose prints read poorly are pushed out first
  • This is why the guideline makes alternatives a standalone item, expressly citing fingerprints damaged by occupation or unreadable because of injury

What gets heavier because it is payment

A false accept at a door lets the wrong person through. A false accept at a till means someone else's money leaves. And once money is involved a question follows: who authorised this transaction, and when they say they did not, what settles it?

Here the weakness of biometrics shows. Unlike a signature or a password, a fingerprint gives the user almost no way to be careless. Saying you never told anyone your password and saying you never lent anyone your finger are claims of different character. What the system retains is a record that 「a similarity score above the threshold was produced」, and that record does not prove that particular finger was on that sensor at that moment. Non-repudiation is weaker than it looks.

The law has already brought biometrics into the payment context. Article 2(10) of the Electronic Financial Transactions Act enumerates access media and lists 「a user's biometric information」 at item (d). Article 9(1)1 of the same Act provides that where a user suffers loss from an incident arising from forgery or alteration of an access medium, the financial company or electronic financial business operator bears liability for damages. That provision binds financial companies and electronic financial business operators, and whether a given system falls in that category is a separate determination. The point is not the conclusion but the direction. Using biometrics as a payment credential opens the question of how liability for payment incidents is allocated.

The burden-of-proof structure in the privacy statute points the same way. Article 39(1) lets a data subject who suffers loss from a violation claim damages, and provides that the controller cannot escape liability unless it proves the absence of intent or negligence. Article 39(3) lets a court set damages at up to five times the loss where personal data leaked through intent or gross negligence, and Article 39-2 lets a data subject claim statutory damages of up to 3 million won without proving the amount of loss. Multiply by headcount and being small stops being reassuring.

Consent is a doorway, not a licence

The separate consent in Article 23(1)1 is a threshold, not a permit. Consent only lets processing begin, and requirements follow in a queue. First, the form of the consent. Article 15(2) requires disclosure of the purpose of collection and use, the categories collected, the retention and use period, and the fact that consent may be refused along with any disadvantage of refusing. Article 23(1)1 requires those disclosures and consent obtained separately from consent to the processing of other personal data. That is why the guideline's model consent form draws the raw-data block and the sensitive-data block as entirely separate sections.

Purpose limitation comes next. Data collected for authentication or identification cannot be repurposed for personnel management or research and development without authority. The guideline adds a specific caution to ensure that sensitive attributes unrelated to authentication, such as ethnicity or health status, are not extracted or used from the raw capture. Once the retention period expires or the purpose is met, Article 21 requires destruction without delay by a method that prevents recovery or reconstruction.

The notice adds one more item on top. Article 8(1) of the security-measures notice requires access logs to be retained for at least one year, and its proviso item 2 extends that to at least two years for a system processing sensitive information. A single decision to use fingerprints doubles the log retention period. Contrast that with Article 4(1) of the same notice, whose proviso lets small businesses, individuals and organisations processing fewer than 10,000 data subjects skip the internal management plan. Some obligations shrink with scale, and some attach regardless of scale because of what the data is.

What actually changes when minors are involved

One common misconception is worth clearing up. People often say that any minor requires a parent's consent, but the line is not drawn there. Article 22-2(1) requires that where consent is needed to process the personal data of a child under 14, the legal representative's consent be obtained and verified. Minors aged 14 and over are not within that provision.

That does not make the burden disappear. Article 22-2(3) requires that notices to children under 14 use an easily understood format and clear, plain language. The purpose behind that does not stop cleanly at a birthday. A confirmation button pressed by someone who cannot tell what a fingerprint template is, where it is stored or when it is destroyed leaves only the form of consent.

Harder still is the voluntariness of consent. In a school or a workplace, where there is effectively no alternative, the very structure in which refusing means accepting inconvenience blurs what consent means. The guideline's decision to make alternative means a standalone item, and its statement that 「provision of a service may not be refused on the ground that the user did not consent to collection of personal data beyond the minimum necessary」, speaks directly to that. The sanctions are not light either. Article 71(4) provides imprisonment of up to five years or a fine of up to 50 million won for processing sensitive information in violation of Article 23(1), and Article 64-2(1)3 makes processing sensitive information without consent subject to an administrative fine.

After a breach, what is owed and by when

The post-incident procedure is written in units of time. Article 34(1) requires that on becoming aware of a leak the controller notify data subjects without delay of five items: the categories of personal data leaked, the time and circumstances, what the data subject can do to limit harm, the controller's response and remedy process, and the department and contact point receiving reports. Article 39(1) of the Enforcement Decree fixes that notification at within 72 hours.

Reporting is separate. Article 40(1) of the Enforcement Decree imposes a 72-hour reporting duty in three cases: a leak affecting 1,000 or more data subjects, a leak of sensitive information or unique identifiers, and a leak caused by unlawful external access. The absence of a headcount threshold in the second is the point. If fingerprint templates leak, the report is due however few people are affected. Reports go to the Commission or to the specialist body designated by Article 40(3), the Korea Internet and Security Agency.

There is also a point that governs the severity of sanctions. Article 64-2(1)9 makes a leak or corruption of personal data subject to an administrative fine, with a proviso excluding cases where the controller took every measure necessary to secure the data under Article 29. Article 64-2(4)4 requires that efforts to implement security measures such as encryption be considered when setting the fine. Even where an incident was not prevented, what was put in place beforehand changes the outcome.

  • Notification - to data subjects, within 72 hours, five items
  • Report - to the Commission or the Korea Internet and Security Agency, within 72 hours
  • A leak of sensitive information is reportable with no headcount requirement
  • Where details are not yet established, notify what is known first and supplement as facts are confirmed
  • Where contact details are unknown, posting on a website for 30 days or more can substitute for notification
  • Whether encryption and other safeguards were implemented feeds directly into the fine assessment

Who carries the liability

Where liability lands after an incident in a self-built system is a common point of confusion. Start with the terms. A personal information controller is the entity operating a personal information file for business purposes; a handler is a person who actually processes personal data under the controller's direction and supervision; a trustee is a third party entrusted with processing work.

Two provisions matter in practice. Article 26(7) provides that for liability arising where a trustee violates the Act in connection with entrusted work, the trustee is regarded as an employee of the controller. Outsourcing does not move responsibility toward data subjects. Article 26(8) applies most provisions, including Articles 23 and 29, to trustees as well. Entrust the work and both sides are caught. Article 64-2(1)5 allows an administrative fine against the entrusting party where inadequate supervision or training led the trustee to violate the Act.

This is where a self-built system gets ambiguous. Whether the builder is the controller, a handler, or a trustee that supplied the system depends on the arrangement. And doing that classification after an incident is too late. If it is not settled whose data it is, whose server holds it and who decided the processing, that settling happens in the middle of a dispute. The guideline's decision to make policy publication and handler supervision mandatory items is partly a demand to put that record on paper in advance.

Getting the same convenience without fingerprints

By this point a question follows naturally: was there really no way other than fingerprints? The guideline makes alternative means a standalone protective measure and lists access cards, keypad passwords, in-person verification, PINs and one-time passwords as knowledge-based or possession-based options, precisely because an answer usually exists. What matters is not only how much convenience an alternative gives up but how much regulatory weight it sheds. A card or a PIN is not sensitive information. No separate consent requirement attaches, no two-year access-log retention, no headcount-free breach report. Above all, when something goes wrong there is a recovery path: reissue everything.

The last row is the compromise people most often miss. Identify the person by card and use the fingerprint only to confirm, and the comparison becomes one to one, which removes the scaling problem in false accepts. But the fact that sensitive data is being handled does not change. Not collecting fingerprints and collecting them less dangerously are different choices, and the difference in legal weight is correspondingly large. So the accurate sentence is this: not using biometrics is the cheapest possible compliance strategy. That is also why proportionality sits first in the list of principles.

The line between building and deploying

There is no reason to undervalue building something yourself. Reading a sensor protocol, measuring latency, finding the bottleneck and rearranging the structure teaches things that reading documentation does not. But at some point the character of the work changes. While you are testing on your own finger, it is learning. The moment you take someone else's fingerprint, it becomes personal data processing. That line is drawn independently of polish, code quality or good intentions, and once crossed there is no way back, because a collected fingerprint can be destroyed but one that has already escaped cannot be recalled.

So the question to ask at that line is not 「did I build it well」 but 「did I need to collect this at all」. That is exactly what the guideline asks when it puts a necessity review first among protective measures and says to check, before adopting a service, whether another means could achieve the same purpose with less infringement risk. The question does not go away as technical skill rises. If anything, the more you are able to build, the more often it needs asking.

Questions to ask before you commit

These are the questions that can actually be answered in a room where biometric authentication is being considered. They do not settle whether the answer is good or bad, but they show where the weight accumulates.

  • Can this purpose be met without fingerprints? If not, you should be able to write why not in one sentence
  • Where does matching happen? On the terminal or on a server? On a server, feature data concentrates in one place
  • Is it one to one or one to N? Write down how many times N is planned to grow as well
  • Who can change the threshold, and when? When the queue gets long and someone suggests lowering it, who decides?
  • Is consent being taken separately? Bundled on one screen with other items is not separate consent
  • Is the path for someone who refuses genuinely open? Written down as available while the queue is twice as long is not open
  • Is the raw image being kept? If so, you must be able to state the basis, the retention period and whether it is stored separately
  • Is encryption at rest applied? And where are the keys?
  • How long are access logs retained? Two years for a sensitive information system
  • Is the destruction trigger defined? Are graduation, departure and account closure wired to deletion?
  • Would you notice a leak? The 72-hour clock runs from becoming aware, and with nothing watching, awareness comes late
  • Who notifies and reports if something happens? Is that name written in a document right now?

Failing to fill in this list is itself the answer. Taking other people's fingerprints while items remain blank means handling irreversible data on the assumption that it is reversible. If the list does fill in, then one question remains: does the benefit truly exceed this weight? No provision answers that one.

Again for the record. This is general information written against the law of the Republic of Korea, not a determination about any individual matter. Whether a given system meets the requirements turns on its specific facts, and the bodies that decide are the Personal Information Protection Commission and the courts. Anyone about to adopt or operate such a system should confirm the position with a lawyer, the Korea Legal Aid Corporation, or the Commission.

What I could not verify

  • The later instalments of the source write-up could not be opened. The community summary mentioned that authentication time fell sharply in a later version, but as that could not be confirmed in the source, only the figures established for the early version are used here
  • Whether that system is a personal information controller or met any requirement was not examined and cannot be. The material needed to judge is not public, and the judgement belongs to a body with authority
  • Measured false accept and false reject rates for fingerprint recognition vary widely with algorithm, sensor and operating conditions, so no specific figure is given. The numbers used are a required baseline from United States federal guidance and a published figure for one consumer device, and neither represents general performance
  • Quantitative research on how far a raw fingerprint can be reconstructed from feature data could not be confirmed in a primary source and is not asserted. The piece goes only as far as the guideline's requirement that manufacturers raise the cost of reconstruction
  • Which size and form of payment system Article 9 of the Electronic Financial Transactions Act reaches is not settled by the provision itself, so no conclusion on applicability is drawn. Only the textual fact that it binds financial companies and electronic financial business operators is stated
  • The biometric protection guideline consulted is the September 2021 original. Whether a later revision exists could not be confirmed, so anyone doing a real review should check the Commission's document library for the current version
