What Source Review Cannot See - A Non-Compiling Utility Rode a Distribution Bootstrap, and Which Verifications Still Hold

When the trusting-trust problem steps outside the compiler, what breaks and what survives

2026-09-07 Dev & Infra

What do you look at before bringing a package into a system. Usually three things. Is the source public, is there a signature, and is there evidence that this binary came from that source. When all three line up, most people call it sufficient. In the overwhelming majority of situations that judgement is correct.

At the end of July 2026, a result appeared showing that an entire distribution can be compromised while all three of those checks still pass. The carrier is not a compiler. It is GNU strip, the post-processing utility that removes debug information from finished executables. It performs no compilation. It neither reads nor produces source. It only touches ELF files that have already been built.

What matters here is not the internals of that tool but the boundary of trust. How far the region that source review cannot see actually extends, and which of the verification methods already in hand still do useful work.

There is nothing below about how to build such an attack. No description of what goes where, no procedure for modifying a binary, nothing about evading detection. What is treated here is only which assumptions broke and which verifications still hold. The conclusions and numbers from the paper are stated as fact, but the technical detail needed to reproduce the work is deliberately left out.

The trigger - a tool that does not compile caused a compiler problem

The paper is titled Trusting-Trust Attack against an Entire Linux Distribution through Binary Manipulation. The authors are Julien Malka, Aman Sharma, Martin Monperrus, Stefano Zacchiroli and Théo Zimmermann. It carries the arXiv identifier 2607.24888, and the first version was submitted on 27 July 2026. It is filed under both cryptography and security and software engineering.

The first two sentences of the abstract summarise the whole result. The trusting-trust attack is widely regarded as a threat specific to compilers, and the paper shows that it is not. The sentence that follows names the carrier: a complete trusting-trust attack constructed around GNU strip, an ordinary build utility that neither inspects nor generates source code, using only manipulations of finished ELF files.

The word carrying the most weight there is ordinary. strip holds no privilege, and it sits on no security boundary. It belongs to the finishing pass that reduces the size of an artifact once the build is already done. Very few organizations put a star next to that position when drawing a threat model.

The paper also explains why that position is attractive. Because strip processes a large fraction of the executables a distribution produces, compromising it offers both a route for the compromise to propagate and a route to affect downstream software. Two properties overlapping in a single tool is the whole point.

Neither property is exotic on its own. A tool that rebuilds itself exists everywhere in a distribution. A tool that touches almost every shipped executable exists everywhere too. What is unusual is that both descriptions fit the same small utility, and that nobody had written down the consequence.

The trusting-trust problem - what was actually said in 1984

The origin of this subject is Ken Thompson's Turing Award lecture, Reflections on Trusting Trust, published in Communications of the ACM, volume 27, number 8, August 1984, pages 761 to 763. Four decades later it remains the three-page paper most often found in the footnotes of supply chain security documents.

An argument built in three stages

The lecture proceeds in three stages. The first establishes that a program can print its own source, that self-reproduction is achievable. The second establishes that a compiler can be taught to recognise a particular pattern and to translate that one pattern differently. The third is the decisive move: teach the compiler to recognise that it is compiling itself, and it can reinsert both of the previous devices into the compiler binary that comes out.

The consequence is stated plainly in the original. The devices can now be removed from the compiler source, and the new binary will put them back whenever it is compiled. The targeted program stays affected with no trace in source anywhere. The source is clean and the binary built from that source is not. Both statements being true at once is the trusting-trust problem.

The door the original lecture already left open

The closing lines are the more widely quoted ones. You cannot trust code that you did not totally create yourself, and no amount of source-level verification or scrutiny will protect you from using untrusted code.

Less quoted, and directly connected to this result, is the sentence that comes right after. In demonstrating the possibility of this kind of attack the author picked on the C compiler, but could have picked on any program-handling program such as an assembler, a loader, or even hardware microcode. And as the level of the program gets lower, the text adds, these bugs get harder and harder to detect.

So this research pushed open one of the doors the lecture had already unlocked. It did not invent a concept. It attached an implementation and a set of numbers to a branch that had stayed conceptual for forty-two years. That distinction keeps mattering when the defenses are assessed further down.

What was demonstrated - numbers and conditions

Restating the abstract directly: in the bootstrap of the NixOS Linux distribution, a single tampered strip in the binary seed implants a payload that propagates from one generation of the utility to the next, and it survives into the final standard environment after the seed leaves the dependency closure.

The figures run as follows. The experiment used the real nixpkgs revision fef9403a3e4d, and the resulting closure contains 1,199 package outputs and 3,799 user-invokable ELF executables. Of those, 3,791 were judged invokable from the command line, and 3,790 printed the infection marker when invoked normally. In the paper's own words, the trojanned seed had no consequence for the buildability of the package set, and the whole graphical ISO image was obtained successfully.

What survival after the seed leaves the closure means

That sentence is the sharpest part of the result, because the criterion a Nix-style distribution uses to judge a successful bootstrap is precisely reference and containment. If the final standard environment does not reference the seed files and does not contain them, the bootstrap is considered to have met its goal.

What this result shows is a case where all of those criteria are satisfied and only behaviour remains. The seed is gone from the dependency graph, and the file is nowhere in the final environment. Yet the executables that environment produces carry the property the seed installed. The reference is severed while the lineage continues.

That gap is not an oversight by the distribution. Reference and containment can be checked mechanically, cheaply, and on every build, which is precisely why they became the criteria in the first place. Behavioural lineage cannot be checked that way at all. The goal that was chosen is the one that could be measured, which is a reasonable engineering decision, and it is also the exact seam this result walks through. A criterion that can be evaluated in seconds on every build will always be preferred over one that cannot be evaluated at all, and the cost of that preference only becomes visible when somebody constructs the case that fits between them.

Separating what was demonstrated from what was claimed

The boundary most often smudged when work like this is summarised is the one between what was measured and what the authors assessed. The two carry different weight. The first can be contested with the replication package. The second depends on how much literature the authors read.

The sentence most likely to be quoted is this one. To the authors' knowledge, this is the first documented end-to-end implementation of a self-propagating trusting-trust attack carried by a non-compiling post-build utility through the bootstrap of a production Linux distribution. The qualifier about the authors' knowledge is explicitly inside the sentence. The word first is not a measurement, it is a statement grounded in a literature survey, and quoting it that way is what keeps it accurate.

The limitations the paper states deserve equal reading. The implementation assumes a particular shape of ELF file, and the prototype carries markers and instrumentation that make it deliberately easy to detect. Whether the approach transfers to another distribution depends on that distribution's bootstrap structure. In other words the artifact itself is not built for use in the field. What is threatening is not the artifact, but the fact that a position believed to be closed turned out to be open.

The blind spot created by guarding only the path from source to binary

For the past decade the design of supply chain verification has pointed in largely one direction: making the path from source to binary independently verifiable. That is close to a literal quotation of how the Reproducible Builds project describes itself. The premise is that once that path is verified, anything inserted along the way becomes visible.

The premise carries a picture in which the transformer is a compiler. Source goes in, a binary comes out, and the box in between is what needs watching. So the sophisticated parts of the defense also aimed at compilers: diversify the compiler, prove the correspondence between compiler binary and compiler source, shorten the compiler's bootstrap chain.

A post-build utility sits outside that picture. Its input is a binary and its output is a binary. Source never participates, so the vocabulary of source-to-binary correspondence has no way to describe the stage at all. Source review never reaches it, and a correspondence proof does not look at what happens after the correspondence ends. The stage is not defended badly, in other words. It is not described, and a stage that the model cannot describe is a stage the model cannot rate as safe or unsafe.

  • What source review catches - what is written in the source. As the lecture concluded, that alone cannot catch a tool that betrays its own description
  • What source-to-binary correspondence catches - that this binary came from that source. Stages appended afterwards are not part of the definition of correspondence
  • What this result exposes - nobody had counted how many stages in a build pipeline never look at source, nor what share of shipped artifacts pass through them
  • The practical question that follows - can you list the tools in your pipeline that take a binary as input and emit a binary. Most teams cannot, and the list is longer than expected

What collapses when a distribution path changes is laid out in what downstream loses when release tags disappear, on the side of hashes and pinned references. This piece is the other side of that: the problem that remains when every hash matches.

The bootstrap chain - where the seed sits and when it disappears

A distribution builds the tools that build itself, using itself. Building a compiler needs a compiler, and building that compiler needs another compiler. The point where that circle is cut is occupied by a bundle of prebuilt binaries. That bundle is the seed.

The goals the distribution states for itself

The Linux standard environment definition in nixpkgs describes the process directly. It starts from prebuilt seed files, creates a series of instances, and through those stages gradually rebuilds the standard environment. That environment then builds every other package, including the seed files themselves.

The same file states three goals explicitly. The final standard environment must not reference any of the bootstrap files, must not contain any of them, and must not contain the files directly generated by the bootstrap code generators. All three are statements about presence and reference. There is no statement there about where the behaviour of the output came from.

This is less a design flaw than the result of choosing a goal that can be measured. Reference and containment are mechanically checkable. Behavioural lineage is not. And this result passes exactly through that gap.

The long-running effort to shrink the seed exists for the same reason. The statement Bootstrappable Builds puts at the front is compact: to gain trust in our computing platforms, we need to be able to tell how each part was produced from source, and to that end the amount of bootstrap binaries should be minimised.

What reproducible builds catch and what they do not

The official definition is a single sentence. A build is reproducible if, given the same source code, build environment and build instructions, any party can recreate bit-by-bit identical copies of all specified artifacts.

Read the definition literally and the boundary appears

Three conditions live inside that sentence: source, build environment, build instructions. Reproducible builds guarantee that when those three are the same, the result is the same. Put the other way round, they do not catch contamination in any of the three. What they catch is a case where the three are claimed to be identical and the results differ.

The paper aims precisely at that point. A rebuild that reuses the same seed reproduces the same implant bit-for-bit. The recipe, the dependency records and the source all stay intact. So neither check fires.

None of that makes reproducible builds useless. The threats this practice has aimed at are a single compromised build machine, artifacts altered during distribution, and incidental variation in the build environment. Against those three it remains effective. What this result changes is not the effectiveness itself but where the edge of that effectiveness lies.

One practical distinction separates here. Rebuilds that share the same seed and rebuilds that start from genuinely different bootstrap paths are not the same instrument. The first says nothing at all about this threat. The second produces a signal when the results diverge. Very few organizations run the second.

The distinction matters because the usual language collapses the two. A rebuilder that fetches the same base image, the same pinned toolchain and the same bootstrap tarball as the original builder is, from the point of view of this threat, the same builder running twice. Agreement between them is a statement about determinism, not about provenance. Divergence is what carries information, and divergence can only occur where something upstream actually differs. That is why the value of a rebuilding network scales with how unlike each other its members are, rather than with how many of them there are.

Bootstrappable builds - the answer that shrinks the seed

If the seed is the problem, shrink the seed. The furthest anyone has taken that direction is the Full-Source Bootstrap announced by GNU Guix in April 2023. In the announcement's own words, a package graph of more than 22,000 nodes was rooted in a 357-byte program, something that had never been achieved, to their knowledge, since the birth of Unix.

The same announcement contains the philosophy in one line. If you think one should always be able to build software from source, then it follows that the trusting trust attack is only a symptom of an incomplete or missing bootstrap story. That reframes the problem away from attack technique and toward a gap in provenance.

The same text is equally frank about what remains. The set of binaries from which packages are built still includes a 25 MiB statically linked Guile that serves as the driver for building the initial packages. The number 357 bytes and the number 25 MiB appear in the same article. The seed shrank dramatically, and it did not disappear.

The condition this paper adds to that direction is the decisive one. A full-source bootstrap removes the carrier only when it rebuilds the post-build utilities from source as well, and not the compiler alone. The existing effort, the paper notes, targets the compiler and the language runtime. That is a request to widen the target by one notch, not a rejection of the direction.

What diverse double-compiling assumes

The most refined answer to the trusting-trust problem is usually taken to be diverse double-compiling, formalised by David A. Wheeler in his 2009 doctoral dissertation. It is commonly abbreviated as DDC.

The structure is this. The source of the compiler under test is compiled once with a second, trusted compiler, and the result is then used to compile the same source again. If the two results match, that is evidence that source and executable correspond. A device present on only one side shows up as a mismatch.

Several assumptions come attached. The trusted compiler must be deterministic. It must be able to compile the parent of the compiler under test. The defender must be able to choose which compilers serve as the trusted side. And the verification itself must take place in an uncompromised environment. The scope of what is proved is bounded too. The correspondence between source and executable becomes visible, but it does not establish that the compiler is free of malicious behaviour. Wheeler says as much himself. The technique converts a hard problem, binary analysis, into a tractable one, source review.

On this result the paper's judgement is explicit. Diverse double-compiling diversifies the compiler and not a build utility like strip. The tampered utility therefore sits on both sides of the comparison, produces no difference, and cancels out.

That is not a call to abandon the technique. It says that the axis chosen for diversification determines the range over which the defense is effective. If the axis is the compiler alone, everything outside the compiler drops out of the comparison. A comparison can only reveal what the two sides do differently, so anything both sides hold in common is invisible by construction, no matter how carefully the rest of the procedure is carried out.

The order to work in today

Reading the table, no single row blocks this threat by itself. So the practical goal is not prevention but being in a state where the scope can be narrowed. When something surfaces, the difference between an organization that knows where to look first and one that does not accounts for nearly all of the response time.

The first thing to write down is the list of seeds. Where the build starts, whose signature that starting point carries, and when it last changed. In most organizations those three lines do not exist as a document anywhere.

[Build starting-point register - keep at the repository root, update on change]

1. Base images
   - Name and digest (the digest, not the tag)
   - Source registry, date last updated, who approved the update

2. Toolchain
   - Compiler, linker, archiver, and every post-build utility
   - Version and distribution source for each, and whether the publisher signature was checked

3. Stages that take a binary in and emit a binary
   - List every stage in the pipeline with that property
   - What share of shipped artifacts passes through each

4. Rebuild comparison
   - Which artifacts, rebuilt by whom, on what cadence, compared against what
   - Does the counterpart share our starting point (if so, the signal is weak)

The second is comparison. A signature check only speaks to origin, so important artifacts need a separate procedure that matches hashes obtained through genuinely different routes. The minimum form, all of it read-only, looks like this.

# 1. Is this the thing the publisher signed (origin)
gpg --verify package.tar.gz.sig package.tar.gz

# 2. Does it match the value we recorded (change)
sha256sum package.tar.gz
grep package.tar.gz manifests/expected-hashes.txt

# 3. Are different mirrors serving the same thing (route)
curl -sL "$MIRROR_A/package.tar.gz" | sha256sum
curl -sL "$MIRROR_B/package.tar.gz" | sha256sum

# 4. Does our own build match the published artifact (reproduction)
sha256sum build/out/package.tar.gz

The point is that the four steps answer four different questions: origin, change, route, reproduction. Passing all four still does not answer this particular threat, but it is a long way from doing none of them. The argument for reducing dependencies in the first place is in reusing less software, and the problem of installation being equivalent to execution is covered in the AUR incident involving 408 packages.

Where to overstate this and where not to

Results like this are routinely inflated or routinely dismissed as they travel. Neither is accurate. It is more useful to split what changed from what did not.

What this result changesWhat it does not change
There is no longer a basis for confining the trusting-trust problem to compilersThere is no evidence anywhere that a distribution in use today is compromised
Threat models need to include build stages that never look at sourceSignature checks and hash comparison remain effective against other threats. No reason to stop
The target of bootstrap reduction widens by one notch beyond the compilerThe direction of bootstrappable builds and reproducible builds is not refuted
When designing a diversity comparison, the choice of axis visibly sets the effective rangeDiverse double-compiling remains effective against the compiler threat it was built for
The phrase about almost every binary now has a measured numerator, 3,790The denominator is the closure of a graphical installer image, not a whole distribution repository

One more qualifier belongs here. This paper is an arXiv preprint. Its listing carries no journal reference and no venue comment. There is no basis in the currently public information for judging whether it passed peer review, and citing it together with its preprint status is the accurate form. The published replication package is a favourable condition on the verifiability side, since it moves the claim out of the category of assertions that can only be taken on trust and into one that another group can attempt to reproduce, contest, or refine.

Where does trust end

Read the lecture's conclusion again and it becomes clear that it was not pessimism but a demand to draw a boundary. The line about not trusting code you did not totally create yourself is not an instruction to write nothing. It is closer to an instruction to know how far you verified and where delegation began.

Seen that way, what this result offers is not a new fear but a correction to the position of the line. Many organizations had drawn it somewhere near the compiler. The real line runs further back, down to the last tool that touches a binary. And behind that there is a linker, a packager, an image builder, firmware. That is why the lecture noted that these problems get harder to detect as the level of the program gets lower.

The practical conclusion is plain. There is no complete defense, and the absence of a complete defense is not a reason to stop defending. What is available is the work of listing the delegations: what was verified, what was handed to someone else, and which of the handed-over pieces touch the majority of what ships. That last line is the one this result newly demands.

What could not be confirmed

  • The ACM edition of the Thompson lecture could not be opened. The ACM Digital Library address returned a 403, so the quoted wording follows a public transcription. The bibliographic details, Communications of the ACM volume 27 number 8, August 1984, pages 761 to 763, are the values on which that transcription and other citations agree
  • Whether the paper has passed peer review is unknown. The arXiv listing shows no journal reference and no venue comment. Its present state is a preprint
  • No description of ethics procedure or responsible disclosure was located in the paper. Neither notification to NixOS nor an explicit statement that the work stayed inside an isolated environment was found. An absent statement is not the same as an absent procedure
  • Whether the paper's judgements about the defenses come from actually running the tools or from reasoning about their definitions could not be separated. No table showing a reproducible-build tool being run and failing to fire was found
  • Which nine of the 3,799 executables produced no marker, and why, could not be determined. The numbers only show that eight of them were not command-line invokable
  • Whether the same outcome appears on other distributions is outside the experiment. The paper itself notes that generalization depends on the bootstrap structure of the distribution in question
  • The contents of the replication package on figshare were not opened. It contains an attack implementation and is not needed for a defensive summary
  • The description of the nixpkgs bootstrap rests on the source file as it stands today. Whether it is identical to the bootstrap structure at the revision used in the experiment was not compared
