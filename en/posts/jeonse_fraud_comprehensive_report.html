I. Introduction: An Unending Nightmare -- The Current State and Severity of Jeonse Fraud

A. Surging Statistics and Scale of Damage

Jeonse (lump-sum rental deposit) fraud spread into a nationwide social problem in South Korea from around 2022. The clearest official measure of its scale is the number of cases the Jeonse Fraud Victim Support Committee has designated as victims under the Special Act on Support for Jeonse Fraud Victims and Housing Stability, in force since June 1, 2023. That cumulative total stood at 10,944 in early January 2024, seven months after the act took effect (Korea Policy Briefing, 2024-01-05), reached 30,400 by May 2025 (Korea Policy Briefing, 2025-06-02) and rose to 40,278 by July 2026 (Korea Policy Briefing, 2026-08-07). According to the same August 2026 release, the committee had also approved 1,212 requests to postpone urgent auctions and public sales, and designated victims had received a cumulative 72,483 instances of housing, financial and legal support. In July 2026 alone, 609 new cases were approved.

The special act behind these designations has had its validity extended to May 31, 2027, so applications for victim designation can be filed until then. However, tenants who signed their first lease on or after June 1, 2025 are excluded (Korea Policy Briefing, 2025-05-02). Whether someone qualifies is judged against the requirements set by law, so it is best not to assume either way and to check with the competent authority or a professional.

There is also an enforcement-side count. The Korean National Police Agency announced that its 24-month nationwide crackdown on jeonse fraud, launched in July 2022, led to the arrest of 8,323 suspects, 610 of whom were detained; 40 large-scale fraud rings were uncovered, 15 of which were charged with organizing a criminal group under the Criminal Act; and criminal proceeds worth about KRW 191.88 billion were secured for recovery (Korea Policy Briefing, 2024-09-02). Press coverage of the same announcement put the victims identified during the investigations at 16,314 and total losses at KRW 2.4963 trillion (as of the September 2024 announcement). The committee's designation count and the number of victims identified by investigators use different criteria and periods, so they cannot be added together or compared directly.

These statistics make it clear that jeonse fraud is no longer a matter of personal bad luck or a problem confined to certain neighborhoods -- it has escalated into a national-level crisis. The fact that dozens of fraud rings were uncovered, and some were even charged with organizing a criminal group, shows that many of these scams were not isolated acts but organized operations.

B. Background: How Jeonse Fraud Became a Social Crisis

Multiple factors have converged to make jeonse fraud such a severe social problem. What was once limited to specific regions or demographics has spread nationwide, closely linked to structural instability in the real estate market. In recent years, South Korea's real estate market has experienced dramatic price swings driven by changing economic conditions and shifting government policies, causing tremendous confusion for tenants.

The sharp rise in jeonse prices and the broader housing price boom intensified the shortage of jeonse units, pushing tenants to rush into contracts even under unfavorable conditions. Government policy failures and resulting market distortions have also been cited as key drivers of the crisis. This market environment has further widened the information asymmetry between landlords and tenants.

Jeonse fraud has evolved beyond simple individual crimes into a threat that undermines society's entire trust system -- a consequence of these accumulated structural problems. This suggests that the issue cannot be solved through criminal punishment alone; a multifaceted approach including market stabilization and institutional reform is essential.

C. The Primary Victims: The Harsh Reality for Young Renters

The greatest victims of jeonse fraud are people in their 20s and 30s -- young adults who are relatively inexperienced and financially vulnerable. Of the victims recognized in the first seven months after the special act took effect, 73% were in their 20s or 30s: people in their 30s made up the largest share at 48.2%, followed by those in their 20s (24.8%) and 40s (15.7%) (Korea Policy Briefing, 2024-01-05). Press coverage of the police crackdown results likewise put victims in their 30s or younger at 62.8% (as of the September 2024 announcement).

The disproportionate exposure of young people to jeonse fraud is inseparable from their socioeconomic position. With limited assets and less experience, young renters have lower access to critical information during the leasing process and tend to rush contracts to escape housing instability. For many of them, the jeonse deposit represents virtually their entire savings, making fraud not just a financial loss but a devastating blow to their entire lives.

For a generation that already struggles to afford homeownership due to skyrocketing housing prices, jeonse has served as nearly the only rung on the housing ladder. But even this option is now exposed to the risk of fraud, severely threatening housing stability for young adults. This transcends individual harm -- it undermines the economic independence and social stability of the very generation that will lead the country's future, potentially deepening intergenerational inequality and sapping the vitality of society as a whole.

II. Types and Methods of Jeonse Fraud: How They Target Tenants

Jeonse scammers employ a wide range of methods to target tenants' precious deposits. Their tactics are becoming increasingly cunning and organized, making it extremely difficult for ordinary citizens to detect them.

A. Analysis of Major Jeonse Fraud Types

Kkangtong Jeonse (Empty-Shell Jeonse; "Underwater" Deposits)

This is the most fundamental form, where the landlord has neither the intention nor the ability to return the jeonse deposit. In the market, a combined jeonse deposit and mortgage above 80% of the sale price is often treated as a warning sign, but this is a rule of thumb, not a legal threshold. It is closely associated with so-called "gap investment" (gaeptuja), where the landlord purchases property using only the tenant's deposit and bank loans, with no personal capital. This is especially common with newly built villas (low-rise apartment buildings) where accurate market values are hard to determine. Developers or landlords set the jeonse deposit at or above the property's market value, then transfer ownership to an insolvent shell landlord or straw buyer, absconding with the funds. Small-deposit tenants whose deposits are at or below a regional threshold can recover a set amount ahead of other secured creditors at auction, known as top-priority repayment. In Seoul that means up to KRW 55 million for deposits of KRW 165 million or less; the four regional tiers are summarized in the guide to tenant rights and lease protection (Enforcement Decree of the Housing Lease Protection Act, Articles 10 and 11; the amounts set in the February 21, 2023 amendment are still current).

Forged Documents and Fake Landlord Scams

In this scheme, fraudsters use forged property registry documents (deunggibu deungbon) or fake identification during the contract process. They may also present a fake landlord who is not the actual property owner, then sign the contract and steal the deposit.

Double Contracts (Overlapping Leases)

This method involves signing jeonse contracts with two or more tenants for a single rental property and pocketing the deposits. In some cases, an agent tells the actual landlord they are arranging a wolse (monthly rent) contract while secretly signing a jeonse contract with a new tenant and embezzling the difference.

Trust Fraud (Sintaksagi)

This type exploits South Korea's real estate trust system. When a property is under a trust registration -- meaning ownership rests with the trust company -- the original owner (the trustor) may sign a lease without the trust company's consent, rendering the contract void and leaving the tenant's deposit unprotected. In some cases, the building owner transfers ownership to a trust company, takes out bank loans, then colludes with a real estate agent to falsely assure tenants that their claim has priority over the trust company's, signing jeonse contracts and embezzling the deposits.

No-Money-Down Gap Investment (Mujabongaeptuja)

In this scheme, perpetrators purchase large numbers of properties using jeonse deposits alone -- with zero personal capital. When housing prices decline or they fail to secure additional tenants, they become unable to return deposits. Cases have been uncovered in the Seoul metropolitan area where hundreds of homes were acquired this way, with total embezzled deposits reaching tens of billions of KRW. The Korea Housing and Urban Guarantee Corporation (HUG) jeonse deposit return guarantee system has sometimes been exploited in this process, and developers have been found to facilitate simultaneous purchase and jeonse contracts to deceive tenants. As one of its jeonse fraud prevention measures, the government announced in February 2023 that it would lower the jeonse-to-price ratio required for HUG's jeonse deposit return guarantee from 100% to 90% and value homes primarily on actual transaction and officially assessed prices rather than appraisals; the 90% standard has applied to new jeonse contracts since May 1, 2023 (Korea Policy Briefing, 2023-02-02). Detailed eligibility rules may have changed since, so check the current criteria with the guarantor before applying.

The prevalence of no-money-down gap investment suggests that this high-risk investment method was tolerated or insufficiently restrained in parts of the market, raising a warning that institutional loopholes will continue to be exploited.

Jeonse Loan Fraud

In this type, the landlord and tenant collude to fabricate a jeonse contract, then use it to fraudulently obtain government-subsidized jeonse loans from financial institutions.

## 2. Warning signs by scheme

| Scheme | Warning sign | Check first |
|---|---|---|
| Kkangtong jeonse | Deposit plus mortgage is close to the sale price (80% is a market rule of thumb, not a legal threshold). New-build villa with hard-to-verify prices | What share of the sale price the total represents; whether ownership passes to someone else after signing |
| Forged documents / fake landlord | The counterparty may not be the real owner | Whether the registry copy and ID are genuine and the counterparty is the actual owner |
| Double contracts | An agent drives the deal | Confirm with the actual owner which lease type (jeonse or wolse) the agent described |
| Trust fraud | The home is under trust registration, yet the trustor wants to lease it | Whether the trust company has consented (without it the lease may be void) |
| No-money-down gap investment | A developer runs the sale and the jeonse contract at the same time | Whether the home meets HUG's return-guarantee standard (90% jeonse-to-price ratio) |
| Jeonse loan fraud | A fake jeonse contract used to obtain government-subsidized jeonse loans | Whether the contract reflects a real tenancy |
