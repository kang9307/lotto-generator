Introduction: a personal bill and an organizational bill are different problems

At the end of the month the AI coding tool invoice is double what it was. The team has not grown, and neither has the deploy count. Ask who did what to get here and nobody can answer. The first proposal in that situation is almost always the same one: put a per-person cap on it.

Why that proposal tends not to work, and what to do instead, is the subject of this article. The angle of an individual dissecting their own spend locally is already covered in CodeBurn - Analyzing AI Coding Tool Spend Locally. If you want per-tool pricing structures and where the money goes from an individual's point of view, that is the right piece. Here the scope is only how an organization controls cost through policy and architecture: budgets and quotas, model routing, caching, usage visibility, and the gateway that enforces all of it.

One baseline first. The Databricks engineering blog published Managing AI Coding Costs at Scale on 7 August 2026. It documents a setup they actually run internally, and a few figures are cited from it below. Each time, note that the reported savings come from that company's workload. The point of this article is to borrow the method, not the number.

1. What grows the bill is context, not headcount

An intuition from the seat-license era is still around: more people means more cost, and the same people means the same cost. Agentic coding breaks that intuition. The same headcount working the same hours can double the bill.

The reason lies in where the tokens come from. The prompt a developer types is a negligible share of total input. The rest the agent gathers on its own: it reads files, runs searches, executes tests and attaches the output, then resends that inflated context wholesale on the next turn. Databricks points at the same spot, noting that context bloat dominates real-world cost while user input becomes negligible inside agent-gathered context.

So organizational cost grows by multiplication, not addition.

Monthly cost is roughly active users x sessions per user x turns per session x context tokens per turn x price per token

Which of those five axes you touch changes both the effect and the side effect completely. Most organizations reach only for the first, which is the term with the smallest leverage and the largest backlash.

Table 1. Cost multiplication axes and the controls that actually apply
AxisControlExpected effectSide effect
Active usersReclaim seats, restrict accessLinear reductionHits productivity directly, invites shadow IT
Sessions per userUsage guidance, cleaner task boundariesSmallHard to measure, harder to enforce
Turns per sessionBetter task specs, early-stop criteriaModerateIf rework rises, total cost rises with it
Context per turnCaching, smaller tool output, context editingLargeOver-compression degrades quality
Price per tokenMoving the model frontier, routingLargeSwapping models mid-session breaks the cache

The two bolded axes take up most of this article. They require the least code change, disturb the user experience the least, and show up as numbers immediately.

2. The efficiency frontier - not the smartest model, the one that is enough

An organization's default model is usually chosen once and then left alone. Whatever looked best at adoption time hardens in place, and nobody re-measures the cheaper models that shipped afterwards. That is exactly where Databricks locates its largest savings: moving coding spend to more efficient models.

The governing idea is the efficiency frontier. Instead of chasing maximum intelligence, you track the point of best price to performance. The premise underneath is the observation that most real work does not require frontier-level capability.

Two concrete calls that company made appear in the post. Their own benchmarking found GLM models highly competitive on price and performance, which prompted an internal rollout; conversely, Opus 4.7 showed cost regressions without meaningful quality gains in their evaluation, so they declined to adopt it.

Do not carry those two verdicts away as conclusions. They come from one company's workload, one snapshot of a model lineup, and their harness. Models refresh on a scale of months and prices move with them. What is worth transferring is not the verdict but the procedure: keep a representative task set fixed, re-measure with that same set whenever a new model ships, and decide whether to move the default.

Measuring the frontier requires three things to already exist. Without them, model comparison degenerates into an argument about taste.

  • A representative task set - roughly 30 to 50 tasks drawn from your actual repositories, with a mix of difficulty
  • Cost per completed task - not price per request but cumulative cost to finish one task. If the cheap model burns twice the turns, the expensive model is cheaper
  • A success check - an automatically evaluated criterion such as tests passing, lint passing, or reviewer approval. The model's own self-report does not count

The third is the one most often missing. Measure cost without a success check and the cheapest model always wins, because a model that finishes nothing spends the fewest tokens.

3. Routing 1: request level - what do you decide on

Dropping the default model one tier is a one-time saving. The next step is using different models per request, which is routing.

Databricks reported that its in-house Smart Router cut average task cost by more than 30 percent while roughly matching the quality of the most expensive model in the working set. That sentence contains the whole appeal of routing: it is one of the few levers that lowers unit price while holding user-visible quality steady.

There is exactly one hard part. At the moment a request arrives, you cannot tell whether it is hard. A one-line question sometimes turns into an architecture rewrite, and a page of requirements sometimes resolves with one file edit. So practical routers give up on perfect difficulty prediction and exhaust the signals that are cheap to obtain first.

  • Input size - prompt token count, number of attached files, repository size
  • Tool surface - how many tools are enabled. More tools means more turns
  • Task type - autocomplete, inline edit, conversational query, and autonomous agent sit in different price bands to begin with
  • Entry point - an inline IDE request and an autonomous CI job have different latency tolerances
  • History - has this session already failed once

One trap catches people repeatedly here. The router itself is a cost. If you call another LLM to judge difficulty, you are invoking a model twice on every request. However cheap the classifier is, a meaningful share of the savings disappears there. The safe order is to go as far as rule-based signals take you, then layer model-based classification only onto the ambiguous remainder.

4. Routing 2: task level, escalation, and the cache trap

There is also a way to split one layer above the request. A meta-harness looks at the character of the work and dispatches accordingly, sending routine structured work and open-ended work to different models and attaching the expensive model only to the open-ended side.

Attached to that is escalation. The cheap model tries first, and on failure the work is promoted. The concept is simple; two things get in the way in practice.

4.1 What counts as failure

Do not leave the promotion decision to the model's own assessment. Models that decline to say they cannot do something are the majority. The decision has to come from external signals.

  • Test suite failure or compile failure
  • Repeat edits to the same file exceeding a threshold
  • Turn count or cumulative tokens exceeding a budget
  • The user resending the same request

4.2 Promotion breaks the cache

This is the spot that quietly eats routing savings. Prompt caches are scoped per model. Change models mid-session and every cache entry built up in that session is invalidated, and the promoted expensive model re-reads the entire conversation as a cache miss. A single promotion can wipe out that session's cache benefit outright.

There are two responses. First, allow promotion only at session or task boundaries, never mid-turn. Second, split the escalated work into a subagent so the main loop keeps its model fixed and only the sub-task goes to a different one. The latter is especially favorable because it preserves the main session's cache prefix.

5. Caching is an architectural decision, not a discount

Treat prompt caching as a billing option and it usually does nothing. The cache works by prefix match. Render order is tool definitions, then the system prompt, then messages, and if one byte changes at the front, everything after it is invalidated. In other words, your cache hit rate is decided by the structure of the code that assembles prompts.

5.1 Multipliers and break-even

Per Anthropic official documentation, a cache write costs 1.25x the base input price at a 5-minute TTL or 2x at a 1-hour TTL, and a cache read costs 0.1x. The Claude Opus 5 example prices from the documentation are reproduced below. Model prices change often, so read these as confirmed on 10 August 2026.

Table 2. Cache multipliers and break-even (Anthropic official docs, confirmed 2026-08-10)
CategoryMultiplierOpus 5 example price (per 1M tokens)Break-even
Base input1xUSD 5-
5-minute TTL cache write1.25xUSD 6.25Profitable from the 2nd request
1-hour TTL cache write2xUSD 10Profitable from the 3rd request
Cache read0.1xUSD 0.5-

The 1-hour TTL keeps entries alive across bursty traffic with gaps between requests, but the write costs twice as much, so it needs more reads to pay for itself. For a workload that cycles at short intervals, the default 5 minutes is enough.

5.2 The minimum length that fails silently

The same documentation states the minimum cacheable prompt length. Claude Opus 5, Fable 5, and Mythos 5 are 512 tokens; Opus 4.8, Sonnet 5, Sonnet 4.6, Sonnet 4.5, Opus 4.1, and Opus 4 are 1,024; Opus 4.7 and Haiku 3.5 are 2,048; Opus 4.6, Opus 4.5, and Haiku 4.5 are 4,096. A prefix shorter than that is not cached even with a cache directive attached, and no error is returned. The code looks fine and only the invoice stays the same.

5.3 What kills hit rate at organizational scale

A few patterns are invisible in a personal tool and fatal across a whole company.

  • Stamping the current time into the system prompt - the prefix differs on every request and company-wide hit rate converges to zero
  • Slipping a user ID or session ID near the front - the cache splinters per user and sharing disappears
  • Assembling the tool list per user - tool definitions render first. If they differ per person, nobody gets a cache
  • Not fixing JSON serialization order - unstable key order means different bytes
  • Conditional system sections - every flag combination creates its own prefix

The invalidation hierarchy the documentation spells out matters too. Changing tool definitions invalidates the tool, system, and message caches together; changing the system prompt invalidates system and below. Toggling web search or citations, changing the speed setting, and changing the effort level also touch the cache. Handle those separately from items like tool_choice that affect message blocks only.

5.4 Put hit rate on a dashboard

Three values arrive in the response usage: cache_creation_input_tokens, cache_read_input_tokens, and input_tokens, and their sum is total input tokens. Cache hit rate is read tokens divided by that sum. Chart that ratio per team and the day somebody puts a timestamp in the system prompt shows up immediately.

6. Where tokens actually leak - the harness and tool output

Databricks reported cutting generated token count by roughly 50 percent while holding quality through harness tuning and caching configuration adjustments. What makes that number interesting is that it came without changing the model. Same model, same tasks, half the tokens.

Where to look is fairly settled.

  • Full tool output inlined - 2,000 lines of test logs, whole build output, entire large files. Cap it, spill the overflow to a file, and pass only the path
  • Re-reading the same file - the agent reads a file it already read on a previous turn. One in-session read cache solves it
  • Search instead of full reads - rewrite tool descriptions so the agent retrieves only the relevant span instead of pulling whole files
  • Return diffs, not whole files - do not take edited results back in full
  • Residue of abandoned plans - discarded attempts stay in context and get billed again on every subsequent turn. Context editing that clears stale tool results attaches here
  • Bloated system prompts - 2,000 tokens of unused instructions multiply across every request

There is a limit here as well. If compression causes rework, total cost goes up. When trimmed context makes the agent search for and re-read the same file, the added turns cost more than the tokens saved. The verdict is always cost per completed task, never tokens per request.

7. Budgets 1: why hard caps fail, and progressive friction

Setting a monthly per-person limit and blocking on overage is intuitive and easy to build. Databricks treats it as counterproductive. In its place they put integrated cost visibility, tiered approval gates, model downgrades, and progressive friction instead of immediate denial.

Why counterproductive? Two reasons.

First, a developer who hits a cap does not stop working. They move to a personal account, paste company code into a personal chat window, or take a cheaper path with worse results. The organization has not reduced spend; it has simultaneously lost visibility and security control over it.

Second, a cap conveys no information. Knowing you have used 80 percent of a monthly limit tells you nothing about what the task you are about to run will cost. People hit a wall with no basis on which to have adjusted their behavior.

Table 3. Progressive friction - stages instead of a block
StageHow it worksWhat the user experiencesPurpose
1. VisibilityNear real-time spend feedbackImmediately sees what the last task costSupplies a basis for adjusting behavior
2. Self-clearing gateWarning at threshold, proceeds on acknowledgementA momentary pause, not a blockFilters only unconscious waste
3. DownshiftAutomatic switch to a lower-cost model past a thresholdCan keep working, with different output qualityKeeps the work, lowers the unit price
4. SuspensionLast resortBlockedIncident prevention

The other side is also true: an unbounded organizational budget does not prevent incidents. One misconfigured agent loop burning a monthly budget in a few hours is a thing that actually happens. So the practical setup splits into two layers.

  • Progressive friction for people and teams - give information, give alternatives, block only at the end
  • Hard caps for individual sessions and keys - automated execution nobody is watching needs an absolute ceiling

That distinction matters. A limit placed on a person and a limit placed on a process serve different purposes. The first tries to change behavior; the second tries to stop a runaway.

8. Budgets 2: where to attach the limit - scope design

Once the budget policy is decided, where to attach it remains. Looking at the scopes a gateway layer offers makes the design choices concrete. Taking the open-source proxy LiteLLM as an example, the scopes fan out across several layers.

Table 4. Control points by budget scope (LiteLLM proxy)
ScopeSettingWhat it stops
Globalmax_budget and budget_duration in general_settingsCompany-wide runaway spend
Teammax_budget at team creationOne team eating the company budget
Member within a teammax_budget_in_teamSkew inside a team's allocation
Internal userBudget at user creationWorking around limits by minting several keys
Virtual keyBudget at key generationRunaway automation and CI pipelines
Per model (enterprise)model_max_budgetOveruse of only the expensive model on one key
End userThe user parameter on the requestPer-customer abuse without issuing keys

One setting causes incidents especially often. budget_duration accepts formats such as 30s, 30m, 30h, and 30d, and if it is not set the budget never resets. Leave it blank believing it renews monthly and the whole team stops the moment the first month's budget is exhausted.

Enforcement accuracy is worth checking too. LiteLLM uses a cross-pod counter in Redis to keep enforcement fast and consistent across workers, with the database as the authoritative record reconciled in the background. For deployments where accuracy matters, fail_closed_budget_enforcement validates against the database on every request. In other words, budget enforcement carries an accuracy versus latency tradeoff, and the default generally sits on the latency side.

8.1 Rate limits come before budgets

A budget is a lagging indicator. It counts money already spent, so it cannot cut off a runaway in real time. What cuts off in real time is the rate limit. Put tokens per minute, requests per minute, and concurrent requests in place first and the maximum size of an incident is bounded.

  • Tokens per minute - you can choose whether to count input only, output only, or the total (token_rate_limit_type)
  • Requests per minute - the first thing a runaway retry loop trips
  • Concurrent requests - max_parallel_requests. Especially important in harnesses with many parallel subagents
  • Per-model limits - model_rpm_limit and model_tpm_limit. Squeeze only the expensive models

In order: rate limits bound the size of an incident, session caps cut off automated execution, budgets manage the trend, and friction changes behavior.

9. Usage visibility - what you have to measure to control anything

This is where most organizations actually get stuck. There is an invoice, and it contains no person's name, no repository name, no task name. The only decision available in that state is to reduce the total.

9.1 You have to change the unit

Monthly spend per person is not a controllable unit. The controllable unit is cost per task: cost per pull request, per bug fixed, per test suite brought back to green. Converted into that unit, the effect of changing a model or fixing a harness becomes a visible number. And only when spend can sit next to the output it produced can you tell reduction apart from investment.

9.2 Take metric names from the standard

Invent your own metric names in the collection layer and you rebuild the dashboards every time you change tools. The OpenTelemetry generative AI semantic conventions define this part.

Table 5. OpenTelemetry generative AI metrics (stability: development)
MetricType and unitKey attributesUse
gen_ai.client.token.usageHistogram, {token}gen_ai.operation.name, gen_ai.provider.name, gen_ai.token.type (input/output), gen_ai.request.model, gen_ai.response.modelBase unit for cost attribution
gen_ai.client.operation.durationHistogram, secondsSame as above plus error.type on errorLatency and failure rate
gen_ai.client.operation.time_to_first_chunkHistogram, secondsOperation, provider, modelPerceived responsiveness
gen_ai.client.operation.time_per_output_chunkHistogram, secondsOperation, provider, modelStreaming quality

That the requested model and the responding model are separate attributes is especially useful when operating routing. Cases where the requested model differs from the one that answered, meaning routing or a fallback intervened, can be separated directly in the metrics.

One caution. These conventions are still at development stability. Names and attributes may change, so it is better to keep a mapping layer in the collector and avoid having dashboards reference raw names directly.

9.3 Getting AI spend onto the same sheet as cloud spend

Manage AI spend in a separate spreadsheet and eventually nobody looks at it. The FinOps Foundation's FOCUS is an open specification that normalizes billing data across AI, cloud, SaaS, and data center into one schema, currently at version 1.4. Conform your billing data to it and AI spend can be viewed on the same axis as the existing cloud cost dashboard.

9.4 Latency decides how much control you have

What Databricks emphasizes in the visibility section is ultimately speed as well: dashboards giving near-instantaneous spend feedback. A month-end invoice is not a control. Information that is 30 days old changes no behavior. Daily gives you a trend; per-session and people adjust their own behavior.

10. The AI gateway - what a central layer buys and costs

Every control above shares a premise: it has to be enforced in one place. If model selection is baked into each team's code, you cannot follow the frontier when it moves; if budget rules live per application, there is no consistency; if metric formats differ, you cannot compute cost per task.

Databricks runs central infrastructure too. One place owns model menu management, budget tracking including progressive friction policies, configuration enforcement, and session logging for analysis. They describe it as the mechanism that maintains flexibility across model transitions.

10.1 What a gateway makes possible

  • Model swaps as a config change - change the default model without a deploy. That is the precondition for tracking the frontier
  • A single enforcement point for budgets and rate limits - there is no path around it
  • Unified cache policy - standardize system prompt structure to raise hit rate across the whole organization
  • Audit and data control - a record of which code went to which provider
  • Provider diversification - an alternate path during outages or rate limiting

10.2 The new risks a gateway creates

  • Single point of failure - if the gateway dies, company-wide development stops. Decide in advance whether the default on failure is pass-through or block
  • An added layer of latency - latency-sensitive paths such as inline autocomplete may need a bypass
  • Cache destruction - if the gateway reassembles prompts or injects headers and changes the prefix, the cache breaks wholesale. This is the classic case of a layer added to cut cost that raises it
  • Feature lag - new provider features are unusable until the gateway supports them

If you adopt one, at least three things are worth confirming before adoption: whether it preserves cache prefixes byte for byte, whether budget enforcement is consistent across instances, and what the default behavior on failure is.

11. Rollout order - what to touch first

Do all six at once and you cannot tell which one worked. The order has a reason: each step builds the basis for measuring the next.

  1. Visibility first - attribute spend to team, repository, and task. Without it everything else is guesswork. Usually the answer already emerges here, since spend is commonly concentrated in a handful of workloads
  2. Recover cache hit rate - the least code change and the fastest visible effect. Strip variable elements out of the system prompt, make the tool list deterministic, and chart hit rate
  3. Clean up the harness - cap tool output, prefer search, return diffs, clear stale tool results. This is the last stretch you can cut without changing models
  4. Re-measure the default model - measure the frontier with the representative task set and move the default. With a gateway it is one line of config
  5. Routing - the reason not to start here is that without the preceding steps there is no baseline against which to measure the routing effect
  6. Budget friction policy - ongoing. That said, session-level hard caps and rate limits are better installed alongside step 1. Incidents do not wait their turn

12. Common mistakes

  • Choosing a model by price per request - if the cheap model burns twice the turns, total cost is higher. Judge on cost per completed task
  • Enabling caching and never checking hit rate - falling under the minimum length and prefix drift both fail silently with no error
  • Swapping models mid-session - the cache loss offsets the routing savings
  • Capping only people - if automated execution has no cap, that is where the incident comes from
  • Leaving the budget reset period blank - a budget that never resets stops the team the moment it is exhausted
  • Adopting another company's savings figure as a target - 30 percent or 50 percent, it came from their workload. What transfers is the measurement procedure
  • Making total users or total tokens a KPI - the easiest way to reduce either is to stop using the tools
  • Not noticing that the gateway breaks the cache - a cost-reduction layer that raises cost does happen in practice
  • Applying friction before visibility - block people without giving them information and they route around it

13. What could not be confirmed

Collected here are the things left out of the body and the cited items whose verification scope is limited.

  • Measurement conditions behind the cited savings - the sample size, measurement window, workload definition, and quality criteria behind the more-than-30-percent and roughly-50-percent figures are not published. Treat them as directional only
  • The Smart Router implementation - which signals it routes on and which model lineup it targeted are not in the public post
  • Batch and asynchronous processing discounts - trading latency tolerance for a lower unit price is widely offered, but the discount depth varies by provider and by date. Because it must be verified against your own contract and the current rate card, no number is printed here
  • Current per-model prices - the prices in Table 2 are examples from the official documentation, confirmed on 10 August 2026. Re-verify before making an adoption decision
  • Effective unit price for organizations mixing seat licenses with metered APIs - structures differ per tool and no basis for generalizing was found
  • When the OpenTelemetry generative AI metrics stabilize - they are at development stability and no GA date is stated
  • Details of the AI-related columns in FOCUS 1.4 - that the specification covers AI billing data was confirmed, but individual column definitions require the separate column library document

Conclusion

In order: make it visible first, then fix the structure, and impose limits last. Most organizations walk that order backwards. They see the invoice, panic, cap first, people route around it, visibility gets worse, and next month's invoice surprises them again.

Of the six items covered here, the largest effect per unit of code change is caching. Stripping variable elements out of the system prompt and freezing the tool list is a day of work, and hit rate shows up in the metrics the next day. The one that takes longest but pays repeatedly is the procedure for periodically measuring the model frontier. Build it once and every new model release resolves into a decision within a day.

And on limits: never mix a limit placed on a person with one placed on a process. Give people information and options and block only at the end, and put a hard ceiling from the outset on automated execution nobody is watching. That single distinction decides most of whether the policy works.
