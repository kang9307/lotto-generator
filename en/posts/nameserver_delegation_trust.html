Introduction: what two lines actually change

After changing two nameserver lines in a domain control panel, the next thing most people do is check whether the site loads. If it loads, the job is considered done. If mail stops arriving, that is when the MX record gets moved, and fixing that one thing feels like the end of the migration.

Those two lines do not stop at changing who answers for the name. If one toggle elsewhere on the same screen is on, the HTML your visitors receive also passes through the other side. From that moment, code that is not in your repository can execute in a visitor browser under your domain name. This is not an accident, it is the design. A proxy has to be able to rewrite responses for caching, compression and firewalling to work at all.

In August 2026 a developer posted that after moving the nameservers for a static, JavaScript-free site, an analytics snippet had appeared in the pages, and the post carried over into Korean-language communities. This article does not retrace that incident. The incident is only the entry point. The subject is what handing over your nameservers actually delegates, layer by layer. Once the layers are separated, it becomes possible to tell the difference between something that went wrong and something that was built to work this way.

1. The four layers - four powers that move in one screen

In practice all four usually sit with one company, because running the registrar, managed DNS and CDN from a single account is convenient. Convenience blurs the boundaries, and blurred boundaries make it hard to point at a cause when something breaks. Separate them first.

LayerWhat you delegateWhat the other side can now doCost of reversing
Domain registrarRegistration record and accountChange the nameserver designation, approve transfers, publish DS recordsTransfer procedure and waiting period, unlocking
Authoritative DNSAnswer generation for the whole zoneDecide A, AAAA, CNAME, MX and TXT values, sign with DNSSEC, create domain-control validation recordsRebuild the zone, then wait for TTLs to drain
Proxy and CDNThe HTTP(S) traffic pathTerminate TLS, cache, block requests, modify response headers and bodyPoint records back to origin, wait for TTLs, re-expose the origin IP
CertificateIssuance requests and private key custodyObtain and renew certificates for your domain name, handle plaintext at the terminating pointChange issuers, consider revoking existing certificates

The table makes the difference in character visible. The registrar is the layer that decides who gets the other layers, so it sits on top. Authoritative DNS is the layer that produces the final answer for a name, so it becomes the premise for the rest. The proxy is the layer the actual bytes travel through, and the certificate layer decides who is allowed to see those bytes in the clear.

What matters is that the upper layers effectively determine the lower ones. Whoever holds the registrar account can change nameservers, whoever holds authoritative DNS can pass domain control validation and obtain certificates, and whoever holds the proxy can rewrite the response body. Worrying from the bottom up is worrying too late.

2. Handing over authoritative DNS - final answers and certificate issuance

2.1 An NS record is a pointer, not a service contract

In the domain name system the parent zone does not hold the child zone answers. The parent only says, through NS records, the answer for this name comes from over there, and the designated server produces the actual answer. That structure has been in place since the original specifications, RFC 1034 and RFC 1035. Designating a nameserver is therefore not the outsourcing of query handling but the transfer of authority over your name.

So whoever holds authoritative DNS can produce every record value in the zone. Not only where the web points, but where mail goes, what the sender authentication policy says and what the ownership-verification string is - all of it lives there. The familiar accident where mail stops right after a migration is a side effect of the same property. If the MX record was not copied into the new zone, the new authoritative server answers no such record authoritatively.

2.2 DNSSEC and who holds the signing key

If DNSSEC was in use, delegation means the signing party moves. The new provider holds the key that signs the zone, and the DS record pointing at that key is published into the parent zone through the registrar. Getting the order wrong makes the entire domain fail validation and become unresolvable, which is why the usual procedure is to turn DNSSEC off, migrate, and turn it back on. This ordering problem resists automation, so a human has to verify it.

2.3 Certificate issuance hangs off DNS

This is the part that gets skipped most often. Automated certificate issuance today mostly runs on the ACME specification (RFC 8555), and its dns-01 method proves domain control by asking whether you can create a TXT record at a given name. The conclusion is simple. Whoever holds authoritative DNS is in a position to obtain certificates for that domain. Recalling that wildcard certificates require dns-01 gives a sense of how far the scope reaches.

A CAA record (RFC 8659) can restrict which certification authorities may issue, but CAA is itself a record in the same zone. Whoever controls the zone controls CAA. So CAA is a defense against mis-issuance by outsiders, not a defense against the authoritative DNS provider. That is less a flaw than the definition of the trust model. Authoritative DNS sits inside the trust boundary.

In short - delegating nameservers is not the outsourcing of query responses but the transfer of a substantial share of domain control. Certificate issuance hangs off it, so registrar and DNS account security deserves the same grade of attention as web server security.

3. What changes when the proxy goes on - passing through and terminating

3.1 Lookup answers stop pointing at your origin

When a managed DNS provider also sells a proxy, each record carries a proxy indicator. Only address records, A and AAAA, and the alias record CNAME can be proxied. Everything else, MX and TXT included, is always direct. That distinction alone tells you what a proxy is: it pulls in HTTP(S) traffic only.

For a proxied record, the lookup answer carries the provider anycast address instead of your origin IP. The browser connects there, the request is handled inside the provider network and forwarded to the origin, and the response returns the same way. Product documentation states that proxying is on by default when a domain is added through the dashboard (checked 2026-08-17). If the migration flow imports existing records automatically, records can arrive in the proxied state without the user ever choosing it explicitly.

3.2 TLS ends there

Once the proxy is on, the visitor TLS session terminates at the proxy. The provider holds the certificate for that leg and handles the decrypted request headers, cookies and body. Wrapping the proxy-to-origin leg in TLS again produces a separate connection, not a continuation of the first. Anyone who has stood up a reverse proxy directly will recognize the structure. The only difference is that this proxy is not under your control.

The property is a precondition, not a defect. Caching requires reading the body, rule-based blocking requires parsing the request, and compression or image conversion requires rewriting the response. Being able to rewrite means both headers and body can change in transit.

3.3 What origin IP masking charges you

The most common reason to turn the proxy on is to hide the origin address. The gains are clear.

  • Volumetric attacks never reach the origin directly
  • Static assets leave from a point near the visitor, improving response time by region
  • A web application firewall, rate limiting and bot classification can be layered on without touching server code
  • Certificate issuance and renewal are handled by the provider, which cuts down expiry incidents

The price is just as clear.

  • One point on the path handles every request and response in plaintext
  • A feature addition or default change at that point becomes a behavior change on your site
  • Provider downtime becomes your downtime, and routing around it means reverting records and waiting out TTLs. If the masked origin IP leaks through historical records or outbound mail, the masking loses its effect anyway
  • Access logs now show the proxy as the source address, so the origin has to be reconfigured to trust forwarded headers

The last item is where security incidents start. If the origin is told to trust forwarded headers unconditionally and the proxy is later turned off, anyone can declare their own source address. When you turn a proxy off, revert that setting with it.

4. Why automatic HTML injection exists

If a point on the path can rewrite responses, code can be added to pages without going through a deployment. That is a value proposition several CDN and proxy products share. The typical uses look like this.

  • Real user performance measurement - the browser measures paint timings and reports them
  • Traffic analytics - page views and referral paths are aggregated
  • Automatic optimization - lazy image loading or script load ordering gets adjusted
  • Bot defense and regional notices - classification code, challenge pages, and consent banners that vary by visitor region

Take measurement as the example and the principle is simple. Only the browser knows when the screen was painted, so real user performance metrics cannot be measured server-side. Rather than opening every repository to add a tag, a single line gets inserted just before the closing body tag as the response passes through. The provider loses adoption friction and the user has nothing to touch.

Once the structure is clear, so is the judgment. The existence of the feature is not the problem. The problem is where the default sits and how a change to that default is communicated. If the default is off, only code from your repository runs on your domain. If it is on, code that is not in your repository executes under your domain name. The latter touches your content security policy, your privacy notice and your audit trail.

On this particular case, the following can be confirmed from public material (checked 2026-08-17).

  • In September 2025 the provider stated on its official blog that Web Analytics would be enabled by default for all free-plan domains starting 15 October 2025
  • Per product documentation, automatic setup targets proxied sites, with the beacon inserted as the response passes through the provider network. Non-proxied sites require manual installation
  • The settings offer enabling, enabling while excluding EU visitors, enabling with manual snippet installation, and disabling
  • Documentation states in effect that once the product has been disabled it will not be re-enabled, and per the provider the beacon uses no client-side state and does not track individuals

5. How to read this report

The report goes as follows. After moving the nameservers of a static site that uses no JavaScript, an analytics snippet was present in the pages, and disabling it required first adding the site in the analytics dashboard before the disable option appeared. The poster also states the domain arrived in the proxied state during migration, and argues that both of these should be opt-in.

The same thread carried rebuttals. One pointed out that when only lookups are delegated and the proxy is off, there is no point at which the response could be rewritten, so no snippet can be inserted. That point is structurally correct. If injection was observed, the domain was in the proxied state. So the question narrows from was something inserted silently to where did the defaults for proxying and analytics sit, and how visible were they during migration.

6. How to check what state your site is in

Dashboard layouts differ by product and change often, so memorizing click paths is pointless. Holding what to look at as a concept works on any product.

CheckWhat you look atWarning sign
Delegation stateThe NS list the parent zone hands out for your domain, and the servers actually answeringServers you do not recognize, or old and new providers coexisting
Whether a path existsWhether the name resolves to your origin IP or a provider rangeConnections land somewhere other than the origin and you did not know
Intermediary tracesWhether responses carry server names or trace identifiers your origin never producedHeaders absent from your origin config appear consistently
Body differencesThe diff between HTML fetched directly from the origin and HTML fetched through the public URLScript or link tags that are not in your repository
Per-record stateThe proxy toggle on each A, AAAA and CNAME recordRecords you never intended to proxy are proxied
Response-rewriting featuresThe state of analytics beacons, automatic optimization, script management and injected widgetsShown as on when you have no memory of enabling it
Policy violation reportsThe source list that arrives when a content security policy runs in report-only modeSources you never allowed are reported repeatedly

The cheapest and most reliable of these is the fourth: comparing two copies of the HTML. Put the response fetched directly from the origin next to the response fetched through the public URL and look only at the difference. No difference means nothing was added along the path. A new tag means something was. Neither human memory nor a dashboard display sits in the middle, so there is no room for misunderstanding.

This comparison belongs in your deployment pipeline. After a release, extract only the script source list from both responses, compare, and fail if they diverge. Narrowing the target to sources filters out noise from caching and compression differences.

A content security policy is a valid instrument too, with limits worth knowing. The policy header is part of the response and travels the same path, and subresource integrity does not apply to code inserted inline. Treat the policy as a detection mechanism and make the final decision in the settings screen. If you manage certificate issuance yourself, it is worth confirming whose hands create the dns-01 validation records.

7. Consent and notice - when code runs in a visitor browser

What follows is a statute-level summary. Individual determinations depend on the facts, so this is not legal advice and any real review belongs with a lawyer or qualified professional. The examples below cite Korean statutes because that is the jurisdiction this site is anchored in; the equivalent provisions in your own jurisdiction will differ in numbering while covering similar ground.

The starting point is simple. From the visitor perspective that screen is your domain. Even if a third party inserted something along the path, the name in the address bar is yours, and visitors hold the site operator responsible for what appears. Article 10 of the Act on Consumer Protection in Electronic Commerce, which requires an online mall operator to display trade name, representative, address, contact details and business registration number, rests on the same premise: the screen belongs to the operator.

On the privacy side, three provisions are worth separating.

  • Personal Information Protection Act, Article 30 - establishing and publishing a privacy policy. What is processed and for what purpose must be written down and made available
  • Same Act, Article 26 - restrictions on processing delegated to an outsourced party. Delegation must be set out in a document and the fact of delegation disclosed
  • Same Act, Article 28-8 - cross-border transfer of personal information. The special provisions on overseas transfer were added in March 2023 and took effect on 15 September that year

There is a point where judgment splits. If a provider states that a beacon uses no client-side state and does not identify individuals, whether that processing constitutes personal information processing at all is a fact-specific determination. That said, once a request leaves the browser, the connecting address and user agent string reach the other side. The practical position is therefore singular. You need to know the list of code that runs on your domain. Without the list you cannot write the policy either.

European traffic adds another axis. The general data protection regime and the electronic communications rules attach consent requirements to storing on or accessing information in a terminal device. Measurement designs that avoid cookies and local storage are aimed precisely at that point, which is also why a setting exists to exclude the beacon by region. If you have European visitors, that option is not one to ignore.

The point of this section is not to frighten anyone about legal exposure. It is to keep the ability to explain what runs on your screen, and that state is produced by the checks in the previous section.

8. Alternatives and their price

There are four options. Each has a clear gain and a clear loss, so pick by situation.

8.1 Run authoritative DNS yourself

You produce every answer in the zone, and the certificate issuance path and signing key stay in your hands. In exchange you carry availability whole. Run at least two servers on separate networks and keep up with query surges and software updates. When lookups stop, it is not a service that disappears but the name, so the blast radius differs from losing one web server. Unless the regulatory requirements are strict or you already have infrastructure staff, it is hard to recommend.

8.2 Use managed DNS but keep the proxy off

The provider carries lookup availability and traffic goes straight to the origin. Since there is no way for a response to change in transit, the injection problem this article covers does not arise at all. The price is origin IP exposure and the loss of the cache and firewall layers. For a mostly static site with modest traffic and its own defenses in front of the origin, the loss is small. For a public service, choosing this without a replacement leaves the attack surface wide open.

8.3 Split the layers across providers

Putting the registrar, managed DNS and proxy in different places means one compromised account does not hand over everything. Each layer carries its own authentication, with registrar locks and multi-factor authentication on top. The price is operational complexity. Identifying which layer failed takes longer, and there are more support channels to work through. For a service where the domain itself is a business asset, that complexity is worth paying.

8.4 Keep the proxy on but explicitly lock down response rewriting

This is the compromise that fits most commercial services. Defense and performance stay, injection risk drops. For it to hold, though, the HTML comparison from the previous section has to be running automatically. Locking a setting once is only a statement about that moment, and when the provider adds a feature the verification starts over. The recurring inspection cost is exactly what makes this option safe.

Whichever you pick, the criterion converges. Leave yourself room to reverse anything you cannot verify. If you turn the proxy on, write down the procedure and the time it takes to revert. If you use managed DNS, export the whole zone to a file and keep it. Not knowing how long reversal takes is the worst state to be in.

9. What could not be confirmed

Here in one place are the items that cannot be settled from the outside.

  • The actual settings history on the poster account and the selection state at migration time
  • Whether an individual notice about the default-enablement policy reached that user
  • The onboarding screen layout and wording at the time of the report
  • Whether the provider issued an official confirmation or rebuttal on this post. Nothing of the kind was confirmed inside the public thread
  • Whether defaults apply differently by plan tier or region, and whether domains with a history of other measurement features are handled differently from those without

Until that list is filled in, no conclusion about who was at fault can be reached from the outside. What the product documentation does confirm is that in this structure, a proxied response can change, and that alone is enough to determine what to do now.

10. Frequently asked questions

Can my HTML change just because I moved my nameservers?

Delegation on its own does not change it. Authoritative DNS produces answers for names, and if those answers point at the origin, the browser connects there directly. For the HTML to differ, traffic has to pass through something in the middle, and that something is a proxy. When a managed DNS provider also sells a proxy, each record carries a toggle, and when it is on the lookup answer carries the provider address instead of the origin IP. So the question narrows from did I move nameservers to is the proxy on.

Where does TLS end once the proxy is on?

The visitor TLS session ends at the proxy. The provider holds the certificate for that leg and handles the decrypted request and response. Wrapping the proxy-to-origin leg in TLS again creates a separate connection. The property is the precondition for caching, blocking and conversion, so it is not a defect to call out. It does mean end-to-end encryption is not an accurate description here, and the only variable is whether you made that trade knowingly.

Why does automatic script injection exist at all?

So instrumentation can be added without a deployment. Real user performance metrics can only be measured in the browser, and planting a tag in every site and cutting a release is expensive. Inserting one line as the response passes through produces the same result. Image optimization, bot defense and consent banners work the same way. The contention is not the feature but where the default sits, and an on-by-default means code outside your repository executes under your domain name.

How do I check what got inserted into my site?

Comparing HTML fetched directly from the origin with HTML fetched through the public URL is the most reliable method. A script or link tag that was not there before was added along the path. Next comes checking the address connections land on and the response headers, and last the per-record proxy toggles and the state of response-rewriting features. For something continuous, run a content security policy in report-only mode and collect unexpected sources.

What do I lose by turning the proxy off?

The origin IP goes back into the lookup answer. Volumetric attacks have to be stopped directly, the cache disappears so every request travels the full distance, and the firewall and rate limiting layers go with it. In exchange no intermediate point sees plaintext and the response cannot be rewritten in transit. For a mostly static site the loss is small. For a public service, turning it off without a replacement is hard to recommend, and when you do, revert the origin setting that trusts forwarded headers as well.

11. Conclusion - three things to settle today

First, write the layers down. Which registrar, which authoritative DNS, is the proxy on, who issues the certificates and where the private key lives. It is four lines of work, and the absence of those four lines is why so many teams do not know where to look when something breaks.

Second, compare the two copies of your HTML. Run it once today to confirm there is no difference, then turn that comparison into a check that runs automatically after every deployment. Comparing only the script source list keeps it noise-free. With that check in place, it does not matter which provider turns on which feature and when - the next deployment catches it.

Third, write down the reversal procedure and how long it takes. Turning the proxy off exposes the origin IP, requires reverting the forwarded-header setting, and costs you a TTL of waiting. Learning those three things in the middle of an incident is a completely different situation from reading them out of a document.

Delegating nameservers is not a bad choice in itself. Running it yourself means carrying availability yourself, and for most organizations that cost exceeds the cost of delegation. What has to be understood is that delegation is not query handling but the transfer of the final answer for your name, the certificate issuance path, and - if the proxy is on - the bytes your visitors receive. Delegating knowingly produces verification steps, and with verification steps a changed default shows up in the next deployment.
