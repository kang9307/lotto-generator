The Finger That Catches and Snaps - Why Trigger Finger Is a Tunnel Problem, Not a Joint Problem

What gets stuck at the A1 pulley, and how the stage of the condition changes what is still on the table

Sep 1, 2026 Health & Wellness

One finger will not straighten easily in the morning. Push harder and at some point it snaps open. By the afternoon it behaves, and the next morning the same thing happens again. Most people who meet this for the first time suspect the joint. The place that bends and straightens is a joint, so the misreading is a natural one.

But the catch is not in the joint. The formal name is stenosing tenosynovitis, and in the international classification of diseases M65.3 covers trigger finger together with nodular tendinous disease, splitting from M65.31 for the thumb down to M65.35 for the little finger. The word sheath in the name is the answer. The problem sits not where bone meets bone but in the tunnel the tendon runs through. Once that is clear, why joint supplements aim at the wrong tissue and why forcing the finger straight is a bad idea both follow from the same line.

This is general information, and diagnosis and treatment follow a clinician's judgement. The figures here come from different samples at different times and cannot be transferred directly to any individual course. If a finger is locked bent and will not straighten, or there is fever with the whole finger swelling, stop reading and be seen.

What catches is the tendon and the tunnel, not the joint

The force that bends a finger does not come from the finger. As the patient information from the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons puts it, the flexor tendons are long cord-like structures connecting the forearm muscles to the bones of the fingers, and the finger folds as that cord is pulled. The devices that hold the cord against the bone so it does not lift like a bowstring are pulleys, and the same material compares the arrangement to the guides on a fishing rod.

The one sitting on the palm side at the base of each finger is the A1 pulley, and it is the pulley most involved in trigger finger. The mechanism is simple. A size mismatch develops between the tendon and the tunnel. The tendon thickens or swells into a bump at one point, and the A1 pulley thickens and narrows as well, leaving less room to pass through. The moment the swollen part forces its way through the narrowed entrance is the catch, and the moment it clears is the snap.

There is a place where the name and the reality diverge. The tissue findings summarised in a 2025 clinical review are not a state of increased inflammatory cells but something closer to fibrocartilaginous change from repeated friction. The inflammation in the name does not describe the pathology.

  • Where it catches — the base of the finger on the palm. The pain is sometimes felt at the middle knuckle instead, which makes the location easy to misplace
  • What moves — the flexor tendon. The problem is not the act of bending but the passage on the way back out
  • What narrows — the A1 pulley. It is not tissue that widens under pressure from a thumb
  • The joint's role — none. Though leaving a finger bent for long enough adds a stiffening joint on top

Why it is worst in the morning

The Academy material states that stiffness and locking tend to be worse after periods of inactivity, and gives waking up as the standard example. The observation is documented. The widely repeated explanation of overnight swelling and a flexed sleeping posture, however, could not be confirmed as a verified mechanism in a primary source, so this piece treats only the observation as fact.

What clinicians actually work with is not the cause but the pattern. How many movements it takes to free the finger, and whether one digit is involved or several, sets the direction of the next question. In particular, several fingers catching together reads not as a worse case but as a different signal. In a 1997 study the insulin-dependent diabetic group had significantly more multiple-digit involvement than controls, and a 2025 clinical review describes the same pattern in rheumatoid arthritis. The same phrase, morning stiffness, is a single tunnel problem for one person and a window onto systemic disease for another.

Stages, from pain to a locked digit

What sets this apart from other hand conditions is that the stages are visible. The Quinnell grading introduced in the 2025 clinical review runs from grade 0 to a fixed deformity at grade 4, and the criterion is not how much it hurts but whether the catch can be released without help. When the stage changes, the options change.

StageWhat is observedWhat changes at this stage
Pain onlyPressing the base of the finger on the palm hurts. No catching yetWhere activity change and splinting are discussed. Shorter symptom duration reports better results
CatchingSomething catches at a point during movementThe main target for splinting. Several six-week wear trials exist
LockingIt stops, and the other hand is needed to straighten itSplinting and injection are discussed together. The band studied in joint-blocking orthosis trials
FixedNeither the hand itself nor the other hand can straighten itAn approach based on restricting movement no longer applies. Be seen without delay

The logic of that last row is plain. A splint reduces irritation by restricting movement, and a finger that already does not move has no movement left to restrict. Before the stage marks severity, it marks whether options remain.

Who gets it

It is not a rare condition. The Academy material states that it affects two percent of the general population, and the 2025 clinical review gives a lifetime risk of 2.6 percent, a population prevalence of two to three percent, a female to male ratio of three to one, and a peak in the fifth and sixth decades. Which finger catches is fairly settled too, with the ring finger most common and the thumb second.

The last row is deliberately left unresolved. Appearing on a risk factor list and being called a cause are claims at different levels. You cannot conclude that hand use caused it, and activity change is still one arm of first-line treatment.

In diabetes it behaves like a different condition

Diabetes here is not background information but a variable that changes the plan. A 1997 study in the Journal of Diabetes and Its Complications compared 60 people with diabetes and 60 without. The insulin-dependent group had significantly more multiple-digit involvement, the diffuse type was 1.45 times more frequent, and symptom duration was significantly longer. Recovery on steroid injection was significantly higher in the control group, and in 13.3 percent of the diabetic patients the surgical outcome was not successful.

A 2018 meta-analysis in Orthopedics narrowed the gap into numbers. Pooling 381 diabetic and 449 non-diabetic digits, it reported failure after a single injection at 78 percent for insulin-dependent diabetes, 47 percent for non-insulin-dependent diabetes and 49 percent for people without diabetes. After three injections the figures were 57, 39 and 30 percent. The line worth noticing is that the single-injection failure rate for non-insulin-dependent diabetes was almost the same as for people without diabetes. Which kind of diabetes mattered more than whether diabetes was present.

Three things are worth knowing. The Academy material states that in patients on insulin, blood sugar can rise for about 10 to 14 days after an injection. The 2025 clinical review reports that above an HbA1c of 6.5 percent both injection failure risk and wound healing complications rise, and that infection risk after release is 65 percent higher. The same review also notes that some recent studies found no significant difference in injection efficacy between diabetic and non-diabetic patients. The direction is consistent; the magnitude has not settled.

Trigger finger after carpal tunnel release

Some people find a finger starting to catch a while after carpal tunnel surgery. This is not coincidence but a phenomenon with a measured incidence. A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis in the International Journal of Surgery identified 827 cases of trigger finger across 9,207 carpal tunnel releases in 10 studies, giving a pooled incidence of 7.7 percent, 95 percent confidence interval 5.5 to 10.8 percent.

Mean onset was 5.5 months after surgery, mostly within the first year. Women had 2.02 times the odds of men, while age and a history of diabetes showed no significant association. The thumb was involved most often at 31.44 percent, followed by the middle finger at 22.26 and the ring finger at 20.04. The authors' proposed mechanism is that dividing the flexor retinaculum produces a bowstring effect and increases tendon excursion, raising the compressive force on the A1 pulley.

Incidence is not proof of causation. The two conditions already tend to appear in the same hand, so how much would have appeared without surgery cannot be separated out from this data alone. One practical conclusion follows. A finger that starts catching within six months of carpal tunnel surgery is not a signal that the surgery failed, but a situation that may be handled as a separate diagnosis.

Not every finger that will not straighten is this

The sentence my finger will not straighten is a shared entrance for several conditions, and the place where self-diagnosis goes wrong most often. What separates them usually comes down to whether there is a snap and which tissue is at fault.

ConditionTissue at faultHow it separates from trigger finger
Trigger fingerFlexor tendon and A1 pulleyIt catches, then snaps. Tenderness and a nodule at the base of the finger on the palm
Dupuytren's diseasePalmar fascia. The Academy states explicitly that the tendons are not involvedNo snapping. A cord-like band forms and the finger bends gradually. Ring and little fingers are common, and pain is not prominent
ArthritisThe joint itselfSwelling and deformity appear at the joint line and often across several joints
Flexor tendon ruptureContinuity of the tendonThe power to bend disappears. With trigger finger the finger can still bend
Pyogenic flexor tenosynovitisBacterial infection of the sheathAn emergency. The whole finger swells in a fusiform shape with warmth and redness

Dupuytren's is the most confusing from the outside and the most different underneath. According to the Academy material, what thickens and shortens is the palmar fascia, and cords form that hold the finger bent. Tenderness in the early nodules usually goes away. The risk factors differ too. Northern European and Scandinavian ancestry, family history, diabetes, seizure disorders and alcohol are cited, and men are affected more than women. That is the opposite direction from trigger finger, which is more common in women.

Nonsurgical care - activity change and splinting

The logic of nonsurgical care is single. Stop repeating the passage so the irritation falls. Both academies list activity change and splinting, oral anti-inflammatories and steroid injection as the nonsurgical items.

The question that actually divides splinting is which joint to immobilise. The 2025 clinical review puts wear time at three to 12 weeks with an average of six, and reports that in acute cases with symptoms under three months, 55 percent had complete resolution after six weeks in a metacarpophalangeal joint splint. The same review also carries the statement that a proximal interphalangeal joint-blocking orthosis is more effective.

OptionWhat has been shownLimits

The real obstacle with splinting is not effect but keeping it on. Reviews of the literature repeatedly flag adherence as the problem. Before concluding that a splint did not work, it is the right order to check whether it was actually worn as planned.

What a steroid injection does and where it stops

Injection is the most used intervention here, and the shape of the evidence is clear. A 2009 Cochrane systematic review pooled two randomised controlled trials with 63 participants and reported a relative risk of 3.15 for treatment success within four weeks, 95 percent confidence interval 1.34 to 7.40, with a number needed to treat of three. Treat three people and one more benefits. The authors rated the quality of the evidence as low, noting it rested on two small trials of poor methodological quality. Their conclusion was still to place injection ahead of surgery as initial treatment.

The figures quoted in practice run higher. The 2025 clinical review puts symptom resolution at 70 to 90 percent in patients without diabetes. The two numbers differ not because they contradict each other but because a comparison against placebo and a response rate observed in clinic measure different things. The larger number is an expectation; the smaller one is the evidence.

The ceiling on repeat injection is mentioned consistently across sources. The Academy material states that up to three injections may be given, and the 2025 clinical review sets a minimum interval of four to six months with three as the ceiling before surgery is discussed, while also reporting that going beyond three can raise the risk of recurrence after A1 pulley release. Listed adverse effects include local pain, fat atrophy and necrosis, skin depigmentation and transient hyperglycaemia in diabetic patients. This piece does not prescribe an agent, a dose or a timing. Injection is not an option that can be deferred indefinitely.

Surgery - what opening the A1 pulley means

What surgery does follows directly from the explanation above. It opens the narrowed tunnel. The Korean Orthopaedic Association material describes releasing the pulley so the tendon can move freely, and notes it can be done under local anaesthesia as an outpatient procedure. The Academy adds that in long-standing cases part of the tendon is occasionally trimmed.

Results are reported fairly consistently. Open release in the 2025 clinical review exceeds 90 percent success with a recurrence rate of 2.39 percent. The complication rate in a cohort of 1,879 was 17.1 percent, and opening that up, 14.8 percent were resolved with conservative care alone, 2.1 percent needed further surgery and 0.2 percent developed complex regional pain syndrome. The percutaneous approach performed blind reports 87 percent long-term success alongside a 5.7 percent risk of iatrogenic digital nerve injury, rising to 91 to 100 percent with ultrasound guidance.

It is better to set expectations about recovery in advance. The Academy material states that the incision heals within a few weeks but swelling and stiffness take four to six months to settle completely, and that on average people feel fully recovered six months after surgery. The point at which the catching stops and the point at which the hand feels like it used to are not the same. This piece neither recommends nor discourages surgery. It is decided against the stage, the duration of symptoms, how many injections have already been given, and the associated conditions.

Signs that need care without delay

From here on these are not items to watch. If any one of them applies, this is when to be seen without delay.

  • The finger stays locked bent and will not straighten. If neither the hand itself nor the other hand can straighten it, that is the last stage, and treatment based on restricting movement no longer applies. The longer it is left, the more a joint contracture is added on top of the tunnel problem
  • Fever, the whole finger turning red and swollen, or pus. Pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis, a bacterial infection of the flexor sheath, is an emergency. The four Kanavel signs described in 1912 are severe tenderness along the sheath, a resting flexed posture of the finger, severe pain on extending it, and fusiform swelling of the whole finger
  • You do not need all four signs to suspect it. According to a 2015 account in Clinical Orthopaedics and Related Research, one study found all four present in only 22 of 41 patients, or 54 percent. The same piece also notes that no published study has validated the sensitivity or specificity of these signs
  • Late treatment is hard to reverse. The same piece describes diagnostic delay leading to tendon necrosis and adhesions, marked loss of motion, deformity and risk of loss of limb
  • Reduced sensation or weakness across the whole hand. This condition does not reduce sensation and strength across the hand. Symptoms like that call for a different cause
  • The power to bend disappeared after an injury. If the finger does not bend rather than catching, continuity of the flexor tendon is ruled out first
  • Several fingers began catching together over a short period. That is the pattern where an association with systemic disease is looked at first

What not to do

Self-management gets dangerous here because the pain is not dramatic. It is bearable, so it gets borne, and the options narrow while it is being borne.

  • Do not force it straight. The catch comes from a narrowed tunnel, and forcing the tendon through does not widen it. There is a more important reason. In pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis, severe pain on extending the finger is one of the diagnostic signs. Dismissing that pain as the expected result of forcing the finger erases your own chance to separate out an infection
  • Do not try to massage it open. What narrowed is the pulley, and it is not tissue that widens under pressure. No primary evidence of benefit was found
  • Do not push through the pain and keep the same motion. Activity change is one arm of first-line treatment, and this is exactly the opposite. The Academy material states that injections are less effective when symptoms are long-standing. Time spent enduring may be time in which the response rate erodes
  • Do not repeat injections indefinitely. Going beyond three is reported to raise the risk of recurrence after release. Counting is something the patient can do too
  • Do not stop at self-diagnosis. Dupuytren's, tendon rupture and infection all arrive as the same it will not straighten. The three are handled differently and one of them is an emergency

Pediatric trigger digit is a different condition

It carries the same name, but children are not scaled-down adults here. First, the thumb dominates. The 2025 clinical review notes that pediatric involvement of a digit other than the thumb is ten times less prevalent than pediatric trigger thumb. The natural history data are also shaped differently from the adult picture.

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis in the Journal of Orthopaedic Surgery and Research pooled 599 cases in children up to 14 and reported spontaneous resolution at 43.5 percent. Follow-up of 24 months or more gave 58.9 percent against 26.8 percent below that, while age at presentation and initial severity did not separate the groups. A 2008 prospective study from Korea in the Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery followed 71 thumbs in 53 children for at least two years and reported resolution without treatment in more than 60 percent, with a median of 48 months. Even where resolution was incomplete, the flexion deformity showed an improving pattern.

Two things follow. One is that observation can be discussed as a first option, though the median of four years has to be read alongside it. Waiting means years, not weeks. The other is that pediatric cases where a digit other than the thumb catches are handled separately. The same review reports that 29 percent of those come with an associated condition, including diabetes, juvenile rheumatoid arthritis, chromosomal abnormalities and connective tissue disorders. Surgical resolution runs 80 to 90 percent, and it is also noted that delaying surgery past age three leaves a risk of residual flexion contracture. Whether to wait or intervene belongs to pediatric hand care, not to a parent.

Four common misconceptions

Four of the most repeated claims, set beside the evidence.

  • Plenty of finger exercise will fix it. A 2024 randomised trial in Prosthetics and Orthotics International split 55 participants into a joint-blocking orthosis arm and a hand exercise arm over six weeks. The orthosis arm succeeded in 53.6 percent and the exercise-only arm produced no successful outcomes. A 2025 trial in Scientific Reports reported that adding finger gliding exercise after a steroid injection made no difference to pain, functional grading or recurrence at 24 weeks compared with usual care. Not that exercise is harmful, but that there is no evidence a tunnel widens through exercise alone
  • A joint supplement will sort it out. The tissue at fault is simply different. No primary evidence was found that supplements aimed at joints changed the course of this condition
  • It is inflammation, so enough anti-inflammatories will do. The tissue findings lean towards fibrocartilaginous change rather than inflammatory cell proliferation. Anti-inflammatory analgesics address pain; they do not reverse the narrowing
  • It goes away on its own anyway. The side with real spontaneous resolution data is pediatric trigger thumb. No data of comparable size was confirmed for adults. Collapsing adults and children into one condition is the root of this one

Summary

Trigger finger is not a joint disease. A size mismatch develops between the flexor tendon and the A1 pulley at the base of the finger on the palm, and the passage stops being smooth, so it catches and snaps. That one structure explains most of this article. Why joint supplements aim at the wrong tissue, why a splint blocks a particular joint, and why force does not widen a tunnel all come from the same sentence.

What remains practical is stage and time. The pain-only band, the catching band, the locking band and the fixed band each leave different options open. And the statement that injections work less well when symptoms are long-standing points the same way as the report that more than three injections raise the risk of recurrence after release. Time spent enduring carries a price. If diabetes is present, this condition is a variable in the plan, and a child's thumb sits on a different body of evidence from an adult's finger.

To repeat: this is general information, and diagnosis and treatment follow a clinician's judgement. The staging and figures here are a map for preparing a consultation, not a basis for reaching your own conclusion.

What I Could Not Verify

  • The mechanism behind the morning pattern. The explanation of overnight swelling and a flexed sleeping posture is widespread but could not be confirmed in verified form in a primary source. What is confirmed is the observation that stiffness and locking worsen after inactivity
  • Whether repetitive gripping is a cause or an association. The Academy lists it as a risk factor; the Korean Orthopaedic Association writes that the cause is not clear. With nothing found to resolve the gap, both are kept side by side
  • The magnitude of the hypothyroidism association. It appears on the list of associated conditions, but no data giving a magnitude the way diabetes does was confirmed
  • How much harm forced extension causes. No study quantifying it was found. What this piece rests on is not injury statistics but the three structural reasons above
  • Spontaneous resolution in adults. One clinical review reports up to 90 percent within a year of first consultation, but the original source could not be checked, and the order of magnitude differs so far from the pediatric data that it was left out
  • Differences between steroid preparations, and shockwave therapy. Reviews exist, but the full texts would not open, so no figures were used
  • Patient counts and age distribution in Korea. These are queried through the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service open system, so no value could be confirmed from a static page
  • The Korean Society for Surgery of the Hand's public-facing material. That page would not open, so the Korean Orthopaedic Association's account is the only domestic society source cited
  • What this piece does not write. Drug names and doses, injection timing and surgical indications belong to the clinic and are not covered
