Introduction: 12 to 52, and what happened in between

In about six months, OpenAI's official "things you can ask Codex to do" catalog grew from twelve entries to fifty-two. The headline number reads like marketing copy, but the move underneath it is not a simple list expansion. OpenAI changed the definition of Codex itself. The product slid from "code completion assistant" to "engineering work agent you can hand entire tasks to," and the 52-entry catalog is the visible surface of that shift, presented in a format users can act on immediately.

By May 2026 the AI coding tool market has settled into three credible centers: OpenAI's Codex, Anthropic's Claude Code, and Cursor's Composer. The three are not doing the same job. Each one is strong somewhere and weak somewhere else, and Codex's 52-entry catalog is OpenAI's explicit declaration of where it intends to be strongest. Reading the list as a map of OpenAI's strategic territory is more useful than reading it as a checklist.

This post is not a translation of the 52 entries. It is a working analysis. After six months of mixed use across Codex, Claude Code, and Cursor in a real production setting, here is what stands out: a six-bucket categorization of the 52, the seven new entries I think matter most, an honest comparison with Claude Code and Cursor, three measured ROI cases from my own work, eight patterns that map cleanly to Korean SI environments, the half-of-52 that exists for marketing reasons, and most importantly the procedure for building your own use case library that beats OpenAI's official list for your specific team.

1. The six-month arc - from Codex Goals to 52 use cases

The short story: in late 2025 OpenAI repositioned Codex from "coding assistant" to "agent you hand work to in Goal-sized units." A Codex Goal is something like "migrate this codebase to TypeScript" or "extract the payments domain into a microservice." Underneath each Goal, OpenAI started attaching a growing menu of concrete execution scenarios.

The 52-entry expansion is the next move in that arc. If Goals are the planning unit, the use cases are the copy-pastable execution unit. Where the 12-entry catalog stopped at "generate code, add tests, refactor," the 52-entry catalog reaches "Rails 5 to 7 migration," "automatic i18n key extraction," and "GraphQL schema evolution" - work that maps directly to engineering backlogs rather than to abstract capabilities.

The intent is clear. OpenAI wants to shorten the gap between "I have Codex installed" and "I am running Codex against a real task right now." The 52-entry catalog is an explicit invitation to hand specific work over, and it is also a territory marker against Anthropic and Cursor: this is the surface area OpenAI claims to cover. The number itself will keep growing.

2. A six-bucket reading of the 52

OpenAI presents the 52 alphabetically and by surface theme, but for operational thinking it is much more useful to compress them into six buckets.

2-1. Migrations (about 12 entries)

Large-scale batch conversion work: API v1 to v2, JavaScript to TypeScript, Go to Rust, Python 2 to 3, Rails 5 to 7, Vue 2 to 3. This is the largest bucket of the 52 and also the one with the most defensible ROI. Tasks that take humans days collapse to hours, and the consistency across files is higher than humans deliver on average.

2-2. Refactoring (about 10 entries)

Internal cleanup: function extraction, module separation, deduplication, applying a specific design pattern. Similar to migrations but inward-facing. In my own measurement Codex performs best on modules in the 1,000-3,000 line range. Beyond that the agent needs more human steering than the marketing implies.

2-3. Tests (about 8 entries)

Unit test generation, integration test scaffolding, end-to-end scenarios, repairing broken tests, filling coverage gaps. The category where humans procrastinate the most, which is exactly why automation pays off the fastest.

2-4. Documentation (about 7 entries)

README drafts, automatic API documentation, Architecture Decision Records, changelog cleanup, inline comment generation. The communication cost with non-technical stakeholders drops noticeably as soon as this bucket is running.

2-5. Operations automation (about 8 entries)

CI/CD pipelines, Dockerfile and docker-compose generation, Terraform and Kubernetes manifests, monitoring alert configuration, deployment script automation. SREs and platform engineers see the biggest immediate effect here.

2-6. Learning and onboarding (about 7 entries)

New codebase walkthroughs, dependency graph visualization, core module summaries, new-hire onboarding guides, internal learning material. A new engineer joining a complex codebase can compress the first week of comprehension by roughly 3x with this bucket alone.

3. Seven of the new 40 that actually matter

Out of the 40 entries added in this expansion, these are the seven I think are most worth attention.

3-1. Legacy Rails 5 to Rails 7 migration

The fact that a specific framework major-version migration shows up as an official use case is itself a signal. Major-version upgrades of frameworks like Rails are the canonical time-sink in mature web shops. Putting it on the list means OpenAI is confident Codex can handle real production code, not just toy examples. The same logic applies to plenty of Korean services still stuck on Rails 5.

3-2. Microservice extraction

Pulling a single domain out of a monolith into a service. This is not just code movement - it involves dependency analysis, contract definition, and data separation. If a 1-2 month human job compresses to days, that alone is a structural change in how companies plan modernization.

3-3. Security patch automation

Workflow that takes vulnerability output from npm audit, snyk, or OWASP scanners and produces fixes. Quarterly security review is mandatory in most enterprises but rarely handled cleanly by humans at scale. The automation effect is immediate and visible to leadership.

3-4. Performance regression hunt

Given the last N pull requests and APM/profiler output, narrow down which change caused a regression and propose a fix. In my own measurement Codex cut a half-day human investigation to roughly thirty minutes. That is not full replacement, but it is a meaningful first-pass triage.

3-5. Automatic i18n key extraction

Code that was written in a single language gets scanned, i18n keys are added, and message catalogs in English, Japanese, and Chinese are generated as drafts. Running BrainDetox in four languages, I can confirm this is one of the most procrastinated categories of work. Automation here pays off twice: once at extraction time and again every time you add a new language.

3-6. SQL index recommendations

Codex reads EXPLAIN ANALYZE output and query patterns and proposes indexes to add, indexes to drop, and composite index candidates. A meaningful chunk of DBA work moves into the hand-off zone.

3-7. GraphQL schema evolution

Existing schema is analyzed for deprecation candidates, new fields are proposed, and client compatibility is checked automatically. Companies running large GraphQL surfaces should treat this entry as a direct win.

4. Codex use cases vs Claude Code tasks vs Cursor Composer

More than half of the 52 entries can be executed at comparable quality by Claude Code or Cursor. The difference is in each tool's center of gravity.

  • OpenAI Codex: cleanest Goals + use-case catalog, strongest at large-scale batch conversion, CI/CD, and ops automation. The Codex CLI's background execution and GitHub Actions integration are the smoothest of the three.
  • Claude Code: long context window and deep codebase exploration, strongest at net-new feature implementation, architectural discussion, and complex refactoring. "Tasks that require holding the whole codebase in your head" go here.
  • Cursor Composer: in-editor real-time edits, strongest at multi-file simultaneous changes and the daily code-editing loop. The "I need this fixed in five minutes" workflow is fastest here.

After six months of mixed use I am convinced that using only one of these tools is the most inefficient option. The fastest workflow rotates the tool by task type: Claude for new features and architecture, Codex for big migrations and ops automation, Cursor for day-to-day editing. Of the 52 official entries, only about fifteen to twenty actually need Codex specifically. The rest are achievable in any of the three tools or are unrelated to your particular work.

5. Three measured ROI cases from real work

Three concrete measurements from running BrainDetox and its sibling tools.

5-1. Automatic i18n key extraction - 8 hours to 40 minutes

Some BrainDetox tools had Korean text hardcoded into the source. Extracting i18n keys and producing English, Japanese, and Chinese catalogs by hand was an eight-hour job. The Codex "i18n automatic addition" use case finished it in forty minutes. The remaining human work was catalog review and fixing a few awkward translations. That is a 12x reduction on a job humans actively avoid.

5-2. Legacy Bash scripts to Python - 2 days to 3 hours

Roughly thirty operational Bash scripts accumulated over almost a decade had to be moved to Python. A two-day human estimate became a three-hour Codex run plus my own verification and adjustment. The bigger gain was consistency: human-written conversions would have ended up in thirty different styles. Codex produced thirty scripts in one shared pattern.

5-3. README and API documentation - 4 hours to 30 minutes

Several smaller personal repositories had no real README. A four-hour writing job became a thirty-minute Codex draft plus fact-checking and tone editing. The output was more internally consistent than what I would have produced by hand under deadline pressure.

The shared lesson across all three: the highest-ROI work is the work humans were already postponing. Net-new feature implementation and complex architectural decisions did not show comparable gains. Treat that pattern as your selection rule.

6. Eight production-ready patterns for Korean SI environments

For readers in Korean SI, SM, or in-house teams - or any environment with a similar legacy stack - these eight map directly to the debts that are already on your plate.

  1. Legacy Spring 3.x to Spring Boot 3.x migration: the single largest debt sitting in most Korean enterprise codebases.
  2. JSP to Thymeleaf or React: UI modernization with immediate visible payoff.
  3. jQuery to vanilla JS or React: long-overdue frontend cleanup.
  4. Oracle PL/SQL to PostgreSQL functions: directly relevant to license-cost reduction programs.
  5. JUnit 4 to JUnit 5 plus test backfill: the most-postponed category, where automation lands hardest.
  6. Log format standardization plus monitoring alerting: immediate operational stability win.
  7. Korean-only i18n code to multilingual key-based code: prerequisite for any global expansion.
  8. Automatic onboarding guide generation: drops the cost of bringing in juniors or external vendors.

These eight align with the specific debt structure of Korean enterprise IT. The exercise that pays off most is to run a one-page diagnostic of your team's accumulated debt and pick whichever item ranks highest as the first Codex pilot.

7. AdSense and SEO measurement - the patterns that work for content sites

This section is for the smaller but very real audience that runs multilingual content sites. Out of the 52 entries, the following five are immediately useful for site operators.

  • Multilingual content generation and translation reinforcement: for sites like BrainDetox that operate in four languages, the per-page production cost drops sharply.
  • Automatic structured data (JSON-LD) generation: BlogPosting, FAQPage, HowTo schemas added without manual code changes.
  • Internal link graph analysis and recommendations: orphaned and under-linked pages get surfaced.
  • Sitemap, RSS, and robots.txt consistency checks: at scale these can no longer be managed by hand.
  • Automatic generation of analytics-reading code for Google Analytics and Search Console: a chunk of the "what is this number telling me" workflow can be templated.

Running BrainDetox, I can confirm all five deliver visible effects within a single quarter. Multilingual content and JSON-LD automation in particular have moved the site's SEO score up by 5-10 points each quarter for the last three quarters.

8. The trap: half of the 52 exist for marketing reasons

Time for an uncomfortable observation. The number 52 is partly a marketing artifact. In my actual usage, fewer than 20 of the 52 entries map to ongoing real work. The other 30+ entries exist as "look, this is also possible" demos. Specific traps to avoid:

  1. Do not try to use all 52. Pick the 15-20 that match your workflow and go deep on those.
  2. "Possible" is not "production-ready." A demo that ran cleanly in OpenAI's video does not guarantee identical behavior on your codebase. Always run PoC, then a narrow scope, then broader rollout.
  3. Do not let the 52-entry catalog frame your thinking. The list is OpenAI's recommended usage, not your team's actual needs. Build inward from your own backlog.
  4. Measure token cost and wall-clock cost. Some use cases turn out to be more expensive than the human alternative, especially for short tasks where the round-trip overhead dominates.
  5. License and IP traps. Generating or converting external code can create license and copyright issues. Korean SI environments in particular need legal review before broad adoption.

9. The catalog you actually need - your own use case library

This is the core message of the post. Do not adopt OpenAI's 52 entries wholesale. Build your own catalog tuned to how your team works. The recommended procedure:

  1. Write down 20 tasks you repeat in a typical week. Coding, refactoring, documentation, meeting prep, status reports - all of it.
  2. Score each item for AI delegability. Fully delegatable (above 80%), partially delegatable (30-70%), not delegatable (below 30%).
  3. For the fully delegatable items, write Codex prompt templates. Each template should specify the input format, expected output shape, and verification step. Save them in Notion or Obsidian.
  4. Run a monthly retrospective. Strengthen prompts that worked; remove or rewrite the ones that did not.
  5. Promote the catalog to the team. Once stable, move it to the team wiki so the next hire inherits it instead of starting from zero.

Six months of this rhythm produces a personal library of 20-30 validated, team-specific use cases. That library is a much stronger asset than OpenAI's official 52, precisely because it is fitted to your actual work rather than to a public demo.

10. What Codex looks like in late 2026

My working prediction for the second half of 2026 has three branches.

First, the use case catalog grows again, probably to 80-100 entries. The new entries will skew toward domain-specific work: finance, healthcare, education, security and compliance automation, data engineering pipelines. The horizontal coding surface is mostly covered already; the next expansion is vertical.

Second, multi-agent workflows become a first-class concept. One Codex agent writes code, another reviews it, a third writes the tests. Variations of this pattern already exist in the wild; the move is to put them in the official catalog with templates and guardrails.

Third, expansion beyond code. Marketing content automation, operational decision support, sales material generation. Once non-code work shows up under the Codex label, the product stops being a "coding assistant" and becomes a general-purpose work agent. At that point the 52 entries look quaint.

11. Conclusion plus three things to do this week

The number 52 is not just a marketing flourish. It is the visible surface of OpenAI redefining Codex from "coding assistant" to "engineering work agent." But do not let the number push you around. The pieces you actually need are 15-20, and the work of selecting and templating those into your own catalog is far more valuable than the act of reading the official list.

Three concrete moves you can make this week:

Move 1 - write the list of 20 weekly tasks. By the end of the weekend, put twenty repeating tasks on a single sheet of paper and tag each one as fully, partially, or not delegatable. That sheet is the seed of your own use case library.

Move 2 - pick the most-postponed task and hand it to Codex. Whatever you have been avoiding the longest - README, test backfill, internal documentation - go first. The highest ROI consistently lives in the work humans were already procrastinating.

Move 3 - run the same task through all three tools for a week. Push identical work into Codex, Claude Code, and Cursor and keep a one-paragraph note per run. By the end of the week you have your own evidence-based rule for which tool wins which task type.

The 52-entry catalog is a starting line, not the race itself. Where you stand six months from now depends on how disciplined you are about translating that public list into your own private one. A team that ships a private library of twenty validated use cases by the end of the year will look, from the outside, like it has somehow doubled its engineering capacity. The mechanism is simply that everyone is repeatedly executing the work that used to be improvised. The improvement is real and durable, but it does not show up automatically from a vendor announcement. It shows up because someone in the team decided to build the internal catalog and then defended that discipline against the daily pressure to skip it.

One more practical note. As your private catalog grows, expose it on the team wiki with three columns per entry: the prompt template, the verification step, and the most recent calibration date. The third column is what keeps the library alive. Templates rot when models change, and a six-month-old prompt may behave differently on a newer Codex revision. A one-line "last verified on YYYY-MM-DD" next to every template turns the catalog from a static document into a living asset, and it makes the maintenance cost legible at a glance. Companies that institutionalize this kind of curation are the ones that will actually capture the 52-entry value, while companies that just bookmarked OpenAI's page will discover, in retrospect, that they did not change anything about how their team works.
