Trigger — Figures Revealed by Copying and Pasting a Redacted Box

On September 30, 2026, the Lincoln, Nebraska station 10/11 NOW (KOLN) published an article titled “UPDATE: Improper redaction reveals Lincoln’s Google Data Center water and electricity usage.” The sub-headline reads “Nebraska data centers submit 2026 annual report.” The top of the page shows the article as first published on September 30 at 10:10 AM Central Daylight Time (CDT) and updated the same day at 10:00 PM CDT.

According to the report, Nebraska data centers are required to turn in an annual report to the Nebraska Department of Water, Energy, and Environment (DWEE), but some state statutes prevented the public from seeing how much electricity and water the data centers use. The Google data center in Lincoln, named Agate LLC, claimed its electricity usage and water usage were trade secret information, citing Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 81-1527 and 84-712.05 and NAC Title 115, Chapter 2. The article adds that Google did this for all three of its data centers, including the sites in Omaha and Papillion.

The article then says that highlighting the redacted text box in the report to DWEE with a computer cursor, and copying and pasting it into a separate document, reveals the hidden values. The figures it presents from this method are the 52.65 megawatts Agate LLC reported during peak electrical demand and 13.299 megagallons of water used across cooling towers, evaporative systems and site operations in the last year. The article writes that the same kind of improper redaction also exposed the water use of another site and the tax refunds the data centers expect.

10/11 NOW says it filed a public records request with DWEE on September 30 to see the redacted information in Agate LLC’s report. If the request is granted, the full redacted cells could be seen through an official channel, but the report gives no result. The values that can be read now are the ones the report quotes and those that follow from its numbers by arithmetic.

  • Reporting outlet: 10/11 NOW (KOLN). The byline is Madison Pitsch, and the copyright line at the foot of the page names KOLN.
  • Publication and update: First published September 30, 2026 at 10:10 AM CDT and updated the same day at 10:00 PM CDT.
  • The outlet’s own wording: The headline says “improper redaction” revealed the water and electricity usage of Lincoln’s Google data center.
  • Notation: Figures from the report appear as they are, and every division, sum and unit conversion is labeled a calculation to keep it apart from the original values.

Regulatory Context — The July 20 Executive Order and the September 30 Deadline

The report explains that the filings stem from Governor Jim Pillen’s July 20 Executive Order. In the report’s words, the order requires data centers in the state to self-report their impact on Nebraska’s resources, primarily water, power grids and local infrastructure. The words “Executive Order” in the article link to a document on govdocs.nebraska.gov whose file name is 26-17.pdf. The reporting deadline of September 30 falls 72 days after the order of July 20 (calculated).

  • What the order requires: According to the report, data centers must self-report their impact on water, power grids and local infrastructure.
  • Oversight of the order: The article says the DWEE Data Center Task Force is overseeing the execution of the executive order.
  • Reporting deadline: Usage reports are due by September 30, and the sub-headline under the title concerns the submission of 2026 annual reports.
  • How to find the filings: The submitted reports can be read at a Nebraska public access site linked in the article, where readers are told to enter DCR in the DEQ Program field.

On the trade secret claim, the report says exactly what the first section above describes. The Lincoln Google data center claimed its electricity and water usage were trade secrets and cited three legal sources, and the article says Google did the same for all three of its sites. The report does not discuss whether that claim is legally sound; it says only that some state statutes were preventing the public from seeing the usage. What action followed once the redacted figures had effectively become visible is not stated in the report.

Reading the table out gives the following. The ratios and conversions in the list below are not sentences from the report; they are calculated from the report’s numbers.

  • Size of Agate LLC: The report compares the 288,530 square feet to about five football fields. Dividing by five gives about 57,700 square feet per field (calculated), so the comparison and the number do not conflict.
  • Agate LLC’s water: 13.299 megagallons is 13,299,000 gallons. The report describes this as about 13 million gallons, enough to fill around 20 Olympic-size pools, and less than half of what the City of Lincoln reports using on September 29. The article’s wording does not make clear whether that comparison is between daily amounts.
  • Fireball Group LLC’s water: 547.88 megagallons is 547,880,000 gallons. Out of the 765 megagallons total, Fireball’s share is 547.88 divided by 765, about 71.6% (calculated).
  • Comparing the two sites: 547.88 divided by 13.299 is about 41.2 times (calculated). The report does not say whether the two sites run at the same scale, use the same cooling method or have the same reporting scope, so the multiple is best read as an arithmetic result only.

If both Agate and Fireball are included in the 765-megagallon total, which is an assumption the report does not state in so many words, the other four sites add up to 765 − 547.88 − 13.299 = 203.821 megagallons (calculated), an average of about 51 megagallons each (calculated). That average is the total divided evenly and not the real value for any of the four, and the report does not name them. The report also does not spell out whether every figure inside the total uses the same year as its basis. The average across all six sites is 765 divided by 6, or 127.5 megagallons (calculated); Fireball’s 547.88 megagallons is about 4.3 times that average and Agate’s 13.299 megagallons about 10.4% of it (calculated). These comparisons are arithmetic resting on the assumption that both sites are included in the total.

What the Report Says About the Redaction and What It Does Not

What the report says about how the hidden values became visible amounts to roughly one sentence. Highlighting the redacted text box in the report submitted to DWEE with a computer cursor and pasting it into a separate document revealed the hidden values. The report does not analyze document internals or causes; it refers to the matter with the phrase “improper redaction.”

  • What is confirmed: The report says the mere act of highlighting with a cursor and copy-pasting made the hidden values appear.
  • First thing not stated: The report does not say whether the document was a PDF or which program was used to cover the values.
  • Second thing not stated: No explanation or statement from Google or DWEE about how this came about appears in the report.
  • What followed: The public records request that 10/11 NOW filed on September 30 is as far as the report goes on follow-up, and its outcome is not in the report.

The report puts its weight on the values rather than on the method. It is not possible to know which table or page of the filing the values sat on, or whether all of the covered cells were handled in the same way. The items for which the report quotes values are limited to Agate LLC’s power, water and floor area, Fireball’s water, the three sites’ expected refunds and the six-site water total. What was written in any other cell cannot be learned from the report.

What the Exposed Figures Cover and the Gaps They Leave

The revealed values show only a few cross-sections of how a data center operates. Separating what the report says from what it does not gives the following.

  • Peak demand is not the average: Agate’s 52.65 MW is the value during peak electrical demand. Neither annual average use nor total energy appears in the report.
  • Water is a single total line: 13.299 megagallons combines cooling towers, evaporative systems and site operations, and the report does not itemize the three.
  • No power figure for Papillion: For Fireball the report gives only the 2025 annual water use, with no power figure, floor area or breakdown of water use.
  • The basis for comparison stays open: Explanations such as the comparison with the City of Lincoln’s usage and the pool conversions help readers gauge scale, but the report contains no values that would support comparing the efficiency of one site with another.

Because of these limits, these values cannot show which of the two sites is more efficient or whether either figure is high or low compared with other regions. A comparison would need further values on the same basis, such as energy totals, operating capacity and cooling method, none of which are in the report. What the report attaches next to the values are the Olympic pool conversion and the comparison with Lincoln’s usage, and it is safer to read them as aids to gauging scale. For the same reason, the 71.6% calculated above as one site’s share of the 765-megagallon total should be taken as an arithmetic result and not as an interpretation. For reference, 52.65 MW divided by 288,530 square feet is about 182 W per square foot (calculated), but it is a peak-demand value and not an everyday power density.

Reading the Numbers — Unit Conversions and Calculations

The report mixes units such as megagallons, megawatts and Olympic pools. Below are the conversions that can be made from the report’s numbers alone, spelled out line by line. All are calculated values, with the gallon taken as the US gallon (about 3.785 liters) given the report’s American context. The report does not say which gallon it means.

  • Megagallons: One megagallon is one million gallons. So 13.299 megagallons is 13,299,000 gallons and 547.88 megagallons is 547,880,000 gallons.
  • Consistency of the pool conversion: From the report’s figure of 1,159.93 pools for 765 megagallons, one pool is 765 divided by 1,159.93, about 0.66 megagallons (calculated). Dividing 13.299 by that value gives about 20.2 pools (calculated), which agrees with the report’s “around 20.”
  • Averaged per day: Dividing 13.299 megagallons by 365 days gives about 36,400 gallons per day (calculated), and 547.88 megagallons gives about 1.5 million gallons per day (calculated). These are simple averages of annual values and not the real daily distribution.
  • In liters: On the US gallon basis, 13.299 megagallons is about 50.3 million liters, 547.88 megagallons about 2.07 billion liters and 765 megagallons about 2.90 billion liters (all calculated).

The power value of 52.65 MW is a unit of instantaneous demand, so it cannot be compared with or added to a quantity of water the way gallons can. Converting it to annual energy would require hourly load, and the report has no such information. For that reason a conversion such as multiplying 52.65 MW by 8,760 hours does not hold. Such a figure would rest on the assumption that peak demand continues all year, which departs from what the report conveys.

What Could Not Be Verified

  • The filings themselves: Only the full text of the article was checked; the DWEE report files, including those on the public access site, were not opened, so the numbers could not be checked against them.
  • Text of the executive order: The server for the executive order PDF (file name 26-17.pdf) linked in the article could not be reached, so the order could not be opened and none of its specific provisions was checked.
  • The other four data centers: Of the six sites making up the 765-megagallon total, the names and individual values of those other than Agate and Fireball are not in the report. Westwood’s water and power figures are also absent.
  • Document format and handling: The report does not give the file format of the redacted document, the tool used for the redaction, or any explanation from Google or DWEE.
  • Result of the records request: How DWEE answered 10/11 NOW’s September 30 request, and whether any refund has actually been made, could not be confirmed.
