The Money Has Not Moved Yet When You Tap Your Card - Where Authorization and Settlement Split Apart

In a single payment, the route the message travels and the route the money travels are not the same. Here is what that gap produces

2026-09-11 Finance & Economy

You tap a card against a terminal and a receipt prints a few seconds later. It is easy to assume the money crossed into the merchant account during those seconds, but it did not. What happened in that moment was a question and an answer, and the funds have not been moved anywhere yet. The actual movement of money comes later, and the interval sitting between the two produces most of the confusion that surrounds card payments.

A cancelled purchase that does not restore the available limit. A charge that is missing from the statement while the limit is already lower. A fuel pump that displays one number and bills another. A merchant deposit that arrives days after the sale. These look like unrelated phenomena, but they share one root. Authorization and settlement occur at different times, and the path taken by the message is not the path taken by the money.

This is general information and does not recommend any particular card issuer or product. It is not financial advice. The description of Korean rules reflects the position as of September 2026, and the timing of any individual transaction or the outcome of any dispute should be confirmed with the card issuer and the relevant authority.

The trigger - what the source article actually explains

On 1 June 2026 a post titled What do Visa and Mastercard do? An intro to card networks appeared on the personal technology blog tautology.town, and it reached Korean readers through GeekNews topic 33453. It starts from the observation that a card network is not a bank, and then works out what kind of business it actually is. It is written as an introduction rather than a survey.

The author organises the answer around four responsibilities.

  • Running a telecommunications network - routing transaction messages between the party sending them and the party receiving them. The network operates data centres and leases fibre optic cable so that issuing and acquiring institutions are connected electronically.
  • Coordinating the banking network - moving money and completing settlement. Here the network sits in the position of computing the differences owed between participating institutions.
  • Setting incentives - designing the fee structure so that participants use the network more. Which way the split leans changes how the participants behave.
  • Making and enforcing rules - writing network rules, enforcing them, and providing a procedure for resolving claims between participants.

The author is also explicit about the boundaries of the piece. Restated directly, they are these.

  • The explanation is built on a US transaction - when the fee example arrives, it is framed as an average American credit card transaction from the outset.
  • It focuses on one brand - the author says the remainder of the discussion will concentrate on the network they are most familiar with, and notes that the other major international brand is more or less the same with different names for things.
  • It names a side effect of the structure - the same machinery that creates trust in card payments is described as having opened the door to what is commonly called friendly fraud.

What this article takes from the source is a single structural point: that authorization and settlement are different moments. Two further axes that the source does not treat are added on top, namely the case where one company issues and acquires at the same time, and the way a domestic statute reaches into the rate itself. The line between what is borrowed and what is added is drawn explicitly in what the source article does not cover.

Authorization - what really happens at the moment of payment

An authorization is not a transfer of funds. It is the procedure that asks whether this card may be used for this amount right now and returns an answer. The message travels in this order: from the merchant terminal to the acquiring institution, from the acquirer to the network, and from the network to the issuing institution. The response comes back along exactly the reverse path.

Several layers of judgement are compressed into that short window on the issuing side. Whether the card is valid. Whether enough of the limit remains. Whether the pattern departs from how the account is normally used. Whether the merchant category and the country trip a risk rule. If it passes, the issuer returns an approval code and at the same time places a hold on that amount against the limit. A hold does not mean the money has been taken; it means no other transaction may occupy that space until the item resolves.

What passes through each participant at this instant differs. The point of the table below is that the set of participants the message crosses is not the same as the set the money crosses.

The row worth staring at is the last one. At the authorization stage the network handles messages only. No money leaves any account at that point.

Settlement - the money moves afterwards

After the sale, the merchant submits the final amount. The source article calls this clearing, describing it as the merchant later submitting the final transaction amount, which is what starts the money moving. In everyday practice this is the step where a transaction is captured. The authorized amount and the captured amount may be identical or they may not. In sectors where a tip is added, or where an estimate is authorized first and actual consumption is billed afterwards, they usually differ.

Settlement follows. The network gathers the day's transactions, offsets what each institution owes against what it is owed, and moves only the remaining difference. This is the point at which tens of millions of individual transactions compress into a handful of interbank transfers.

On timing, what the source establishes is this. Most US dollar payments settle on the same day, while currencies other than the dollar typically remain outstanding for one to two business days. That outstanding window is an exposure for the network. Citing the brand's annual report for its 2024 fiscal year, the author gives a maximum daily settlement exposure of 137.4 billion dollars and an average of 84.3 billion dollars, against 11.2 billion dollars held as settlement liquidity. Because settlement is not instantaneous, an exposure of that size sits on the books.

What the gap produces

Several everyday phenomena follow from the single fact that authorization and settlement are pulled apart. None of them has to be learned separately.

  • An approved item that has not been captured - the payment succeeded, but the merchant has not yet submitted the final amount. Transactions in this window usually appear with a distinct marker in an issuer application.
  • Only the available limit has fallen - the hold lands immediately while billing comes later, so there is a period in which nothing appears on the statement yet the limit is short.
  • Pre authorization versus the actual charge - fuel, lodging and car rental settle the final figure after the fact, so an estimated amount is authorized first and the real amount is fixed at capture. The two numbers differing is the normal case.
  • The wait before a merchant is paid - the funds only move after capture and settlement, so the structure is not one of instant deposit. The actual payout date depends on the acquiring contract and the sector.
  • The same day falling into different billing months - an item enters a billing cycle based on the date it was submitted, so an end of month payment can roll into the following month.

A void and a refund are different procedures

Treating these two as the same word produces a great deal of misunderstanding. The procedures are entirely different and so are the timings that follow from them.

  • Voiding an authorization - the approval itself is reversed before the item has been submitted for clearing. The original transaction never enters settlement, and the matter ends once the held limit is released. Frequently it leaves no trace on the statement at all.
  • A refund - a transaction that has already cleared and settled is reversed. The original is not erased; a new transaction in the opposite direction is created instead. That is why the original and the refund sit side by side on a statement.
  • Why the timings diverge - the first case only requires the hold to be lifted, while the second requires the new transaction to travel the capture and settlement cycle again before it reaches the cardholder side.
  • When the hold actually lifts - releasing a hold is an act of the issuing institution and does not always coincide with the moment a reversal message arrives. This is the stretch that feels slow.

In short, before or after capture is the fork. Handled within the day of the sale it tends to take the first route; handled several days later it takes the second.

Four parties and a network - what each of them sells

This is commonly called the four party model. The cardholder, the merchant, the issuing institution and the acquiring institution are the four parties, and the network sits on top of them. Rather than listing the participants, it is faster to grasp the structure by asking what each of them sells and what each receives in return.

  • The issuing institution - sells a means of payment and credit to the cardholder. It is paid through annual fees and interest, and through the interchange that arrives from the acquiring side on each transaction.
  • The acquiring institution - sells the merchant the state of being able to accept cards. It is paid by the merchant fee, a large part of which is not its own share but money passing through.
  • The card network - sells both sides the condition that a transaction can complete even though the two ends do not know each other. Routing, rules and netting are the substance of that.
  • The merchant - pays a fee and in exchange reduces refused sales and the burden of handling cash. In many sectors accepting cards is itself a precondition of trading.
  • The cardholder - accepts credit terms in exchange for deferring immediate payment. Rewards and benefits are largely funded out of the interchange split.

One sentence in the source article summarises this arrangement. A split weighted toward the issuing side is what underwrites customer acquisition and card reward programmes. That is where the answer lies to the question of why card benefits come from the issuer rather than from anyone else in the chain.

What the network does not do

What the network does not do matters as much as what it does. Half of the common misconceptions live here.

  • It does not carry the credit risk - in most markets the risk that a cardholder fails to pay sits with the issuing institution. The network is not a party to that credit exposure.
  • It does not deposit money into merchant accounts - putting funds into a merchant account is the acquiring side's work. The network computes the difference between institutions and instructs its movement.
  • It does not keep interchange - the network publishes the rate schedule, but the money is not network revenue. It is a transfer from the acquiring side to the issuing side. What the network receives is a separate assessment fee.
  • It does not adjudicate every dispute - the source article states that most disputes are resolved between the issuer and the acquirer. The network only steps in at the final stage.

Put those four together and the character of the network becomes visible. It is not a place that lends money or holds it. It is a place that sells rules, arithmetic and wiring.

How a single company doing everything differs

If the four party model has issuing and acquiring in separate companies, there is also a closed loop structure in which one company performs both roles. This distinction does not appear in the source article. It is added here because a contrast is needed to see why the fee splits the way it does.

AxisFour party structureOne company performing both roles
Issuing and acquiringSeparate institutionsThe same company does both

Merchant acceptanceDivided among many acquiring institutionsThe operator carries it alone

The two structures are not better or worse than one another. They differ in where cost and control are placed. The company that does both avoids negotiating a split, but carries merchant acquisition and credit risk entirely on its own balance sheet.

The fee is not one lump

What the merchant pays looks like a single rate. Inside it, however, is a sum of portions with different characters. Reproducing the example the source article uses, an average US credit card transaction of 100 dollars, the split is as follows.

Interchange2%2.00 dollarsIssuing institutionA transfer from acquiring to issuing

Three of those portions are decided by different actors. Interchange comes from the network's rate schedule, the processing fee comes from the contract between the merchant and the processor, and the assessment is set by the network itself. The boundary between what a merchant can negotiate and what it cannot runs exactly along that line.

When regulation reaches the rate, the split changes. The source article notes that in the European Union interchange is restricted to 0.3 percent. The same card and the same procedure, but a different jurisdiction places a different ceiling on how much can flow to the issuing side. The separate question of choosing a payment service provider and a pricing model is set out in the article on a public sector payment provider switch.

Korea decides the rate a different way

Everything described so far is a split arranged by market contract. In Korea the statute reaches inside the way the rate is decided. This is the point at which transplanting the international model directly produces a wrong answer. What follows is the Korean position; where the jurisdiction differs, the requirements differ with it.

Article 18-3 paragraph 1 of the Specialized Credit Finance Business Act (여신전문금융업법) provides that a credit card business operator, in setting the merchant fee rate applicable to a credit card merchant, shall set it fairly and reasonably and shall not unfairly discriminate in the merchant fee rate. Paragraph 2 of the same article directs the Financial Services Commission to establish the governing rules, and paragraph 3 requires that a preferential rate determined by the Commission be applied to small and mid sized credit card merchants. Paragraph 4 prohibits improper conduct by large merchants.

The consequence is that Korea has rates by revenue band that the law contemplates in advance. The figures in the reduction announced by the Financial Services Commission on 17 December 2024 are these.

Application was scheduled, after amendment of the supervisory regulations, from the first half 2025 selection date for small and mid sized merchants, namely 14 February 2025. The same announcement also changed the recalculation system. The Commission stated that the qualified cost recalculation cycle, currently carried out every three years, would be adjusted to six years in principle, while allowing a fresh recalculation where a review every three years of economic conditions and the operating position of card companies indicates that one is warranted.

Why a merchant cannot refuse a card

If you have ever been asked for cash, or told that the price changes when a card is used, there is a specific provision that addresses the situation. Article 19 of the Specialized Credit Finance Business Act separates merchant obligations paragraph by paragraph. These are Korean requirements and other jurisdictions treat the same conduct differently.

  • Paragraph 1 - a credit card merchant may not refuse credit card payment, or treat a credit card member unfavourably, on the ground that the transaction is made by credit card.
  • Paragraph 2 - on each transaction the merchant shall confirm that the credit card is being used legitimately by the person entitled to use it.
  • Paragraph 3 - for the protection of member information, the merchant shall install and use a card terminal registered with the Financial Services Commission.
  • Paragraph 4 - a merchant shall not cause the merchant fee to be borne by the credit card member.
  • Paragraph 5 onward - prohibited acts are enumerated in subparagraphs, with separate paragraphs addressing large merchants and payment gateway operators.

One distinction matters here. Refusing the payment and pushing the fee onto the customer are prohibited by different paragraphs. The first is paragraph 1, the second is paragraph 4. A demand for an extra amount because a card is being used falls under the second.

The direction to take when meeting such a situation is the legitimate one. Keep the record of the transaction and use the reporting channels of the Credit Finance Association of Korea and the Financial Supervisory Service. Which paragraph a particular case falls under is for the competent authority to determine.

Disputes - who pays first and who is billed back

When a payment goes wrong, money returns along the reverse of the path it took. What matters is that the party who returns the money first and the party who ultimately bears it are not the same. Seen as a sequence of stages, it goes like this.

StageWho actsWhat happensWhere the burden sits at that point
1. Raising the issueCardholderStates a reason to the issuing institutionNothing has moved yet
2. Charging backIssuing institutionAttaches a reason code and returns the item through the network to the acquiring sideThe issuer sometimes credits first
3. Relay and evidenceAcquirer and merchantThe merchant supplies delivery proof, signature or termsIt shifts toward the merchant
4. RepresentmentAcquiring institutionRe presents the transaction if the evidence sufficesIt shifts back toward the issuer
5. Network reviewCard networkDecides under the rules when neither side yieldsA review cost attaches separately

The source article puts a number on that last stage. A network dispute review costs 600 dollars, and an appeal 1,000 dollars. Because that cost is high, the author explains, most disputes are settled between the parties at an earlier stage. The author then goes one step further and writes that the structure as a whole is weighted toward facilitating transactions and processing efficiency rather than fairness or fraud prevention. That passage is an assessment rather than a statement of fact, so it is marked here as the author's argument rather than carried across as given.

In Korea the consumer facing channels overlap with, rather than replace, that international procedure. Filing a dispute with the issuer, exercising a defence in an instalment transaction, and lodging a complaint with the Financial Supervisory Service each rest on different grounds and different conditions. Which route fits depends on the nature and timing of the transaction, so confirm it with the issuer and the competent authority. The order of response after an online payment goes wrong is set out procedurally in the article on online payment security.

Where a reader actually meets this

Once the structure is clear, certain scenes acquire explanations. Starting with the ones encountered most often.

  • Being asked abroad whether to bill in your home currency - according to guidance from the Credit Finance Association of Korea, paying in the local currency means the local currency is converted into US dollars for the international brand and the domestic issuer converts that into won. Paying in won inserts one more step converting won back into the brand currency, so a currency conversion charge is applied one additional time and a fee of 3 to 8 percent is added for that service.
  • Blocking it in advance - issuers offer a service that pre emptively blocks home currency billing abroad. The enrolment route and the scope of coverage differ by issuer, so confirm them with the issuer.
  • Why the merchant name on a statement looks unfamiliar - what appears on a statement is not the shop sign but the settlement identity registered with the acquiring institution. A franchise head office name, a corporate entity name, or a payment gateway name all show up this way.
  • Why the limit stays used after a cancellation - before capture, the interval is a wait for the hold to be lifted; after capture, it is a refund transaction travelling a settlement cycle. The gap between the purchase date and the cancellation date usually indicates which one applies.
  • Why a single amount arrives as several charges - partial shipment or partial cancellation splits the capture into multiple items. One authorization can become several settled transactions.

Whatever is chosen on a currency selection screen is the consumer's own choice, and the purpose of this section is to have the arithmetic of that choice worked out beforehand. What actually moves an exchange rate is covered separately in the introduction to exchange rates.

What the source article does not cover

Here is the line between what came from the source and what was added.

  • One company issuing and acquiring together - the contrast does not appear in the source. It was added here to explain the character of the fee line items.
  • Korean statutes and the rate system - the source works from US transactions and mentions the European interchange restriction in a single line. Everything about the domestic regime was added in this article.
  • Home currency billing abroad - it does not appear in the source. The source does not address Asian markets at all.
  • Wallets and tokenisation - a structure in which a token travels instead of the real card number falls outside the scope of the source.
  • The separate scheme for debit style payments - the fee example in the source is a single credit card transaction.
  • Comparison between brands - the author states a focus on one brand and says only that the other is much the same with different names for things.

What could not be confirmed

  • The Financial Supervisory Service pages on home currency billing - the relevant press release and the consumer portal page both returned an error response, so the text could not be opened. The 3 to 8 percent figure in this article rests on the guidance of the Credit Finance Association of Korea instead.
  • The standard interval between authorization and capture - no official standard fixing the number of days was located. It appears to be an area governed by the acquiring contract and the sector.
  • The legal basis for the merchant payout date - the provision setting the period before a merchant is paid was not pinned down here.
  • The components of qualified cost - the 17 December 2024 release states only that the rate is based on qualified cost and does not itemise its components.
  • The text of each subparagraph of article 19 paragraph 5 - it is established that there are five subparagraphs, but their individual wording was not retrieved.
  • The underlying European regulation on the interchange ceiling - the 0.3 percent figure is the source article's statement. It was not cross checked against the regulation itself.
  • The network dispute review costs - the 600 and 1,000 dollar figures are likewise the source article's statement, and were not compared against the brand's published rulebook.
  • Domestic chargeback deadlines - the filing deadlines set by international rulebooks and domestic terms were not pinned down here.
  • Later movement in the reduced preferential rates - the table reflects the announcement of 17 December 2024. Any subsequent readjustment is not reflected.
