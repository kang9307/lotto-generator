The Unit Price Dropped 20% and This Month's Bill Went Up

Where the cost actually gets cut, and why that saving never reaches your invoice

Aug 23, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: when the price cut and the invoice disagree

A price cut lands. Input is down 20 percent, output down further. Then you open the usage screen and the number is higher than last month. Nothing was misconfigured and traffic did not explode.

The mismatch is arithmetic, not a bug. A bill is not set by a rate. It is set by rate × token count. The announcement touches the first term only, and the second one had been growing quietly. The context got a little longer, the reasoning effort went up a notch, and the agent that used to make three calls per task now makes five.

Two things are worth separating here: why inference rates fall, and why that saving does not reach your spend. The second is the substance of this piece. Along the way, how to read a price sheet line by line and where cost is actually decided.

About the prices below. Every rate here was read from an official pricing page on August 23, 2026, with model name, unit and currency attached. Inference pricing changes frequently. Promotions end, tiers get reshuffled, new models land — so anything you calculate from these figures must be checked against the vendor page at the time you use it. This article recommends no provider and ranks nobody by price.

1. What was confirmed: the numbers and their conditions

The trigger first, briefly. On August 21, 2026 OpenAI lowered API pricing for its flagship GPT-5.6 Sol. The values read from the official pricing page on August 23, 2026 are below. Every unit is US dollars per one million tokens.

The prior rates were reported as $5 input, $30 output and $0.50 cached input. Since the official page shows only current pricing, the pre-cut figures rest on press reporting, and that is worth stating plainly. On those numbers the cut works out to 20 percent on input and roughly 33 percent on output.

The most interesting line is not any single rate but the fact that the cut is uneven between input and output. If cost had fallen evenly, the two would have moved together. Moving apart means either that input and output consume different resources, or that price is not set by cost alone. In practice, both.

Lower tiers on the same page make the spread clearer. GPT-5.6 Terra is $2.00 / $0.20 / $12.00 and GPT-5.6 Luna is $0.20 / $0.02 / $1.20 (input / cached input / output). Within a single provider, top and bottom differ by 20× on input. That gap comes back later.

2. Four levers that push rates down, and what each one cuts

Write inference cost in one line: the cost of running an accelerator for an hour ÷ the tokens it served in that hour. The only ways down are shrinking the numerator or growing the denominator, and everything that happens in practice falls into one of those two.

2.1 Hardware — shrinking the numerator

Inference has a different bottleneck than training. Every generated token requires reading model weights out of memory, so during decoding the speed limit is usually memory bandwidth rather than raw arithmetic. When a generation changes, bandwidth and capacity say more than headline compute figures.

  • More bandwidth — pulling weights faster raises tokens per second. The denominator grows
  • Native low precision units — when the silicon supports narrow formats directly, the same watt buys more arithmetic
  • More memory — holding the state of more concurrent requests on one device raises concurrency
  • Interconnect — when a model is split across devices, communication between them becomes idle time if it lags

Hardware, though, is the slowest item to reach the price sheet. Fleets ride contract and depreciation cycles, and an installed generation stays in the cost base until it retires. That is why announced multipliers and actual service cost do not move at the same pace. That side is covered in inference hardware bottlenecks.

2.2 Serving optimization — growing the denominator

Getting more tokens out of the same machine moves faster, because it is software.

  • Continuous batching — reading the weights once costs about the same whether the batch holds one request or many. The more requests share that read, the smaller each token's share. Bigger batches mean higher latency and lower cost, so this is a trade, not a free win
  • KV cache management — as a conversation grows, keys and values from earlier tokens pile up in memory. Reserving one large contiguous block leaves unusable fragments behind. The paper that proposed managing this in pages reports that cutting fragmentation and redundant duplication raised throughput by 2–4× at the same latency
  • Separating prefill and decode — reading a prompt in one pass and emitting tokens one at a time are opposite workloads. Share one pool and they interfere
  • Quantization — storing weights and KV cache in narrower formats shrinks the memory footprint and the bandwidth demand at once. The cost is accuracy, and how far you can go depends on the task
  • Prefix caching — when many requests share a leading segment, that computation is reused. This is the rare case where a cost saving shows up as a line item on the price sheet

2.3 Model efficiency — same quality, less computation

The third lever has the widest range. Reach a given quality level with a smaller model and the price of that quality level collapses outright.

Stanford HAI's AI Index puts the trend in one sentence: the cost of querying a model that scores at GPT-3.5 level on MMLU (64.8 percent accuracy) fell from $20 per million tokens in November 2022 to $0.07 by October 2024. That is more than 280×. The same report notes the spread is wide, anywhere from 9× to 900× per year depending on the task.

One condition has to travel with that number. It is the price of a fixed quality level, not the price of frontier quality. Last year's top tier becomes this year's mid tier; this year's top tier does not cost one two-hundred-eightieth of last year's. The two curves move differently, which is why the sentence about AI being 280 times cheaper never quite matches what practitioners feel.

2.4 Competition and strategic pricing — unrelated to cost

The last lever is not technical. Price is not always cost plus margin; there is a band where positioning sets it. This announcement shows two traces of that.

  • The word promotional — an end date means a time bounded adjustment rather than a structural change in cost. The phrase about lasting at least through November 21, 2026 promises nothing beyond it
  • The uneven cut — if cost had fallen evenly there would be no reason to cut unevenly. Cutting unevenly may reflect which number gets quoted most often in comparisons

This is not a complaint, it is a reading skill. A price falling and a cost falling are separate events, and blurring them means the budget breaks when the promotion ends. The first thing to check in any cut announcement is not the percentage but whether the rate carries an expiry.

3. Cheaper means more usage — the real reason spend holds

Here is the substance. All four levers can be real, the rate can genuinely halve, and many teams still see no reduction. The structure matches an old observation from the coal era: as engines grew efficient, coal consumption rose rather than fell. Cheapness opened new places to spend.

In inference it shows up along four paths.

  • Context grows — with a 1.05 million token window there is no reason to chunk a document. Passing the whole thing is simpler to build and usually works better, so that is what happens. Input tokens per request quietly go up tenfold
  • Reasoning depth grows — raise the effort a notch and answers improve. But reasoning tokens bill as output, and output typically costs several times input. The most expensive way to buy quality sits behind the easiest knob
  • Agents call repeatedly — one task is not one call. In a loop that invokes a tool, reads the result and decides again, each turn resends the entire prior context as input. More turns also means more input per turn, so cumulative input grows faster than the turn count
  • Experiments get cheap — so you run them. Eval sets on every deploy, ten prompt variants compared, a second opinion from another model whenever output looks shaky. Each is reasonable; together they are the invoice

The arithmetic is blunt. A 20 percent cut is a factor of 0.8. Calls per task going from three to five is a factor of 1.67. Multiply and you get 1.33. The rate fell and spending rose 33 percent. Both are true, and without separating them there is no way to explain the situation.

If one sentence has to carry this article, it is this. A price cut is a saving only while usage stays the same, and usage does not stay the same once the price falls. That is not sloppiness on the team's part — when something gets cheaper, the rational choice itself changes. Long context used to be waste; now it is sound design.

4. Reading a price sheet: it is not one line

Compare rates on the input line alone and your model will not match the invoice. Learn the kinds of line item that exist and you can read any provider's sheet the same way.

Line itemWhat it measuresWhere it surprises you
Input ratetokens sent to the modelthe most prominently displayed number, yet only part of the effective rate
Output ratetokens the model generatesseveral times input. Output heavy workloads are decided here
Cached inputreused prefixhit conditions are strict — minimum length, exact prefix match, retention window
Cache writescost of populating the cachesometimes a premium, not a discount. Without reuse it is a loss
Batchprocessing with latency traded awayroughly half price, but no promised completion time
Context length bandlong promptswhether the multiplier hits only the excess or the whole request changes the total sharply
Reasoning tokensinternal generation you never seebilled as output while invisible on screen, so it fights intuition
Modalityimage and audio inputtoken conversion rules differ from text
Built-in toolssearch, code execution and similarsometimes charged per call, separately from tokens

The current price page shows exactly how these bite. Cache reads are 0.1× uncached input, a large discount — but cache writes are 1.25×. Caching engages automatically for prompts of 1,024 tokens or more, and a single changed character in the prefix means no hit. Put a timestamp at the top of your system prompt and the hit rate is zero every time while the write premium remains. Retention for the GPT-5.6 family is documented as 30 minutes.

The long context rule works the same way. Above 272K input tokens, 2× input and 1.5× output apply to the entire request. Assume the multiplier hits only the excess and your estimate will be far off: a 272,001 token request costs roughly twice a 271,999 token one.

Reasoning tokens are documented clearly. Their content is not exposed through the API, yet they occupy the context window and are billed as output tokens. The count appears separately in the usage object, so metering is possible. The guidance also suggests reserving at least 25,000 tokens for reasoning and output. A side-by-side rate table across providers lives in the API pricing comparison — which also needs re-checking after its own confirmation date.

5. Design sets the bill, not the rate

The rate is not negotiable. For most teams it is simply given. Token count, on the other hand, is entirely a product of design. The same model at the same rate can produce bills that differ several fold.

Design decisionHow it shows on the billQuestion to ask
Composition of the prompt prefixdecides cache hit rate outrightis anything volatile sitting near the front
How conversation history is keptmore turns also means more input per turnis there a defined summarize or truncate point
Size of tool outputsearch results and file dumps become input verbatimis it trimmed before it reaches the model
Retry policyfailed calls still bill for what was generatedare there retry caps and backoff
Verification callsgrading output with another call doubles the call countis it every item or a sample
Aborted streamscutting off midway does not unbill what was produceddoes the stop condition trigger early
Default reasoning effortone default sets the most expensive line itemis it differentiated by task

The third row leaks most often. From the moment tools are attached, a human no longer writes the text going into the model. Twenty search results, whole files and stack traces become input verbatim and get resent on the next turn. However much you polish the prompt, the total will not drop without touching this.

Default reasoning effort matters too. The quality gain is visible, so the default tends to be set high, and the cost lands on the most expensive line item, output tokens. It is worth checking whether short-answer work like classification and extraction is running at that same effort. 6. Does a cheaper tier lower total cost?

On the rate alone it looks obvious — as seen above, input differs 20× within one family. But total cost is not the API line alone.

  • Retries — a higher failure rate means more calls to reach the same result, offsetting part of the rate gap
  • Human review time — if a person checks output, a small rise in the share needing attention overtakes the API line quickly
  • Downstream cleanup — the cost of a bad result that slipped through unnoticed. Hard to model, usually the largest
  • Routing itself — deciding which tier gets the request is another call or another delay. Routing is not free

So the better criterion is the nature of failure rather than the rate. Tasks where failure is filtered automatically and a rerun settles it — format conversion, classification, first drafts — favor the cheaper tier. Tasks where failure pulls in human time or is hard to reverse — code changes, external documents, final judgments — can be cheaper overall on the expensive tier.

There is one way to get a rough feel: put one hour of a person's time and one million tokens on the same scale. A million tokens covers dozens of ordinary tasks, and if that costs less than an hour of attention, then trading a review pass for a higher tier makes sense. If the pipeline requires a human to inspect everything regardless, upgrading buys much less.

7. What to measure before you can manage

Trying to cut cost without instrumentation usually ends at writing shorter prompts, even when the large items sit elsewhere. Here is what is worth capturing.

  • Keep money and tokens as separate series — this matters most. Money alone blends rate changes with usage changes and the cause cannot be separated. Token series stay comparable across price changes
  • Input, output and cached tokens per request — broken out, not summed. Totals only move once it is already late
  • Cache hit rate and why hits break — prefix change, below the minimum length, or past the retention window
  • Calls per completed task — the blow-up point in agent systems. Request counts get watched; calls per task often do not
  • Reasoning tokens as a share of output — exposed separately in the usage object, so it can be aggregated directly
  • Share of requests in the long context band — requests hovering near the boundary are a design signal in themselves
  • Attribution tags by feature and team — without knowing which feature spends what, there is nothing to choose between

Use cost per completed task rather than cost per request. Cost per request falls just by splitting prompts into smaller pieces while the total stays flat or rises. What one delivered result costs is the only unit that does not wobble.

Provider dashboards have also been dropping their breakdowns, which makes a single point of observation risky. That is covered in what remains observable when cost displays disappear.

8. What falling prices genuinely opened up

Read this far and price cuts start to look pointless. They are not. Falling rates worked by widening what is possible rather than by shrinking spend, and that effect is real.

  • Cost of experiments — revising a prompt twenty times and running the eval set each round no longer needs a budget meeting. Iteration is the surest route to quality, and iteration got cheap
  • Access for small teams — touching a top-tier model no longer requires a contract and a minimum commitment. Building a prototype on a frontier model and throwing it away is now a normal development step
  • Volume work becomes viable — when a fixed quality level gets cheap, high-count low-value-per-item work like bulk classification, translation and summarization pays for the first time. The 280× from the AI Index is felt in this shape
  • New architectural options — having batch and cache as line items means trading latency for cost becomes a product level decision. Overnight bulk processing is a reasonable choice again

So the payoff from falling prices shows up in scope, not on the invoice. It means doing more with the same money, not doing the same thing for less. Separate the two and the question of why costs stayed flat after a price cut turns out to have been the wrong question.

9. What to watch: settled and unsettled

No forecasts here. But sorting what is established from what is not saves time on the next announcement.

  • Settled — the rates displayed as of August 23, 2026, the promotional nature and minimum duration, the long context threshold and multipliers, cache read and write multipliers, and that reasoning tokens bill as output
  • Not settled — pricing after the promotion ends, whether this cut reflects falling cost or competitive response, and how other providers will move

When the next cut lands, check in this order.

  • Does the rate carry an expiry — if so, budget on the old price
  • Is the cut on input or output — an output heavy workload barely feels an input cut
  • Did cache conditions change alongside it — minimum length, retention and write multiplier move quietly
  • Did the surcharge threshold move — one boundary can matter more than the headline percentage
  • Did the default reasoning effort change — the classic path to output tokens rising without a code change

What could not be confirmed

Everything left undecided in this article, in one place.

  • Pre-cut rates — the official page displays current pricing only. The $5 / $30 / $0.50 figures above rest on press reporting
  • The motive for this cut — no official statement was found on whether it reflects lower cost or competitive positioning
  • Actual inference cost and margin per provider — not disclosed. The cost structure described here is a general account drawn from public technical literature
  • Quality gaps between tiers quantified per task by an independent source — rate multiples and quality multiples are separate questions
  • Currency conversion and tax treatment — these vary by billing arrangement, so the dollar figures here may not match what is actually charged

Conclusion: three criteria that hold

First, watch the token count, not the rate. An announcement touches one coefficient; what moves the bill is usually the term beside it. Without recording money and tokens separately, the two can never be pulled apart.

Second, a price sheet is not one line. Output multiples, cache reads and writes, batch, length bands and reasoning tokens combine into the effective rate. Compare the input line alone and the comparison does not hold.

Third, savings come from design, not negotiation. Fixing the prompt prefix so the cache survives, trimming tool output before it enters, differentiating reasoning effort by task — each moves the number more than an announcement does. And none of it requires waiting for one.

One last note: flat spending is not automatically a bad sign. If the same money is buying work that used to be out of reach, that is the plan working. The problem is when the increase cannot be explained. Instrumentation is what makes the explanation possible, and it has to be in place before the invoice arrives.
