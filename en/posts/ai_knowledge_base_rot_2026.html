Your Internal Knowledge Base Rots Quietly - Document Governance Six Months After RAG

Why retrieval is right and the answer is wrong - validity dates, canonical status, conflict handling, reindex triggers

Jul 27, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: retrieval was right. The answer just wasn't

Six months since you loaded internal documents into a vector store and put a chatbot in front of them. One day an employee asks: “How many days of leave can I carry over?” The bot answers without hesitation. “Up to 10 days may be carried over.” There is even a citation. Click it, and a genuine internal document opens. The trouble is that the document is a draft amendment to the employment rules that HR uploaded three years ago and that was rejected at executive review. The rule in force allows five days.

In this incident, retrieval did nothing wrong. It found the document that best matched the query, the model read that document faithfully, and the citation link resolves. There is no point at which an automated check would have fired. And yet the answer is wrong. The reason is singular: a stale document was sitting in the index.

Material on how to build RAG is now abundant. Chunking strategies, hybrid retrieval, rerankers, evaluation metrics - one search away. What is scarce is material on what happens after you build it, as time passes. This article covers only that stretch. The argument is three lines long.

  • A knowledge base is at peak quality on the day it launches, and from then on it decays monotonically unless something is done.
  • Decay does not cause outages. No server dies, no alert fires. So if you do not measure it, you never see it.
  • The countermeasure is not retrieval tuning but document governance: validity periods, canonical status, owners, and a disposition procedure - old records-management ideas, needed verbatim.

What this article does not cover. It does not explain how to build RAG. The difference between vector retrieval and knowledge graphs, graphing a codebase, and semantic layer design are covered in code knowledge graphs, graphify and executable data context respectively. Verifying the claims in an AI output is handled in the fact-checking process and output review design. This piece takes the opposite side: how to manage the knowledge that goes in.

1. Why better retrieval surfaces stale documents better

The uncomfortable part of this problem is that effort spent improving retrieval can make things worse. The reason sits in the structure of the ranking function.

1.1 The similarity function has no time axis

Vector ranking is decided by the distance between the query vector and the document vector. Nowhere in that computation is there a term for when the document was written or whether it is still in force. Being out of date does not shave a millipoint off cosine similarity. As a result, every improvement to recall pulls in correct documents and stale documents equally well.

1.2 Stale documents often resemble the query more closely

The crueller part is this: superseded documents tend to outrank current ones. Three effects compound.

  • Vocabulary ages together. When a programme is renamed, employees keep using the old name. If “summer leave” becomes “refresh leave”, questions still arrive as “summer leave”, and the only documents containing that phrase are pre-rename.
  • Drafts are friendlier than the canonical text. Draft amendments, explanatory decks and FAQs spell out background and examples. The canonical rule is terse legal prose. Lexical overlap with a natural-language question is far higher for the former.
  • There are simply more stale documents. Behind one current version sit several prior versions, review copies and circulation copies. Pull the top k and the old ones fill the seats by weight of numbers.

1.3 Models get more confident when context is insufficient

The analysis Google Research published alongside the ICLR 2025 paper Sufficient Context: A New Lens on Retrieval Augmented Generation Systems is direct on this point. The researchers built a classifier that judges whether retrieved context is sufficient to settle the answer, then re-partitioned RAG failures with it. When context was insufficient, models tended not to abstain but to answer confidently and wrongly. In the example cited on the blog, one model's hallucination rate rose sharply when given insufficient context compared with being given no context at all.

A stale document is the worst kind of insufficient context under that lens. It is not insufficient because the answer is missing; it is insufficient because a plausible answer is present and it is the old one. The model registers the context slot as filled, so the abstain signal never switches on.

2. The missing cell in RAG failure taxonomies - when the data itself is wrong

The most widely cited systematisation of RAG failure is Seven Failure Points When Engineering a Retrieval Augmented Generation System by Barnett and four co-authors, presented at CAIN 2024. It distils failure points observed across three real deployments - research, education and biomedical - into seven. The table below regroups those seven by which layer the fix lives in.

Failure pointWhat happensLayer the fix lives inIn scope here
FP1 Missing contentThe answer is simply not in the corpusCorpus expansionPartly
FP2 Missed top-rankedThe answer exists but does not rank high enoughRetrieval and rerankingNo
FP3 Not in contextRetrieved, but not assembled into the contextChunking and context assemblyNo
FP4 Not extractedPresent in context but the model fails to pull itPrompting and modelNo
FP5 Wrong formatRequested table or list format is ignoredPrompting and output parsingNo
FP6 Incorrect specificityToo vague or too narrow for the needPrompting and query rewritingNo
FP7 IncompleteNot wrong, but leaves things outContext assemblyNo
(Empty cell) Data errorThe answer is there and it is stale or wrongDocument governanceAll of it

The row to read is the last one. All seven failure points are pipeline defects. Fix something in the retriever, the chunker, the prompt or the model and they improve. But content that is itself wrong is never caught by polishing the pipeline. If anything, the more perfect the pipeline, the more faithfully the wrong content is delivered.

The same paper concludes, in effect, that validating a RAG system is only feasible during operation and that robustness evolves rather than being designed in at the start. Translated into document terms: the quality of a knowledge base is not settled on launch day. Which leads to a concrete consequence - instrument the corpus, not just the pipeline. Section 9 is about that.

3. Five ways internal documents age

Lumping everything into “stale” produces lumped countermeasures. In practice there are at least five kinds of ageing, each with a different detection difficulty and response.

3.1 Why partial staleness is the most dangerous

Of the five, the one that causes the most incidents is partial staleness. The document as a whole is alive, so no superseded flag is attached. The last-modified date may be recent. It has an owner and it has been reviewed. Yet one specific table inside it is out of date. And RAG retrieves chunks, not documents. If precisely the chunk containing that stale table is the one pulled, the freshness of the document as a whole provides no protection at all.

From this comes one working rule. Metadata has to ride on the chunk, not the document. If validity lives only at document level, it does not travel with the chunk at retrieval time. The minimum requirement is that the indexing pipeline copies document metadata onto every chunk, and blocks with their own refresh cadence - rate tables, price lists - are better off carrying their own metadata.

3.2 The index throws away the signals humans use

Humans filter stale documents remarkably well. But that judgement runs on signals around the document rather than in it. Is the folder /archive/2022/? Does the filename end in _draft? Has the person who uploaded it already left? Did a colleague say “oh, that's the old one”? The extract, chunk, embed pipeline discards every one of those signals. What survives is the body string.

The fix is straightforward: restore the discarded signals as metadata. Keep source path, source system, folder depth, authoring department and filename pattern on the chunk, and you can build filters and metrics from them. That step alone tends to deliver a substantial early improvement.

4. Two contradictory documents - the model picks one and cites it

A stale document on its own is the easier case. The real problem is when the stale document and the current one are both in the index.

The EMNLP 2024 survey Knowledge Conflicts for LLMs: A Survey splits knowledge conflict three ways: context-memory conflict between the model's learned knowledge and the supplied context, inter-context conflict between the supplied documents themselves, and intra-memory conflict where the model's own parameters disagree. What we meet in an internal knowledge base is the second, and the same survey names two causes for it: misinformation and outdated information. Facts evolve, and a retrieved set can contain updated and superseded material at the same time.

4.1 Which way the model leans

Faced with conflict, the model does not abstain; it picks. The biases the survey records are below. Note that none of them is “the more recent one”.

  • It leans toward passages that are superficially closer to the query wording.
  • It prefers passages that agree with the parametric knowledge it already holds.
  • It is sensitive to the order in which evidence is presented.
  • A tendency to follow whichever claim is supported by more documents has been reported.

The fourth stings. As noted above, stale documents usually exist in several copies while the current one exists in one. Majority behaviour plus copy accumulation produces a predictable result.

4.2 The real problem is concealment, not selection

That the model picks is unavoidable. What is fatal in practice is that the act of picking leaves no trace on screen. The answer is one paragraph and the citation is one line. The losing document cannot be found without digging through logs. The user never learns that there was a disagreement at all.

So the countermeasure for this one lives in the interface, not the model. When conflict is detected, the system should show both and hand the judgement back to the human instead of quietly choosing. Section 12 returns to this.

5. Drafts, meeting notes and personal memos indexed at canonical weight

An indexer eats any file it is pointed at. It checks whether you have permission, but never what standing the document holds inside the organisation. The result is that the following become grounding evidence with exactly the same authority as canonical internal policy.

  • Drafts and review copies: _draft, wip, v0.9, final_final_actually_final
  • Rejected or withdrawn items: approvals that were turned down, notices that were retracted
  • Meeting notes: “it was discussed that…” reads as “it was decided that…”
  • Personal memos: tidy-up notes in someone's personal drive, an individual's private wiki
  • External documents: vendor proposals, consulting reports, competitor material - somebody else's claims cited as our policy
  • Training material: onboarding decks summarising the rules, with the exceptions trimmed away in simplification
  • Translations: the lag between an amendment and its translated version becomes a contradiction on its own
  • Chat and mail exports: short lines stripped of context get indexed as if they were assertions

5.1 Documents need a state

One discipline confronted this long ago: KCS (Knowledge-Centered Service, Consortium for Service Innovation), the knowledge-management methodology from the service-desk world. KCS v6 assigns each article a state - work in progress, not validated, validated - so the system knows which stage a document is at. It even specifies that a work-in-progress article should exist only while the related incident is open, and that on closure it must be deleted or promoted.

The one-line lesson. In a repository with no state field, every document is, as far as the system is concerned, in the validated state. Declining to mark status is not neutrality - it is declaring everything canonical.

5.2 The axis that separates canonical from reference

Separate from state (which stage of authoring), you need a second axis: authority. Three grades are enough in practice.

  • Canonical: the single document the organisation recognises on this subject. Preferred as grounding.
  • Reference: explanations, commentary, external material. Searchable, but loses to canonical on conflict.
  • Record: meeting notes, postmortems, ADRs - documents that capture facts as of a point in time. Not subject to refresh, and never to be cited as current policy.

Those three grades alone remove a large share of the “meeting notes cited as policy” class of incident. Record-grade documents should be excluded from answer grounding by default and surfaced only when a user explicitly asks about history.

6.1 Last modified and last reviewed are different values

The field most often omitted, and most painful when omitted, is last_reviewed. Most systems carry only last modified, and that value fails decay judgement in both directions.

  • False freshness: one typo, a logo swap or a link fix bumps last modified. The content has been untouched for three years but reads as “edited yesterday”.
  • False staleness: an accurate document that has needed no change in five years shows a five-year-old date. Automated filters discard exactly these first.

Reviewing is a different act from editing. There must be a separate field recording “I read this and it is still correct”, so that the conclusion “nothing to change” leaves a trace in the system. Operationally, let a completed review update metadata without touching the body, while ensuring the action lands in an audit log.

6.2 Where to put it and how

Implementation depends on the repository, but there are only three options.

  • Front matter at the top of the document: suits markdown and git-based repositories. Metadata travels with the document and changes are versioned.
  • Properties or labels in the wiki or DMS: Confluence, SharePoint and similar. Bulk editing is easy and required values can be enforced.
  • A separate mapping table in the indexing pipeline: the fallback when originals cannot be touched. Assign defaults by path pattern and hand-manage the exceptions.

Whichever you choose, the pipeline must copy these values onto chunks and the retrieval filter and answer interface must actually use them. Metadata that is filled in but never consumed does not survive.

7. An exclusion list - not indexing is the cheapest control

The highest-leverage work in knowledge base quality is not rank adjustment; it is not ingesting the document in the first place. Demoting something already indexed needs rerankers, filters and evaluation. Not ingesting it needs a few path rules.

7.1 Excluding is not deleting

A misunderstanding creeps in here. Excluding from an index and deleting a document are completely different acts. The international records-management standard ISO 15489-1:2016 frames the record lifecycle from creation and capture through to disposition, with retention periods, disposition authority and legal holds executed under defined procedure. During an audit or a dispute you must be able to answer “what did the rule say at that time?”, and that requires the stale document to remain retained.

So the working principle is: retention follows records management; index inclusion is a separate decision. Deleting files because they are old can breach policy, and it also erases the reasoning behind the judgement.

7.2 Exclusion rules produce false positives too

Exclusion rules cannot be written once and forgotten. A large share of “why can't it find that document?” tickets trace back to exclusion rules, and rules do sometimes swallow canonical material quietly. At minimum, run these two.

  • An exclusion log: record which document was dropped by which rule. Logging the rule name is what makes reversal possible.
  • A monthly sample review: pull 20 to 30 excluded documents at random and have a human look. If canonical material is in there, fix the rule.

Large vendors treat the same issue as a pre-deployment prerequisite. Microsoft's guidance for Microsoft 365 Copilot presents content clean-up as preparatory work, on the grounds that unaddressed oversharing and neglected content let AI surface existing permission gaps and stale material far more easily. From this article's angle, permission hygiene and freshness hygiene are two faces of the same job. The permission axis is covered separately in AI agent permission design and LLM data security policy.

8.1 Owners must be roles, not names

The most common route to collapse is ownerlessness. Write an individual's name on a document and it loses its owner the moment that person leaves or transfers. Review notifications then go to a dead account, nobody receives them, and the document ages quietly.

  • Record the owner as a role: “payroll team lead, HR”.
  • Keep the role-to-person mapping in one place, the HR system.
  • Track documents with no owner and documents owned by leavers as separate metrics (section 9).
  • A document nobody will own is a signal that it should not be indexed. Do not let AI ground answers in material no one is accountable for.

8.2 Some documents must never be rewritten

Marking ADRs and postmortems as “not reviewed” in the table is not an oversight. These documents record the situation and the judgement as they stood. Rewriting them to match later circumstances destroys their value as records. If a decision was reversed, the correct move is to write a new record and mark the old one superseded. The problem is when such records are cited as current policy - which is exactly why authority=record exists.

9. Decay detection metrics - what to measure so rot becomes visible

As said in the introduction, decay does not fire alerts. A dead server announces itself; answers drifting slowly out of date announce nothing. So unless you measure deliberately, it stays invisible.

The single thing to do today. Change the query log schema to record cited chunk IDs, document IDs and each document's last_reviewed, status and authority. Every metric above derives from that log, and that log cannot be reconstructed retroactively. Skip it today and three months from now you still compute nothing.

9.1 The golden set - the device that works best in practice

Metrics show a trend; a regression query set (golden set) catches incidents directly. The method is simple.

  • Write 50 to 200 queries whose answers are settled - internal rules, procedures, figures with one correct value.
  • Record the expected answer and the expected grounding document ID for each. Recording the grounding is the crucial half.
  • Run it automatically once a week and alert when either the answer or the cited document changes.
  • After any incident, add that query to the golden set. It is the only reliable way to stop the same failure recurring.

The golden set doubles as the before-and-after baseline for reindexing, model swaps and chunking changes. The index cut-over decision in section 11 rests on this device.

10. Detecting and resolving contradictions - from discovery to disposition

Here is how to actually process the inter-context conflict from section 4. Detection and resolution are separate.

10.1 Detection - four routes

  • Static detection: find pairs sharing a subject tag, with overlapping effective ranges, and no supersedes relation. It runs as a batch, so it is cheap.
  • Query-driven detection: collect queries where the effective_from spread across the top-k evidence is wide. The advantage is that it surfaces only conflicts that actually affect answers.
  • Golden set regression: if an answer changed, usually a new document arrived or a stale one climbed the ranking.
  • User flags: KCS holds a “Flag It or Fix It” principle - whoever finds a problem either fixes it or marks it. A single report button is often more precise than batch detection.

10.2 Resolution - five steps

  1. Triage: is it a real contradiction, or two different scopes? If the latter, a scope field resolves it.
  2. Assign an arbiter: if the two owners differ, the parent organisation arbitrates. Always attach a deadline. Arbitration without one never ends.
  3. Rule: flip one to superseded and link it with superseded_by. If both remain valid, split them by stating scope explicitly.
  4. Apply: do not delete from the index - change the state and reindex. A state change alone already alters filter results.
  5. Register a regression: add the query to the golden set. Skip this and the same contradiction returns by another path.

The step that collapses most often is number two. “Nobody can say for certain which one is right” is a genuinely common state. When that happens, the best technical move is to display the conflict in the answer interface while the ruling is pending. It beats quietly picking one by a wide margin.

11. Reindex triggers - embeddings age too

Documents are not the only thing that ages. Vectors age as well. But vector decay behaves differently from document decay, so it needs its own trigger list.

Metadata or state changedChunk metadata for that documentSuperseded flags never reach the filter
Permission or audience changedApplied immediatelyContent is summarised to users who lack access
Document deleted or movedImmediate delete or path rewriteBroken links offered as evidence
Chunking strategy changedEverythingOld and new chunks mix, causing duplication and gaps
Embedding model swapped or upgradedFull regenerationRetrieval quality collapses with no explanation

11.1 Embeddings belong to the model that made them

The costliest trigger is swapping the embedding model. The reason is simple: vectors from different models do not live in the same space. Vendor documentation is explicit.

  • Microsoft's Azure AI Search documentation states that the vectorizer added to an index should be the same embedding model used to generate the vectors in that index, and describes the embedding space as being made of vector fields populated by the same model.
  • AWS documentation states that for Bedrock knowledge bases the embedding model type cannot be changed after creation and a new knowledge base must be created. Chunking configuration likewise requires recreating the data source.
  • The models themselves turn over. OpenAI announced the text-embedding-3 family in January 2024, with improvements over the prior generation and adjustable dimensions. Once a new generation appears, existing vectors stay tied to the old one unless regenerated.

So swapping an embedding model is a rebuild project, not a configuration change. Do it after the corpus has grown and the cost has grown with it. Four preparations pay off.

  • Never treat the vector store as the source of truth. Keep source text and metadata outside it. The vector store should be a derivative you can throw away and regenerate.
  • Do not let reindexing be a one-off script. Make it a job that can be rerun any time, that pauses and resumes, and that reports progress.
  • Blue/green indexes. Build the new index in parallel, compare old and new with the golden set, then cut over. Leave a way back.
  • Measure full reindex duration during the first build. That single number governs every later decision. Half a day means you can decide lightly; two days means you need a plan.

11.2 What ages even if you never change models

Some drift happens with the embedding model held constant: internal vocabulary changes. When product, programme or system names are reorganised, query vocabulary and index vocabulary diverge. What is needed then is not re-embedding but a synonym and alias dictionary. Placing a mapping from old names to new names in front of retrieval recovers a large share without any full reindex. That dictionary is itself a document and needs an owner and a review cycle.

There is also the model's weakness on facts postdating its training. The FreshLLMs work (ACL Findings 2024), with Google participation, built a benchmark from fast-changing knowledge and false-premise questions and reported flat scaling curves - increasing model size barely improved performance. Recency is not solved by a bigger model, which is precisely why freshness management on the retrieval side plays an irreplaceable role.

12. Answer interface principles - expose the evidence and its date

Governing only the document side gets you halfway. The other half sits in the answer interface. An interface that reveals staleness creates pressure to fix it; one that hides staleness ensures nobody does.

  1. Show the evidence with its date, not just a link. Put document title plus last reviewed plus validity period on one line.
  2. Use relative dates. “Reviewed 3 years 3 months ago” reads far better than “2023-04-11”. People do not compute absolute dates.
  3. Warn visibly on expired evidence. If a cited document is past its review cycle, distinguish it by colour and wording.
  4. Badge non-canonical evidence. If a draft, reference or record grounded the answer, say so beside the answer.
  5. Show both sides when conflict is detected. Do not quietly pick one; present both documents with their validity periods.
  6. State the as-of point in the answer. “As of July 2026, the most recent evidence is the revision reviewed 2026-03-14” gives the reader room to judge.
  7. Produce no answer when evidence is missing or wholly expired. Give the owner's contact instead. “No current evidence found for this topic - please check with the HR payroll team” beats a plausible wrong answer by a mile.
  8. Put the flag button on the document, not the answer. “This document is out of date” is a far more actionable signal than “this answer was unhelpful”. Route the flag straight to the owner.
  9. Tie confidence language to evidence state. When grounding is expired or reference-grade, design the prompt and post-processing so the answer speaks in conditionals rather than assertions.

These principles are governance because exposure is pressure. When a stale document shows up daily with a badge on it, the owning department eventually fixes it. When it blends into answers unmarked, nobody even learns there is a problem. This is why KCS's “Flag It or Fix It” works: once people take responsibility for the content they interact with, content is continually reviewed and improved.

13. A 90-day plan - what to start next Monday

If you finish this article and begin with “let's tag every document in the company”, you will almost certainly stall. Order matters.

13.1 Weeks 1 to 2: make it visible

  • Add cited document ID, chunk ID, document date and status to the query log schema. This week.
  • If you have historical data, pull the top 100 most cited documents. If not, two weeks of collection is enough.
  • Write a 50-question golden set with correct answers and expected grounding documents.

13.2 Weeks 3 to 4: stop ingesting

  • Exclude personal drives, draft folders, trash and copies. Start with path patterns.
  • Run the golden set before and after to confirm recovery rather than regression.
  • Turn on the exclusion log and eyeball a 20-item sample.

13.3 Weeks 5 to 8: clean up only the top 100

  • Fill status, authority, owner and last_reviewed on the top 100 cited documents only.
  • Make the pipeline copy those values onto chunks and make the retrieval filter actually use them.
  • This stage surfaces conflict candidates in bulk. Process them strictly in citation order.

13.4 Weeks 9 to 12: make it stick

  • Assign the per-type review cadences from section 8 and wire notifications to owners.
  • Add dates, badges and a flag button to the answer interface (section 12).
  • Put only three of the section 9 metrics on a dashboard: median cited-document age, expired citation rate, ownerless document count.

13.5 Ongoing: harden it

  • Weekly automated golden set run with alerting on failure.
  • Monthly metric review and exclusion-rule sample check.
  • Quarterly full consistency check between index and source, plus a re-measurement of full reindex duration.
  • Give the job a name. One knowledge base owner plus document owners per domain. Part-time is fine; unowned is not.

The core of this order is Pareto. Tagging documents nobody cites is waste. The documents that actually appear in answers cluster into a tiny slice, and cleaning that slice recovers most of the perceived quality. Exhaustive clean-up comes after - and it is usually fine for “after” to mean never.

Conclusion: sort the documents before you tune retrieval

That internal documentation goes stale is not a new discovery. When GitHub surveyed over 5,500 respondents in its 2017 Open Source Survey, 93% said incomplete or outdated documentation was a pervasive problem, while 60% said they rarely or never contribute to documentation. That figure comes from a 2017 study of open source projects, so it cannot be transplanted directly onto corporate document environments. What transfers is not the percentage but the structure: everyone knows documents rot, and few people fix them.

What changed is the cost. Previously a human read the stale document, thought “ah, that's the old one”, and moved on. Now AI reads it, presents it as evidence, and propagates it in confident prose. The unconscious filtering humans performed using signals around the document has been removed wholesale from the pipeline. Rebuilding that filter out of metadata and procedure is what this article calls document governance.

One last note without overstatement. Doing everything here does not make hallucination disappear. Models can still be wrong with sufficient context, and that stretch belongs to output verification. But the “confidently wrong on stale evidence” class is different. That class is not a model limitation but a problem with the data we supplied, and it therefore shrinks under management. Before touching ranking, check what you put in.

There is one thing to do next Monday: log the cited document ID and its date in your query log. That log does not come retroactively.
