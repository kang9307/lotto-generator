Spyware Aimed at the European Parliament — Even the Pegasus Investigator Got Hacked

The watchdog got watched - what the mercenary spyware industry's reach into the EU Parliament tells us (an analysis as of July 2026)

2026-07-05 Trends & Culture

Introduction: The Person Investigating Spyware Was Spied On

On July 3, 2026, Citizen Lab, a research group at the University of Toronto, published a report whose subject was no ordinary victim. Stelios Kouloglou, a Greek journalist and former Member of the European Parliament, had served on the PEGA committee - the special committee the European Parliament created specifically to investigate the abuse of commercial spyware. A member of the very body created to watch the watchers had been infected by the exact spyware he was tasked with investigating.

This is not just another hacking headline. It is the oldest question in surveillance politics - who watches the watchmen - posed in its most literal form. It means the mercenary spyware industry has shown both the capability and the will to reach inside the legislative body trying to regulate it. In this piece I want to use this case as a starting point to work through the structure of the mercenary spyware industry, why zero-click attacks are so hard to stop, and what citizens, journalists, and officials can concretely do about it, as of July 2026.

1. What Happened - Citizen Lab's Forensic Findings

1.1 The victim and the timeline

Stelios Kouloglou is a Greek journalist and politician who served as a Member of the European Parliament from 2015 to 2024. He sat on the PEGA committee - the Committee of Inquiry on the use of Pegasus and equivalent surveillance spyware - which the Parliament ran from March 24, 2022 to July 18, 2023. When Citizen Lab conducted a forensic analysis of his iPhone in May 2026, it found that the device had been compromised with Pegasus spyware three separate times while the committee was actively working: around October 21, 2022, and again on March 6 and 7, 2023.

1.2 What the PEGA committee was set up to do

The PEGA committee was formed after an international media consortium exposed the scale of Pegasus abuse in 2021. Over roughly a year, the committee interviewed more than 215 people, sent fact-finding missions to Israel, Poland, Greece, Cyprus, Hungary, and Spain, and adopted a 145-page final report in March 2023. Kouloglou was hacked three times in the middle of exactly this work, by the very tool the committee was scrutinizing.

2. Why This Case Is Extraordinary - The Irony of the Investigator Becoming the Target

2.1 The watchdog institution itself got watched

Citizen Lab described this as the first publicly identified case of a PEGA committee member being victimized by spyware. Up to now, the committee had been investigating abuses suffered by people in other countries. This time, the investigating body itself became a victim of the very thing it was investigating. I think the weight of this case goes far beyond one person's privacy being violated - it demonstrates that the act of scrutinizing the spyware industry can itself become a trigger for being targeted by it.

2.2 Implications for the committee's work

It matters that the infections line up precisely with the committee's most active periods. It cannot be ruled out that the compromised device gave an attacker access to confidential deliberations, witness identities, or internal documents. That would mean the Parliament's own deliberative privilege and confidentiality were compromised, and it raises real questions about the integrity of the investigation's findings.

3. The Structure of the Mercenary Spyware Industry - NSO Group and Pegasus

3.1 A business model built on selling weapons to customers

Pegasus is commercial spyware developed and sold by NSO Group, an Israeli cyber-arms company. Instead of a state building its own tools, NSO Group's business model is to sell surveillance capability to paying "customers." The 2023 PEGA committee investigation found evidence that Pegasus and similar spyware had been used in over 50 countries, with journalists, human rights activists, lawyers, and opposition politicians - people whose job is to hold power accountable - as recurring targets.

3.2 Why attribution is so hard - and why no single country should be named

What stands out to me most in this case is that Citizen Lab did not attribute the deployment to any specific government, and explicitly said there is no evidence the Greek government was involved. It did find, however, that the email address used in the first infection overlapped with one used in an earlier campaign against Russian- and Belarusian-speaking exiled journalists and activists in Europe - suggesting the same customer may have run Pegasus operations across multiple European countries. This industry's structure makes it genuinely difficult to pin an attack on one country. That is arguably its most dangerous property: the blurrier accountability gets, the blurrier both regulation and deterrence become.

4. What Is Zero-Click Infection - and Why Is It So Hard to Stop

4.1 An attack that needs no click at all

One reason Pegasus is so notorious is its reliance on zero-click infection. A zero-click attack compromises a device purely through a vulnerability in a messaging app or the operating system - no clicked link, no opened attachment, no action from the victim required. From the victim's side, nothing appears to happen at all, while in that moment calls, texts, location data, and even the camera and microphone can fall into an attacker's hands.

4.2 The structural limits of detection

The biggest problem with zero-click attacks is that victims have essentially no way to notice on their own. In Kouloglou's case, infections that occurred in 2022 and 2023 were only uncovered by forensic analysis in May 2026 - nearly three years later. This kind of case only surfaces when a specialized forensics organization like Citizen Lab or Amnesty International painstakingly reconstructs device logs, and there simply aren't many organizations in the world with that capability.

5. A Threat to Democracy and Press Freedom

5.1 A risk to checks and balances

When the target list for mercenary spyware includes not just journalists and activists but the very legislators tasked with overseeing it, that is a signal that democratic checks and balances are themselves under threat. Source protection, parliamentary deliberative privilege, and judicial independence - basic democratic safeguards - can all be neutralized in the face of commercial spyware.

5.2 Not just a problem for authoritarian regimes

This is why it is hard to dismiss the problem as something only authoritarian regimes do. The PEGA committee's own investigation identified numerous cases of spyware abuse by EU member state governments themselves. This case reconfirms that mercenary spyware can be deployed inside democracies too - even against the very bodies meant to oversee it.

6. Defenses for Citizens, Journalists, and Officials - Lockdown Mode, Updates, and Device Hygiene

6.1 How effective is Apple's Lockdown Mode

Fortunately, this is not a hopeless situation. As of March 2026, Apple stated it was not aware of any successful mercenary spyware compromise on a device with Lockdown Mode enabled, and Amnesty International's security lab reportedly confirmed the same result. Lockdown Mode works by drastically shrinking the remotely reachable attack surface - blocking message attachment previews, blocking FaceTime calls from unknown senders, and more. Apple has also raised its top bounty for spyware-grade exploit chains to $2 million (over $5 million with bonuses) and runs a program that provides free devices to at-risk civil society figures.

6.2 A practical checklist

I'd boil practical defense down to five principles. First, always keep your operating system and messaging apps updated - many zero-click exploits reuse vulnerabilities that have already been patched elsewhere. Second, if you are in a high-risk group such as journalism, activism, or public office, consider keeping Lockdown Mode on as a default. Third, reboot your device regularly; some spyware is known to lose persistence after a restart, which is not a perfect defense but does lower risk. Fourth, periodically use free mobile forensic checkup tools provided by groups like Citizen Lab and Amnesty International. Fifth, keep work devices and personal devices separate to limit the blast radius if either is compromised.

7. Where the Regulatory Debate Stands - PEGA's Recommendations and Their Limits

7.1 A recommendation from three years ago, still unchanged

The European Parliament adopted the PEGA committee's final recommendation in June 2023. It called for stronger regulation of spyware manufacturing, trade, and use; stricter enforcement of existing law, including the Dual-Use export control regulation, the Anti-Money-Laundering Directive, procurement rules, and the Whistleblower Directive; and a clear definition of the vague concept of "national security."

7.2 The limits of a non-binding recommendation

The problem is that this recommendation carries no legal force. As of July 2026, neither the European Commission nor individual member states have taken substantive binding action on it. Kouloglou's case exposes that gap all over again. Three years after the recommendation was adopted, the fact that a member of the very body that adopted it was hacked by the same spyware is itself evidence of just how slowly this regulatory debate is turning into anything enforceable.

Conclusion: What to Watch, as of July 2026

The message this case leaves us with is clear. Even the attempt to scrutinize the surveillance industry can become a target for it, attribution to any single country remains structurally difficult, and binding regulation still lives only on paper. In the short term, personal-level defense - updates, Lockdown Mode, device hygiene - remains the most realistic response available. In the long term, real enforcement of dual-use export controls and market transparency is needed, but given that three years of recommendations have not translated into action, that path still looks distant. In an era where even the watchers of the watchers get watched, no one can say for certain when or whose next infection will come to light.
