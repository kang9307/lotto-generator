The Complete Guide to Obsidian Zettelkasten Note-Taking

From creating your first vault to internal links, backlinks, graph view, and Dataview - how to grow local Markdown notes into a knowledge network

Jul 8, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: Why Local Notes Are Making a Comeback

Even with cloud note apps everywhere, plenty of people are going back to plain local Markdown files. Obsidian sits at the center of that shift. Without any special server or account, you keep your notes in a single folder on your own computer and grow them into a knowledge network by linking them to each other. This guide covers what Obsidian is and why people use it, the concept behind the note-taking method it's often paired with - Zettelkasten - the concrete steps for creating a vault and linking notes, and the pitfalls beginners commonly hit. This is written as of July 2026, and it focuses on concepts and workflows that stay valid across specific UI versions rather than screenshots that go stale.

1. What Is Obsidian?

1.1 Local Markdown, Works Offline

Obsidian's defining trait is that notes are stored as plain Markdown (.md) files in a folder on your own device, not on a cloud server. You can write, read, and search notes with no internet connection, and because the files are plain text, you can open them in any other editor if you ever switch apps. If a company's servers disappear or a service shuts down, your notes stay exactly where they were - on your computer. That's not a minor convenience; it's a question of data ownership.

1.2 The Vault - the Folder Where Your Notes Live

In Obsidian, a collection of notes is called a vault. A vault is really just an ordinary folder containing Markdown files plus Obsidian's own configuration files. You can keep separate vaults for work, personal life, or study, or split areas within a single vault using regular folders.

1.3 A Free Core App and a Plugin Ecosystem

The core Obsidian app is free for personal use. On top of it, a community-built ecosystem of over 1,000 plugins adds features like calendars, kanban boards, template automation, and database-style queries. You can start light by installing only what you need, then assemble the workflow you want as you get more comfortable.

2. What Is the Zettelkasten Method?

2.1 German for "Slip-Box" - Luhmann's Method

Zettelkasten is German for "slip-box." It is widely known as the note-taking method the 20th-century German sociologist Niklas Luhmann practiced throughout his life. Luhmann is reported to have written short notes on paper cards, numbered them, and filed them in drawers, building a stack of roughly 90,000 cards over his lifetime - a system he credited with helping him write around 70 books and nearly 400 articles. The point was never the sheer volume of notes, but a structure where notes connected to each other and grew on their own.

2.2 Three Layers of Notes: Fleeting, Literature, and Permanent

A common way to explain Zettelkasten is through three layers of notes. A fleeting note is a quick, temporary jot-down of a thought as it occurs to you. A literature note is a summary or excerpt you take while reading a book or an article. A permanent note is what you get when you revisit your fleeting and literature notes and rewrite the idea entirely in your own words. The key distinction is that a permanent note is not a copy of someone else's claim - it's your own restated understanding of that claim.

2.3 Atomic Notes and Links - Why Connections, Not Folders

Zettelkasten notes are supposed to be atomic: each note should hold exactly one complete idea. Rather than filing a note into a predetermined folder hierarchy, you search your existing notes for related ideas and connect them with a link. Guides on the method also recommend jotting a short line explaining why the two notes are related, rather than just linking them. Structure isn't a drawer you designed in advance - it emerges as links accumulate. Luhmann is said to have described his own slip-box as a kind of "conversation partner" that surprised him with ideas he hadn't consciously put there.

3. Getting Started in Obsidian - Vaults, Markdown, Links, and Tags

3.1 Creating Your First Vault

The first thing you do after installing Obsidian is create a vault. You can point it at any folder on your computer, and notes accumulate inside it as Markdown files. Rather than designing an elaborate folder structure up front, it's enough at first to just create one note and write freely in it.

3.2 Basic Markdown Syntax

Obsidian notes are written in Markdown syntax. Knowing just enough to make a heading with #, emphasize text with **bold**, and build a list with - is plenty to get started. Markdown is plain text, so it's easy to learn and stays compatible with other editors and tools.

3.3 Connecting Notes with Internal Links [[ ]]

The core of using Obsidian is the internal link. Wrapping another note's title in double square brackets, like [[Note Title]], automatically creates a link to that note. You can even link to a title that doesn't exist yet and fill in the note later. Linking every time one idea leads to another is the basic move of practicing Zettelkasten inside Obsidian.

3.4 Cutting Across Notes with Tags

Unlike links, which connect individual notes, a tag (#tagname) groups many notes under one shared keyword. For example, tagging notes on several different topics with #toread lets you pull all of them together later with a single tag.

3.5 Checking Connections with Backlinks and Graph View

When Note A links to Note B, Note B automatically shows a backlink - a list of "notes that link to this note." In other words, links work bidirectionally. Graph view visualizes every note and link in your vault as dots and lines. As your vault grows, it lets you see at a glance which topics are densely connected and which notes are isolated.

4. Practical Use - Daily Notes, MOCs, and Plugins

4.1 Building the Habit with Daily Notes

A daily note is a per-day note that collects short logs, to-dos, and fleeting thoughts for that day. If you set it up so the same template opens automatically each day, you can dump a thought into the daily note first and organize it later. It's the easiest way to turn Zettelkasten's fleeting-note stage into a habit.

4.2 Grouping Scattered Notes with MOCs

A MOC (Map of Content) is a hub note that gathers links to notes related to a specific topic in one place. Instead of physically moving notes into folders, you create a single note that acts as a topical table of contents and lists related notes as links. Even with hundreds of notes, maintaining a handful of well-kept MOCs lets you find what you need quickly.

4.3 Treating Notes as a Database with Dataview

Dataview is one of Obsidian's best-known community plugins. It automatically builds tables or lists from properties you attach to notes, such as status, date, or rating. Write a query like "show all notes with status = in progress as a table" inside a note, and Dataview renders a live, self-updating table of notes matching that condition. It isn't required, but once your vault grows, it's a handy way to re-view your notes from whatever angle you need.

5. Comparing Obsidian to Other Tools - Local vs. Cloud

5.1 A Different Storage Model

Notion is the tool Obsidian gets compared to most often. The most fundamental difference is where the data lives. Obsidian stores notes as Markdown files on your own device, and the app functions more like a viewer for that folder. Notion, by contrast, stores notes on Notion's cloud servers, and the app is a window into that data.

5.2 The Choice Comes Down to Priorities

If real-time collaborative editing, team-wide workflows, and web-based access matter most, Notion is the natural fit. If keeping data in your own hands, working seamlessly offline, and deep plugin customization matter most, Obsidian fits better. Neither tool is categorically better - it's more accurate to say they're built for different jobs.

5.3 Cost Structure

As of July 2026, Obsidian's core app remains completely free for individual users, and syncing across devices is an optional add-on - official Sync runs roughly $4-5 a month depending on billing cycle. Notion offers a free personal plan, but plans with the features teams need for collaboration run roughly $10-20 per user per month. For a single-user note app, Obsidian carries a smaller cost burden; if your whole team needs to work on documents together in real time, Notion's collaboration features can easily justify that cost.

6. Syncing and Backup

6.1 Official Obsidian Sync

Using the same vault across multiple devices requires syncing. The simplest option is Obsidian's own official Obsidian Sync service. It supports end-to-end encryption and keeps version history, but keep in mind it's a paid add-on.

6.2 Free Alternatives - Cloud Folders, Git, and Syncthing

If you'd rather not pay, you can place the vault folder itself inside a general-purpose cloud folder such as iCloud Drive or Google Drive and let that sync it. If you also want version control, a widely used approach is committing the vault to a Git repository through a community plugin. If you'd rather sync devices directly without a server, tools like Syncthing are another option. Whichever route you pick, the fact that a vault is ultimately "just a plain folder" is what makes all of these options possible.

6.3 Backup Is a Separate Concern

Syncing and backup solve different problems. Syncing keeps multiple devices in the same state; backup keeps a copy you can restore from if a file gets accidentally deleted or corrupted. Periodically copying your vault folder to a separate drive or cloud location, or using the Git approach above, gives you version history that doubles as a backup.

7. Common Beginner Mistakes

7.1 Designing a Perfect Structure Before Writing Anything

The most common mistake is trying to design a perfect folder and tag system before writing a single note. Zettelkasten's whole premise is order that emerges from links rather than a structure decided in advance, so over-engineering the system up front just burns the energy you should be spending on actually writing notes. Structure can always be refined later with MOCs once notes have accumulated.

7.2 Collecting Without Connecting

A common failure mode is copying down passages from articles or books without rewriting them in your own words, or creating notes and never linking them to anything else. No matter how many notes pile up this way, if they stay disconnected they become hard to find and reuse later. Building the habit of asking "is there an existing note related to this?" every time you save a new one matters more than volume.

7.3 Cramming Too Much Into One Note

Ignoring the atomic-note principle and mixing several topics into a single note makes it harder to link that note to others later. Not hesitating to create a new note every time an idea comes up produces a vault that's easier to work with in the long run.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Obsidian completely free?

The core app is free for personal use. You can add official Sync for multi-device syncing or official Publish to host notes as a website as paid add-ons, but the core local features remain free indefinitely.

How many notes do I need to start a Zettelkasten?

There is no fixed number. What matters is not volume but building the habit early: write each note as one complete idea, kept short, and link it to related notes as you go.

Should I use Obsidian or Notion?

If you want your data on your own device and reliable offline access, Obsidian fits better. If your team needs real-time collaborative editing, Notion fits better. Neither is strictly superior - they are built around different storage models and collaboration needs.

Do I need to install the Dataview plugin?

No, it is not required. You can practice Zettelkasten with just internal links and tags. But once your vault grows, a community plugin like Dataview is useful for automatically assembling tables or lists from note properties.

I'm worried my vault will become unmanageable as it grows.

Organize around links and MOCs (maps of content) instead of a rigid folder hierarchy. As note count grows, graph view and backlinks let you navigate back to what you need. The key is not trying to design a perfect classification system from day one.

Conclusion: The First Step You Can Take Today

Obsidian and Zettelkasten are less about flashy features and more about a habit you keep. The smallest first step you can take today isn't designing an elaborate structure - it's creating a single vault and writing one thought into one note. From there, link it to another note and watch how that connection shows up in backlinks and graph view. As notes accumulate, use daily notes and MOCs to keep the flow organized, and reach for a plugin like Dataview when you need a different angle. A small habit of writing that continues for a few days does more to make Zettelkasten actually work than any perfect system ever will.
