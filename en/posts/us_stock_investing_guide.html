Complete Guide to US Stock Investing — Accounts, ETFs, Taxes, and Risks

Introduction: Why Invest in US Stocks?

As of June 2026, the US stock market represents approximately 45–50% of total global market capitalization, making it the world's largest equity market. Companies like Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Amazon, and Alphabet — whose products and services billions of people use every day — are listed on this market. Investors around the world, including those in Korea and other Asian countries, can become shareholders in these companies through a single brokerage account.

However, investing in US stocks is not as simple as "buy and watch it go up." There are key factors every investor must understand: the account opening and currency exchange process, US dollar exchange rate risk, annual tax reporting obligations, and the principles of diversification. This article is not about recommending specific stocks or guaranteeing returns. It is general educational information to help beginners understand the structure of US stock investing and make informed decisions on their own.

1. Opening a Brokerage Account for US Stocks

1.1 What Account Do You Need?

To trade US stocks, you need a brokerage account that supports overseas stock trading. In Korea, most major brokerages — including Toss Securities, Kiwoom Securities, Mirae Asset Securities, Samsung Securities, Korea Investment & Securities, KB Securities, and NH Investment & Securities — allow you to open such an account entirely online through their smartphone apps.

1.2 Account Opening Process

The typical steps are:

  1. Download the brokerage app and sign up
  2. Verify your identity (photo ID scan plus video call or digital certificate)
  3. Select a comprehensive account that includes overseas stock trading
  4. Complete the investment suitability questionnaire (required by regulation)
  5. Fund your account after it is activated

Account opening is free and generally has no minimum balance requirement. Some brokerages offer fractional share purchases, allowing you to invest with even a small amount.

1.3 What to Compare When Choosing a Brokerage

Each brokerage offers different overseas trading commission rates, currency exchange rates, and levels of research support. Trading commissions typically range from about 0.015% to 0.25% of the transaction value. For long-term investors, low commissions and favorable exchange rates are the most important factors. For those who want more market data and analysis, a brokerage with strong research services may be worth the trade-off.

2. Currency Exchange — Converting to USD

2.1 How Currency Exchange Works

US stocks are priced and settled in US dollars (USD). You must convert your home currency to USD before buying. Most brokerages provide an in-app exchange function. The better the brokerage's exchange rate, the lower your effective cost of entry.

Some brokerages offer an automatic exchange service: you place a buy order in your home currency, and the brokerage automatically converts exactly the amount needed on the settlement date. This is convenient for beginners who do not want to manage foreign exchange manually.

2.2 The Case for Dollar-Cost Averaging on Exchange

Converting a large lump sum all at once exposes you to the exchange rate on that single day. To reduce this timing risk, many advisors recommend splitting your currency conversion across multiple dates — converting smaller amounts regularly. This averages out your effective exchange rate over time, smoothing the impact of short-term fluctuations.

3. Individual Stocks vs. ETFs — Choosing as a Beginner

3.1 Individual Stocks

Buying individual stocks means purchasing shares in a specific company — Apple (AAPL), Microsoft (MSFT), Nvidia (NVDA), and so on. If that company grows substantially, your returns can be large. But if the company faces setbacks — declining earnings, management failures, or competitive disruption — your losses are concentrated. Investing in individual stocks well requires ongoing analysis of a company's business model, financials, and competitive landscape.

3.2 ETFs (Exchange-Traded Funds)

An ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund) is a basket of multiple securities that trades on a stock exchange like a single share. For example, an S&P 500 ETF gives you exposure to 500 of the largest US companies in a single purchase. ETFs offer automatic diversification, low fees, and eliminate the need to research individual companies. They are widely considered a starting point for investors who want broad market participation without stock-picking.

3.4 Why ETFs Often Suit Beginners Better

The S&P 500 has historically delivered a long-term average annual return of roughly 10% over multi-decade periods (past performance does not guarantee future results). Rather than betting on individual winners, broad ETF investing lets you participate in the overall growth of the US economy. The lower time cost and built-in diversification make ETFs a practical choice for most people who are not full-time investors.

4. Diversification and Long-Term Investing Principles

4.1 Why Diversification Matters

One of the foundational principles of investing is not putting all your eggs in one basket. Concentrating all capital in a single stock or sector means a downturn in that stock or sector hits you especially hard. Spreading across many companies, industries, and even geographies means that weakness in one area can be partially offset by strength in another.

ETFs handle this automatically. Buying one S&P 500 ETF instantly spreads your investment across sectors including technology, healthcare, finance, consumer goods, and energy — 500 companies at once.

4.2 The Power of Long-Term Thinking

Stock markets fluctuate significantly in the short term, but historically have trended upward over sufficiently long horizons (10 years or more) — though again, past trends do not guarantee future outcomes. Panic-selling during a sharp decline, or only buying after a long rally, are behaviors that historically erode long-term returns.

Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) — investing a fixed amount at regular intervals regardless of market price — means you automatically buy more shares when prices are low and fewer when prices are high, naturally reducing your average cost per share over time. Research consistently suggests that disciplined DCA outperforms attempts to time the market for most retail investors.

5. Taxes — General Overview for Non-US Investors

The tax information below is a general educational overview as of June 2026 and is primarily written with Korean residents in mind. Tax laws vary by country and individual circumstance, and can change. Always verify with official government sources or a qualified tax professional before filing.

5.1 Capital Gains Tax on Stock Sales

Korean residents who sell overseas stocks at a profit are subject to capital gains tax and must self-report. The general rules as of 2026:

  • Tax period: January 1 – December 31 of the calendar year
  • Basic deduction: 2.5 million KRW per year
  • Tax rate: approximately 22% (20% capital gains tax + 2% local income tax) on gains above the deduction
  • Reporting period: May 1–31 of the following year via the National Tax Service's Hometax
  • Gains and losses can be netted within the same tax year

For example, if you realized a net gain of 10 million KRW from overseas stock trading in one year, subtracting the 2.5 million KRW deduction leaves 7.5 million KRW taxable, resulting in a tax of approximately 1.65 million KRW (simplified illustration only). Rates and thresholds may change.

5.2 Dividend Withholding Tax

US-listed stocks and ETFs typically have dividends withheld at 15% in the United States under the US-Korea tax treaty. Since Korea's standard dividend withholding rate is 14%, the 15% already withheld in the US generally means no additional withholding is applied in Korea.

However, if total financial income (interest + dividends) exceeds 20 million KRW per year, the comprehensive financial income tax applies, potentially increasing the overall tax burden. Investors receiving substantial dividends should plan accordingly.

5.3 Preparing for Tax Filing

Most Korean brokerages provide free overseas stock capital gains statements and may offer tax filing assistance services. Obtain your trading records from your brokerage well before the May filing period. Many brokerages also offer guided or automated filing assistance through the Hometax system.

6. Currency Risk — The Other Side of Dollar Assets

6.1 How Exchange Rates Affect Returns

Because US stocks are priced in dollars, your actual return in your home currency is affected by the exchange rate between USD and your currency at the time you sell and repatriate funds. If the dollar weakens against your home currency after you invest, your foreign gains shrink when converted back. Conversely, a stronger dollar can partially cushion a loss in stock price.

For example: if you buy $1,000 worth of stock when the USD/KRW rate is 1,300, and later sell when the stock is up 10% but the rate has fallen to 1,200, your realized return in KRW is substantially lower than 10%. Currency risk is most pronounced for short-term investments.

6.2 Managing Currency Risk

  • Split currency conversion: exchange in smaller tranches over time, not all at once
  • Regular investing (DCA): investing periodically averages your entry exchange rate
  • Long-term holding: the longer the holding period, the less short-term exchange swings tend to dominate total returns
  • Domestic-listed international ETFs: some markets offer locally-listed ETFs tracking US indices, settled in local currency, eliminating currency exchange steps — though tax treatment and product characteristics differ, so verify before using

7. Common Beginner Mistakes

7.1 Frequent Trading for Short-Term Gains

Reacting to every price movement and trading frequently increases transaction costs and, in some jurisdictions, creates more complex tax reporting requirements. Attempting to time the market consistently is difficult even for professionals. Frequent trading tends to underperform a steady, long-term hold strategy for most retail investors.

7.2 Over-Concentrating in One Stock

Putting most or all of your capital into a single "sure thing" stock is a common and costly mistake. Even historically dominant companies have seen dramatic declines due to industry shifts or internal failures. Diversification is the most basic defense against this risk.

7.3 Not Knowing About Tax Reporting Obligations

Unlike domestic stocks in many countries, overseas stock gains often require self-reporting. In Korea, failing to file the capital gains tax return in May results in penalties. Know your country's requirements before you start trading.

7.4 Following Unverified Social Media Tips

Tips from social media, YouTube channels, or messaging groups urging you to buy a specific stock "right now" should always be verified for source credibility and potential conflicts of interest. Investment decisions should be based on your own research and financial situation, not on anonymous recommendations.

7.5 Panic-Selling During Market Downturns

When markets fall sharply, the instinct to sell and stop losses is natural. But selling during a downturn locks in losses and often means missing the subsequent recovery. Long-term investors need the psychological resilience to hold through temporary declines — which is why only investing money you can afford to leave invested for years is a foundational principle.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How much money do I need to start?

There is no minimum. With fractional share trading, some brokerages let you start with the equivalent of just a few dollars. What matters more than the starting amount is consistency — investing regularly over a long period. Always make sure you have an emergency fund in place before committing money to stocks.

Q: US market hours don't align with my timezone. Is that a problem?

For long-term investors who buy and hold index ETFs, market hours are rarely an issue. Most brokerages accept limit orders and scheduled orders, which execute automatically when the market opens. You do not need to be awake at 10:30 PM Korean time to invest.

Q: What is the difference between a US-listed ETF and a domestically-listed overseas ETF?

A US-listed ETF (like VOO or QQQ) is traded on a US exchange in dollars. A domestically-listed overseas ETF (for example, TIGER US S&P500 listed on the Korea Exchange) tracks the same index but is traded in Korean won on the Korean exchange. The tax treatment differs: gains from domestically-listed ETFs are typically taxed as dividend income (15.4% in Korea) rather than capital gains. Which is more advantageous depends on your situation — consult a tax professional.

9. Conclusion: Long-Term, Diversified, Consistent

Investing in US stocks does not require special expertise or large sums of money. Opening a brokerage account, exchanging currency to dollars, and regularly purchasing a broad-market ETF is a straightforward starting point that is accessible to most people. The core principles — long-term thinking, diversification, and disciplined consistency — are more important than any single stock pick or market timing call.

Understanding the tax reporting obligations, the nature of currency risk, and the importance of verifying information provides a much more stable foundation than chasing hot tips. This guide aims to give you that foundation. All investment decisions, however, must reflect your own financial situation, goals, and risk tolerance — and where needed, the guidance of a qualified professional.
