Gmail Thinks I'm Stupid, So I Left - A Critique of Forced AI in 2026

Forced AI and the Ethics of Product Design in the Age of Mandatory Intelligence

Jun 4, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: I Opened My Email, and It Offered to Read It for Me

When I open my inbox, the first thing I see is not the message - it is a gray card. A box labeled "Summary of this email" sits on top of the sentences I was about to read. When I hit reply, a "help me write" button blinks instead of an empty field. I clearly opened the mail to read and write for myself, yet the tool jumps ahead and says, "let me do it for you." That is exactly the heart of a recent user's critique. The point is simple: these features seem to treat me as someone who cannot read or write on their own, so I left.

This article is not just a retelling of that anecdote. Beyond one person's complaint about a mail client, I want to seriously examine the design ethics of an era that injects AI into products regardless of user choice. The trend of embedding AI into productivity tools is unstoppable. The problem is not what you add, but how you add it - and whether the user can refuse. From the perspective of users and product teams, I will lay out calmly why forced AI erodes trust, and how to design so that help never curdles into coercion.

1. What the Problem Actually Was - The Weight of "Forced"

First we must name the complaint precisely. What angered the user was not the "AI summary" feature itself. A summary, used well, is useful. The real problem lives in one word: "forced."

  • Cannot refuse: There is no clear single switch to turn it off, or the off path is buried deep. The user meets the same feature again every day.
  • Intrusion of position: Summary cards and writing assists occupy a spot ahead of, and more prominent than, the body. The core act of reading is demoted to an afterthought.
  • The rudeness of the premise: The assumption that "you will struggle to read this yourself" is etched into the interface. It is a signal that the tool does not trust the user.

The key is that this is not "feature versus no feature." The very same summary becomes the opposite experience depending on whether it lives as a collapsed button you expand when you want it, or as a forced card that is always open. The essence of the forced-AI critique is the removal of control.

2. "It Thinks I'm Stupid" - The Substance of an Emotional Reaction

"It seems to think I'm stupid" sounds emotional, but it is in fact a very accurate UX diagnosis. An interface communicates wordlessly. A permanent "let me summarize this for you" at the top of the inbox means the designer baked in a default assumption: "the average user does not, or cannot, read the original."

A good tool assumes the user is capable. A bad tool assumes the user is incapable, and scatters that assumption all over the screen.

There is a reason this emotional reaction cannot be dismissed. People stay loyal to tools that respect them and leave tools that condescend to them. Independent of how good a feature is, "how it treats me" decides long-term use. No matter how accurate an AI summary is, if it arrives with the message "you can't read this," the user remembers the insult, not the accuracy.

Respect, it turns out, is something you can design. An interface that collapses advanced options by default and hands control back cleanly says, "I trust that you know what you are doing." An interface that smears summaries, rewrites, and generated drafts across your face says, "I decided for you, because you probably can't." Users may not be able to articulate these subtexts, but they feel them reliably. Over time the first accumulates loyalty and the second accumulates resentment - and resentment, on the day switching cost finally drops, cashes out as churn.

3. Opt-In vs. Opt-Out - A Small but Decisive Difference

The most frequently ignored yet most important distinction in product design is opt-in versus opt-out.

Model Default state What the user does The message it sends
opt-in off turns it on if wanted "The choice is yours"
opt-out on turns it off if disliked (must find the path) "Use it first; work to turn it off"
forced (no opt) on cannot turn it off "We won't ask your opinion"

The greatest sin of forced AI is the third row - removing the choice to turn it off at all. Even the second-best, opt-out, becomes indistinguishable from forced when the off path is hidden deep. Good products pair powerful features with a clear off switch. The freedom to turn it off is what builds trust when it is on.

4. Why the Reading Workflow Must Not Be Disrupted

Email is fundamentally a reading-centered tool. The user opens a message to take in information, judge it, and reply if needed. This flow is more delicate than it looks.

What happens when an AI summary inserts itself above the body? Attention anchors to whatever is seen first (anchoring). Read the summary first, and you read the original through a frame already formed. If the summary is accurate, fine - but if it dropped a nuance, the user may never see the missing nuance at all. The moment a summary becomes a replacement for reading rather than an aid to it, the tool stops delivering information and starts pre-processing and injecting it.

This resembles other flows where AI touches notifications and messages. The controversy over AI editing or summarizing notifications at the OS level is covered in the Apple/Google notification AI editing debate, and the common lesson is the same: take away the user's right to face the original directly, and convenience turns into control.

5. The Paradox of "Help Me Write" - The Trap of Offering Help

The writing-assist feature, often called "help me write," carries the same problem. Asking "shall I write it for you?" before you have even started writing looks like kindness on the surface, but underneath sits the assumption that "you will struggle to form your own sentences."

Of course, for formulaic replies, scheduling, or boilerplate notices, writing assists save time. The problem is when they are always present by default. If "write this with AI?" appears first every time to someone who wants to write directly, then writing - a basic human ability - is gradually recast as a "special effort." When a tool assumes incapacity instead of capacity, people over time actually use that ability less. The fatigue of having to talk to AI at all is something I also examined in Tired of Talking to AI.

6. The Light and Shadow of Injecting AI into Productivity Tools

For balance, let me clearly show the other side. The trend of embedding AI into productivity tools has a real upside.

  • Genuine time savings: Summarizing long threads, drafting repetitive replies, and automating search and triage create value people feel in practice.
  • Improved accessibility: For users who struggle to read long text, summaries are genuinely helpful. As long as it is not forced, for some people it is an essential feature.
  • Competitive pressure: When every rival ships AI, choosing to omit it can mean falling behind in the market.

But the shadow is just as real. Pressure to lift usage metrics easily leads to dark patterns that make features hard to turn off. Short-term metrics like AI-card impressions and clicks rise easily when you "force them into view." But behind those numbers trust gets shaved away, and some users quietly leave. The line between light and shadow comes down to one thing: does the user have the freedom to turn it off?

There is also a subtler cost that rarely shows up in any dashboard: the erosion of skill. When a tool insists on summarizing every message, the muscle of reading carefully atrophies. When it insists on drafting every reply, the habit of composing your own thoughts weakens. Over months, the convenience that felt like a gift starts to feel like a dependency. A well-designed AI feature should leave the user more capable over time, not less; a forced one tends to do the opposite, because it never gives the user the chance to decide when assistance is actually warranted. The healthiest relationship between human and tool is one where the human grows, and growth requires the freedom to sometimes do the hard thing yourself.

7. User Choice and Trust - An Invisible Asset

Trust is a product's most expensive asset and one that never appears on a balance sheet. Forced AI slowly eats this asset away. A single forced impression looks trivial, but repeated daily it hardens into the perception that "this company does not ask my opinion."

This perception is especially dangerous in tools with high switching costs like email. People cannot easily change their email address, so they linger even when dissatisfied. Product teams easily mistake these "enduring users" for satisfied ones. But staying because you are locked in by switching cost is entirely different from staying because you are satisfied. The moment switching cost drops even slightly, accumulated dissatisfaction erupts as churn all at once. Trust is lost quietly, but the consequence is sudden.

8. Alternatives - Where the Leavers Go

"So where do you go?" is the practical question. The options a user fleeing forced AI tends to consider look roughly like this.

  • Privacy-focused mail services: Services that either omit AI features entirely or enable them only by explicit choice. Their data-handling policies also tend to be transparent.
  • Standalone mail clients: A desktop or mobile app that speaks standard IMAP/SMTP lets you keep your existing address while changing only the "window" you view through. Control of the interface returns to the user.
  • Services controllable via settings: Services that include AI but provide a clear global off. They give you a reason to stay rather than leave.

Let me re-emphasize the key criterion. The question for a good choice is not "does this tool have AI" but "can I turn this tool's AI off." The mature user of 2026 judges tools by control over AI, not by the mere presence of AI.

9. The Pattern Repeats - Why This Is Bigger Than Email

It would be a mistake to read this as a story only about one mail provider. The same pattern is spreading across the entire category of productivity software, and recognizing the pattern matters more than any single example.

Consider how it tends to unfold. A team ships an AI feature behind a flag, measures engagement, and sees the numbers climb. Encouraged, they promote it from optional to default. Then, because a buried toggle "hurts adoption," the off switch quietly migrates deeper into settings, or disappears for certain account tiers. Each step is locally rational - every product manager can defend their own decision - yet the cumulative result is a tool that no longer asks. This is how forced AI emerges: not from a single villainous choice, but from a chain of metric-driven optimizations that never stop to ask whether the user wanted any of it.

The document editor that rewrites your sentence before you finish it, the spreadsheet that auto-suggests a formula you did not request, the chat app that drafts your reply - each is the same move in a different costume. The common thread is that the AI assumes the initiative. It acts first and asks permission later, if at all. And once initiative shifts from the user to the tool, the relationship has quietly inverted: you are no longer operating the software; the software is operating on you.

This is why the email anecdote deserves more than a shrug. It is an early, legible symptom of a design philosophy that, left unchecked, will define how an entire generation of software treats the people who use it. The question "can I turn it off" is therefore not pedantic. It is the single clearest test of whether a product still regards you as its operator or merely as a surface to optimize.

10. A Korean User's Perspective - The Specifics of the Mail and Messaging Landscape

Mapping this onto the Korean context adds a few textures.

  • The weight of work email: In Korea, formality matters heavily in official communication. If AI arbitrarily summarizes or recommends a tone-shifted draft, the subtlety of honorifics and formal expressions is at high risk of breaking. The Korean honorific system is exactly where summary and generation AI most often slips.
  • A messenger-centered culture: Because daily talk happens on messengers while official, on-record matters go through mail, AI that enters email is received especially sensitively when it shakes "the accuracy of the record."
  • Platform lock-in: Many are tied to a specific global or domestic mail provider, so switching costs are high. That makes the value of "the freedom to turn it off" all the more acute.

In short, for Korean users forced AI is more than an annoyance - it arrives as a threat to the practical values of formality and accuracy.

11. Lessons for Product Teams - How to Help Without Forcing

So what should the builders learn? Not to strip AI out. To design it so that it is an invitation, not a command.

  1. Keep defaults conservative: The more a feature occupies the core screen, the more carefully you should set its default. When in doubt, start with opt-in.
  2. Make the off switch a first-class citizen: Do not bury the off path; place it clearly at the top of settings. The fact that it can be turned off is what builds trust when it is on.
  3. Never hide the original: Summaries and generated text belong in an assistive position that does not replace the source. Always guarantee the user's right to see the original directly.
  4. Distrust your metrics: Do not look only at short-term metrics like AI-card impressions and clicks; track long-term signals like trust, churn, and cancellation alongside them.
  5. Assume the user is capable: Set the interface's baseline premise to "the user can do this." Help should be an option layered on top of that.

The common denominator of these five principles is ultimately handing control back to the user. AI is a powerful ingredient, but the more powerful it is, the more you must put the steering wheel in the user's hands.

12. Conclusion - Between Help and Coercion

"Gmail thinks I'm stupid, so I left" is one person's expression of feeling, but inside it sits the most important question of 2026 product design: does AI help the user, or try to replace the user? The line between help and coercion lies not in the sophistication of the feature but in the user's control.

In the age of forced AI, what the user can do is clear: do not stay with a tool you cannot turn off; signal the market by choosing tools you can. What product teams must do is just as clear: assume the user is capable, and ship a clear off switch alongside every powerful feature. If injecting AI is the trend, then making AI turn-off-able is maturity. In the end, the product that hands choice back to its users wins trust - quietly, but decisively.
