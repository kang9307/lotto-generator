Complete Guide to Copyright Basics — Protected Works, Fair Use, Infringement and Response

Rights that arise the moment you create — what is protected, what is allowed, and how to respond when your rights are violated

2026-06-26 Legal

Introduction: Why Everyone Needs to Understand Copyright

Writing a blog post, sharing a photo on social media, or adding background music to a YouTube video — each of these everyday actions falls squarely within the domain of copyright law. Use someone else's creative work without permission and you risk civil damages or criminal liability. Conversely, when your own writing, photos, or code is copied without consent, knowing the basics of copyright is the first step to asserting your rights effectively.

This guide covers, as of June 2026, the fundamentals of copyright: what it protects, when it arises, the types of rights involved, how long protection lasts, fair use and its limits, practical cautions for online images, fonts, and music, how to respond to infringement, and the fast-moving issue of AI-generated works. This article provides general information only and does not constitute legal advice. For any specific situation, consult a qualified legal professional.

1. What Is Copyright?

Copyright is the set of exclusive rights granted to the creator of an original work — a copyrightable work — that expresses human thought or emotion in a tangible form. The copyright holder can reproduce, distribute, publicly perform, broadcast, or transmit the work, and can authorize or prohibit others from doing so.

Copyright divides into two broad categories: moral rights, which protect the author's personal connection to the work, and economic rights, which protect the financial rewards that flow from the work.

1.1 Moral Rights

Moral rights (recognized widely outside the United States) bind the author personally to the work regardless of who owns the economic rights. They typically include:

  • Right of disclosure: deciding whether, when, and how a work is made public
  • Right of attribution (paternity): the right to be identified as the author and to object to false attribution
  • Right of integrity: the right to object to modifications that harm the author's honor or reputation

Moral rights are personal to the author. They generally cannot be transferred, and in many jurisdictions they survive even after the economic rights are sold or assigned.

1.2 Economic Rights

Economic rights give the copyright holder the power to authorize or prohibit specific uses of the work and to receive remuneration. These include the rights of reproduction, distribution, public performance, broadcasting, digital transmission, display, rental, and the creation of derivative works. Economic rights can be sold (assigned) or licensed, which is the foundation of copyright commerce — from streaming platforms to stock image marketplaces.

2. What Does Copyright Protect?

Copyright protects original works of authorship fixed in a tangible medium of expression. The two key requirements are originality (independent creation with at least a minimal degree of creativity) and fixation (the work must be captured in some permanent or stable form).

2.1 Types of Protected Works

Copyright law covers a wide range of creative output:

  • Literary works: novels, poems, essays, articles, speeches, scripts
  • Musical works: compositions and lyrics
  • Dramatic works: plays, screenplays, choreography
  • Artistic works: paintings, drawings, sculptures, applied art, calligraphy
  • Architectural works: buildings, architectural drawings, models
  • Photographic works: photographs and similar images
  • Audiovisual works: films, TV shows, animations, YouTube videos
  • Maps and technical drawings: diagrams, charts, blueprints
  • Computer programs: software source code and object code

2.2 What Copyright Does Not Protect

Several categories fall outside copyright protection. Ideas, concepts, principles, methods, and discoveries are not protected — only the specific expression of them. The idea of "a love story set in space" belongs to everyone; a specific novel that expresses that idea belongs to its author. Facts and data are likewise unprotected, though a creative selection and arrangement of facts may be. Many governments also exclude their own official documents — statutes, court decisions, government reports — from copyright protection.

3. When Does Copyright Arise? — Automatic Protection

Under the Berne Convention (1886), the international treaty governing copyright among 180+ member states, protection must be automatic. No registration, no copyright notice (©), no deposit, and no other formality is required. Copyright arises the instant an original work is created and fixed in tangible form.

This principle — often called the automatic protection principle or no-formalities rule — means that a blog post you publish today, a photo you take tomorrow, or code you write next week is protected by copyright law from the moment it is created, even without a © symbol.

That said, voluntary registration (with national copyright offices or equivalent bodies) is strongly advisable. In the United States, registration is a prerequisite to filing a copyright infringement lawsuit and enables statutory damages. In South Korea, registration creates a legal presumption of authorship and date of creation, making disputes far easier to resolve. Keep time-stamped drafts, cloud version histories, or other records of creation to support your claims if needed.

Copyright is not perpetual. When the term expires, the work enters the public domain — free for anyone to use.

5. Fair Use and Other Exceptions

Copyright does not prohibit all uses of protected works. Most legal systems provide exceptions that allow use without the rights holder's permission in defined circumstances.

5.1 Fair Use (United States)

The U.S. doctrine of fair use (17 U.S.C. § 107) is an open-ended, case-by-case balancing test weighing four factors:

  1. The purpose and character of the use (commercial vs. educational; transformative vs. reproductive)
  2. The nature of the copyrighted work (factual vs. creative)
  3. The amount and substantiality of the portion used
  4. The effect on the potential market for the original

No single factor is decisive. Commentary, criticism, parody, news reporting, research, and teaching are frequently — but not automatically — found to be fair use.

5.2 Fair Dealing (UK, Canada, Australia)

Many Commonwealth countries use fair dealing, a more enumerated but similarly purposive exception. Permitted purposes vary by country but typically include research, private study, criticism, review, and news reporting. Unlike fair use, purposes outside the specified list are generally not covered.

5.3 Quotation and Educational Use

Most Berne Convention signatories permit quotation of published works for purposes such as criticism or review, provided the quotation is compatible with fair practice, does not exceed what is justified, and attributes the source. Educational exceptions allow reproduction for classroom instruction, typically subject to compensation systems managed by collective licensing bodies.

5.4 General Fair Use Clause (South Korea)

South Korea introduced a general fair use provision in 2011 (Article 35-5 of the Copyright Act), modeled partly on the U.S. four-factor test. A use that does not conflict with a normal exploitation of the work and does not unreasonably prejudice the legitimate interests of the author may be permissible even if it falls outside specific enumerated exceptions.

6. Online Images, Fonts, and Music — Practical Cautions

Three areas account for most everyday copyright disputes: online images, fonts, and music.

6.1 Online Images

A common misconception is that images published online are free to use. They are not. Every photograph or illustration is protected from the moment of creation. To use an image legally:

  • Check whether it is in the public domain (term expired or donated) via sources like Wikimedia Commons
  • Use images licensed under Creative Commons (CC) — but always read the specific license conditions (attribution required? commercial use allowed? no derivatives?)
  • Use images from free stock platforms (Unsplash, Pixabay) — verify the platform's current license terms, as they change
  • Purchase a license from commercial stock agencies (Getty Images, Shutterstock)

Using Google Image Search results directly is risky even with the "Creative Commons" filter — always verify the license on the original source page.

6.2 Fonts

Font files are protected as software works. A font described as "free" may be free only for personal use, with commercial use requiring a paid license. Many trademark and logo disputes have arisen from unauthorized commercial use of fonts. Before embedding a font in a website, YouTube thumbnail, or printed material, check the font's End User License Agreement (EULA) for commercial and embedding rights.

6.3 Music and Sound Recordings

Music involves overlapping rights: the composition copyright (melody and lyrics, owned by composers/lyricists) and the sound recording copyright (the specific recorded performance, owned by the performer and/or record label). Using a commercial track as background music for a YouTube video — even briefly — can trigger Content ID claims, ad revenue redirection, or takedowns. Legal options include: platform-provided free music libraries, music licensed through performing rights organizations, or tracks released under permissive CC licenses (e.g., Free Music Archive).

7. Copyright Infringement and How to Respond

7.1 Elements of Infringement

Civil copyright infringement generally requires proof of: (1) ownership of a valid copyright in the original work; (2) the defendant had access to the original; and (3) the allegedly infringing work is substantially similar to the protected expression. Independent creation of a similar work is a complete defense.

7.2 Civil Remedies

  • Cease-and-desist letter: Notify the infringer in writing, demand removal or cessation, and document everything.
  • Platform takedown: File a DMCA notice (U.S.) or equivalent to have infringing content removed from social media, YouTube, marketplaces, etc.
  • Civil lawsuit: Sue for injunctive relief (court order to stop) and monetary damages. In the U.S., registered works may claim statutory damages of up to $150,000 per willful infringement plus attorney fees.

7.3 Criminal Liability

Willful copyright infringement for commercial gain can be prosecuted as a criminal offense. In the United States, penalties can reach five years imprisonment and $250,000 in fines. Criminal copyright cases are handled by federal prosecutors and typically involve large-scale or commercial piracy operations, though individual infringers have also been prosecuted.

8. AI-Generated Works and Copyright — 2026 Status

The copyright status of content generated by AI tools is one of the most actively debated legal questions of 2026. The landscape is evolving and varies significantly by jurisdiction.

8.1 General Principle: Human Authorship Required

In virtually every jurisdiction that has addressed the question, copyright requires human authorship. Purely AI-generated content — images, text, code produced without meaningful human creative input — does not qualify for copyright protection. The U.S. Copyright Office reaffirmed this in its 2025 report. South Korea's Ministry of Culture likewise stated in 2023 guidelines (updated in 2025) that AI outputs absent human creative intervention cannot be registered.

8.2 Human Contribution Can Be Protected

Where a human makes creative selections, arrangements, or modifications to AI output, that human contribution may be protectable. The threshold is meaningful creative input, not merely clicking a "generate" button. An author who writes detailed prompts, selects among many outputs, and edits the result into a coherent work stands on stronger footing than one who copies raw AI output unchanged.

8.3 AI Training Data

Whether training AI models on copyrighted data constitutes infringement is actively litigated. A significant U.S. ruling in 2025 (Bartz v. Anthropic) held that training on lawfully acquired books was fair use, but that training on pirated materials was not. The U.S. Copyright Office's 2025 Part 3 report concluded that AI training is not categorically fair use and that using pirated datasets weighs against a fair use finding. South Korea is considering amendments to its Copyright Act to address AI training use.

8.4 Practical Guidance

When using AI-generated content commercially: (1) check the AI tool's terms of service regarding ownership of outputs; (2) avoid prompts that reproduce substantial portions of specific copyrighted works; (3) where possible, document your creative contributions to the final result; and (4) for high-value commercial uses, obtain legal review.

9. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Do I need a copyright notice (©) to be protected?

No. Under the Berne Convention's automatic protection principle, copyright arises without any notice, registration, or formality. The © symbol is a courtesy indicator, not a legal requirement. However, including a notice may deter infringement and strengthen your legal position in some jurisdictions.

Q2. If I credit the source, can I use copyrighted content freely?

Attribution is required for legal quotation but does not by itself authorize unlimited use. You must still satisfy all other requirements — the use must serve a permitted purpose, the amount used must be appropriate, and the use must not substitute for the original. Credit does not equal permission.

Q3. My blog is non-commercial. Does copyright still apply?

Yes. Copyright infringement occurs regardless of whether the infringing use is commercial. Non-commercial purpose may reduce the damages awarded and can be a factor in a fair use analysis, but it does not provide blanket immunity from liability.

Q4. Can I freely use old works?

Works in the public domain (copyright expired) can be used freely. However, a new translation, arrangement, or edited compilation of a public domain work creates a new copyright in the creative additions. Also note that a work may be in the public domain in one country but still protected in another due to different term lengths.

Q5. How do I prove I created a work first?

Register with a national copyright office where available. Maintain time-stamped drafts (cloud storage version history, email threads showing early versions, metadata in files). Keep creative process records. Formal registration is the most reliable evidence in litigation.

Conclusion

Copyright law touches every creative act in the digital age. Understanding that protection arises automatically at creation, knowing what kinds of works are protected, grasping the limits of fair use, and taking care with images, fonts, and music online will prevent the vast majority of everyday copyright problems. The AI and copyright space is moving fast — what is written here reflects the state of affairs as of June 2026 and will continue to evolve. For specific legal situations, consult a qualified attorney.
