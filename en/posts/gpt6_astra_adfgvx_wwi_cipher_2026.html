1. Background of the 1918 WWI ADFGVX Cipher and Unsolved Radiograms

Generally speaking, classical military ciphers combine substitution and transposition to complicate frequency analysis.

The German science blogging portal Scienceblogs.de has maintained a list of 50 unsolved ciphers. Included in this collection is a set of German radio messages from World War I encoded using the ADFGVX method. The original text notes that while experts such as George Lasry decoded hundreds of these messages, more than a dozen had eluded efforts to solve them.

On September 17, 2026, author prinz published a newsletter report stating that GPT-6 Astra solved one of these messages. The sources cited in the report are page 217 of J. Rives Childs's monograph The History and Principles of German Military Ciphers, 1914–1918 and the Scienceblogs.de list. The original text states: over a dozen have thus far eluded efforts to solve them, including (to my knowledge) this one, originally transmitted on November 27, 1918.

The original author added a specific qualification: I am not aware of this particular message having ever been decoded before, so sharing it here as a minor (but I think really cool) result. The confirmed facts regarding this message comprise the following:

  • The radiogram in question was originally transmitted on November 27, 1918.

2. GPT-6 Astra's Decoding Report — TRUPPENVERSCHIEBUNG Keyword and 19-Column Transposition Structure

The original report states that the model used was GPT-6 Astra. The original text does not state the prompt wording, tool usage, retry counts, or execution time.

The decoding procedure described in the text applies the keyword TRUPPENVERSCHIEBUNG, documented on pages 214–215 of Childs's book. Letters of the keyword are rearranged into alphabetical order to establish the column sequence. In the author's example, T is the 16th letter and R is the 13th letter.

The encrypted message consists of 170 characters. When written in rows of 19 under the keyword, it produces 8 rows of 19 symbols and 1 row of 18 symbols. In vertical columns, this corresponds to 18 columns of 9 symbols and 1 column of 8 symbols (column G). Symbols are extracted from columns in alphabetical order.

The original post notes that because column T is 16th alphabetically, it is preceded by 14 nine-symbol columns and 1 eight-symbol column (9×14 + 1×8 = 134), corresponding to the 135th symbol in the message, A. Column R is 13th alphabetically, preceded by 11 nine-symbol columns and 1 eight-symbol column (11×9 + 1×8 = 107), corresponding to the 108th symbol, V. The resulting pair AV is mapped via the substitution square to the plaintext letter E.

3. The Reconstructed Plaintext and the Uncertain Date Digit

The German plaintext recovered by GPT-6 Astra is recorded verbatim in the original report as: EIN ENGLISCHER KREUZER EINLIEG X SEWASTOPOL X S4STEN X EIN GESCHWADER DER X ALLIIERTEN FOLGT 26STEN X.

The English translation provided by author prinz is: AN ENGLISH CRUISER ARRIVED AT SEVASTOPOL ON THE ?4TH AN ALLIED SQUADRON FOLLOWS ON THE 26TH. One date digit is reported as uncertain, rendered as ?4TH.

In the German message, S4STEN represents an ordinal date ending in 4, with the tens digit remaining unconfirmed. The original text explicitly identifies this single date digit as uncertain.

The latter half of the transmission, EIN GESCHWADER DER X ALLIIERTEN FOLGT 26STEN X, states that an allied squadron follows on the 26th. The established characteristics of this plaintext include:

  • The German text reports the arrival of an English cruiser at Sevastopol and the subsequent arrival of an allied squadron.
  • Uppercase letters X serve as delimiters between clauses.
  • The cruiser arrival date is rendered as S4STEN, with the tens digit uncertain.
  • The allied squadron arrival date is transcribed as 26STEN (the 26th).

4. The 12-Day Gap — Key Activation Date Discrepancy and Unknown Reason

According to the original post, the message was transmitted on November 27, 1918. However, military records indicate that the keyword TRUPPENVERSCHIEBUNG was designated for use starting December 9, 1918.

The text quotes Astra's reasoning: Astra's hypothesis for why this particular message was previously unsolved is that 'TRUPPENVERSCHIEBUNG' was used as the key starting on December 9, 1918 - whereas, as noted above, this message was transmitted earlier, on November 27, 1918. A 12-day difference exists between the key date and transmission date.

Regarding this discrepancy, the original text explicitly records: The reason for this discrepancy is unknown. The author reports that the reason for the date mismatch is not known.

The facts surrounding this 12-day gap comprise the following:

  • The message transmission date is recorded as November 27, 1918.
  • The keyword TRUPPENVERSCHIEBUNG was designated for use starting December 9, 1918.
  • A 12-day temporal interval separates the transmission date from the designated key activation date.
