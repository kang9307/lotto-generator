Who Gets the Bill for Crawlers - What Kernel Git Hosting Actually Measured 2026

Fourteen of ninety cores running for the two percent of traffic that is real, and the order in which each defence stopped working

Sep 1, 2026 Dev & Infra

Anyone who has run a public git repository viewer knows the shape of this graph. From some day onward CPU usage never comes back down to the floor, the client addresses in the log are different every time, and the commit hashes being requested are not some specific change a person is curious about but simply all of them. On 29 August 2026, the person who runs git hosting for the Linux kernel project put numbers on that situation and published them.

The part worth reading is not the conclusion that crawlers are a nuisance. Everyone already knows that. The part worth reading is exactly which layer the cost landed on, and in what order the defences that were already in place stopped working. Both of those carry over to services many orders of magnitude smaller. The numbers shrink; the structure is the same.

The figures here come from the published measurements of one hosting operation and from a public report by the Wikimedia Foundation. Ratios vary enormously with architecture, so they cannot be transplanted onto your own site. Nothing here is an assessment of any particular company or service, and every defence described below has a price, so work out what you lose before switching any of them on.

The trigger - one day counted out in numbers

git.kernel.org receives about 6 million requests a day. What those requests want is random commit pages. That is a different animal from a person chasing a bug and opening one commit. It is a sweep across the address space with no order and no purpose.

Before anything reaches the back end, these requests hit a gate. Each connection is handed a small computation, and only the requests that come back with an answer are passed through. At that gate, 66 percent are batted away immediately. The remaining 33 percent actually solve the maths and come in. And by the operator's estimate, legitimate requests are about 2 percent of the whole.

Line the three numbers up and an uncomfortable conclusion follows. Even after 66 percent was turned away, the overwhelming majority of the 33 percent that got through is still bots. The defence reduced the volume without changing the character of it. That is the point this piece hangs on. The hard part of crawler defence is not standing up the first barrier. It is that barriers have short lifespans.

Two percent legitimate, one hundred percent of the bill

Translate the numbers into hardware and it looks like this. The service runs on 90 cores spread across five geo-distributed nodes. Of those, 14 to 16 cores are permanently occupied doing nothing but turning commits into HTML, and that output goes to crawlers. Twenty percent of total capacity.

It is worth checking the denominator on a percentage like that. Here, 20 percent is not the bots' share of total server load; it is the share of provisioned cores tied up rendering pages for crawlers. Measured as load, there are certainly short windows that spike far higher. Counting in cores has a different virtue. It is a number that converts straight into money. Cores are contracted and paid for up front, and fourteen of them are effectively being leased to crawlers.

There is a reason this stings especially on public infrastructure. In a commercial service, a rise in traffic has at least some path to revenue. On open source hosting there is no such path. The cost books at 100 percent and the revenue books at zero. Meanwhile, the parties putting that data into training runs never see the invoice.

  • 14 to 16 of 90 cores held continuously. That is not a peak reading; it means the state persists
  • Had those same cores been serving people, the 2 percent of legitimate requests would have had far more headroom
  • Scaling up does not help, because most of the new capacity goes to the crawlers too. The ratio does not move
  • So this is not a capacity problem. You either change what the requests are, or you reduce the requests

Why a git viewer in particular - where URLs explode

An ordinary blog scraped down to the last page still has a finite total. Three thousand posts means three thousand pages, and once they are taken, that is the end of it. A git web viewer has no such property. Its addresses grow by multiplication.

linux.git holds about 1.48 million commits. And the same host carries roughly 922 forks of that repository. Forks share the history of the original, so a single commit can be requested separately under each of 922 repository paths. In the original author's framing, that leaves several billion valid URLs available to scrape, and scraping all of them delivers the same content duplicated up to 922 times.

It helps not to misread the bot behaviour here. The bot is not knowingly fetching duplicates. Crawlers mostly run on a rule as simple as follow the links you find. Under that rule, this URL space looks like an endless pile of fresh pages. On the server side, it means re-rendering the same commit more than nine hundred times.

The same trap shows up at small scale all the time. Product listings with filter combinations in the URL, calendars that take a date range as a query, boards where the sort option rides in the address are all the same family. The number of pages is finite while the number of addresses is not.

  • 1.48 million commits multiplied by 922 forks gives an address space with no practical end. Storage does not grow; render requests explode
  • Sort, filter and page parameters in the query string reproduce the same explosion in miniature
  • Calendar and archive screens are prone to generating infinitely many valid addresses reaching into the future. It is an old and well-known trap
  • The first move is not blocking but cutting the number of addresses that point at the same content. Canonical URLs and link hygiene live here
  • That only works on crawlers that behave, though. For the rest you need another layer

Proof of work held, and then it did not

The idea behind a proof-of-work gate is simple. Before a page is served, the browser must complete a set computation. For one person reading a handful of pages that cost is negligible; for something scraping millions of pages a day it multiplies into something real. The point is to build an asymmetry. Anubis, the best-known implementation of this pattern, describes itself as a utility that weighs the soul of your connection using one or more challenges in order to protect upstream resources from scraper bots.

And at first it worked. Then, over time, the bots started solving difficulty 4. The difficulty went to 5. Not long after, the bots started solving difficulty 5 as well. That progression is not bad luck; it is economics. The parties collecting training data are precisely the ones sitting on large amounts of compute. A defence that demands computation is weakest against an adversary for whom computation is cheap.

One thing is worth stating plainly. The fact that 66 percent got turned away does not mean the defence is pointless. That 66 percent genuinely never had to be rendered. The problem is that the remaining 33 percent is already enormous in absolute terms, and that when you try to shrink it by raising the difficulty, the cost lands somewhere you did not intend.

User-Agent based blockingBots identified themselvesThey started posing as ordinary browsers
robots.txtEnough when everyone in play observed the conventionNo enforcement. Ignoring it costs nothing

The compute bill also comes out of the visitor's pocket

The real limit of a proof-of-work defence is not that it gets broken. It is that the price is charged to both sides. Difficulty 5 takes several seconds on a mobile device, and the phone gets uncomfortably warm while it grinds through the numbers. That is not a figure of speech; it is battery and heat, paid in physics.

What matters more is that this cost is distributed unevenly. A visitor on a recent high-end handset sails through without noticing the gate exists. A visitor on an old budget device waits several seconds per page. A decision to raise the defence level is, in practice, a decision about which class of visitor this site becomes worse for.

Clients that do not execute JavaScript get caught here too: command line tools, scripts, assistive technology, and archival crawlers. The people who build these tools do not present them as gentle measures either. The Anubis documentation says outright that the approach is a bit of a nuclear response and that it may inhibit good bots such as the Internet Archive along with smaller scrapers. In other words, know what you are switching on.

  • Gate difficulty raises the bots' cost and the visitors' cost with a single lever. You cannot tune them separately, and that is the structural weakness
  • Compute is cheap for the bot operator and battery is expensive for the visitor. That asymmetry runs against the defender
  • Legitimate non-JavaScript clients get cut alongside. This is a common cause of internal scripts or monitoring suddenly failing
  • Search indexing and archival collection can be severed at the same time. For a public documentation site that loss may exceed the benefit
  • Which is why a gate belongs in the category of last resort rather than default. Reduce what can be reduced earlier in the chain first

Where IP blocking falls apart

The basic form of blocking is to identify a source and shut it out. For that premise to hold, the source has to stay in one place long enough. The collection pattern in play now breaks that premise head-on.

The kernel write-up puts it exactly. Such an address would make four or five requests and then never show up in the logs again. By the time you have concluded that an address is a bot, it is already done with you. Adding it to a blocklist means blocking something that will not return.

The state this creates is what makes observation so difficult. The aggregate is out of control while every individual source is quiet. Requests per IP, requests per second, pages per session — none of them produce an outlier no matter how long you stare. The rules are written per source and the load is not organised per source.

Where the addresses come from compounds the problem. The original post noted that as long as app makers keep looking for ways to turn a profit, they will keep turning household appliances into attack vectors. When requests genuinely originate on residential lines, range-level blocking cuts real users along with the rest. It is not that blocking rules got less precise; it is that the raw material for precision is gone.

  • Four to five requests per address. However low you push the threshold, real visitors trip it first
  • When aggregate and per-source metrics disagree, rewrite the metrics around paths rather than sources. Which URL patterns were called, and how often, is far more useful
  • Range-level blocks can sever residential lines. On a public service the collateral damage is hard to see, which makes it more dangerous
  • Geographic blocking is blunt for the same reason. A proxy exit can be placed anywhere

From bots that announced themselves to bots that hide

Early on, this problem was far easier. As the original post puts it, the bots helpfully told you who they were via their user-agent. You read the string and acted on it. Then the bots wised up and started pretending they were random vanilla browsers.

What that transition destroyed is more than one useful tool. It changed the nature of the signal. The user-agent of the earlier era was a product of cooperation; the user-agent of today is a product of evasion. Rules built on an assumption of cooperation cannot be aimed at evasion. That is precisely where robots.txt now sits. Why that convention never became more than a promise is covered separately in robots.txt does not block AI crawlers, so this piece does not go further into it.

Even so, the user-agent is not worth discarding entirely. The signal is weak as grounds for blocking and still useful as grounds for classification. What an empty or malformed string does and does not tell you is set out in is an empty user-agent a bot.

One more thing deserves noting from this case. The kernel post says that cgit, the software in use, was designed for the times when the Internet was for humans or crawlers who obeyed robots.txt. The tool did not get worse; its premise disappeared. A great deal of older web software is in precisely that condition.

The same bill arrives at other public infrastructure

If this were peculiar to one kernel host, the story would end here. Evidence from elsewhere says it is not. A report the Wikimedia Foundation published in April 2025 is one such piece.

According to that report, the bandwidth used for downloading multimedia content has grown 50 percent since January 2024. And at least 65 percent of the resource-hungry traffic to the site comes from bots, while bots account for about 35 percent of overall pageviews. Putting those two figures side by side is the heart of the report. Bots select the expensive requests.

The cache architecture explains why. Human reading concentrates on popular articles, and those articles are already sitting in the edge cache. Bot reading spreads out without regard to popularity. That means constantly requesting items the cache does not hold, and those requests travel all the way back to the origin. Even at one pageview each, the cost is several times higher.

One sentence from that report also summarises this entire article: the content is free, the infrastructure is not.

What they shareIn both cases the party publishing the data for free carries the entire cost

Why caching does not end this

Raise this and the first response is usually that a cache would fix it. Half right. A cache pays off when the same thing is requested many times. The trouble is that the requests arriving here have exactly the opposite character.

A sweep across random commits is, by definition, a different address every time. Walking 1.48 million commits in no particular order keeps the hit rate low. Suggest making the cache arbitrarily large and storage and invalidation costs rise in its place. And holding an address space multiplied by 922 forks in cache in its entirety was never on the table.

A distinction is worth drawing here. The cost a crawler creates is not of one kind. It differs by layer, and so does the remedy that actually works.

Layer carrying costWhat the crawler inducesWhy the usual remedy underperforms
Network bandwidthRepeated transfer of large files and imagesEdge caches are tuned for popular assets. Bots sweep the unpopular ones
Application CPUPages rendered fresh every timeAddresses differ each time, so the hit rate never climbs
Storage and databaseDisk seeks driven by random accessNo locality in the access pattern, so read caches never fill usefully
The defence layer itselfChallenge verification and logging consume resources tooBlocking is not free. Grow the defence and the defence bill grows
Operations staffContinual rule tuning and false-positive handlingAutomate it and the judgement calls still belong to a person. This is the most underestimated cost

So who pays

Compress the structure into one line. Whoever published the data for free also pays the cost of it being taken. Being taken is not strange in itself; that is what publishing means. What is strange is that the manner of taking can burn as much of someone else's resource as it likes without appearing anywhere on the taker's ledger.

Which is why treating this as a question of manners does not resolve it. The parties that respond to manners are already honouring robots.txt. The parties generating this load have no incentive to reduce it. As the original post observed, as long as new outfits wanting training data appear daily, the pressure will not ease.

For an operator, that framing matters practically, because it means the problem belongs to design rather than negotiation. If you cannot make the other side follow the rules, the remaining option is to rebuild the service so that not following the rules costs you nothing. It is an unwelcome conclusion, but it is the one that is actually left.

  • The cost model of public data was designed around a headcount of humans. Automated collection sits outside that assumption
  • On donation-funded infrastructure, this excess directly subtracts from the budget for the actual mission
  • A private service has pricing tiers or access keys to push the cost back. Public infrastructure has no such mechanism
  • That leaves two levers only. Lower the unit cost of expensive requests, or take expensive requests out of anonymous access.

Defending while staying open

This is the direction the kernel side actually chose. Not a full block, but reducing the number of crawlable URLs and gating the actions that are expensive to run. They stated the price alongside it: some functionality is lost, at least when accessing resources anonymously. And they wrote that the data itself will still be offered for download to anyone who asks, with the caveat that there may be more hoops to jump through.

What makes this compromise instructive is less the outcome than what they decided to protect. They protected access to the data and gave up convenience in the web interface. They could have decided the other way. Moving everything behind a login would certainly reduce crawlers, but it would also change what a public repository is.

Laying the options out makes it easier to work out where your own service should stand. The key point is that no column is free.

What a small site can do, in order

A ninety-core story does not transplant onto a personal blog. The diagnostic order does. Start by finding out whether distributed collection is happening on your site at all. Pulling the distribution of requests per IP out of the access log settles it immediately.

# count requests per IP and keep the result
awk '{print $1}' access.log | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn > /tmp/ip_counts

# how many addresses came once or twice and vanished (distributed collection)
awk '$1 <= 5' /tmp/ip_counts | wc -l

# how many addresses arrive in bulk (where classic blocking still works)
awk '$1 >  5' /tmp/ip_counts | wc -l

If the first number dwarfs the second, IP blocking is not the answer. At that point you have to look at paths rather than sources: count which URL patterns were called how often, and which of those are expensive for the server. A longer treatment of what to read in the logs and what to conclude from it is in a year of fighting scrapers.

  • Shrink the address space first. Check whether sort, filter or session values in the URL are producing many addresses for the same content
  • Write down the expensive screens. They are usually the top entries by response time, and crawlers hit exactly that list
  • Find the infinite address generators. Calendars, endless pagination and arbitrary date range queries are the classics
  • Make static whatever can be static. When render cost approaches zero, being scraped stops hurting. This is the most reliable fix
  • Block last. Add it after the four steps above still leave you short. Invert the order and you will spend all your time on false positives
  • If you do turn on a gate, check what gets cut with it. Search indexing, archives, RSS readers and internal scripts are the candidates
  • Make it switchable. A defence that is hard to roll back does far more damage when it catches the wrong thing

Wrapping up

Three numbers give the shape of it. Six million random commit requests a day, of which roughly 2 percent are real, and 14 to 16 of 90 cores turning continuously to serve the rest. This is not a story about traffic growing. It is a story about a new line item in the budget.

The lesson on the defence side is not which measure is best but that every measure has a lifespan. User-agent filtering ended when bots started dressing up, IP blocking ended in front of proxies used four or five times and discarded, and proof of work thinned out as difficulty 4 and then 5 fell in turn. It is safer to assume the next measure will trace the same curve.

So the workable posture is a single one. Stop aiming to keep them out, and aim instead to make their arrival not hurt. Shrink the address space, take the expensive screens out of anonymous access, make static what can be made static. The kernel side writing that the data stays available reflects the same judgement. Rather than giving up on being open, lower the unit price of being open.

What I could not verify

  • The original post does not enumerate exactly which cgit features or paths were turned off. What is confirmed is the direction — reducing crawlable URLs and gating expensive actions — and the statement that some functionality is lost when accessing anonymously
  • Whether anonymous repository cloning stays open is not confirmed in those words. What the post contains is a commitment to keep offering the data for download to anyone who asks, with a caveat about more hoops. The specific access method is not described
  • The figure of about 2 percent legitimate traffic is the operator's estimate. No explanation is given of the criteria used to separate legitimate from not
  • It is not stated over what period the 14 to 16 core figure is averaged. Instantaneous peaks may be higher
  • The several-billion URL space is understandable as commits multiplied by forks, but the exact derivation does not appear in the original
  • There is no way to confirm which company or service these crawlers belong to. Spoofed user-agents and residential proxies make attribution impossible, and this article does not attempt it
  • The Wikimedia figures are as of April 2025. Anything that changed since is not reflected
