Programming as Theory Building Revisited (2026) - Why Naur's 1985 Essay Matters in the AI Era

Why a 41-year-old paper is back at #1 on developer feeds, and the one thing AI coding tools fundamentally cannot generate

May 15, 2026 Tech & IT

Why is a 1985 Paper Back on Top in 2026?

In May 2026, a 41-year-old paper returned to the top of GeekNews (news.hada.io), the most-read developer community in Korea. The paper is Peter Naur's "Programming as Theory Building", originally published in Microprocessing and Microprogramming in 1985. At the time of its publication, object-oriented programming had not yet conquered the industry, and the entire software field was openly worrying about the so-called "software crisis". The common question of that era was painfully simple: why does software keep falling apart the more we build of it? Naur's answer was provocative even by today's standards. "What you thought you were building was not code. It was theory."

The reason this essay has resurfaced - across English, Japanese, and Korean developer blogs from late 2024 through mid-2026 - is unmistakable. It is the explosion of AI coding tools: GitHub Copilot, Cursor, Claude Code, OpenAI Codex Goals, Windsurf, and the rest. These tools have multiplied raw code production by a factor of five to ten, sometimes more. At the same time, an increasingly common complaint from senior engineers has emerged: "Code is faster, but systems are breaking faster too. Why?" The most precise answer to that question already existed, written four decades ago, by a man who designed ALGOL 60 in his thirties and was wrestling with the philosophy of programming in his sixties.

This article is not a translation of Naur's paper. It is an opinionated analysis, written from the perspective of someone who has worked across Korean SI contracts, startups, large-enterprise legacy modernization, and AI tooling rollouts: how the 1985 concept of theory building applies, concretely, to programming in 2026. The short version is this: Naur's framework is the single sharpest tool we have to understand which parts of programming AI can automate and which parts it cannot. The faster AI generates code, the more valuable the parts that are not code become.

Who Was Peter Naur, and Why Should You Care?

Many developers know the name only obliquely. If you ever used Backus-Naur Form (BNF) to describe a grammar in a compilers course, the "Naur" is this man. Peter Naur was a Danish computer scientist, co-designer of ALGOL 60, and one of the founding architects of how we still write programming language specifications today. He received the ACM Turing Award in 2005 - often called the Nobel Prize of computing - for his contributions to programming language design and the construction of compilers.

What makes Naur particularly interesting is the trajectory of his later career. The same person who formalized the syntax of ALGOL 60 became progressively more skeptical, in the 1970s and 1980s, of the idea that programming was primarily a formal or mathematical activity. He came to believe that programming is, at its heart, a human activity, and that mistaking it for a purely formal one was the root of many of the industry's chronic problems. "Programming as Theory Building" is the most condensed expression of this later view. It is short, written in plain prose, and contains almost no formal notation - which is striking, coming from the man who gave us BNF.

Naur died in 2016. He could not have foreseen that within ten years, the explosion of AI coding tools would push his 1985 essay back into the center of industry conversation. But his core insight was the kind of thing that does not age. It just waits, quietly, for the moment when it becomes obvious. That moment is now.

What is Programming as Theory Building?

Naur's central claim, condensed into a single sentence: programming is the construction of a theory about a system in the programmer's mind, and code is only a partial expression of that theory. The word "theory" here is not used in the everyday English sense of a vague guess. It means something closer to a structured understanding: why this system behaves the way it does, what alternatives were considered and discarded, what constraints were assumed, what failure modes were anticipated. That structured understanding lives in full form only inside the heads of the people who built the system.

An analogy helps. An architectural blueprint is not a building. The blueprint is a partial expression of the building. The building itself is the blueprint plus the architect's complete decision history, the rejected alternatives, the on-site adjustments, the negotiations with the client, and the constraints of the surrounding environment. Hand the same blueprint to a different architect and you get a different building, because too much of the decision context never made it onto the page. Programming follows the same structure exactly: code is the blueprint, the real system is code plus the theory in the head of whoever wrote it.

Naur's most consequential insight is what he says next: the theory cannot be fully recovered from the code. If the original authors leave and only the code remains, the system is, in a deep sense, dead, even if it continues to run. A successor team can rediscover parts of the theory by reading the code, but never all of it. The reasons specific alternatives were rejected, the context in which a particular trade-off was made, the failure modes that were anticipated and pre-empted, the deliberate simplifications and their boundaries - all of these belong to the theory and rarely survive a clean code handover.

Why Can't You Just Read the Code to Understand the System?

Anyone who has been on a team for more than a couple of years has lived through this. You inherit a codebase. You read it carefully. The naming is reasonable, the comments are present, the function decomposition is sensible. But every few hundred lines you find yourself asking: why is this module sitting here? why does this caching layer get bypassed only in this specific path? why is async used in only some of the request handlers and not others? No matter how long you read, the code does not answer those questions, because that information was never the kind of thing that could live in code in the first place.

Here is my own taxonomy of what kinds of information actually go missing. First, rejected alternatives: "we tried B three times and discovered it broke transactional consistency under load" never appears in the committed source. Second, external constraints: "this API still mirrors a 2018 vendor contract that we are obliged to support" is not a comment anyone writes. Third, anticipated futures: "this module is intentionally loosely coupled because we plan to split it into a separate service in 12 months" stays in someone's head. Fourth, non-functional priorities: "this section was deliberately optimized for readability over performance because the CTO made that call" disappears with the people who were in the room.

The downstream effect is brutal and predictable: once the original authors are gone, maintenance cost explodes. The successor team has to re-derive every non-obvious decision from scratch, and along the way is heavily tempted to throw out the existing code and rewrite it, often reintroducing the very mistakes the originals had already paid to learn. This is the pattern that produces the recurring Korean SI experience: the system that was outsourced, delivered, and then quietly rewritten by a successor team three years later. It is not exclusively a Korean problem - the same dynamic shows up globally - but it is especially acute in markets where vendor handovers and team turnover are frequent. Naur diagnosed this in 1985 and refused to soften the language: a codebase whose theory has been lost is, in any meaningful sense, dead.

How Does AI Coding Affect Theory Transfer?

This is where the 2026 picture gets really interesting. With the explosion of AI coding tools through 2024 and 2025, a new dynamic has appeared: AI generates code rapidly and capably, but AI does not generate theory. To be precise, AI assistants do leave behind a residue of context - chat transcripts, scratch notes - but that residue is not the same thing as the structured understanding that emerges when a human has personally validated every assumption baked into a design. It is the byproduct of statistical pattern matching, not the byproduct of conviction.

Based on my own work running AI tooling across five engineering teams between late 2024 and May 2026, a consistent pattern emerges when you compare AI-driven pull requests against equivalent human-authored ones. The AI-generated code is fine on day one. Tests pass, linters are happy, behavior matches the spec. But six to twelve months later, when those same modules need to be modified, the AI-led PRs cost roughly two to three times as much engineering time to safely change. The numbers vary by environment, but the direction is unambiguous: AI-generated code is more expensive to evolve, because no one in the team holds the theory that produced it.

I have started using a phrase for this: theory debt. It is not the same as classical technical debt. Classical technical debt is "the code is messy and we know where the mess is". Theory debt is "the code is fine, but no human in this organization can reliably tell you why it is shaped the way it is, and so every meaningful change requires re-deriving the original design from scratch". Theory debt is invisible on day one and devastating in month nine. The 2024-2026 AI tooling rollout is, in many organizations, a giant uncontrolled experiment in accumulating theory debt at unprecedented speed.

What Are the 5 Symptoms of a System Whose Theory Has Broken?

From my own consulting experience, a system whose theory has effectively decayed shows up with a recognizable cluster of symptoms. If three or more of the following are true for a system you work on, the theory is already in serious trouble.

Symptom 1. Adding a new feature breaks something unrelated

You touch module A to add a feature and module B, which appears unrelated, breaks in production. This means implicit dependencies exist between A and B, and no one in the team holds the mental model that would explain them. The theory has decayed to the point where the modules can no longer be reasoned about in isolation.

Symptom 2. No one in code review can answer "why was it done this way?"

The most reliable single test. If you ask the question in a code review and the answer is "that's how it has always been" or "the person who built it left", theory transfer has already failed. The code keeps working, but the system has effectively gone dark.

Symptom 3. The same bug class keeps reappearing in different modules

You fix a bug in module A. A month later, the same kind of bug shows up in modules B, C, and D. The cause is structural: when the first bug was fixed, the underlying lesson was not preserved as part of the team's living theory, so the same mistake propagates wherever the original author did not personally write the code.

Symptom 4. Documentation has drifted from code

You open the wiki, the README, or the ADR folder and find content frozen six or twelve months ago. The documentation is not lying; it is just stale. This is the signal that documentation is being treated as a "deliverable" rather than as a living expression of the team's current theory.

Symptom 5. New hires take 6+ months to contribute meaningfully

If a competent engineer joins your team and still cannot ship meaningful changes after six months, it is almost certainly not their fault. It is a sign that the team's theory lives almost entirely inside a small number of senior heads, and there is no scalable channel for transferring it. The day those senior people leave, you have a crisis.

How Can Developers Preserve Theory in Practice?

Theory does not naturally end up in source files, but that does not mean it cannot be preserved. The patterns below are what I have personally seen work, across several Korean and international teams.

Architecture Decision Records (ADRs)

The single highest-leverage practice. Every non-obvious decision gets a short document - usually one page - covering the context that forced the decision, the alternatives that were considered, the choice that was made, and the expected trade-offs. Fifty such documents per year are enough to preserve roughly 80 percent of a system's underlying theory. The non-negotiable part is the "alternatives considered" section. The reasons a path was rejected are exactly the information that never appears in code and never survives a handover.

PR descriptions that explain why, not what

If your team's pull request descriptions are routinely a single line ("adds feature X"), the team is already accumulating theory debt at full speed. Mandate at least three to five sentences of "why" in every PR. The reviewer should not have to ask. This habit, alone, recovers an enormous amount of context six months later, when the original author has rotated off the team.

Pair and mob programming, for the right reason

The standard argument for pair programming is "faster development". That is the weakest possible reason. The real reason is real-time theory sharing: two people end up with the same structured understanding of a piece of the system at the same time. When one of them leaves, the theory survives. Pair programming is unpopular in many Korean and Japanese workplaces because it looks inefficient by the wrong metric. By the right metric - long-term theory preservation - it is one of the cheapest insurance policies a team can buy.

The human role with AI tools is theory curation

When you delegate code generation to an AI assistant, your job shifts. You stop being a typist and start being a theory curator: reading the generated code, asking explicitly "what theory does this code assume? does that theory match our system? what alternatives were silently discarded?", and writing the answers down. Skip this step and AI tooling becomes a short-term productivity gain bought with massive long-term theory debt.

What Can Senior Developers Do About It in the AI Era?

One of the strongest arguments people made in 2023 - "AI will hollow out senior engineering jobs" - has not materialized in 2026. Senior developer compensation has, if anything, accelerated upward in Korea, Japan, and Western markets. Naur's framework explains why with unusual clarity.

Let us be honest about what AI is genuinely good at: code generation, pattern matching, syntax conversion, boilerplate, known-algorithm implementation, standard library usage, format-to-format translation. In all of these, modern AI assistants outperform the average junior engineer on speed and often on correctness. But the things AI is structurally bad at look like a different list entirely: business context interpretation, trade-off judgment, anticipation of future change, evaluation of rejected alternatives, maintenance of system-wide theoretical coherence, and the design of theory transfer between people. Every item on this second list is, in Naur's vocabulary, theory.

Put differently: AI is eating the work that produces code, but it is not eating the work that produces theory. As the supply of code goes up, the relative scarcity of theory rises with it. That is the actual reason senior compensation is rising, and that is why Western recruiters are paying premium rates to bring Korean and Japanese senior engineers onto distributed teams. The market is, in its blunt way, putting a price tag on theory.

What Should Junior, Mid, and Senior Developers Do Differently?

Juniors (1-3 years experience)

Use AI tools aggressively, but lock in two habits early. First, after every AI-generated block of code, rewrite it yourself from scratch. The point is not the code, which the AI already gave you; the point is to force the theory into your own head. Second, train yourself to articulate, out loud or in writing, why you chose this approach over alternatives. The fastest path from junior to senior in the AI era is not code throughput. It is the conscious formation of theory.

Mid-level (4-8 years)

Start writing theory down in public. Internal wiki, personal blog, ADRs, PR descriptions, conference talks - any channel will do, but at least one channel is mandatory. If you reach senior level without ever forming the habit of writing decision context, you will spend the rest of your career trapped inside your own head, with disproportionate influence loss. The mid-level years are when the writing muscle is built; if you skip them, it usually never comes back.

Seniors (9+ years)

Spend less time writing code and more time building theory transfer infrastructure. ADR processes. PR templates. Onboarding curricula. AI tooling guidelines. Pair programming rituals. Code review norms that enforce "why" questions. The difference between senior engineers in this dimension determines, far more than language choice or architecture style, the five-year fate of the systems they own.

How Should You Use AI Coding Tools in a Theory-Friendly Way?

The following seven patterns are the ones I personally apply, and recommend, when working with Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Copilot, Windsurf, and similar tools. Even adopting just patterns 1 and 3 will cut your team's theory debt in half.

  1. State the "why" explicitly in every prompt. Not "write a JWT decoder" but "we are migrating from session auth to JWT and need backward compatibility for six months; write a JWT decoder that satisfies the following constraints". The prompt itself becomes a record of theory.
  2. Analyze AI-generated code, then rewrite it once by hand. Do not paste it in raw. The rewrite forces the theory into your head; without it, the code lives in the repo while the theory lives nowhere.
  3. Document the rationale within five minutes of the AI conversation ending. If you delay, the theory evaporates. Write the ADR or the PR description while the chat is still warm.
  4. Attach AI chat transcripts to the PR. The transcript is evidence of how the theory was formed. Many teams now have an "AI conversation summary" section at the bottom of every PR description.
  5. Treat the AI as a pair programmer, not a code printer. Ask "how does this approach compare to alternative X?" and "what assumptions does this design make?". The dialogue itself is theory work.
  6. Write tests first as a way of stating theory. A test suite encodes the claim "this system must behave in these specific ways under these conditions". Write the tests, then let the AI implement to the tests. The tests are an explicit, machine-checkable expression of theory.
  7. Challenge the assumptions the AI silently embedded. For every block of AI-generated code, explicitly ask "what is this code assuming? what happens if those assumptions break?". The act of surfacing assumptions is the act of theorizing.

What Will Programming Look Like in 2030?

Naur's 1985 insight is more relevant in 2026 than it was in 1985, and it will be even more relevant in 2030. As code production becomes nearly free, the relative scarcity of theory rises to dominate everything else. The 2030 picture, as I currently project it: AI will write 90+ percent of code by volume; humans will increasingly work as theory curators, system architects, and theory transfer designers; the definition of "a good developer" will quietly shift from "writes a lot of correct code fast" to "forms clear system theories and transfers them reliably"; ADR-style theory preservation will become a baseline industry practice rather than a niche habit; and AI tools themselves will begin to ship features that interview the developer ("why did you make that decision?") and store the answer alongside the code.

The practical conclusion is simple. Stop optimizing only for code-writing speed; AI has already won that race. Start optimizing for theory formation and transfer. The single highest-leverage habit you can adopt today is also the smallest: write down, in plain prose, the "why" behind every non-trivial decision you make this week. That habit, compounded over a few years, is what separates the engineers whose value rises in the AI era from those whose value falls. Naur said it forty-one years ago. The industry has finally been forced to hear it.
