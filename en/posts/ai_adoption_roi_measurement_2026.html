How to Measure AI Adoption Outcomes

The gap between perceived and measured productivity, and how proxy metrics quietly damage an organization

Jul 27, 2026 Finance & Economy

Introduction: the trouble with saying AI made us faster

By 2026 almost every organization has plugged AI into something. The difficulty starts immediately afterwards. When an executive asks how much better it got, the answer is usually some version of «the teams say it definitely feels faster» or «satisfaction scores are high». Both sentences describe perception, not measurement. And perception, it turns out, sometimes points in the exact opposite direction from what actually happened.

This article does not argue for or against adopting AI. It is about what to measure, and about why the things organizations habitually count push them in the wrong direction. It recommends no vendor and no consultancy, and it relies only on published research and surveys. Every figure carries the name of the body that produced it and the year it was produced, along with the limitations that body stated about its own work. That variation between studies is the starting point of the whole argument.

If you are interested in the causes of pilots that fail to produce results, there is a separate piece on why AI pilots fail. This article covers the step before that: how you decide whether something succeeded at all.

1. The moment perception and measurement diverged - METR, 2025

The most frequently cited counterexample in this debate is the randomized controlled trial published by the non-profit research organization METR on 10 July 2025. What makes it important is not that the result was negative, but that it measured perception and elapsed time on the same people at the same time.

1.1 Study design - real repositories, not toy problems

  • Participants: 16 experienced open-source developers, each working in a repository they had contributed to for years.
  • Tasks: 246 real issues, averaging roughly two hours each.
  • Repositories: mature projects averaging over 22,000 stars and more than one million lines of code.
  • Treatment: AI use was randomly allowed or forbidden per task. Tooling was mainly Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5 and 3.7 Sonnet.
  • Measurement: actual elapsed time to close the issue, taken from screen recordings rather than self-report.

Where many widely quoted experiments use small greenfield tasks, this one captured people who already understood the context working inside a large existing codebase. Since most enterprise work resembles the latter, the design is unusually relevant to practice.

1.2 The result - 19% slower, while believing they were 20% faster

Three numbers summarize it. Before the study, the developers expected AI to cut their task time by 24%. After the study, they still estimated AI had made them 20% faster. What was actually measured was 19% slower, with a confidence interval running from +2% to +39%. The direction holds across the interval, but the width is considerable.

The crucial figure here is the roughly 39-percentage-point gap between belief and measurement. It did not arise because developers lied. Working with AI makes waiting feel active, removes the cognitive cost of facing a blank editor, and puts something on screen immediately. All of that raises subjective satisfaction, which is a genuinely valuable outcome — but it sits on a different axis from elapsed time. The measurement collapses the moment those two axes are flattened into one number.

1.3 The limits METR stated, and its February 2026 admission

Anyone citing this study must carry its limitations along with it. METR explicitly said the result does not establish any of the following.

  • That AI fails to speed up most developers — a sample of 16 does not represent the wider population.
  • That AI provides no benefit outside software development.
  • That later model generations would produce the same result.
  • That optimized prompting or domain-specific training could not improve the outcome.

METR also noted that learning effects beyond about 50 hours of tool use could not be ruled out. Then, on 24 February 2026, METR published something more interesting still in a post about its follow-up experiment (57 participants, over 800 tasks). Recruitment had become harder because developers increasingly refused to work without AI at all, and between 30% and 50% of participants admitted they avoided submitting tasks they believed AI would help with substantially. METR wrote that it was «systematically missing developers who have the most optimistic expectations about AI's value», and stated that developers in early 2026 are likely more sped up than its early-2025 estimate implied.

The lesson here is not that AI makes people slower. It is that (1) perception is not a substitute for measurement, (2) selection bias seeps into the measurement design itself, and (3) even a research organization keeps narrowing the range within which its own result applies. That is exactly why another organization's number cannot be copied into your conclusion.

2. Yet the opposite result also exists - why studies disagree

Citing METR alone would produce a biased article. Well-designed experiments pointing the other way exist on the same question.

2.1 Comparing the major studies

Study (body, year)SampleHeadline resultWhat to watch for
Randomized controlled trial (METR, 2025) 16 experienced developers, 246 real issues Completion time 19% longer; self-estimate was 20% faster Small sample, mature repositories only. METR explicitly limits generalization
GitHub Copilot experiment (Peng et al., GitHub and Microsoft, 2023) 95 freelance developers, one task Task completed 55.8% faster (2h41m to 1h11m) A single greenfield JavaScript HTTP server task, no existing codebase context
Three enterprise field experiments (Cui, Demirer, Jaffe et al., Management Science) 4,867 developers at Microsoft, Accenture, and a Fortune 100 firm 26.08% more completed tasks (standard error 10.3pp) The metric is task count. Task size and quality are not measured
DORA report (Google and DORA, 2025) Roughly 5,000 technology professionals surveyed 90% use AI. Throughput rose, but instability rose too Survey correlation, not causation
State of AI (McKinsey, published November 2025) 1,993 respondents across 105 countries, surveyed June-July 2025 88% use AI. Only 39% report any enterprise EBIT impact, and most of those put it below 5% Self-reported. How financial impact is attributed varies by respondent
Business activity panel analysis (Korea Chamber of Commerce and Industry, 2025) Statistics Korea business activity survey, 2017-2023 Positive for revenue and value added, but the productivity effect is unclear Authors note this reflects an early diffusion stage

2.2 Three variables that explain the divergence

  • The nature of the task: well-specified greenfield work favours AI; deeply context-dependent edits to existing code do not. Much of the distance between 55.8% and 19% comes from here.
  • The skill level of the participants: several studies report larger gains among less experienced people. A senior engineer already knows their own codebase better than the model does.
  • Which metric was chosen: measure time and you can get a 19% deterioration; measure task counts and you can get a 26% improvement. The fact that changing the metric changes the conclusion is the core claim of this article.

For completeness, the widely quoted MIT Project NANDA report of July 2025 — the source of the claim that 95% of generative AI pilots produce no measurable profit-and-loss impact — was built from a review of over 300 publicly disclosed AI initiatives, 52 structured interviews, and 153 survey responses from senior leaders, gathered between January and June 2025. Critics have raised selection bias, a roughly six-month observation window (against the argument that enterprise programmes need nine to eighteen months), and data transparency. The number is striking, but quoting it without the sample and the window invites misunderstanding.

3. Separate outcome metrics from proxy metrics

The first place these conversations go wrong is the conflation of outcome with activity. An outcome metric is what the organization actually wants; a proxy metric is a value assumed to correlate with it. The problem is that proxies are generally easy to count while outcomes are hard to count.

CategoryExampleWhat it actually measuresHow it gets distorted
Proxy metrics
(activity)		 Lines of code, commit count Volume of characters typed Rises when duplicated or verbose code is added; falls when you improve things by deleting
Pull requests, tickets closed Number of work units Rises if work is sliced finer; rises if hard problems are deferred

Proxies are not inherently bad. As diagnostic aids they are useful: when lead time worsens, the distribution of pull request sizes helps narrow the cause. The damage begins the moment a proxy is promoted to a target.

4. Four dangerous proxy metrics - Goodhart's law in action

Goodhart's law originates with the British economist Charles Goodhart, who wrote in a 1975 paper on monetary policy that any observed statistical regularity tends to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes. The phrasing in common use today — «when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure» — was formulated by the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern in a 1997 paper on the misuse of accountability models in education. Here are the four metrics where that law bites hardest in AI measurement.

4.1 Lines of code - cannot tell an asset from a liability

GitClear's 2025 report, based on an analysis of 211 million changed lines of code, found that the frequency of duplicated code blocks rose eightfold in 2024. Copy-pasted lines went from 8.3% of changes in 2020 to 12.3% in 2024, a relative increase of roughly 48%, while refactored or moved code fell from about 25% of changed lines in 2021 to under 10% in 2024. In that dataset, 2024 was the first year in which copy-paste exceeded moved code. The share of new code revised within two weeks of its first commit also grew, from 3.1% in 2020 to 5.7% in 2024.

Under a line-count target, every one of those shifts reports as improvement. Meanwhile a team that removes duplication and shrinks the codebase registers as having gone backwards. The measurement punishes the improvement.

4.2 Pull request count - slice the work and the number grows without limit

Pull request and ticket counts depend entirely on how a team defines a unit. Split the same job into three pull requests and the metric triples. The 26.08% increase in completed tasks from the three-company field experiment cited above carries exactly this limitation. The experiments were well designed and the result is credible, but task size, difficulty, and quality are not inside that number. That caveat has to travel with the citation.

4.3 Response time - a fast answer is not a correct answer

When AI is attached to customer support or internal help desks, the number that improves first is time to first response. On its own it is meaningless without repeat contact rate, resolution rate, and escalation rate alongside it. If you answered in three minutes and the customer came back twice, total handling time went up. Make response time the KPI on its own and answer fast now, handle it properly later becomes the optimal strategy.

4.4 Tool usage rate - mistaking habit for outcome

«Our team's AI usage rate is 92%» says nothing about outcomes. In DORA's 2025 survey, 90% of technology professionals used AI, spending a median of two hours a day with it, and over 80% reported that AI improved their productivity. Yet in the same survey only 24% expressed high trust in AI (4% «a great deal» plus 20% «a lot»), while 30% trusted it «a little» (23%) or «not at all» (7%). That gap between usage and trust is itself evidence that usage is a poor proxy for outcome.

5. What to measure instead - flow, stability, and rework

The alternative is to use an already validated delivery-metric set and keep the definitions identical before and after adoption. There is no need to invent new metrics.

5.1 Five are enough

MetricDefinitionWhy it matters for AI adoptionMeasurement caution
Lead time Elapsed time from starting work to running in production Measures delivery of value, not typing speed. If the bottleneck is review or deployment, writing code faster changes nothing overall Read the median together with the 85th and 95th percentiles. Averages hide the tail
Change failure rate Share of production changes that cause an incident or rollback Shows whether speed came with breakage. DORA 2025 reported that AI adoption travels alongside higher instability Fix the definition of failure before adoption. Narrowing it later fakes improvement
Rework rate Unplanned deployments caused by production issues as a share of all deployments Added to DORA's metric set in the 2025 report. Directly captures how often output has to be touched again Unplanned deployments must be tagged automatically to be trustworthy
Review round trips Number of revision rounds before approval Distinguishes a real saving from time shifted onto reviewers, which can be a net loss for the organization Track load per reviewer. Concentration on one person distorts the metric
Recovery time after incidents Time from detection to restoration of normal service The more code you did not write yourself, the longer repair can take With few incidents, aggregate over a quarter or half-year

5.2 Numbers alone are not enough - read multiple dimensions

The core claim of the SPACE framework (Nicole Forsgren, Margaret-Anne Storey and colleagues, ACM Queue, February 2021, vol. 19 no. 1, pp. 20-48) is simple: productivity is multidimensional, and no single metric captures it. SPACE proposes five axes — Satisfaction and well-being, Performance, Activity, Communication and collaboration, and Efficiency and flow — and recommends reading at least two or three of them together.

Applied to AI measurement, that yields these working rules.

  • Collect both quantitative metrics (lead time, failure rate) and qualitative ones (subjective satisfaction, cognitive load).
  • But never add them together or compose them into a single score. Their pointing in different directions is the information.
  • As the METR case shows, the moment the two disagree is the most instructive one. Make the disagreement itself a reporting item.
  • If satisfaction rose while time stayed flat, evaluate that as retention and reduced burnout. Do not force it into a time-savings figure.

6. Practical measurement without an A/B test

Most organizations cannot run a randomized controlled trial. But comparing three months before against three months after will almost certainly produce a wrong answer.

6.1 Four traps in before-and-after comparison

  • Seasonality: quarter-end pushes, year-end code freezes, and summer holidays move the numbers by themselves. If adoption happens to land in a quiet period, it looks like improvement.
  • Staffing changes: if joiners, leavers, or a reorganization happened in the same window, the causes cannot be separated. You end up reporting the effect of two new senior engineers as an AI effect.
  • Product phase changes: greenfield development and stabilization have fundamentally different lead-time distributions.
  • Selection bias: teams that adopt first are usually the ones most open to change. Whether they perform well because of AI or because of who they already were is indistinguishable. Even METR ran into this in its follow-up.

6.2 Six mitigations

  1. Staged rollout: instead of enabling everyone at once, enable teams a few weeks apart. Teams not yet enabled become a natural comparison group — the closest practical substitute for randomization.
  2. Year-over-year comparison in parallel: compare against the same quarter last year as well as the previous quarter, to strip out seasonality.
  3. Stratify by work type: separate bug fixes, new features, refactoring, and documentation, and compare each. AI effects differ sharply by type and get buried in a blended average.
  4. Pre-registration: write down what you will look at and how you define success before you see the data. It is the cheapest available defence against picking the flattering metric afterwards.
  5. Report uncertainty: small samples produce wide intervals. METR's 19% carried a range of +2% to +39%. Do not report point estimates alone.
  6. Collect qualitative data alongside: only interviews and retrospectives explain why the numbers moved. Metrics never tell you what happened.
A realistic minimum design: four or more teams enabled in stages two weeks apart, three work-type categories, three metrics (lead time, change failure rate, rework rate), an eight-week observation window, and results reported with confidence intervals. Most organizations can execute this, and it is far less wrong than a simple before-and-after.

7. Counting the cost - tokens are only half of it

The most commonly omitted part of an AI measurement is the denominator. Many reports count only API charges and subscriptions. But AI output is not finished until a human has checked it. Leave review and rework time out and the savings are overstated.

Cost itemWhat it coversWhy it gets missed
Direct costToken charges, subscriptions, seat licencesIt is the only one that arrives as an invoice, so it is the only one counted
Review timeHuman time spent reading output and verifying facts and qualityBlends into existing working hours and is never booked separately
Rework timeTime spent fixing or regenerating output that was wrongFailed attempts frequently leave no trace in any log
Shifted review burdenColleague hours added as producer time falls and reviewer time risesThe cost moves onto someone else's calendar and becomes invisible
Incident responseRecovery, communication, and prevention work after a failureAttributing the AI contribution after the fact is difficult
Learning and upkeepEarly ramp-up, maintenance of prompts and rule files, internal guidanceTreated as one-off and never amortized
Operations and governanceLog retention, evaluation pipelines, security and privacy reviewBooked as platform-team cost and not allocated to the individual initiative

None of this needs to be measured precisely. A self-reported estimate over a single sample week is usually enough to change the arithmetic materially for most organizations. What matters is not precision but not omitting categories. And DORA's 2025 observation that throughput rises while instability rises with it points directly at where those hidden costs land. The DORA researchers' description of AI as an amplifier reflects the same idea: organizations with good practices get better, and weak practices hurt more visibly.

8. What not to measure - per-person AI usage

Any measurement effort eventually produces the suggestion to «see who is using it and how much». It is technically easy and the dashboard looks good. But the moment individual usage is tied to evaluation or comparison, the following happens.

  • Metric gaming: people invoke AI where it is not needed to push their count up. Goodhart's law starts operating immediately at the individual level.
  • Hidden failures: reporting a task where AI did not help risks being labelled someone who cannot use the tool, so it goes unreported. The organization loses its chance to learn where AI fails.
  • Forced use on unsuitable work: people push the tool into domains it does not fit, and quality incidents follow.
  • Reduced psychological safety: the sense of being monitored suppresses experimentation, which is exactly what a tool-adoption period needs most.
  • Legal and ethical exposure: collecting individual activity data can fall under privacy and workplace-monitoring rules. Scope, purpose, and retention should be reviewed in advance.

The alternative is to raise the unit of measurement from the individual to the team, work type, and value stream. If individual data is needed at all, keep it aggregated only and state explicitly that it exists to support people, not to evaluate them. What an organization genuinely needs to know is not which team uses the tool most, but which types of work it works on and which it does not.

9. A four-week measurement design - practical checklist

Without any elaborate programme, following this order alone prevents most bad reporting.

  1. Week 0 - fix the question: not «is AI good» but something answerable, such as «for which work types does lead time fall».
  2. Week 0 - pre-register the metrics: one primary, two secondary, and two guardrail metrics (change failure rate, rework rate), written down. No changes afterwards.
  3. Week 0 - fix the definitions: write out where lead time starts and ends, what counts as a failure, and how rework is judged. If this wobbles, everything after it is meaningless.
  4. Week 1 - establish a baseline: recompute at least four to eight weeks of pre-adoption history using the same definitions. Different definitions are not a comparison.
  5. Weeks 1-2 - stage the rollout: enable some teams first and keep the rest as a comparison group.
  6. Weeks 2-4 - collect qualitative data in parallel: in weekly retrospectives, gather cases where AI did not help specifically. Success stories collect themselves.
  7. Week 4 - sample the costs: for one week at least, have people record review and rework time.
  8. Reporting - no single number: present metrics separately, with confidence intervals. A headline like «23% productivity improvement» is almost always hiding something.
  9. Reporting - include the counterevidence: put the metrics that did not improve, and the ones that worsened, on the same slide. Showing only the good ones contaminates the next decision.
  10. Fix a re-measurement cadence: repeat quarterly with the same definitions. Models and tools keep changing, so one conclusion does not stay true for long.

Conclusion: measure small, measure often, never report a single number

One fact runs through all of this. Measuring the same thing yields opposite conclusions depending on what you choose to measure. Measure time and you get a 19% deterioration; measure completed tasks and you get a 26% improvement; ask people and you get 20% faster. All three are true, and none of them is your organization's conclusion.

The practical prescription is therefore modest. First, use outcome metrics and keep proxies for diagnosis only. Second, fix the definitions before adoption and do not change them. Third, count the cost all the way through — not just tokens, but review and rework. Fourth, measure work types, not individuals. Fifth, never report a single number. Sixth, re-measure every quarter.

In McKinsey's survey published in November 2025, 88% of respondents said they used AI, yet only 39% could point to any enterprise-level EBIT impact, and most of those placed it below 5%. The Korea Chamber of Commerce and Industry's 2025 analysis of the Statistics Korea business activity survey for 2017-2023 similarly concluded that AI adoption was positive for revenue and value added while the productivity effect remained unclear. A substantial part of that gap exists not because AI is useless, but because organizations are not measuring their own outcomes properly. Fixing the measurement is cheaper than buying another tool, and it usually changes more.
