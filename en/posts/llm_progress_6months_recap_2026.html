Why a six-month recap is the right granularity in 2026

Six months ago, in November 2025, the frontier of language models looked stable. At OpenAI the GPT-5 family was the default, Anthropic had shipped Claude Sonnet 4.5 on September 29, 2025, and Google's Gemini 2.x Flash models were the budget workhorse. The narrative was that scaling laws were softening and the curve had flattened. Six months later, almost every assumption from that paragraph is wrong. The November-to-May window is the busiest LLM release cycle since the original GPT-4 launch, and the speed has caught even full-time AI builders off guard. Many teams are still running 2025-era model assumptions in their stack and are likely leaving available quality, cost, or speed on the table.

This article is a recap of that period. It walks through every major lab and open-weight family, compresses the changes into matrices and tables, and ends with five concrete takeaways and a forward look at the second half of the year. The shape is deliberate: one section per major actor, then trends, then benchmarks, then advice. If you only have time for the matrices, go to the large tables. If you want the underlying story, read straight through. The text follows no lab's marketing tone; the yardstick is what changes a practitioner's decisions.

In the September 29, 2026 update, the model names and release dates in this article were re-checked against each company's official announcements and documentation. The text as published contained wrong dates and names that official announcements do not confirm (for example GPT-4.5o, Claude 4.7 Sonnet, and Llama 4 7B, 30B, and 200B); these have been corrected or marked as unverified. Because this article is a record of that period, releases after publication are collected separately in the section on what happened next.

Three framing notes before the actor-by-actor recap. First, model numbers are not progress measurements; some labs ship a "0.1" that is a bigger jump than another lab's "2.0". This article focuses on what actually changed, not just the version. Second, closed-weight and open-weight progress are now running on parallel curves, with the gap measured in weeks rather than months on some axes. Third, the most consequential change is not any single model release - it is the surrounding stack: agentic harnesses, coding agents, memory primitives, and the Model Context Protocol becoming a de facto standard. The article keeps returning to that point.

OpenAI: GPT-5.x, Goals, and the Operator track

OpenAI's period is dominated by two moves. The first is a continuous run of GPT-5 family updates. GPT-5 had already shipped before this window began, and during the window came GPT-5.x versions such as GPT-5.2 in December 2025 and GPT-5.5 on April 24, 2026 (available in the API, Codex, and ChatGPT). The text as published said "GPT-5 released at the end of January"; that does not match OpenAI's announcements and has been corrected. For developers, the biggest simplification was that the reasoning track previously split across the o-series merged into a single product that adjusts how much it thinks. The second move is Codex Goals, which turned the Codex sub-product from a chat completion into something closer to an autonomous coding agent that takes a high-level objective and works against a repository for hours.

The quieter changes matter for production. As published, this article summarized that the Operator stack went generally available in March with a stable computer-use API, that the Realtime API gained native tool use, and that memory persistence became a documented surface with explicit storage, retrieval, and forgetting primitives - turning "the assistant remembers me across sessions" from a one-quarter project into a one-week one. This paragraph and the staged Codex Goals timeline (late February through April) are statements from the time that could not be re-confirmed against OpenAI's official documentation on 2026-09-29.

Price amounts are not included, because they could not be re-confirmed against official price lists in this update. The text as published also said the 4o family was being deprecated on a timeline ending in Q3 2026; read that as a forecast from the time as well. For background on the Codex Goals shift specifically and how to wire it into your daily loop, our prior piece OpenAI Codex Goals Practical Guide 2026 goes deeper.

Anthropic: Claude Opus 4.5, 4.6, 4.7, and the year of Claude Code

Anthropic moved its Opus line three times in six months: Claude Opus 4.5 on November 24, 2025, Claude Opus 4.6 on February 5, 2026 (with Claude Sonnet 4.6 on February 17), and Claude Opus 4.7 on April 16, 2026. The text as published said "Claude 4.6 in late December 2025", "Claude 4.7 Sonnet in March 2026", and "Claude 4.7 Opus in May 2026"; those dates differ from the official announcements and have been corrected. Anthropic's list of Sonnet announcements has no 4.7 version (4.5, then 4.6, 5, and 5.5). In the official announcement, Opus 4.7 is a notable improvement on Opus 4.6 in advanced software engineering, with particular gains on the most difficult tasks, and it introduced a new xhigh effort level for finer control over the tradeoff between reasoning and latency. Each release was incremental in name, but cumulatively they cemented Anthropic's position in the "send the agent off for two hours and trust it" category.

Around the models, Claude Code matured into a full product. Skills, hooks, sub-agents, and a stable settings.json schema turned the CLI from a power-user toy into something teams actually deploy across a codebase. The Model Context Protocol that Anthropic kicked off in late 2024 began to be used as a de facto industry standard in this period, and as published this article noted that OpenAI, Google, and most major IDE vendors support MCP. By quietly winning the protocol layer, Anthropic put itself at the center of the developer-tools stack regardless of which model anyone happens to be calling.

The text as published named Andrej Karpathy's move to Anthropic in February 2026 as a story of the period. On 2026-09-29 no official announcement of this could be found in Anthropic's newsroom, so this article no longer states it as fact and has removed it from the matrix. For practical day-to-day use of Claude Code itself, our standing guide Claude Code Skills Complete Guide covers the patterns that survived through the 4.6 and 4.7 transitions.

Google: Gemini 3, 3.5 Flash, and the pricing war

Google's strategy in this period combined peak quality with price-performance pressure. The Gemini 2.5 family was already out during 2025, and the Gemini 3 Pro preview on November 18, 2025 put Google back at the center of the frontier conversation. It was followed by the Gemini 3 Flash preview on December 17 and the Gemini 3.1 Pro preview on February 19, 2026. The headline release of the period was Gemini 3.5 Flash, generally available on May 19, 2026, which Google introduced as its most intelligent model for sustained frontier performance on agentic and coding tasks. The text as published said "Gemini 2.5 in December", "Gemini 3.0 in February", and "Gemini 3.5 Flash in April"; those differ from the official Gemini API changelog and have been corrected, and the unverified figures "2-million-token context" and "about 60 percent lower output cost versus 2.0 Flash" have been removed.

AI Overviews in Google Search moved from "people argued whether it was a good idea" to a permanent part of the search experience. This is consequential beyond the model itself: AI-generated answers are now the layer between users and most of the open web, and content strategy in 2026 has to be written for that layer first. Inside Google's developer surface, the I/O 2026 announcements in mid-May previewed deeper Gemini integration into Workspace, Android, and the new Code Assist agent tier. None of this is shocking individually; the cumulative effect is that Google is back to being a default rather than a secondary option.

The price point matters most for teams running heavy automation. If you are building anything that calls an LLM more than a million times a month, this article's judgment is that most of the lower-stakes traffic belongs on Flash-class models, with frontier models reserved for the top slice where the marginal quality lift justifies the cost. That two-tier pattern is now the default architecture for serious LLM products, and we will return to it in the trends section.

Meta: Llama 4 and the open strategy

Meta's Llama 4 was announced on April 5, 2025, before this window. The released models are Llama 4 Scout (17B active and 109B total parameters, 16 experts, a 10-million-token context window) and Llama 4 Maverick (17B active and 400B total parameters, 128 experts), while Llama 4 Behemoth, with 288B active and nearly two trillion total parameters, was introduced as a preview still in training. The text as published described "Llama 4 7B, 30B, and 200B releases between December and April" and context "solid out to 256K"; Meta's official announcement does not confirm that lineup, so it has been corrected.

As published, this article interpreted Meta as betting that "good enough" open weights at common sizes, combined with falling self-hosting cost, would win a larger share of inference workload than chasing the absolute frontier. That is this article's interpretation and is not backed by an official Meta strategy statement from this period. Whether the bet pays out is a six-quarter question, not a six-month one.

The practical lesson is corrected as well. The text as published named "Llama 4 30B" as the size to know, but no such model exists in the official lineup. Both released Llama 4 models are MoE, so although the active parameters per token are small (17B), the full weights (109B or 400B) still have to fit in memory; whether single-GPU deployment works has to be worked out separately from the quantization scheme and memory capacity. If you need a 30B-class dense model, Qwen3-32B in the China section below is an officially confirmed option.

China's Big Three: DeepSeek, Qwen, Doubao

The China story in this period is the most surprising one for outside observers. In November 2025, DeepSeek's mainline was the generation before V4, and the DeepSeek-V4 preview arrived on April 24, 2026. It has two models, V4-Pro (1.6T total and 49B active parameters) and V4-Flash (284B total and 13B active parameters), both supporting a one-million-token context. The text as published said "V4 in February 2026", "671B MoE with roughly 37B active per token", and "the single most-deployed open model of Q1 2026"; these do not match DeepSeek's official announcement and have been corrected (V4 arrived in the second quarter). V4's license terms could not be confirmed in this update.

Alibaba's Qwen3 had already been released on April 29, 2025. It consists of the MoE models Qwen3-235B-A22B and Qwen3-30B-A3B and dense models from Qwen3-32B down to Qwen3-0.6B. The text as published said "Qwen 3 family, released in March 2026", which has been corrected; the model name "Qwen 3 Coder 32B", the claim that it is "the strongest small open coder", and the estimate of Alibaba's open-weight inference volume could not be confirmed from official sources and have been removed. ByteDance's Doubao was assessed at the time as important in the Chinese-speaking market for its native cross-modal capabilities, but the release timing the text gave, "Doubao 1.6 in April 2026", could not be confirmed from an official announcement. Whether you use these models or not depends heavily on your data-handling stance, but pretending they do not exist is no longer a defensible position. The full landscape is covered in our standing comparison DeepSeek vs Qwen vs Doubao - China LLM Comparison 2026.

One technical aside: at the time of publication, steering-vector research on DeepSeek V4 was assessed as opening a small but meaningful interpretability door. Our LLM Steering Vectors and DeepSeek V4 piece dives into the practical implications.

xAI: Grok 4, the underdog narrative

xAI's Grok 4 was announced on July 9, 2025, before this window. The text as published said "Grok 3 in late November 2025 was the first version inside frontier range" and "Grok 4 in April 2026"; these do not match the official announcement and have been corrected, and no official announcement of a Grok 4 successor during the window was checked in this update. This article's assessment at the time was that xAI had stopped being a meme but was still distributed mainly through X and its standalone consumer app, which kept it from being a default developer choice, and that the scale of its Colossus training cluster was showing up in the model.

For practitioners, the Grok family is worth considering as a backup model and as an option when you need real-time web grounding without building your own retrieval layer. The constraint is mostly cultural: many enterprises still treat xAI as too tied to X for procurement reasons. If your stack already calls a router that load-balances across providers, adding Grok as an option costs a few hours and gives you resilience against the next provider outage.

Mistral, Cohere, and the second-tier consolidation

The second tier of frontier labs moved too. Mistral Large 3 arrived on December 2, 2025 with the Mistral 3 announcement. It is a sparse MoE with 41B active and 675B total parameters, released under the Apache 2.0 license, alongside the Ministral 3 series of 3B, 8B, and 14B dense models. The text as published said "Mistral Large 3 in January", and the date has been corrected. Codestral 2 and Pixtral 2, and Cohere's Command R-Plus 2026 update, which the text also mentioned, could not be confirmed from official announcements in this update. The assessment at the time was that EU-resident infrastructure and regulatory fit are Mistral's strengths, while Cohere concentrates on enterprise RAG.

The observation at the time was that several smaller actors still in the conversation in late 2025 effectively dropped out. AI21's Jamba family became a niche choice for very long context, Inflection pivoted away from frontier consumer models, and Reka and a handful of others continue to ship technically interesting work but no longer compete at the top of the benchmarks. This article's judgment is that the market is narrowing to OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta (open), DeepSeek, Alibaba/Qwen, and xAI, with Mistral and Cohere as strong regional or vertical options.

Local LLM: Llama.cpp, Ollama, vLLM, MLX

The local and self-hosted ecosystem moved fast. Llama.cpp strengthened its Vulkan and ROCm paths, weakening the NVIDIA-only assumption. Running large models at usable speeds on Strix Halo PCs (AMD's high-memory APU platform) became a product category of its own. Ollama crossed into mainstream developer tooling with stable model registries and IDE integration. vLLM cut inference cost in production deployments through batching and speculative-decoding improvements, and Apple's MLX made M-series Macs a strong memory-per-dollar local inference option for the 30B-to-70B parameter band. The assessment at the time was that a recent Mac with ample unified memory can host a 30B-class model such as Qwen3-32B comfortably. The vLLM and MLX version numbers and device specs mentioned in this section could not be re-confirmed from official documentation in this update.

The combined effect is that a real share of low-stakes LLM traffic is leaving the API. Personal note-taking assistants, IDE autocompletion, summarization of team documents - tasks where data sensitivity, latency, or cost matters more than the absolute frontier - are moving to local inference. The full hardware picture is in Local LLM Hardware Selection Guide 2026.

AI coding tools: Cursor, Claude Code, Codex, Aider, Cline

AI coding tools were the single fastest-moving sub-category of the half-year. Cursor's agent mode matured into something that handles multi-file refactors reliably. Claude Code became the dominant CLI tool for serious autonomous coding workflows; as of publication, if you were running tasks that take 30 minutes or more, Claude Code with Claude Opus 4.7 was the default. Codex Goals via the OpenAI Codex sub-product is the strongest one-shot generation surface; for tight iteration loops where you want to see the model's output and tweak quickly, it is hard to beat.

Aider stayed strong as the open-source baseline, and Cline filled the VS Code-integrated agent niche for developers who want an in-editor experience without leaving the IDE. This article's assessment is that no single tool dominates every workflow. Cursor for in-editor pair programming, Claude Code for long autonomous tasks, Codex for one-shot generation, and Aider as the open fallback when you want a tool you can fully inspect. Our three-month comparison Claude Code vs Codex Three-Month Review 2026 walks through the rotation pattern in detail.

Six-month core trends

Stepping back from individual labs, four trends define the period.

Agentic AI moved from demo to production. The common feature of frontier model releases this half-year was longer, more reliable autonomous chains. Long autonomous coding sessions became realistic with Claude Opus 4.7, and multi-step research, where the model proposes, browses, summarizes, and iterates on its own plan, works with GPT-5.x and the Gemini 3 family. The remaining failure modes are real but no longer disqualifying for production use.

Reasoning models stopped being a separate product category. The o-series folded into the GPT-5 family's adjustable thinking, Claude moved to a mode where the model decides how much to think, and the Gemini 3 family uses internal reasoning by default. The "thinking model" toggle that confused users for the past year is largely gone, and that simplification is itself a quality improvement.

Multimodal became table stakes. Vision works in frontier models, audio in and out is standard, and video understanding is competent across the top labs. The differentiator is no longer "do you have multimodal" but "how good is your tool use stitched together with your multimodal inputs."

Smaller-better is real at the 30B class. Qwen3-32B, Qwen3-30B-A3B, and the smaller DeepSeek distills all sit in this range, and they handle a large share of tasks where you do not actually need frontier IQ. That this band is the biggest gainer by deployment frequency is this article's observation, not a measurement.

Benchmark shifts: MMLU saturated, SWE-bench surging

The benchmark landscape changed materially. As published, this article stated that MMLU is effectively saturated with frontier models bunched between 88 and 91, that SWE-bench top scores roughly doubled in six months (from 49 percent in November 2025 to above 90 percent on the Verified subset in May 2026), and that GPQA top scores moved from the low 60s to the high 70s. These figures are statements from the time that could not be re-confirmed against official leaderboards on 2026-09-29; if you want to cite them, check the original leaderboards such as SWE-bench directly. The ARC-AGI family is still regarded as the hardest standing public benchmark and is where the labs compete most visibly.

The lesson for engineers reading benchmarks: SWE-bench comes closest to being the most useful single number for "is this model good at coding", and the gap between MMLU performance and real-world utility is wide. A model can score high on MMLU and still fail on production debugging. Always pair benchmark numbers with task-specific evaluations on your own workload.

Five takeaways every developer should internalize

Compressed advice from the six-month picture.

  1. Rotate at least two coding tools. Claude Code plus Codex, or Claude Code plus Cursor, is the most resilient pattern. Their failure modes are different, and you will keep working when one provider has an outage.
  2. Set up a two-tier routing layer. Send 60 to 80 percent of traffic to Gemini Flash or Claude Haiku class, and reserve frontier models for the calls that actually need them. The ratio is this article's recommendation; adjust it to your own call distribution.
  3. Run at least one model locally. A 30B-class model on an M-series Mac or a workstation with a high-end GPU covers offline work and sensitive data, and gives you ground truth for "is this task even possible without the API."
  4. Treat MCP as default. The Model Context Protocol is close to a new "REST for AI." Before building separate bespoke integrations for ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini, check whether one MCP server can connect them.
  5. Re-evaluate your benchmarks quarterly. The model that won your November eval is probably second-tier today. Run your eval suite every three months at minimum, and pick the new default with eyes open.

What to expect in H2 2026 (as written in May)

What follows is the outlook as written in May 2026. It is a forecast from the time, not a verified prediction; the second-half releases that were actually confirmed are listed in the section on what happened next.

First, agentic systems become the default UI for power users (forecast at the time). The interface evolution resembles the one chatbots went through in 2023: experimental in Q1, mainstream in Q4. The expectation was that major labs would ship an agentic surface as part of the default product by year-end, not as an opt-in beta.

Second, model differentiation moves from raw IQ to memory, tool use, and price-per-task (forecast at the time). Competition shifts to the surrounding stack, and whichever lab gets memory right first was seen as likely to take a meaningful share of consumer mindshare.

Third, on-device and laptop-class inference quietly takes over a large share of low-stakes traffic (forecast at the time). With M-series Macs and Strix Halo PCs running 30 to 70 billion parameter models at usable speeds, a substantial share of consumer usage was expected to leave the API. This is bad for budget-tier API revenue and good for user privacy.

If you are planning a second-half roadmap, keep those three in mind, but skip the "wait for the next model" trap. The capabilities you need are already in the stack you can run today.

Conclusion: six months of structural shifts

The November-to-May window was not just another upgrade cycle. It was the period when agentic systems became real, when open weights closed the frontier gap on enough axes to matter, when prices dropped enough to change architecture patterns, and when MCP settled in as the standards layer tools are built on. Any one of those is a normal year's worth of progress. All four happening together is the structural shift.

The practical posture for builders is unchanged from earlier in the cycle: stay close to the tools, rotate at least two of them, evaluate your stack every quarter, and resist the temptation to wait for a perfect default to emerge. The frontier moves too fast for that to ever happen. A builder's competitive advantage is in how quickly the available pieces are composed into something that solves a real problem, not in which lab's logo is on the API call. Read the matrix, run the math, and ship the next thing.

# LLM Six-Month Recap Checklist - What to Check When a New Model Is Announced

As of: 2026-09-29.
Dates were checked against each company's official announcements, model pages, and API changelogs. Price amounts are not included.

---

## 1. What to check when you see model news

- [ ] The name actually appears in the company's official model list (for example, Anthropic's Sonnet list runs 4.5, 4.6, 5, 5.5; there is no "Claude 4.7 Sonnet")
- [ ] You confirmed the date in an official announcement or API changelog (dates in summaries are often off by months)
- [ ] You distinguished preview from general availability (for example, Gemini 3 Pro was a preview on 2025-11-18; Gemini 3.5 Flash went GA on 2026-05-19)
- [ ] For an MoE model, you looked at total and active parameters separately (memory is needed for the full weights)
- [ ] You checked whether one generation name covers several configurations (for example, Llama 4 = Scout and Maverick, with Behemoth as a preview)
- [ ] For open weights, you confirmed the license in the official announcement (for example, Mistral Large 3 = Apache 2.0)
- [ ] You took prices from the company's pricing page, not from a summary
- [ ] You took benchmark scores from the original leaderboard
