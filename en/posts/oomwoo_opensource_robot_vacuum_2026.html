Oomwoo: An Open-Source Robot Vacuum You Build Yourself

A cloud-free robot vacuum built from a Raspberry Pi, ROS 2, and 2D LiDAR — assessing what DIY open-source hardware is really worth

2026-07-04 Dev & Infra

Introduction: The Shadow Behind Commercial Robot Vacuums, and the Return of "Just Build It Yourself"

Roborock, Ecovacs, iRobot, and their peers push out new flagship models every year, and the robot vacuum market has settled into a race for polish. Auto-empty stations, self-washing mops, and AI obstacle avoidance now come standard on flagships priced well over a thousand dollars — but behind that convenience sits a familiar shadow. A full map of your home accumulates on the manufacturer's cloud servers. A single app update can strip out a feature or convert it into a subscription. Crack the case open and you find boards deliberately designed so users can't touch them. None of this is a new complaint. But in June 2026, a project appeared that answers that complaint head-on with "then build it yourself": Oomwoo, started by Ilia O., the developer behind Maker's Pet.

Oomwoo positions itself as a robot vacuum you build from a Raspberry Pi 5, a cheap 2D LiDAR unit, ROS 2, and a 3D printer. What stands out even more is its approach. Rather than releasing a finished product, it publishes an early-stage repository — one that doesn't even have build instructions yet — from the very first commit, running hardware, firmware, and software development entirely build-in-public. This piece, prompted by the topic that surfaced on GeekNews, is my own analysis of what an open-source robot vacuum means as an alternative to commercial products. Not a plain introduction — I want to work through whether it's genuinely worth building right now, and what you'd gain and give up in the process.

1. What Is Oomwoo - An Overview of the Open-Source Robot Vacuum Project

1.1 Build-in-Public, Published From the First Commit

What makes Oomwoo interesting is not that it publishes a finished product as open source, but that it publishes the design process itself. The GitHub repository (makerspet/oomwoo) is, as of July 2026, still closer to an RFC (request-for-comment) stage, and there is no build guide you could actually follow to assemble one. What's up instead is architecture documentation, a draft bill of materials, and a guide to contributing. This is a different kind of experiment than "open-sourcing a finished product" — it's closer to a proposal that the community help decide the direction from the design stage onward.

1.2 The Hardware and Software Stack

Per the published roadmap, the hardware consists of a Raspberry Pi 5 (4GB) or an ESP32, a 2D LiDAR sensor, drive motors, wheels and casters, a 3D-printed chassis, a suction brush and blower fan, and a docking station. The software stack is ROS 2 — the standard robotics middleware — the Nav2 autonomous navigation stack, the Gazebo simulation environment, and native Home Assistant integration. The code is released under the Apache License 2.0, with hardware design files slated for a separate open-hardware license later. The stated bill-of-materials target, excluding the Raspberry Pi, is roughly $100-200, aimed at matching the results of a mid-range $500-600 commercial robot vacuum.

1.3 Where Things Actually Stand - the v0 Milestone

What matters most is where this project actually is right now. As of July 2026, the v0 milestone covers a 3D-printed chassis, a Gazebo simulation, and LiDAR mapping with manual SLAM — nothing more. In other words, there is no robot yet that actually cleans a real floor. Community contributions are split into modules, progress is shared over Discord and GitHub Discussions, and when multiple people tackle the same module, the community votes on which proposal to adopt. Being honest about this matters for a clear reason: if you read this expecting to order parts today and have a finished vacuum, you'll be disappointed soon.

2. Two Branches of Open-Source Robot Vacuums - Oomwoo and Valetudo

2.1 Valetudo: Liberating a Robot You Already Own

When developers hear "open-source robot vacuum," the name that usually comes to mind first isn't Oomwoo — it's Valetudo. Running continuously since 2018, Valetudo doesn't build a new robot. Instead, it roots an already-purchased Xiaomi or Roborock-family robot vacuum and installs alternative firmware that replaces the manufacturer's cloud. The result: mapping and cleaning data never leave the premises, and the robot integrates locally with Home Assistant via MQTT. The catch is that installation requires rooting the device, voids the warranty, and is irreversible — you can't flash it back to stock once you've installed it. Even so, with an installed base running into the thousands of units, it's regarded as a mature "install it and forget about it" solution.

2.2 Oomwoo: Redesigning From Scratch

Oomwoo approaches from the opposite direction. Instead of hacking someone else's robot to free it, it designs the hardware itself as open source from the ground up. That's a much slower, harder path. Rather than trusting already-proven commercial hardware — motor drivers, battery management, bumper sensors, dustbin design — you have to design and validate all of it yourself. In exchange, it offers something Valetudo can't: hardware with no vendor lock-in to begin with, a design where every single part is understood and replaceable, and the freedom to deviate entirely from whatever shape a commercial robot happens to take.

2.3 Why the Two Approaches Complement Rather Than Compete

I don't see these two as competitors. Valetudo is a practical solution for liberating a robot you can use right now; Oomwoo is closer to an idealistic experiment in building a robot that never needed liberating in the first place. For anyone whose priority is privacy today, Valetudo is overwhelmingly the more sensible choice. For anyone who wants to learn robotics itself, or push past the limits of commercial hardware — cleaning paths, suction power, extensibility — a project like Oomwoo carries real meaning. This split showed up repeatedly in international community discussion too: a clear-eyed response that, on pure cost-versus-performance, modifying a cheap commercial robot makes more sense, coexisting with a response that there's inherent value in designing something from scratch regardless.

3. Strengths and Weaknesses of DIY Open Source Versus Commercial Robot Vacuums

3.1 Where Commercial Products Win - Polish and Convenience

Let's start honestly. Where commercial robot vacuums win is clear: years of accumulated data across millions of units on suction, obstacle avoidance, and path optimization; ready-to-use polish out of the box; vendor support when something breaks; and the "set it up and forget it" convenience. The mid-range $500-600 performance level Oomwoo has set as its target is, for now, an unverified aspiration — a long way from the actual state of a v0-stage project. If we don't acknowledge that gap honestly, everything that follows rings hollow.

3.2 Where Open-Source Hardware Wins - Repair, Modification, Privacy

Conversely, there are places open-source hardware clearly wins. First is repairability. When a battery or a brush motor fails on a commercial robot, it's common to have to replace the whole unit because the specific part has been discontinued. An open design, with standard parts and published 3D-printing files, lets you reprint and swap just that one part. Second is freedom to modify. If your room layout is unusual, or you need a brush specialized for a particular pet's fur, or you want to remove the camera entirely, what's impossible on a commercial product becomes a matter of editing a design file on an open design. Third is privacy. The local-first, no-cloud-required architecture Oomwoo advocates shares the same goal as Valetudo in that it eliminates the very path by which mapping data could ever leave the premises.

3.3 A Sober Cost Calculation - the "It's Cheap" Illusion

Here's a trap worth naming explicitly. The $100-200 bill-of-materials target is just parts cost. Missing from it: the Raspberry Pi 5 itself, printing-service fees if you don't own a 3D printer, and above all, your own time spent on assembly, debugging, and tuning. Getting a single robot to map a room without bumping into walls costs far more in time than it does in parts. If your sole goal is genuinely getting the cheapest well-functioning cleaning robot, buying a budget commercial unit and optionally layering Valetudo on top is likely more rational in terms of real cost. Oomwoo's cost appeal isn't the price of the finished product — it's what you gain along the way.

4. Value as a Robotics, SLAM, and Sensor Learning Project

4.1 Treating SLAM as a Component, Not a Black Box

This is where I rate projects like Oomwoo most highly. SLAM (simultaneous localization and mapping) on a commercial robot vacuum is a black box completely hidden from the user. How the robot recognizes walls, corrects odometry drift, or handles loop closure is something the user can't see and doesn't need to. Someone building Oomwoo, by contrast, confronts at the code level how raw LiDAR data becomes a point cloud, how that point cloud accumulates into an occupancy grid, and how Nav2 plans a path over that grid. That's a chance to internalize, through the successes and failures of real hardware, concepts that are otherwise confined to a university robotics course.

4.2 The ROS2/Nav2 Ecosystem as a Standard Learning Path

Oomwoo's decision to build on ROS 2 and Nav2 — the industry-standard robotics frameworks — rather than a from-scratch, bespoke stack matters enormously from a learning standpoint. Just as TurtleBot has long served as the "hello world of ROS" and the standard entry platform for learning robotics, the ROS 2, Nav2, and Gazebo experience gained from Oomwoo isn't locked to a single cleaning robot. The same stack carries over directly to industrial autonomous mobile robots, warehouse logistics robots, and service robots. In that sense, this project is less "build one vacuum" and more "get a working autonomous-robotics stack under your fingers."

4.3 What Remains Even If It Fails

I believe the real value of a maker project is the learning that remains regardless of whether it's ever finished. Discovering firsthand that LiDAR misbehaves against glass or dark surfaces, tracing why a motor driver's heat is resetting the board, and figuring out that a subtly skewed map traces back to accumulated wheel-encoder drift — all of that becomes real skill even when the attempt fails. None of that accumulation happens when you simply use a finished commercial product. If learning is your goal, the very fact that Oomwoo isn't finished can be an advantage — because it's still early, contributors have real room to shape design decisions themselves.

5. An Extension of Maker Culture and the Self-Hosting Ethos

5.1 The Same Impulse as Home Servers and Self-Hosting

I don't see Oomwoo purely as a robotics project. I see it emerging from exactly the same impulse behind the homelab and self-hosting movement that has spread over the past few years. The motivation behind replacing Google Drive with Nextcloud, Netflix with Jellyfin, and a password-manager app with Vaultwarden ultimately comes down to one thing: a refusal to hand control over your own data and your own hardware to a vendor. Oomwoo extends that same attitude from cloud services to physical appliances. The home map, movement patterns, and captured images a robot vacuum generates can be far more sensitive smart-home data than your Netflix viewing history — yet most users never stop to ask where that data actually goes.

5.2 An Answer to "Why Bother Building It Yourself?"

My answer is this: just as people don't run a home server instead of a finished cloud service because it's "cheaper or more convenient," people don't build Oomwoo to "get a cheaper vacuum." The motivation is control, understanding, and the enjoyment of the building process itself. Maker culture has always accepted this kind of inefficiency. Rebuilding a thermometer that already exists with an Arduino, or reprinting a part you could simply buy — these are economically irrational acts, but inside that irrationality sits a different kind of value: learning and autonomy. This spirit has spread far enough beyond cloud services into hardware broadly that entire communities now separately debate things like domain naming systems built for self-hosting.

5.3 Modular Community Governance

Oomwoo's operating model is worth watching too. Splitting work into modules that multiple contributors tackle in parallel, then letting the community vote on which proposal to adopt, essentially transplants open-source software governance onto a hardware project — an uncommon move, since hardware carries far higher fork-and-merge costs than software does. Whether this experiment actually works — which of several competing chassis designs or sensor layouts different people propose ends up adopted, and how smoothly that decision gets made — is something worth watching going forward.

6. Should You Start Now - A Practical Take

6.1 Just How Early Is This

Let's be clear once more. As of July 2026, Oomwoo is at the v0 stage, and no finished robot that actually cleans a floor exists. There's no build guide yet, no verified bill of materials, no stable SLAM pipeline. Jumping in now is closer to co-designing a product than assembling a finished one.

6.2 Who This Is a Good Fit For

Developers who want to learn ROS 2, Nav2, and SLAM on real hardware; makers already running a homelab or self-hosting setup who are looking for the next thing to extend; and open-hardware enthusiasts who'd rather contribute to the design process itself than assemble a finished product — for all of them, now is actually a good time to get involved, precisely because the project's direction is still fluid and early contributors have real room to shape it.

6.3 Who Should Wait

Conversely, if you need clean floors right now, or want a cheap vacuum without a robotics background, it's still too early for you. If privacy is your top priority, buying a proven Valetudo-compatible robot is far faster and safer. It won't be too late to revisit Oomwoo once a v1 build guide exists and the community has accumulated real completed builds.

Conclusion: Three Things Worth Checking Today

Oomwoo is not a finished product — it's an ongoing experiment. What makes that experiment interesting isn't the vacuum as an end result; it's that it re-asks, at the hardware level, the questions of vendor lock-in, cloud dependency, and unrepairability hidden behind the polish and convenience of commercial appliances. Here are three things I'd recommend checking this week.

First, browse the GitHub repository (makerspet/oomwoo) and read the v0 milestone and architecture documents yourself. If you don't judge where things actually stand for yourself, it's easy to fall into either inflated expectations or premature disappointment.

Second, if privacy is your goal, look at a Valetudo-compatible robot first rather than waiting for Oomwoo to mature. These two projects aren't competitors — they're solutions on different timelines.

Third, if learning is your goal, get involved now. ROS 2, Nav2, and SLAM offer something real to learn even if this project never finishes, and because it's still early-stage, the scope for contribution is unusually wide. At the intersection of maker culture and the self-hosting ethos, Oomwoo is a fascinating proving ground that has to demonstrate its value through the process itself, not through a finished product.
