Software Ate the World, Now Hardware Is Eating Software

Where did SaaS's 75-90% gross margins go - why chips, compute, and data are absorbing the value

Jul 16, 2026 Finance & Economy

Introduction: Why This Reversal Matters Now

In 2011, venture capitalist Marc Andreessen declared in a Wall Street Journal essay that "software is eating the world." Fifteen years on, the thesis proved out. Netflix displaced Blockbuster, Uber remade the taxi industry, and SaaS companies built businesses that kept most of their revenue as profit without owning hardware or inventory. Now, in 2026, an essay from venture firm Wing VC argues the current is reversing. The title is deliberately provocative: software ate the world, and now hardware is eating software.

I am not simply relaying that claim. Instead, I cross-checked it against recent earnings reports, industry benchmarks, and semiconductor and SaaS market data to see where the thesis holds and where it overreaches. The short answer: the direction is right, but it is not absolute. Value is genuinely migrating, but software is not collapsing wholesale. This piece is an attempt to find that balance, and it is offered as analysis for information purposes, not investment advice.

1. Andreessen's Thesis and the Economics of the SaaS Era

1.1 Why "Software Eats the World" Worked

Andreessen's 2011 thesis was powerful for a simple reason: software's marginal cost converges toward zero. Once code is written, the extra cost of serving 100 users versus 100 million is negligible. On that foundation, the SaaS industry built 75-90% gross margins, a level of profitability almost unimaginable in manufacturing or retail. Benchmark data backs this up: as of 2025, median software gross margin among SaaS companies sat around 80%, with the top quartile clearing 86% or higher.

1.2 Value Piled Up at the Interface

During this era, value concentrated in the application and interface layer touching end users. Companies like Salesforce, Adobe, and ServiceNow climbed to the top of market-cap rankings without owning the hardware underneath. Servers, networking, and storage became commoditized and were absorbed by a handful of cloud providers, while the software layered on top captured most of the real value and margin. Even cloud infrastructure played a supporting role to the software layer's success.

2. How AI Inverts the Margin Structure - Inference as a Variable Cost

2.1 The Fundamental Difference Between SaaS Server Costs and AI Inference Costs

SaaS-era companies also paid for servers, but economies of scale meant per-user cost fell as usage grew. AI is different. Every request to a large-language-model-based service consumes GPU compute, generating an inference cost that scales almost linearly with usage. In other words, inference cost behaves less like SaaS's largely fixed server bill and more like a manufacturer's variable cost of raw materials. The SaaS rule - cost per user dilutes as scale grows - does not automatically apply to AI services.

2.2 The Margin Squeeze, in Numbers

This difference shows up in actual figures. Research indicates inference cost runs around 23% of revenue at scaling AI companies, and unlike SaaS cost of goods sold, that ratio does not easily fall as companies grow. As a result, many AI-native applications settle into gross margins in the 50-60% range, and some benchmarks show SaaS companies bolting on AI features seeing margins compressed into the 60-70% band. Compared with traditional SaaS's 75-90%, that is a structural gap of 15-30 percentage points.

3. Where the Value Is Going - Chips, Compute, Data Centers, Power

3.1 Nvidia and the Semiconductor Supercycle

Who fills the space margin vacates? The clearest beneficiary is semiconductors. Nvidia's fiscal Q1 2026 data-center revenue hit $75.2 billion, up 92% year over year, with gross margin in that segment reported around 75%. Global semiconductor market revenue totaled $793 billion in 2025 (+21%), and 2026 is tracking toward the $1 trillion mark. The AI accelerator market alone is projected to grow from roughly $80 billion in 2024 to over $280 billion by 2029.

3.2 TSMC and the Packaging Bottleneck

TSMC, sitting atop the foundry stack, posted a record 66.2% gross margin in Q1 2026, with HPC (including AI accelerators) accounting for 61% of total revenue. What's more interesting is where the bottleneck actually sits. Advanced packaging (CoWoS) capacity has expanded from roughly 35,000 wafers per month at the end of 2024 to about 75,000 at the end of 2025, targeting 125,000-130,000 by the end of 2026 - yet TSMC executives say CoWoS capacity is effectively sold out through all of 2026. The constraint, in other words, is not chip design capability but a physical packaging process.

3.3 Hyperscaler Capex and Memory Prices

The capital deployed to punch through this bottleneck is itself telling. In Q1 2026, Amazon spent $44.2 billion, Alphabet $35.7 billion, Microsoft $30.9 billion, and Meta $19.8 billion, an annualized pace of roughly $525 billion. Memory is no exception: HBM demand is projected to grow about 130% in 2025 and roughly 70% more in 2026, and DRAM contract prices reportedly jumped about 90% quarter over quarter in Q1 2026 alone. Physical constraints that money cannot loosen on a quarterly basis - packaging, memory, power - are becoming the new reservoirs of value.

3.4 Power: The Last Bottleneck

As of 2026, AI-optimized servers are estimated to account for around 31% of total data-center power consumption, and data-center power demand is projected to exceed 1,200 terawatt-hours by 2030. New AI data-center capacity announced in 2025 totaled roughly 10 gigawatts, against a packaging base that can only support about 18 gigawatts - a sign that the bottleneck after semiconductors is power itself.

4. How Software Companies Are Responding - Three Margin-Defense Strategies

4.1 In-House Chips and Vertical Integration

Hyperscalers - Google's TPU, Amazon's Trainium and Inferentia, Microsoft's Maia - have moved into in-house chip design to reduce dependence on Nvidia. This is a vertical-integration bid to avoid handing the semiconductor layer's entire margin to an outside supplier. But this strategy requires hyperscaler-level capital and scale; it is not a path an ordinary software company can simply replicate.

4.2 Open Models and Inference Platforms - The Linux Lesson

A more realistic response is open-weight models paired with inference-optimization platforms. Per the Stanford AI Index, the capability gap between closed and open-weight models narrowed from 8.04 percentage points in January 2024 to 1.70 points in February 2025. As open models effectively commoditize, value migrates from the model itself to the platform that serves it. Fireworks AI reportedly reached $800 million in annualized revenue within about three years (up more than 4x year over year), Together AI is at a similar scale, and Baseten has reached roughly $600 million (up more than 5x). The old adage applies here too: Linux itself didn't capture Linux's value - Red Hat and the cloud did. Frontier API pricing runs $10-15 per million tokens, while open models served through an inference platform run $0.40-1.00 per million - a calculation that suggests over 90% cost reduction for the same workload.

4.3 Repricing - Outcome-Based Billing

The third response is changing the pricing structure itself. Shifting from seat-based billing to outcome-based pricing - charging for work completed or results delivered rather than user count - lets rising inference costs pass through to customers more naturally. As I noted in my recent analysis of the new SaaS playbook for the AI agent era, this shift is already underway across several SaaS companies' price sheets.

5. Investment and Industry Implications - Valuations Are Already Reacting

5.1 A Bifurcation in Multiples

The coldest signal comes from equity-market valuation multiples. Traditional SaaS's median revenue multiple has reportedly compressed from a 2021 peak of 18.6x to around 6.7x today. Foundation-model companies, by contrast, are said to trade at 25-50x revenue and AI-native platforms at 25-30x. Notably, "AI wrapper" applications - thin prompt-and-glue layers over a model with no clear moat - reportedly trade at just 5-8x, below even compressed legacy SaaS.

5.2 The Data-Platform Exception

Not every application is getting squeezed. Databricks posted $6.9 billion in annualized revenue (growing roughly 80%), with AI products making up about 26% of revenue, and is privately valued around $170 billion. Palantir's Q1 2026 revenue grew 85% year over year to an annualized run rate of about $6.5 billion, with US commercial revenue up 133%, a Rule of 40 score of 145, and a market cap around $350 billion - roughly 50x revenue. Companies that own the data pipeline and system itself are, if anything, earning a premium.

5.3 The Korea Angle - Strong Memory, Packaging and Power as Homework

Korea's position in this shift is double-edged. Samsung Electronics and SK hynix hold world-class competitiveness in the HBM memory market, and forecasts point to HBM3E demand expansion and a transition to HBM4 driving a memory supercycle in 2026. But the foundry, advanced-packaging, and equipment/materials ecosystem needed to support that is repeatedly assessed as comparatively thin next to Taiwan and the US. In other words, Korea holds a clear stake in memory - one of the "lower-stack layers" this essay says value is flowing toward - but is more of a late mover on packaging, the next bottleneck. Power infrastructure around semiconductor clusters - transmission, distribution, and generation capacity - could also emerge as the next constraint on domestic chip investment.

6. The Counterargument - Is Software Really Just Getting Thinner?

6.1 a16z's Rebuttal - "AI Will Eat Application Software"

Not everyone agrees with this essay. a16z has made the opposite case: AI is not killing the software industry outright, but splitting it into companies with genuine moats and thin wrapper apps. In this view, the real value sits in the orchestration layer that coordinates agents ("AgentOps"), and companies that encode human judgment into repeatable workflows grow stronger, not weaker, as underlying models improve.

6.2 Systems of Record and Data Loops Still Defend

Some analysis argues that applications owning a system of record in deeply regulated workflows, or those tied to financial and legal outcomes, tend to coexist with agents layered on top rather than being replaced by them. Companies with proprietary data loops, switching costs, and distribution may find that as model performance levels up across the board, the value of "process design" at the application layer becomes more pronounced, not less.

6.3 Finding the Balance - Two Stories Can Both Be True, at Different Layers

My own read is that the two views are not mutually exclusive. The observation that "hardware is absorbing value" correctly identifies absolute capital concentration in the lower-stack layers - semiconductors, compute, data centers. The observation that "AI is eating applications" correctly identifies a winner-take-most reshuffling inside the application layer. The software layer's overall pie is thinning, even as a moated minority within it grows its share. The rest of the "AI wrapper" majority absorbs the valuation compression head-on.

7. Conclusion - What to Watch as of July 2026

Andreessen's 2011 thesis was not wrong. Software still defines the world. But AI-era software sits on a cost structure different from the near-zero-marginal-cost SaaS of the past, and as a result a substantial share of margin and value is flowing toward the lower-stack layers of chips, compute, data, and power. This is analysis offered for information purposes, not investment advice; judgments on individual stocks or asset allocation remain each reader's own.

Three practical takeaways from this research. First, when evaluating an application company, don't default to assuming 80%+ SaaS-style margins - check first whether it owns a proprietary data loop or a system of record. Second, physical bottlenecks that capital cannot loosen quickly - semiconductors, packaging, power - are likely to remain a durable source of bargaining power. Third, Korea holds a strong card in memory, but how quickly it moves on packaging and power, the bottlenecks that come after memory, will shape its position over the next five years.
