The End of the Software Engineer Identity - What Should We Call Ourselves in the AI Agent Era of 2026?

A word born at NATO in 1968. Fifty years later, the identity behind it is cracking.

May 22, 2026 Tech

Introduction: A profession shaken in only two years - is "Software Engineer" still the right word?

In May 2026, many developers, myself included, briefly hesitate when introducing what we do. The phrase "I am a software engineer" leaves the mouth, but the image that flashes in the head is something else. What did I actually do yesterday? I instructed Claude Code, reviewed five pull requests written by a Cursor agent one after another, wired workflows in n8n, and re-verified a log analysis that the AI had pre-summarized. The time I spent typing code with my own fingers was less than 30% of the day.

So am I still a "Software Engineer"? By the dictionary, yes. Software was produced and I was the responsible party. But the image the word evokes - a person at a keyboard solving problems and writing functions one line at a time - drifts further from my actual day every quarter. When the "Software Engineer Identity" topic appeared on news.hada.io in May 2026 with only 4 points, the relatively low score did not stop a long comment discussion. Everyone is feeling the same low-grade dissonance.

This article does two things. First, it traces where the term "Software Engineer" came from and what it has carried for 50 years - what exactly we risk losing. Second, it inventories the five concrete ways the job has shifted in the AI agent era, then analyzes ten candidate replacement titles, noting where each one captures the new reality and where it leaks. The article closes with the global big-tech naming pattern, a 2030 labor market forecast and my own provisional answer. The article does not prescribe an answer. It offers five honest questions designed to help readers find one on their own.

1. The history of "Software Engineer" - the 1968 NATO conference and 50 years of identity

The first formal venue for the term "Software Engineer" was the 1968 NATO Software Engineering Conference. The computer industry was in the middle of what was openly called the "software crisis." Hardware was doubling every couple of years per Moore's Law, but software was missing schedules, shipping full of defects, and consuming runaway maintenance budgets.

The conference made a sharp claim: software development must become an engineering discipline, comparable to civil, mechanical and electrical engineering. That meant validated methods, quantitative measurement, repeatable processes and a professional credential system. The word that emerged was "Software Engineering," and the person practicing it was the "Software Engineer."

Across the next fifty years, that label carried five overlapping identities. (1) Rigor - reasoning based on logic and evidence, not guesswork. (2) Responsibility - professional and ethical accountability for the artifact. (3) Expertise - a body of knowledge non-experts cannot easily imitate. (4) Craftsmanship - pride in code that is well-made for its own sake. (5) Problem solver - the person who uses algorithms, data structures and system design to crack new problems. The pride developers attached to the word "engineer" came from these five.

The reality of 2026 is breaking parts of that bundle, especially (3) and (4). AI gives non-specialists a credible level of code production, blurring the entry barrier of expertise. The definition of "good code" is migrating from "what the human author judged well-built" to "what the AI-output reviewer judged well-built." The gap between the identity the 50-year-old word carries and what the work actually looks like today is the starting point of this article.

2. Five ways the job has changed in the AI agent era - my own audit

From 24 months of personal experience, here are the five shifts in the substance of the job that go beyond mere tool change and bite into identity.

2-1) Less writing, more verifying

The largest shift. Two years ago, roughly 60% of my day was hands-on typing. Today that ratio is under 30%, and over 50% goes to reading what the AI wrote, validating it, and judging trade-offs. The center of the job has moved from "producer" to "reviewer and decider." The structural analogy is the automotive industry moving from direct driving a century ago to supervising autonomous driving today.

2-2) From single functions to system outcomes

The unit of work used to be "I wrote this function today." Increasingly it is "I merged this pull request today," and that PR mixes output from several agents. The unit of accountability has widened from a single function to a whole subsystem, and accordingly the resolution shrinks while the breadth grows. Some of the depth is delegated to AI; the human owns the breadth.

2-3) From solo to AI pair

I am never alone at the keyboard anymore. Always one or two AI agents are in the loop. This is not pair programming. In pair programming both parties are human and accountability is shared. In the human-AI pair, accountability remains 100% with the human while parts of the capability are delegated to the AI. That asymmetry quietly reshapes identity because the boundary of "what I made" gets blurry.

2-4) From author to curator

Looking honestly at my own day, the work resembles curation more each month. Which of the five candidate solutions the AI generated should be merged. Which library should be adopted. Which sections must still be written by hand. The added value increasingly comes from "what I chose and what I rejected" rather than "what I authored". The closest professional analogies are gallery curator, editor, and art director.

2-5) From feature implementation to business decisions

The most consequential shift. AI compresses the time to implement features, so my time migrates toward business-shaped questions like "is this feature worth building at all." A decade ago PMs decided and engineers implemented. Now engineers stand much closer to the PM position and decide where the AI capacity should be spent. The ratio of decision-maker time to implementer time is what is really moving.

3. Ten candidate titles - a working analysis

Here are ten candidate words being floated to replace or supplement "Software Engineer." Each captures something well and misses something else.

3-1) AI Conductor

The orchestra metaphor. Intuitive: the human directs multiple AI agents toward a single deliverable. Matches the new reality of less code, more coordination. Weakness: it sounds marketing-flavored and rarely maps cleanly to formal job descriptions. The conductor metaphor evokes musical responsibility for the result, but not the depth of engineering accountability.

3-2) Code Curator

The museum-curator metaphor. Captures shift 2-4 the most precisely - the person who decides what to display (merge) and what to discard (reject). Weakness: the curator does not author the artwork, while the engineer still must produce some parts directly.

3-3) System Designer

Captures shift 2-2 clearly: responsibility scoped at the system level rather than the function. Weakness: too abstract and too old. The term has existed since the 1970s and does not signal the freshness of the AI era.

3-4) Intent Translator

My favorite to chew on. The person who translates business intent, user intent and PM intent into machine-executable specifications. Captures shifts 2-3 and 2-5 simultaneously. Weakness: lacks the gravitas of a primary job title; "translator" reads as auxiliary.

3-5) Builder

Increasingly popular in Silicon Valley. The simplest possible identity: the person who builds. Sam Altman and Sundar Pichai both lean on phrases like "AI is making everyone a builder." Weakness: too generic. Architects build, carpenters build, furniture designers build. It lacks differentiation.

3-6) Software Composer

The composer metaphor. Software gets composed the way music does. Pairs nicely with AI Conductor and preserves the weight of the creator. Weakness: not yet established as a profession in either English or other major labor markets.

3-7) AI Workflow Engineer

The most practical candidate in my view. Already appearing in actual job postings in 2026. Concrete job description: designs end-to-end workflows that chain agents and tools. Weakness: too narrow. Not every software engineer is an AI workflow engineer, and it leaves backend, infrastructure and security gaps.

3-8) Solution Architect

An existing title that fits the AI era better than it did the pre-AI one. Owner of the mapping from business problem to technical solution - aligns with shift 2-5. Weakness: senior-coded title; awkward for junior engineers to claim.

3-9) Product Engineer

Deliberately adopted by companies like Linear, Vercel and Stripe to blur the PM-engineer boundary. Matches the rise of business-decision weight in the role. Weakness: the definition varies wildly by company size and culture.

3-10) Software Crafter

From Sandro Mancuso's Software Craftsmanship movement. Emphasizes craftsmanship, tests, refactoring and domain depth. Paradoxically attractive in a leveling era because it stresses the depth AI cannot reach. Weakness: some readers misread it as an anti-AI stance.

5. How global big tech is renaming the role - Google, Anthropic, Meta

How big tech labels the role is a leading market signal. The pattern I see in 2026:

  • Google: Still uses "Software Engineer II / III / Staff / Senior Staff / Principal." Internally the phrase "AI-augmented engineer" is now common, and job postings increasingly require being "comfortable working with AI agents."
  • Anthropic: Consolidates around "Member of Technical Staff" (MTS). A deliberate revival of the Bell Labs tradition, intentionally blurring the engineer-researcher boundary. Fits an environment where one person owns model training, tool building and evaluation together.
  • Meta: Still keeps "Software Engineer." But "AI Research Engineer" is growing fast as a separate track from generic SWE.
  • OpenAI: Increasing use of "Member of Technical Staff" similar to Anthropic, with "Applied AI Engineer" as a parallel track.
  • Modern startups (Linear, Vercel, Stripe): Adopting "Product Engineer" deliberately to merge the engineer-PM boundary.

Two patterns matter most. (1) The phrase "Software Engineer" survives. (2) New titles appear alongside it, and in AI research and product areas the unified "MTS" label is becoming the standard fast. The non-US labor market will follow within five years.

6. The Korean IT scene - title trajectory in a regional market

Korea is more conservative than the global frontier. The pattern I see:

6-1) Large incumbents (Naver, Kakao, Coupang, LG CNS)

Keep "Software Engineer" or "Backend / Frontend Developer" as the job-family name. Rank systems remain mostly the old "사원-대리-과장" or "팀원-책임-수석" chains, although some have adopted the global "Engineer 1/2/3 → Staff Engineer → Principal Engineer" ladder. AI-related roles tend to be split into a separate family ("AI Engineer," "ML Engineer") rather than integrated into the generic SWE family.

6-2) Modern startups (Toss, Karrot, Line)

Adopt global trends faster. "Product Engineer," "Platform Engineer," and "Infrastructure Engineer" subdivisions are spreading. "AI Engineer" or "Applied AI Engineer" tracks are appearing. Unified labels like "Member of Technical Staff" are still rare in Korean job ads and will need more time to feel natural in the language.

6-3) Small and mid-sized IT

Slowest to change. "Developer" or "Programmer" remain common, and AI work is folded into "AI Developer" or "Data Scientist." Real title turnover here likely starts after 2030.

6-4) Freelancers and solo developers

The freest. Self-defined titles like "AI Workflow Designer," "Indie Hacker" and "Solo Builder" are spreading rapidly on portfolios and LinkedIn. With no HR system in the way, this segment absorbs market trends fastest.

7. Self-audit - five honest questions for the reader

The point of this article is not to hand out a label but to help readers find one inside themselves. Answer these five questions honestly:

  1. What percentage of your last month did you spend writing code with your own hands? Under 30% means the center of the job has already shifted from author to reviewer-decider.
  2. What is the unit of your accountability - the function or the system? If it is the system, you are closer to Solution Architect or Product Engineer than to traditional SWE.
  3. Does your added value come from "what you made" or from "what you chose"? If it is the latter, the Code Curator identity is more accurate.
  4. In your most recent proud deliverable, what percentage of the work was actually yours? Under 50% is a signal that the author identity is breaking.
  5. What title do you want on your business card in five years? If the answer is not coming, that absence is itself the starting point of an identity audit.

8. The new fields - five emerging roles that did not exist in 2024

Five role names that did not exist before 2024 are now common in real postings:

8-1) AI Workflow Engineer

Designs and operates end-to-end workflows that chain agents and tools. Works with n8n, Make, Zapier, or in-house orchestrators. One of the fastest-growing tracks in the labor market.

8-2) Prompt System Architect

The evolved form of "prompt engineer." Beyond writing single prompts, designs full LLM application systems integrating prompts, tool calls, memory and evaluation. Quickly being formalized at model labs like Anthropic and OpenAI.

8-3) AI Evaluation Engineer

Owns automatic and manual quality evaluation of model and agent output. Builds eval harnesses, runs A/B tests, operates human-annotation pipelines. The trust backbone of AI products.

8-4) AI Safety Engineer

Responsible for guardrails, content filters, audit logging and prompt-injection defenses. Likely to become a mandatory function in every AI product team within five years as regulation tightens.

8-5) AI Tooling Engineer

Designs and implements the tools, MCP servers and APIs that agents call. Since agent capability depends on tool quality, this role is fast becoming central.

The common pattern across all five is that the traditional definition of "Software Engineer" does not contain them. The market is signaling that new words are needed.

9. 2030 labor market forecast - fragmentation vs unification

Looking ahead five years, two scenarios compete.

9-1) Fragmentation scenario

"Software Engineer" splits into 5-10 subroles each with its own title: AI Workflow Engineer, Prompt System Architect, AI Evaluation Engineer, AI Safety Engineer, AI Tooling Engineer, Product Engineer, Platform Engineer, Infrastructure Engineer, Security Engineer, Code Curator. I assign roughly 60% probability to this, since modern startups are already executing it.

9-2) Unification scenario

The opposite: boundaries blur and one person covers a wider domain, the Anthropic "Member of Technical Staff" model. Built on the hypothesis that AI neutralizes tool-stack differences and erodes frontend-vs-backend lines. Probability around 30%, concentrated at AI-first companies and frontier labs.

9-3) Hybrid scenario

The most probable outcome is coexistence: fragmentation at conventional IT firms, unification at AI-first ones. About 10% for a balanced split. Korea will likely tilt to fragmentation first.

10. The five skills that matter most in the AI agent era

Regardless of which title wins, the same five skills appear at the center of every viable trajectory:

  1. Systems thinking: reasoning at the system level, not the single function. Direct response to shift 2-2.
  2. Critical verification: never trusting AI output, validating every time. Direct response to shift 2-1.
  3. Domain depth: understanding business context and translating it into code. The area AI does not own.
  4. Trade-off decisions: choosing between performance, cost, time and complexity. AI helps with quantitative analysis but not with value judgments.
  5. Collaboration: working effectively with both humans and AI. The most underrated skill in the market.

The market value of raw coding speed declines while these five rise sharply. Career investment should follow the gradient.

11. Conclusion - what should we become?

My provisional answer to the opening question:

The answer does not have to be a single word. Keep the formal title "Software Engineer" and refresh the meaning it carries. On personal portfolios and LinkedIn, use a slash format ("Software Engineer / AI Workflow Designer") to surface the new identity gradually. Let formal titles tied to HR systems, taxes and visas follow more slowly.

What matters is not the word but the identity behind it. If you can answer honestly what you actually did yesterday, where your added value comes from, and who you want to be in five years, the title is secondary. The market always invents new labels more slowly than individuals reshape their identity.

The real asset of "Software Engineer" in 1968 was never the word itself but the five identities it carried: rigor, responsibility, expertise, craftsmanship, and problem-solving. In the AI agent era all five remain necessary. What has moved is only their surface - from code to system, from authoring to curation, from solo to collaboration. What we risk losing is not the word but the identity the word once carried, and the real question is how to refresh that identity in the new environment.

My answer for myself: I still call myself a "Software Engineer." But the meaning I pack into the label has changed. Not the person who writes code, but the person who owns system outcomes. Not an author but a curator and verifier. Not solo but paired with AI. Once that refresh is done inside, the outer word can be whatever the market settles on. Identity comes before the label.
