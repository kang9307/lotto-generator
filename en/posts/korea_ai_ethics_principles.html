Ethics Principles Are Not Law

What Korea's three values and seven AI principles end up asking of the people who build and the people who use

Aug 31, 2026 Law & Legal

Introduction: answering the question that lands in the group chat

A news link goes around the company chat, and a short question lands underneath it. So what do we have to do now? Answering that means splitting the question in two. What a newly published document asks for and what an already-effective statute demands carry different weight. Blur them and you go wrong in both directions at once: inventing obligations that do not exist, or mistaking a real duty for a suggestion and deferring it.

This piece is about Korea specifically. The Korea AI Ethics Principles, finalised on 21 August 2026, belong to the first category. The government-run AI ethics portal describes the document as a voluntary norm without binding legal force that does not replace statutes. That single sentence points both ways. Breaching it triggers no penalty, and complying with it erases no duty imposed elsewhere.

So is a voluntary norm nothing at all? No. Documents like this acquire force through citation rather than sanction. They become the base layer for sector-specific standards, a row in a public procurement scoring sheet, an annex to a contract, and the yardstick someone reaches for when an incident needs a benchmark for what the field considers ordinary practice. That makes the right response artefact design rather than a compliance sweep. Below, the confirmed scope comes first, then the three values and seven principles in their official wording, then the translation into four bundles: documents, records, explanation, and a remedy path.

Disclaimer — What follows is general information about the Korean regime as of August 2026 and is not legal advice. Whether a given service falls under a particular duty depends on its form and features, and that call belongs to the competent ministry and to a qualified lawyer.

1. What was actually settled - confirmed scope only

The most common error in this area is mixing up the tier of a document. The government issued AI regulation and ministries jointly published a voluntary norm are different statements, and the gap comes back as a credibility problem the moment either is cited internally.

  • Name — the Korea AI Ethics Principles. The name differs from the 2020 National AI Ethics Standards.
  • Timing — deliberated and adopted at the 12th Science and Technology Ministerial Meeting on 21 August 2026, published on 24 August.
  • Author — a joint effort across relevant ministries, prepared by the Ministry of Science and ICT together with the Korea Information Society Development Institute.
  • Legal basis — Article 27 of the Act on the Development of Artificial Intelligence and Establishment of Trust, Act No. 20676, in force since 22 January 2026.
  • Character — a voluntary norm without binding legal force that does not replace statutes. That is the portal's own phrasing.
  • Scope — described as a whole-of-government, whole-of-society principle applying across all areas of social life, rather than one narrowed to a named industry or size threshold.
  • Announced follow-ups — a case-based commentary, sector-specific self-assessment checklists, and ethics education materials for different audiences.

One structural point deserves attention. Each item sets out roles for providers, users, and government and society separately. This is not a list of what builders must observe; the people who use the systems get practice items too.

2. The three values and seven principles - official names

Names and order are the part you quote verbatim. The left two columns below are official, and the one-line gloss on the right stays inside what the source text says.

LayerNameDirection it points
Three valuesHuman DignityPeople respected as ends in themselves rather than means, enjoying dignity and human rights in full
Common Good of SocietyBenefits of AI extending past individual lives into social trust and shared gain
SustainabilityThose benefits shared by present and future generations, with environmental and social foundations maintained
Seven principlesHuman-centerednessDeveloping and using AI so it supports human judgement and choice and strengthens creativity and problem solving
Privacy ProtectionProcessing personal data only for the stated purpose and within the necessary scope, respecting the data subject's right to self-determination
Fairness and InclusivenessPreventing bias so that unjust discrimination does not arise, and working so everyone shares the benefits equitably
AccountabilityClarifying roles and responsibilities across the lifecycle, and on failure identifying who is responsible and pursuing real redress
SafetyDesigning and using AI so it causes no harm to life, body, or mental health and no property loss
ReliabilitySetting performance criteria for the purpose and context, and operating stably under intended conditions
TransparencyProviding, in accessible form, the fact that AI is in use along with the level of use and the basis for judgements

One rendering diverges. Some write-ups give the third principle as Fairness/Inclusiveness, but the government portal and the ministry announcement join the two with a middle dot. When a clause goes into an internal policy or a proposal, matching the notation is worth the effort.

Compared with the 2020 document the structure has shifted. The National AI Ethics Standards then had three basic principles and ten core requirements, and the third basic principle was appropriateness of technology to its purpose. That slot now holds Sustainability, and the sub-items were reorganised from ten into seven. The government describes the new set as carrying forward the 2020 standards while reflecting a changed environment of more advanced technology and an AI framework act now in force.

3. A principle is not a statute - where binding force stops

The same statute's name appears in two places, which creates an illusion. The principles were grounded in Article 27 of the AI framework act, but that grounding does not transfer legal force to them. Conversely, other articles of the same act impose duties directly on particular businesses.

AspectKorea AI Ethics PrinciplesDuties imposed by the AI framework act
CharacterVoluntary norm; does not replace statutesStatutory clauses; compliance is assessed
Who it addressesAll areas of social life, as a shared standardBusinesses meeting the conditions the clause sets
Core contentThree values, seven principles, roles by partyArticle 31 transparency (advance notice, output marking) and others
What follows a breachNo sanction clause. It can still surface in contracts, scoring sheets, and reputationFines up to 30 million won for transparency duties, domestic representative designation, and ignoring corrective orders
What does not followNo fine or criminal penalty arises from breaching a principle aloneCompliance here does not release liability under other statutes

In the right column, the first thing to look at is the Article 31 transparency duty. When a product or service uses high-impact or generative AI, the provider gives advance notice that it operates on AI, and generative outputs carry a marking that they were produced that way. The word transparency overlaps with the principle, but this version is concrete and verifiable from outside. Scoping under the act is covered separately in the framework act compliance walkthrough, so only the boundary matters here. The points that go wrong most often in real conversations cluster like this.

  • Regulation starts now that the principles are out — regulation started in January 2026. This document sits on top as a voluntary standard.
  • It is voluntary, so we can skip it — it was announced as the base for sector-specific standards and guidance. It looks different once it arrives as a procurement row or a contract annex.
  • Writing that we follow the principles is a defence — liability under data protection and consumer statutes is judged independently of that.
  • We do not build models, so none of this applies — the principles assign roles to users and to government and society too, and statutory duties attach to how something is offered rather than to model development.
  • A notice line finishes the job — notice and marking are the starting point. Explaining the basis for judgements, providing an intervention path, and running a remedy process are separate artefacts.

4. Reading the clauses backwards - what they are aimed at

The fast way to read a principles document is to invert each clause. Norms are not written for problems nobody has. A sentence being there means something in that spot kept going wrong.

  • Human-centeredness — the provider role includes not unfairly exploiting people's vulnerabilities; the user role includes not over-relying on outputs. The target is automated handling nobody can step into.
  • Privacy Protection — the phrase to notice is only for the stated purpose and within the necessary scope. Whether training data or prompts, reuse beyond the collection purpose is the problem spot.
  • Fairness and Inclusiveness — bias checks and remediation of discriminatory outcomes come paired with accessibility. The layout keeps people who never reach the service in frame.
  • Accountability — identifying responsibility and pursuing real redress sit in one sentence. It targets incident reports that end at the model did that.
  • Safety — measures matched to risk level and defence against misuse and external attack are provider items; not circumventing safeguards and reporting hazards are user items. Design and use together.
  • Reliability — set performance criteria and operate stably under intended conditions. The gap between demo performance and production performance is the target.
  • Transparency — not just the fact of use but the level of use and the basis for judgements. A single line saying AI is helping you does not fill that sentence.

Line the seven up and a pattern appears. Most of them aim at process rather than outcome. Not there shall be no bias but check for bias; not it shall be safe but put measures in place matched to the risk level. Norms that demand process translate into artefacts.

5. Translating into what builders actually do

Matching seven documents to seven principles only produces paperwork nobody maintains. Planting the clauses inside artefacts you already produce works better. The table shows where to plant them and what happens when the cell is empty.

PrincipleWhere it goes (artefact)What happens when it is empty
Human-centerednessAutomated-handling scope definition, list of intervention pointsAdverse determinations go out untouched by a person, with no route back
Privacy ProtectionPurpose and field register, retention periods for prompts and logs, rights request channelA deletion request arrives and the scope cannot be pinned down
Fairness and InclusivenessBias check records (axis, metric, gap), accessibility checklistA challenge comes in and the result cannot even be reproduced
AccountabilityDecision records, incident report template, appeal and remedy processThe cause field holds only a model name and whoever set the threshold vanishes
SafetySafeguards by risk tier, misuse and attack response procedure, reporting routeThe same class of incident repeats and response depends on one person's memory
ReliabilityPerformance criteria sheet (purpose, conditions, metrics), validation by conditionPerformance degrades in production and nobody can say since when
TransparencyAI usage notice text, output marking, document explaining the basis for judgementsA missing notice becomes a statutory breach and explanation requests go unanswered

Compressed, the seven rows leave four bundles. The smaller the team, the more sense there is in holding only these.

  • 1. Notice and marking — outranks the rest because the statute, not the principle, demands it. Record where on screen, in what wording, and at what moment, with a screenshot.
  • 2. Decision records — for every automated determination that can disadvantage someone, capture model version, threshold, policy version, approver, and final handler. Reconstructable is the goal.
  • 3. Appeal and remedy path — where the channel is, how many days until a reply, and who has authority to reverse. The accountability principle pairs responsibility with real redress.
  • 4. Change history — who changed a threshold, a prompt, or a model version, and when, answerable within thirty seconds.

How far internal data may go into a model is covered in the internal AI data policy design piece, and provenance recording for training data in the legal status of AI training data.

6. What the using side gets - users are named parties too

The part most often skipped is the user column. All seven principles carry a user role: review outputs critically and do not over-rely on them, avoid entering unnecessary personal data, stay aware of possible bias, do not circumvent safeguards and report hazards.

A line has to be drawn here. A voluntary norm creates no new rights for users. There is no claim of the form the principles say so, hand it over. What does provide leverage sits elsewhere.

  • Notice of AI-based operation and output marking — statutory. For a service in scope this is a compliance question, not a request.
  • Data protection rights — access, correction, deletion, and suspension of processing already come from the Personal Information Protection Act. The principle restates the direction rather than creating it.
  • Explanation of the basis for judgements — the principle asks for the level of use and the basis to be provided, which is not itself a claim right. What it does raise is the baseline of what people expect to be told.
  • Whether a person was involved in an adverse decision — since the human-centeredness item covers the possibility of intervention, asking whether something was handled by automation alone is a coherent request. The accountability item's redress language similarly supports asking whether a channel exists.

What this document changes on the user side is not a list of rights but the starting point of the conversation. The answer that the AI did it gets harder to sustain than it used to be.

7. The hardest principle to operationalise - Fairness and Inclusiveness

Of the seven, the one that resists translation most is Fairness and Inclusiveness. Transparency maps cleanly to notice text, accountability to decision records, reliability to a performance criteria sheet. Nothing springs to mind for fairness, for four layered reasons.

First, measuring it needs protected attributes, and collecting those is pulled the other way. Measuring group gaps requires axes such as gender, age, region, or disability status. Yet the privacy principle in the same document says to process personal data only for the stated purpose and within the necessary scope, and Korea's data protection statute treats sensitive data separately. Proving fairness needs exactly the data privacy is trying to narrow. Two principles pull against each other, and the text does not rank them.

Second, fairness metrics are mathematically incompatible. The 2017 result from Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan pins this down: three conditions one would naturally want from a risk score cannot be satisfied simultaneously except in the degenerate cases where base rates coincide across groups or prediction is perfect. So the sentence we removed bias does not stand on its own. Without naming the metric it says nothing, and satisfying one widens the gap on another.

Third, the line for unjust discrimination moves by sector. Age-based distinctions in insurance and in hiring are assessed differently. Looking at income versus looking at postal code in lending differ in character even when they correlate. The principles do not draw that line, and the announced sector-specific self-assessment checklists aim at exactly that gap.

Fourth, fairness and inclusiveness are different axes. Fairness looks at gaps in outcome; inclusiveness looks at whether people can reach the service at all. They sit in one principle but produce different artefacts, and the inclusiveness side is far more concrete. A workable order looks like this.

  • Start with inclusiveness — alternative text, flows completable by keyboard alone, colour contrast, plain-language guidance. Checklists exist and results are unambiguous.
  • Approach fairness as a record, not an absence — which axes were examined, which metric was used, how large the gap was, and what changed.
  • Write down why that metric — without it there is nothing to answer with when someone raises a different metric later.
  • Mark the axes you did not measure — a blank with a one-line reason defends better than a claim that everything was reviewed.

8. Structural difference from regimes abroad - the axis that splits

The usual question here is which one is stronger, and framed that way the comparison does not work. These are objects on different layers. What splits is not strength but the starting point of the judgement.

The EU AI Act is a regulation, so it sorts systems by intended use into unacceptable, high, limited, and minimal risk and attaches duties by tier. High risk brings risk management systems, data governance, technical documentation, human oversight design, and accuracy and security requirements; the limited-risk band carries a transparency duty so people know they are interacting with AI. What you build is classified first, and the obligation list follows.

Korea arranges it differently. The framework act carries the duties, and the ethics principles sit beside it as a voluntary norm. Instead of a risk tier table, the principles divide by party: providers, users, and government and society. The axes line up as follows.

  • Starting point — one begins from the use tier of the system, the other from the role of the party.
  • Force of the document — one is law in itself; the other is a voluntary norm that does not replace statutes, with the legal weight carried separately by the framework act.
  • How it gets specific — one specifies inside the text through annexes and tier tables; the other pushes specificity into commentary, sector checklists, and training materials that follow later.

An organisation covering both markets is safer keeping the tracks apart. A single merged checklist looks convenient but mixes items with different legal grounding into one row, and then nobody can cite a source during an audit.

9. What principles alone do not settle

A principles document sets direction. Some things direction alone does not settle, and knowing that list is how you use the document accurately.

  • Detailed criteria — no numeric threshold appears in the text for unjust discrimination, measures matched to the risk level, or provided in accessible form. The announced commentary and sector checklists aim at that gap.
  • Who enforces — being voluntary, the principles contain no procedure for assessing compliance. Statutory duties belong to the competent ministry, and data protection matters have their own regime.
  • Dispute handling — the principles create no new remedy procedure. Actual disputes run through data protection law, consumer statutes, and contract terms.
  • Form of sector standards — the principles were announced as the base for sector standards, but whether those arrive as public notices, guidelines, or voluntary agreements can differ by sector.

Which leaves one sensible call for now. Do not wait for the detailed criteria; build the records. What the commentary and checklists will call for is mostly evidence of what you were already doing and how, and that evidence cannot be created retroactively.

What could not be confirmed

The following sit outside the publicly available material and were left unasserted in the body.

  • The text of Article 27 itself — that the principles rest on Article 27 is stated in government material, but the wording of each paragraph requires reading the statute clause by clause, so nothing was placed in quotation marks.
  • The article number for the fines — the 30 million won ceiling and the categories it covers agree across several legal-practice sources, but the article number was left out of the body.
  • The grace period on fines — press coverage reports a period of guidance instead of fines early in implementation, but its length and scope could not be pinned to a ministry document.
  • Publication timing for the commentary and checklists — the plan to prepare and distribute them was announced; no specific schedule appears in public material.

Conclusion: what is doable this quarter

Compressed to a sentence: what arrived is not a penalty regime but a document that will be cited. Its force comes from being carried into other documents, and that route runs through sector standards, procurement scoring sheets, and contract annexes. The response is artefact maintenance rather than a compliance sweep.

Lay the three values and seven principles back out and most of the asks are process and record rather than outcome. Against norms like that, an organisation's state reduces to one question. When asked, does a document appear?

Three things are doable this quarter. First, list the automated paths that can disadvantage a person. Second, pick one and find who last changed its threshold, and when. Third, if that takes longer than thirty seconds, it is the first backlog item. Voluntary norm or statutory duty, both arrive at the same question — does a person's name survive on each decision?
