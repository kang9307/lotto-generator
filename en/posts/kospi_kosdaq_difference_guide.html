KOSPI, KOSDAQ and KONEX - What Korea's Market Split Actually Means

Different listing gates produce different companies, and therefore a different checklist

2026-07-29 Finance & Economy

Introduction: the phrase "KOSDAQ is the second division" is wrong from the first word

This article is about the Korean stock market and Korean regulation. All rules, thresholds and tax rates below come from Korea Exchange, the Korean Ministry of Government Legislation, the Financial Services Commission and the National Tax Service, and they apply to securities listed in Korea. Readers outside Korea should treat this as a description of one national market structure rather than a general model.

One of the first things a new investor in Korea learns is the informal ranking: KOSPI is the first division and KOSDAQ is the second. Big companies sit in one, small companies sit in the other, and the small ones are presumed to be those that could not get into the big one. That description captures something real, but it leads to conclusions that are wrong in both directions.

Read as a ranking, it produces either "avoid KOSDAQ because it is risky" or "KOSPI is safe." Neither helps. The first ignores that many KOSDAQ-listed firms never aimed at the Securities Market in the first place. The second ignores that watch-list designation and delisting exist in the Securities Market too.

This article proposes a different frame. The market split is not a grade. It is a statement about which risks each market was designed to carry. Korea Exchange operates three equity markets, and each one asks for different things at the door so that different kinds of company can raise capital. One market asks for a record that has already accumulated. Another assesses technology and business potential instead of realised results.

That gives the article its spine. Different listing requirements mean the companies that pass are different, which means what an investor must verify is different. A company that proved profit should be examined for the durability of that profit. A company admitted on a technology assessment with no profit should be examined for how long its cash lasts. Read both financial statements the same way and one of them will be misread.

So this is not a market tour. It is organised around the gates of entry and exit: what the gates are, what they filter, what filters apply again after admission, and what actually happens to an investor when a stock leaves the market. Trading mechanics themselves, such as session hours, tick sizes and settlement, are covered separately in the guide to Korean stock market trading rules; here we only establish whether they differ between markets at all.

Figures were verified in July 2026 against Korea Exchange, the Ministry of Government Legislation, the Financial Services Commission and the National Tax Service. Listing and delisting criteria in Korea are amended often. Where a figure could not be confirmed, no number was invented; the article says where to look instead.

1. Three markets separated by purpose, not by grade

Korea Exchange operates three equity markets: the Securities Market, widely known as KOSPI, the KOSDAQ Market, and the KONEX Market. The ranking reading is tempting because the largest companies really are concentrated in the Securities Market. But the exchange's own market introductions do not describe them as tiers. They describe them as markets opened for different purposes.

The Securities Market opened in 1956 and is presented by the exchange as Korea's representative securities market, whose role is to develop the capital market through the listing of large, sound companies and to support their overseas fundraising and international expansion. KOSDAQ, by contrast, was opened in July 1996, and the exchange presents it as a market created so that information technology, biotechnology and culture technology firms and venture companies could raise capital. Put the two sentences side by side and the relationship is clearly about use, not about rank.

KONEX is the most recent and has the sharpest purpose. Exchange guidance describes it as a market dedicated to early-stage small and venture companies, opened because small company funding in Korea was concentrated in bank lending and because the time required to reach a KOSDAQ listing had lengthened. The exchange notes that as investor protection on KOSDAQ was strengthened, that market shifted toward more mature small companies and its entry barrier rose. KONEX was therefore designed separately for companies at an earlier stage than KOSDAQ now accommodates.

The second-to-last row carries the article. KOSDAQ has a track that permits listing with no profit at all. That single fact converts the relationship from "the same standard applied more loosely" into "a different standard entirely." The detail follows below.

One more point about KONEX. Exchange guidance states that an applicant must sign a designated adviser agreement with one securities firm licensed for underwriting before it can apply for a new listing. In other words, a procedural gatekeeper replaces a financial threshold. KONEX-listed companies are also exempt from mandatory adoption of Korean IFRS and from auditor designation, and exempt from the duty to appoint outside directors and a standing auditor. For an investor the practical reading is that the density of disclosure and governance information is lower than in the other two markets.

2. Securities Market listing requirements - what is asked at the door

Now the gates, one at a time. Under Korea Exchange listing guidance, a new listing in the Securities Market (ordinary company) involves the following headline items. Figures were checked in July 2026, and many carry conditions and exceptions, so precise application requires the original listing regulations.

  • Operating history - at least three years since incorporation with continuous business activity.
  • Size - shareholders equity of 30 billion won or more and one million or more listed shares.
  • Share distribution - 500 or more general shareholders. The share-count condition has several alternative forms, such as a general shareholder ownership ratio of 25 percent or more or five million shares or more, with a 10 percent threshold where shares to be listed reach fifty million or more.
  • Transferability - no transfer restriction on the issued shares.
  • Audit opinion - unqualified for the most recent fiscal year, and unqualified or qualified for the two preceding years.
  • Lock-up - shares held by the largest shareholder and newly issued shares are held for six months after listing.

The operating performance requirement is satisfied by meeting any one of five alternatives. That structure is itself information: the exchange does not look at profit alone. It combines revenue scale, market capitalisation and equity into several routes.

  • Revenue of 100 billion won or more most recently and 70 billion won or more on a three-year average, together with a return-on-equity or pre-tax profit condition such as 3 billion won most recently and 6 billion won over three years combined.
  • Most recent revenue of 100 billion won or more with a base market capitalisation of 200 billion won or more.
  • Base market capitalisation of 200 billion won or more with most recent pre-tax profit of 5 billion won or more.
  • Base market capitalisation of 500 billion won or more with shareholders equity of 150 billion won or more.
  • Base market capitalisation of 1 trillion won or more, on its own.

The last alternative deserves attention. At a base market capitalisation of one trillion won, no separate profit condition attaches. So a route to listing without profit formally exists in the Securities Market as well. But the threshold is a trillion won, so in practice it is open only to very large firms. Its character is entirely different from the KOSDAQ technology track. One applies a different yardstick because the company is enormous; the other applies a different yardstick because the company is small but holds technology.

3. KOSDAQ listing requirements - a different answer to the same question

Placed beside the Securities Market, KOSDAQ reads less like "looser" and more like "a different axis." Under exchange guidance, the ordinary company track is satisfied by any one of the following. Figures in brackets are the eased thresholds applied to venture companies.

  • Pre-tax profit from continuing operations of 2 billion won (venture: 1 billion won) together with market capitalisation of 9 billion won.
  • Pre-tax profit from continuing operations of 2 billion won (venture: 1 billion won) together with shareholders equity of 3 billion won (venture: 1.5 billion won).
  • Pre-tax profit from continuing operations present at all, market capitalisation of 20 billion won, and revenue of 10 billion won (venture: 5 billion won).
  • Pre-tax profit from continuing operations of 5 billion won.

Share distribution requires 500 or more minority shareholders as of the application date, with a separate public offering ratio condition. The audit opinion must be unqualified for the most recent fiscal year. The three-year operating history and the 30 billion won equity floor demanded by the Securities Market do not appear in this track. Instead, profit is required in a far smaller absolute amount.

The last row is the hinge of the whole article. Both markets have a "list without profit" route, but one is a door for giants and the other is a door for small firms holding technology. Treating them as equivalent misjudges the size of the risk entirely.

Figures deliberately left out should also be named. The detailed KOSDAQ public offering ratio conditions, the exceptions inside the Securities Market share distribution rule, and KONEX financial requirements could not be confirmed in settled form from the exchange's introductory pages alone. Those must be read in the Korea Exchange listing regulations and enforcement rules. Filling such gaps with plausible-looking numbers is the most common error in writing of this kind.

4. Technology special listing - the route that does not require profit

The KOSDAQ technology growth company track is the most important item here, because its existence means KOSDAQ legitimately contains listed companies that cannot be valued from an income statement.

The exchange explains the purpose as replacing a review centred on past operating performance with a review of technology and business potential that can predict future performance. The axis moves from the past to the future. To give that prediction some objectivity, the exchange operates a specialist assessment system.

TrackSize requirementCore gateWhat it means for an investor
Innovative technology trackEquity 1 billion won, market cap 9 billion wonTechnology evaluation at grade A and grade BBB or betterA third-party technology grade is the basis of admission
Business model trackEquity 1 billion won, market cap 9 billion wonRecommendation by the listing underwriter after a business model assessmentA securities firm's judgement is the basis of admission
Ordinary trackProfit, revenue and market cap combinationsRealised operating performanceThe numbers already exist

The responsibility of the listing underwriter must be flagged. The improvement plan for the technology special listing system announced by the Financial Services Commission on 27 July 2023 identified insufficient accountability among underwriters as a problem, and for companies that deteriorate within two years of listing it introduced a six-month put-back option, an extension of the lock-up on underwritten shares from three months to six months, and disclosure of each underwriter's technology special listing count and returns. The same plan also included a leading-edge technology exception for firms in national strategic technology fields with market capitalisation of 100 billion won or more.

The existence of that plan is itself information. The regulator treated "deterioration within two years of listing" as a real phenomenon and rewrote the rules accordingly. That does not mean technology-track companies necessarily fail. It means the possibility is already built into the design of the system.

  • A technology grade is not a guarantee of future results. It is a third-party view of technical merit; when and how much that technology converts into revenue is not what is graded.
  • The business model track rests on the underwriter's recommendation. Which securities firm sponsored the listing, and how its earlier technology-track deals performed, therefore becomes a checkable item.
  • Offering prices are often set on projected results. When a price is built on results that have not yet appeared, the correction can be large if the assumption breaks.
  • Lock-up expiry dates are public. When existing holders become free to sell can be known in advance.
  • Companies admitted through the exception face different exit rules too. The relief provisions in the next section connect directly here.

Retail investors usually meet such companies at the offering stage. The subscription process and allocation mechanics are covered separately in the complete guide to IPO subscription in Korea.

5. How to read the financials of a technology-track company

Reading the statements of a company with almost no revenue and continuing operating losses in the ordinary way yields no information at all. The price-earnings ratio does not compute, the operating margin is negative, and revenue growth is meaningless because the denominator is tiny. The items being read must change.

What follows uses a fictional company, Company A. It is not a real issuer and every figure is invented. Company A listed through a technology track, has annual revenue of 2 billion won, an annual operating loss of 12 billion won, and cash and cash equivalents of 40 billion won.

  • Burn rate - if 12 billion won leaves each year, a simple calculation gives a little over three years of runway. This is the single most important number for this company. What matters is not the size of the loss but how many more years of loss can be absorbed.
  • Traces of further fundraising - if revenue has not arrived within three years, a rights issue or convertible bond becomes necessary, which dilutes existing holders. Past fundraising history and terms must be read in the disclosures.
  • The nature of the revenue - whether that 2 billion won is product sales, a government project or research contract, or a one-off technology fee determines whether it repeats at all. The revenue breakdown sits in the notes.
  • Development progress - clinical phase for a biotech, mass-production validation for a materials or components firm: the pre-revenue progress indicator is the real measure of advancement.
  • Capitalisation of development costs - whether research and development spending is expensed or capitalised as an intangible changes both profit and assets. Heavy capitalisation makes losses look smaller than they are.
  • Remaining relief period - as the next section shows, technology growth companies get temporary exemption from the revenue and loss criteria. The fiscal year in which relief ends is effectively the deadline.
  • Remaining insider and institutional holdings - the lock-up expiry schedule and the volume still locked feed straight into supply and demand.
  • Audit opinion and going-concern language - whether the audit report carries a material uncertainty related to going concern is the fastest available warning.

In short: for an ordinary company, read the income statement first; for a technology-track company, read the cash flow statement and the notes first. The checklist below sets out the order in which to pull items from the disclosures.

[Order of checks for a technology-track listing]
1. Which track was used (technology evaluation / business model / ordinary)
2. Which fiscal year contains the listing date (start of the relief clock)
3. Cash and cash equivalents balance
4. Operating cash flow over the last 12 months (size of the outflow)
5. Item 3 divided by item 4 = months of runway (rough estimate)
6. Revenue mix (product / services / government project / technology fee)
7. R and D spending expensed versus capitalised
8. Rights issues and convertible bonds over the last three years
9. Lock-up expiry dates and the volume released
10. Going-concern uncertainty paragraph in the audit report
11. Fiscal years remaining on revenue and loss criteria relief
12. Distance from watch-list thresholds (market cap / equity / share price)

Item 5 is a question, not a calculation. A short runway means the next fundraising is close, and fundraising generally weighs on the share price. A long runway means more time has been bought. Either way, that number does not appear on the income statement.

6. Watch-list designation does not arrive unannounced

Listing is a beginning, not an end. A listed company that falls below certain thresholds is designated an administrative issue, commonly called a watch-list stock. The Korean plain-language legal information service describes the mechanism as managing, in advance, companies at risk of losing their suitability as a listed share. It is a warning light that comes on before exit.

What matters is that most designation causes come from numbers that were already public. Revenue, capital impairment ratio, shareholders equity, market capitalisation, share price, trading volume and share distribution all appear in periodic reports or on a price screen, and the audit opinion appears in the audit report. For anyone who reads disclosures there is warning; only for those who do not is it sudden.

Read the table across rather than down and the difference in character appears. KOSDAQ carries several items the Securities Market does not have: equity below 1 billion won, a close below 1,000 won, and the continuing-operations loss test. Those exist because the market was designed around smaller companies. Conversely the Securities Market sets its revenue line three times higher at 30 billion won. The line differs because the size of the companies differs, not because one regulator is stricter.

Technology growth companies receive relief. Under exchange guidance, a technology growth company outside the premier segment is exempt from the revenue criterion for five fiscal years including the year of listing, and from the continuing-operations loss criterion for three fiscal years. The conditions attached to that relief have been amended recently, so the terms can depend on when the preliminary listing review was filed. Remaining relief for an individual issuer must be confirmed with exchange guidance and company disclosures.

The implication is direct. During relief, losses and absent revenue are not exit causes. So financial statements filed during that window do not mean "no problem yet." The real deadline is the fiscal year in which relief expires, and as it approaches, whether revenue has appeared weighs more and more on the price.

7. Delisting - mechanical causes and substantive review

After the watch list comes delisting, and here two distinct routes must be separated.

The first is mechanical. Where a defined condition is met, the process proceeds without further qualitative judgement. Based on the plain-language legal service and exchange material, this includes an adverse audit opinion or a disclaimer, full impairment of capital, two consecutive years of failed share distribution, dissolution, final default or suspension of bank transactions, and failure to file an annual report after watch-list designation. Many watch-list causes also convert into delisting if they remain unresolved the following year.

The second is the substantive listing eligibility review. The exchange assesses the continuity of the business, transparency of management, and the realisation of the public interest and investor protection as a whole. In the Securities Market, two consecutive years of revenue below 30 billion won, two consecutive years of capital impairment of 50 percent or more, and cumulative disclosure violation penalty points are described as routes into that review.

  • Mechanical causes are predictable but fast. The moment a condition is met the process starts. Audit opinions are the clearest case, which is why incidents cluster around the end of March when audit reports are filed.
  • Substantive review is hard to predict but slow. Trading is generally suspended while the review runs, so a holder can neither sell nor buy until a decision arrives.
  • Trading suspension is itself a risk. The option of cutting the position disappears. In risk management terms this is the most painful part.
  • There are objection and improvement-period procedures. A delisting decision is not always final at once, so an outcome can be postponed for months.
  • Delisting does not mean the company has ceased to exist. The company may continue while its shares no longer trade. For an investor, the practical result is that liquidity disappears.
  • The Securities Market has delisting too. This is not a KOSDAQ-only phenomenon. The thresholds differ; the mechanism exists in both.

Consider a fictional example. Company B is listed on KOSDAQ. Three years ago its revenue was 18 billion won; last year it was 9.5 billion won. That falls under the 10 billion won revenue threshold and can trigger watch-list designation. What investors most often miss here is the optimism of "it can recover this year." Under the KOSDAQ framework, failing to reach 10 billion won again in the year after designation is a delisting cause. In other words, the window for recovery is one year. Knowing that structure changes the weight of a headline that says revenue slipped a little.

8. Liquidation trading - what happens in the final seven trading days

Once delisting is confirmed, a liquidation trading window opens. The Korean plain-language legal information service states that when a security is delisted, trading is permitted for a period not exceeding seven days counted in trading days. The window exists to give holders a final opportunity to sell, not as a designed discount window.

Trading works differently during it. Under Korea Exchange guidance, issues in liquidation trading are matched by periodic single-price auction to limit violent price swings, and the daily price limit does not apply. Korea Exchange guidance on the KONEX trading system likewise states that issues under liquidation trading are not subject to a price limit.

  • No price limit means a fall of 90 percent in a day, or a rise of several multiples, is possible within the rules. The usual intuition about upper and lower limits does not apply.
  • Single-price auction means orders do not execute at a chosen moment or a chosen price. Orders accumulate over an interval and clear together.
  • Volume often spikes. Not because interest has risen, but because every remaining holder must sell in the same short window.
  • When the window closes, trading ends. After that, finding a counterparty off-exchange is effectively the only remaining route.
  • Trading suspension usually precedes liquidation trading. During suspension, no action of any kind is possible.
  • Wild price movement attracts short-term traders. But the fact that the asset stops trading in a few days outranks any other consideration.

The most practical way to understand liquidation trading is in reverse. Reaching it means a delisting decision came before it, a substantive review or a mechanical cause came before that, a watch-list designation came before that, and a number printed in a periodic report came before all of it. Information that was available at the first step is worthless at the last.

9. Index levels - 3000 and 900 are not comparable numbers

The most frequently misread part of the market split is the index number itself. If KOSPI stands at 3000 and KOSDAQ at 900, KOSPI looks far higher. But the two indices start from different lines.

According to the Korean national indicator system, in material prepared by the Capital Markets Division of the Financial Services Commission, the KOSPI index runs from a base date of 4 January 1980 with a base value of 100, and the KOSDAQ index runs from a base date of 1 July 1996 with a base value of 1,000. Both are market-capitalisation weighted. Korea Exchange describes the KOSPI calculation as market capitalisation at the comparison point divided by market capitalisation at the base point, multiplied by 100.

What a level of 900 meansNot applicableAbout 0.9 times the base dateBelow the starting line
Directly comparableNoConvert to returns over the same period

The point is simple. Absolute index levels cannot be used to compare markets. A KOSDAQ level of 900 means the market's capitalisation today is slightly below where it was in July 1996, not that it is one third of KOSPI. To compare, pick a common start date, set it to 100, and compute each market's change from there.

One more thing. An index tracks the capitalisation of whatever is listed in that market at the time. Listings and delistings happen continuously, so the constituent set changes over time. When the character of a market changes, so does the meaning of its index. Reading a long period of a low KOSDAQ index as "KOSDAQ companies are bad" leads somewhere quite different from reading it as "this market turns over its listings quickly."

10. What is the same and what differs - trading rules and taxes

One misconception is worth settling here. Many people assume KOSDAQ trades under different rules from KOSPI, but the core items are identical.

Under the Korean plain-language legal information service, the regular session runs from 09:00 to 15:30, with order acceptance from 08:30. That schedule is the same in the Securities Market and KOSDAQ. Tick sizes are also identical between the two markets in the same source, with matching bands such as 1 won below 2,000 won and 10 won from 5,000 won. The daily price limit is likewise shared: Korea Exchange trading guidance states that a limit of plus or minus 30 percent applies against the previous closing price, and that band applies to both markets. KONEX, however, uses plus or minus 15 percent against its base price, widening to 30 percent for off-hours block trades.

The transaction tax deserves particular attention in 2026. Under the current text of Article 5 of the Enforcement Decree of the Securities Transaction Tax Act on the Korea Law Information Center, shares transferred in the Securities Market are taxed at 5 out of 10,000, or 0.05 percent, KONEX at 10 out of 10,000, or 0.1 percent, and KOSDAQ at 20 out of 10,000, or 0.2 percent. The provision was checked as effective from 2 January 2026.

On top of that sits the special rural development tax. Under the National Tax Service enforcement standards for the securities transaction tax, shares transferred in the Securities Market bear 1.5 out of 1,000, or 0.15 percent, of the transfer value as special rural development tax. KOSDAQ shares are not subject to it. The result is that the total deducted on sale is 0.20 percent for KOSPI and 0.20 percent for KOSDAQ, that is, the same.

  • This is what changed in 2026. Previously the Securities Market transaction tax rate had been reduced, so there were periods when the total burden was lower than on KOSDAQ. Under the provision in force in 2026 the totals match.
  • KONEX is half, at 0.10 percent. Its character as an early-stage market is reflected in the rate.
  • Securities transaction tax applies on sale whether there is a gain or a loss. It is paid even when selling at a loss.
  • Large shareholder ownership ratios differ by market. Under Article 157 of the Enforcement Decree of the Income Tax Act the thresholds are 1 percent for KOSPI, 2 percent for KOSDAQ and 4 percent for KONEX, while the value test is a common 5 billion won per issue.
  • The reason the ratios differ also comes from market character. In a smaller market the same amount of money buys a higher ownership ratio, so the ratio test is relaxed.
  • Tax law is amended every year. These figures were checked in July 2026 and must be re-checked with the National Tax Service and the Korea Law Information Center before any actual sale.

The opposite deserves saying too. Watch-list designation is not the same as delisting. Companies do resolve the cause and come off the list. But the process typically involves rights issues or asset sales, which reach existing shareholders as dilution or a smaller business. Nor does removal from the list imply a price recovery. 12. Frequently asked questions

Q. Is KOSDAQ the second division of KOSPI?

No. Korea Exchange presents the Securities Market as a market centred on large, sound companies and KOSDAQ as a market opened in July 1996 for information technology, biotechnology and culture technology firms and venture companies. Different purposes produce different entry requirements. What matters more in practice is that when requirements differ, the checklist an investor must work through differs too.

Q. How can a technology-track company list without profit?

Because KOSDAQ contains a track that does not require profit. The innovative technology track adds a specialist technology evaluation at grade A and grade BBB or better on top of equity of 1 billion won and market capitalisation of 9 billion won; the business model track adds a listing underwriter's recommendation to the same size thresholds. For an investor this means a company that cannot be valued from its income statement, so cash, burn rate and remaining relief must be read instead.

Q. Does watch-list designation arrive suddenly?

The designation is a notice, but the cause usually sits in numbers already public. Revenue, capital impairment, equity, market capitalisation, share price and trading volume appear in periodic reports and prices, and the audit opinion appears in the audit report. The substantive listing eligibility review is the exception, because qualitative judgement makes it hard to predict from numbers alone.

Q. Is liquidation trading a chance to buy cheaply?

Liquidation trading is the last exit for a stock whose delisting is already decided. It runs for no more than seven trading days, orders clear by periodic single-price auction, and no daily price limit applies. That the asset stops trading within days outranks every other consideration.

Q. KOSPI 3000 versus KOSDAQ 900 - which rose more?

They cannot be compared directly. KOSPI runs from a base value of 100 on 4 January 1980 and KOSDAQ from a base value of 1,000 on 1 July 1996. So 3000 is roughly thirty times its base and 900 is roughly 0.9 times its base. Comparison requires returns measured over the same period.

Conclusion - the market split is a risk notice, not a ranking table

Three sentences summarise the article. First, KOSPI, KOSDAQ and KONEX are not grades but markets designed for different purposes, as the exchange's own documents state by describing different target companies. Second, because listing requirements differ, the companies that pass differ, and so does what an investor must verify. A firm that proved profit and a firm admitted on a technology assessment cannot be read the same way. Third, exit mechanisms exist in every market, and their signals usually appear in disclosures first.

Four practical takeaways. One, when examining a security, ask which gate it passed through before asking which market it sits in. For a technology-track listing the reading order of the financial statements must change. Two, learn the watch-list thresholds and measure the current distance from them. Revenue, impairment ratio, market capitalisation and share price are all public. Three, for a company under relief, write the fiscal year in which relief ends on a calendar. Four, remember that liquidation trading is a termination procedure, not an opportunity.

The misconceptions to correct are also worth restating. KOSPI and KOSDAQ share the same session hours, the same tick sizes, and the same daily price limit of plus or minus 30 percent. As of 2026, the total tax burden on sale has also converged at 0.20 percent. What differs is listing requirements, the lines drawn for exit, the large shareholder ownership ratios, and the resulting character of the listed population. To the extent that "KOSDAQ is riskier" holds, it holds not because the rules are riskier but because the companies that pass that gate are different.

Finally, a word about the nature of this information. Listing and delisting rules in Korea change often. The technology special listing system was reformed in 2023, the securities transaction tax changed in 2026, and the conditions attached to technology growth company relief were adjusted recently. Much of what remains online reaches conclusions using figures that are no longer current. The sources below therefore record which institution's material was used for each claim. The habit of verifying outlasts any memorised number.
