The facts here come from four Phoronix reports (2025-11-13, 2025-12-22, 2026-01-01 and 2026-10-03) and two official documents (the Linux kernel documentation and the lspci manual). The Occasion — A Year of Work, Not Just One Talk

On October 3, 2026 (US Eastern time), Phoronix published an article summarizing the work of Timur Kristóf of Valve's Linux graphics driver team. According to the article, over the past year he made multiple improvements to the AMDGPU kernel driver so that graphics cards and APUs of the GCN 1.0 and 1.1 generations, hardware from roughly a decade ago, can better handle Linux gaming and other tasks. He presented this work the same week at XDC2026 in Toronto, Canada (XDC being the X.Org Developers Conference, a venue for people who build the Linux graphics stack), and the article says a video of the talk and PDF slides are available. The purpose the article gives is to make these aging cards more viable for Linux gaming use in 2026 and beyond. The article also judges that AMD had not devoted many resources to driver improvements for these old cards in years and that Kristóf, on Valve's Linux team, picked up the slack, which is the assessment of the article's author.

On GeekNews the article was summarized with the phrase about an approximately 30% performance improvement. That 30% figure, however, is not a result of this talk. The article links to a separate benchmark article from last year (the roughly 30% performance gain old Radeon cards got with Linux 6.19) only as a reference for how large the impact was. This explainer reads the measurement conditions of that benchmark article separately and writes out what the number is a percentage of.

For a reader with an old GPU, what matters narrows to three things: which driver path changed, what is different as a result, and what has not been decided yet. General ways of reviving an old PC are covered in the post on reviving old PCs with lightweight Linux, so this explainer stays with the AMD GPU driver path.

The Target GPUs — GCN 1.0 and 1.1, the Generation of About 2012

GCN stands for Graphics Core Next, the name of an AMD architecture, and 1.0 and 1.1 are its first two generations. Phoronix calls them Southern Islands (GCN 1.0) and Sea Islands (GCN 1.1). The kernel documentation uses the same abbreviations: SI is first generation GCN and CIK is second generation GCN, and it states that both are supported by the old radeon driver and the new amdgpu driver.

The Phoronix article reproduces the patch description that Kristóf sent on November 13, 2025, together with a link to the patch in the amd-gfx mailing list archive, and it contains a concrete list of SI-family discrete GPUs. It is the patch message as reproduced by Phoronix, and the years are given only as ranges such as "Tahiti (2012~2014)".

Chip nameYearsRepresentative products in the patch description
Tahiti2012-2014Radeon HD 7870 XT, 7950, 7970, 7990, R9 280 series, FirePro W8000 and W9000
Pitcairn2012-2015Radeon HD 7850 and 7870, R9 265, R9 270/370 series, FirePro W5000 and W7000
Cape Verde2012-2016Radeon HD 7730, 7750 and 7770, R7 250E, 250X, 350 and 450, FirePro W600
Oland2013-2019Radeon HD 8570 and 8670, R5 240, 250, 330, 340, 350, 430, 520 and 610, FirePro W2100
Hainan2013-2016Various mobile GPUs

The product names in the table are only part of the list in the patch description, which also includes the 8000 series, FirePro variants, Radeon Sky and more. This table covers the GCN 1.0 (SI) family. The chip names and product list of the GCN 1.1 (CIK) family are not in the articles consulted, so they are not given. It is clear, though, that GCN 1.1 products are part of the same work, since later reports name cases such as the Radeon HD 7790.

The patch description also states directly what these GPUs can actually do. Tahiti and Pitcairn can play some modern games at lower resolutions and lower frame rates, held back mainly by their small VRAM of 2 to 3 GiB. The other SI gaming GPUs are mainly useful for games from their own era, the mid-2010s, or for less demanding games in general. This is the sentence to hold on to first when setting expectations, because it comes from the developer who made the change rather than from a commentator, and it sets the ceiling on what the driver work can do for these cards.

The Driver Path Changes — From Kernel radeon to amdgpu

The path by which Linux uses a GPU has two layers. The lower layer is the kernel driver that talks to the hardware directly, and the upper layer is the user-space (Mesa) driver that translates OpenGL or Vulkan calls. In the user-space layer, Phoronix names RadeonSI Gallium3D for OpenGL and RADV for Vulkan. The heart of this story is the lower layer. For this generation of GPUs the default kernel driver had been radeon for the past two decades and more, and switching to amdgpu required specifying kernel module parameters by hand.

The kernel documentation describes those parameters as follows. The amdgpu parameters si_support and cik_support decide whether SI and CIK are supported, with 1 meaning enabled, 0 meaning disabled and -1 meaning the default. radeon has parameters of the same names, and the documentation states that when one side is enabled the other should be disabled. The documentation also says that SI discrete GPUs are supported by amdgpu by default.

According to Phoronix (2025-12-22), with Linux 6.19 the default kernel driver for GCN 1.0 and 1.1 GPUs changed from radeon to amdgpu. The article explains that switching to amdgpu through module parameters had been possible for several years, but that AMD allowed the default to change only this year, after Valve's work brought feature parity. In other words, the decision to change the default became possible not because of performance but once the condition that everything radeon offered also exists in amdgpu was met.

The article gives three reasons why amdgpu is the right driver.

  • amdgpu supports graphics processors up to the latest CDNA and RDNA4 and is actively improved.
  • The radeon driver has seen little new feature work in recent years and continues with occasional fixes and maintenance only.
  • The RADV Vulkan driver is implemented only for the amdgpu kernel driver, so after the switch Vulkan works out of the box on these old GPUs.

Put simply, the card is the same and the Mesa OpenGL driver is the same, but the kernel driver underneath is different, and that difference decides whether the Vulkan layer can run at all. Kristóf's patch message of November 2025 carries the same intent. Compared to the old radeon driver, amdgpu offers better performance, more display features through DC (Display Core), and Vulkan 1.3 support through RADV. Phoronix summarized that if the patch is accepted, all AMD GPUs from GCN onward will use amdgpu by default. At the time of the proposal it was written that it was not settled whether this would land in the 6.19 cycle or be pushed to the following cycle, likely Linux 7.0, and the December article was written on the premise of the switch in 6.19.

What Was Fixed — Display, Power Management, Soft Reset

To change the default, what worked on radeon had to work on amdgpu as well. The October 2026 article summarizes what Kristóf handled along the way as defects in the amdgpu display code, several power management issues, and then added soft reset support and other improvements. He is someone who spent years on the user-space Mesa 3D driver code, and the article says he began this kernel driver work as a kernel development exercise.

The details that Phoronix's patch articles point out are as follows.

  • Analog video connectors. The article says Kristóf added analog video connector support to the AMDGPU DC code. A separate article title also says that amdgpu in Linux 6.19 supports analog connectors on GCN 1.0 GPUs.
  • VCE1 support. The patch message says that after DC analog connector support and VCE1 support landed, SI discrete GPUs reached feature parity with the old radeon driver. The source article gives no explanation of what VCE is, so this explainer does not explain it either.
  • Soft reset. The October 2026 article says only that soft reset support was added. How it works and how far it applies are not explained.

This list carries only the items the sources name. The number of defects and the kernel version in which each went in are not given as figures in the articles consulted. The talk slides may contain more detail, but the slide PDF was not opened.

Joining the accounts in time order shows the sequence. First DC analog connector and VCE1 support went in and made the feature set equal to the old radeon (the November 2025 patch message), then the default changed on top of that (Linux 6.19), and improvements such as soft reset continued afterward (the October 2026 article). This fits the earlier explanation that feature parity was the condition for switching the default. Because the sources give a sequence rather than a date for each fix, the order above is the most that can be said about timing, and nothing here should be read as a release schedule.

What the 30% Actually Is — The Conditions Behind the Number

The Phoronix benchmark article of December 22, 2025 uses roughly 30% in its title, but the statement in its text is a conditional average. The table below splits out the conditions the article gives, as stated.

ItemValue stated in the article
What was comparedThe radeon kernel driver against the amdgpu kernel driver on Linux 6.19
Test GPUOne Radeon HD 7950 (GCN 1.0 Southern Islands)
Test systemA Ryzen 9 9950X3D test box with an Ubuntu 25.10 host
User-space driversMesa 26.0-devel (the Mesa ACO PPA for reproducibility), RadeonSI Gallium3D and RADV
Scope of the 30%The average of the OpenGL tests that ran on both kernel drivers
Chart contentsOpenGL side: Unvanquished, Xonotic and others. Vulkan side: GravityMark, Batman: Arkham Knight, DiRT Rally 2.0, Strange Brigade, GPUScore: Breaking Limit and others. The chart descriptions say amdgpu was fastest in almost all of them, and one test, yquake2 at 1920x1080, is described as fastest with the radeon driver
Not included in the 30%The article describes only the OpenGL comparison as the basis of the 30%. It is not stated that the Vulkan test values go into this number

In plain terms, the number answers one question only: how much faster does the same card run OpenGL once the kernel driver underneath is swapped. It does not answer how fast any given game runs on any given old card. There are four points of caution in interpretation. First, the 30% is an OpenGL test average. The article explains it as a comparison in which only the kernel driver changed while the same RadeonSI Gallium3D driver was used. Second, the GPU is a single model, the HD 7950. The article has no statement that the same value appears on other cards of the same generation, especially other chips such as Cape Verde and Oland, or on GCN 1.1 cards. Third, the test machine used a current desktop CPU (Ryzen 9 9950X3D). Values with other CPUs are not covered by the article. Fourth, the last page of the article has a geometric mean chart of all test results, but the range the text calls 30% is the OpenGL comparison. Whether the average is arithmetic or geometric is not written in the text.

The article concludes that simply moving to amdgpu by default with Linux 6.19, with the same RadeonSI Gallium3D driver, can bring meaningful performance gains to those still using the Radeon HD 7000 series and similar GCN 1.0 and 1.1 graphics processors. It also says that the previous radeon versus amdgpu performance comparison it ran was in 2019, and judges that without this year's Valve work these GPUs would have stayed on radeon and never moved to amdgpu by default. That is the article author's assessment and not a judgment this explainer adds.

What Vulkan Opens Up and What It Does Not

RADV is Mesa's Vulkan driver for AMD. As seen above, it is implemented only on amdgpu, so the default driver switch means Vulkan is on by default. The article is restrained about this effect. RADV support will not let these old cards run the very latest big games, but some older games can use their Vulkan code paths, and performance can improve in Steam Play (Proton) when Direct3D 9 and newer run on top of Vulkan.

The effect is easier to judge if the pieces of that description are separated, because they rest on different kinds of evidence.

  • What opens up. The Vulkan API itself (the patch message mentions Vulkan 1.3), Vulkan paths in some games, and Proton's Direct3D translation path.
  • What does not open up. The VRAM capacity limit. The patch message wrote that even Tahiti and Pitcairn are mainly held back by low VRAM (2 to 3 GiB).
  • What has no figures. How many frames a particular game reaches on the Vulkan path. The article's Vulkan chart descriptions say only that AMDGPU was fastest, and it does not promise the felt performance of individual games.

Direct3D 9 and newer games reach Vulkan through the translation layer in Proton, and the article names that combination without giving a single frame rate. The Proton path adds one more translation layer, so results differ from card to card even for the same game. What the article says is a direction, that performance can improve, and there is no list of specific games. This explainer likewise does not say that a particular game works or does not work.

Which Path Is My GPU On — A Read-Only Check

Which kernel driver your card runs on can be checked from the device list. The documentation of pciutils lspci describes the -k option as showing the kernel drivers handling each device and also the kernel modules capable of handling it (it works only on Linux with kernel 2.6 or newer). The command below only prints output and changes nothing on the system.

lspci -k | grep -A 3 -E "VGA|Display"

In the output, look at whether the kernel driver entry is radeon or amdgpu. This command could not be run on a machine with an old AMD GPU, so no sample output is shown. Interpret the result with the table below.

What you seeWhat it can meanBasis
A GCN 1.0 or 1.1 card shows amdgpu on a Linux 6.19 or newer kernelIt looks like the path where the default switch has been appliedPhoronix article of 2025-12-22
It shows radeon on 6.19 or newerIt may be the effect of distribution kernel settings or parameters, but the cause is —Kernel documentation on si_support and cik_support
It shows radeon on a kernel older than 6.19The default path before the switchThe default switch statement in the same article
A CIK generation APUWhether the default has switched reads differently by sourceSee the section on what could not be confirmed

A distribution can turn SI and CIK support on or off through its own kernel configuration. The kernel documentation says SI and CIK support in amdgpu matters only when the corresponding build options (CONFIG_DRM_AMDGPU_SI and CONFIG_DRM_AMDGPU_CIK) are enabled. So the result can differ by distribution even for the same kernel version number. Changing the path by editing module parameters is outside the scope of this explainer. Work that touches boot settings should follow the distribution's documentation after a means of recovery has been prepared.

What Is Still Undecided — The 2026 Plans

Phoronix (2026-01-01) introduced a blog post Kristóf wrote on December 31, 2025 and reproduced the list of what he intended to do in 2026. These are plans, so it is not assumed that they were carried out. They are best read as one developer's stated to-do list rather than a roadmap with dates, because the article gives no date for any item.

  • Add DRM format modifier support to all SI, CIK, VI and Polaris GPUs. He explained that this would make purely Vulkan-based compositors and features such as Zink possible.
  • Add support for the TRAVIS and NUTMEG display bridges so that CIK APUs can also switch to amdgpu by default. He wrote that he could not find the hardware needed for the work.
  • Refactor SI and KV power management so that the legacy power management code can be retired.
  • Eventually retire the non-DC legacy display code from amdgpu.
  • Deal with known bugs. Named examples were the power limit on the Radeon 430, a black screen with the analog connector on the Radeon HD 7790, and VM faults on SI and CIK.
  • Verify sparse mapping (PRT) support. He said he had already written a kernel fix and a Mesa merge request.
  • Implement transfer queue support for old GPUs in RADV.

The article adds that some of these items are not limited to GCN 1.0 and 1.1 and would also help other GPUs such as Polaris. The items most directly relevant to users in this list are two: the default switch for CIK APUs and the known bugs. The October 2026 article does not restate item-by-item progress on this list, and the talk slides may hold the current status but were not opened. So the items in this section should be read only as plans as of the end of 2025.

What Could Not Be Confirmed

  • The contents of the XDC2026 talk video and slides. The PDF the article links to was not opened and the video was not watched. The number of bugs covered or the kernel version in which each fix landed cannot be known.
  • How the 30% average was calculated. The text says only that it is the OpenGL test average, and whether it is an arithmetic or geometric mean and the values of individual tests could not be confirmed.
  • Measured values for GPUs other than the HD 7950, and for GCN 1.1 cards. They are not in the article.
  • The concrete behavior of soft reset, its recovery success rate, and which symptoms trigger it.
  • Whether CIK generation APUs have switched by default. The kernel documentation says CIK discrete GPUs and APUs are supported by amdgpu by default, while the 2026 plan says display bridge support is needed to switch CIK APUs to amdgpu by default. Whether the two statements mean the same thing about the same target could not be confirmed.
  • The state, as of October 2026, of carrying out the items in the 2026 plan. However, Phoronix has an article titled "Mesa 26.3 RADV + RadeonSI Ready With DRM Format Modifiers For Older AMD GPUs", so only the existence of coverage related to the first item (DRM format modifiers) was confirmed; its body could not be opened, so its content is unknown. No item in the list carries a target date in the article, so even the intended timing is unknown.
  • The SI and CIK defaults of each distribution kernel after Linux 6.19. Per-distribution settings were not checked.
  • The October 2026 article has no benchmark figures of its own. The 30% is borrowed from the December 2025 article, and whether the same value reproduces with later kernel and Mesa versions could not be confirmed.
