How to Read AI Usage Pattern Reports About Software Teams

Telemetry and surveys are different instruments - the order in which to check the sample, the field of view, and the incentives

Aug 20, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: when a link lands in the channel

AI adoption doubled in six months. Pull requests are up 111 percent in two years. A link with a headline like that lands in a work channel, and within days those numbers show up in a budget discussion or a goal-setting doc. Nobody is acting in bad faith. The person who shared it opened the report and read the conclusion, and a conclusion never tells you the range of questions the study could actually answer.

This piece is about what to check, and in what order, when you open one of those links. The subject is not any particular tool's performance but the genre of usage-pattern research itself: why telemetry and surveys are different objects, how far the sample determines the conclusion, and which costs this kind of study structurally cannot see.

The main worked example is Linear's product usage report, published in August 2026. It is a good teaching text not because its conclusions are unusual but because it writes the limits of its own data into the body — a rare thing. Every figure quoted below carries its publisher and its character, meaning whether it is vendor telemetry or independent research.

If you are more interested in designing your own measurement, a separate article covers what to measure for AI adoption outcomes. This one takes the opposite side: how to read data somebody else produced.

1. Telemetry, survey, or experiment - start with the instrument

Before you look at a number, settle which apparatus produced it. Material on AI use in software teams comes in effectively three forms, and each one answers a different question.

  • Product telemetry — aggregated event logs from a tool. It answers: what was recorded?
  • Surveys and self-report — answers collected from people. It answers: what do people believe and say?
  • Controlled experiments — treatment assigned at random, outcomes measured. The only format that can answer: is this causal?

None of the three substitutes for the others. Telemetry does not know what anyone felt. A survey does not know what actually happened. Experiments are small enough that generalizing is hard. Three instruments disagreeing about one subject is normal, not contradictory.

2. The sample is the conclusion - no study covers all developers

The most frequently skipped sentence in usage research is who got counted. It decides more than half of what the conclusion means.

Self-selected surveys are answered by people with a reason to answer. The roughly 49,000 respondents Stack Overflow recruited on its own platform are people who still use Stack Overflow in 2026, which is already a specific habit profile. Paid-product telemetry has no response bias, but its population narrows to organizations paying for that product. A team that licenses an issue tracker and connects it to their repositories already has process discipline; teams without that tooling are absent from the statistics entirely.

This resolves a confusion practitioners hit constantly. AI adoption reads as 90 percent from DORA, 84 percent from Stack Overflow, and 30 percent for engineering in Linear's data, and none of them is wrong. The first two are answers to a question about whether you use AI tools. The third is a behavioral record of whether someone actually triggered an AI feature inside one specific product. Those three numbers cannot be placed on the same axis.

Reading order. Before the executive summary, go down to the methodology section and find three sentences: how large the sample is and who is in it, how it was recruited, and what period it covers. If those three are missing, it is safer not to quote the report's numbers at all.

3. Telemetry has bias too - the instrument's field of view is the sample

There is a saying that surveys lie but logs do not. It is half right. Logs do not lie, but anything not recorded is aggregated as though it did not exist.

This is exactly why the Linear report makes a good teaching text: it states the limits of its own field of view in the body. Two points carry the weight. First, it cannot see AI usage that happens outside Linear, so the picture is adoption inside its own customer base, not the market at large. Second, it only counts pull requests in repositories connected to the product, so anyone shipping outside that loop is invisible, which makes the numbers floors rather than ceilings.

Generalized into a rule: when reading a telemetry metric, ask whether this number could rise even if user behavior stayed identical. Several routes exist.

  • Changes in feature exposure — when a product surfaces AI behind a new button, the adoption curve traces the release curve, not a change in behavior. Check whether the steep segment overlaps a product launch.
  • Redefined events — if the threshold for counting someone as an active user changes, the time series breaks. Definition changes usually live only in a footnote.
  • Shifting membership — teams that migrate to another tool are either recorded as churn or not recorded at all. If heavy users stay and light users leave, the remaining average rises on its own.
  • Fixed versus rolling cohorts — tracking the same set of organizations excludes new customers; refilling the set each month makes new signups look like growth. The two point in opposite directions, so you need to know which one you have.
  • Bots mixed into the count — when items created by integration rules land in the same bucket as items created by an agent exercising judgment, the phrase created by AI loses its meaning.

4. Publisher incentives - vendor data is not disqualified

Discarding data because a vendor produced it is an overcorrection. Vendor telemetry has strengths independent research rarely achieves.

  • Scale — observation across tens of thousands of organizations is a size no academic experiment reaches.
  • Non-reactivity — the problem of people changing their answers because they know they are observed is smaller than in a survey.
  • Length of series — values accumulated the same way for more than two years are something a one-off survey cannot imitate.

The weaknesses are equally clear. The population is limited to customers, and which questions get asked and which sections get emphasized are hard to separate from the product narrative. Unfavorable results may simply never be published, and there is no way to check that from outside.

So the usable test in practice is not the type of source but whether the report contains sentences that are inconvenient for its publisher. Linear's report has several. It says an opened pull request says nothing about the value of the change, and it says there is no way of knowing whether the increased output led to positive business outcomes. A company selling the tool writing down that its own headline metric is uninformative is not common. A report containing that kind of sentence is more credible than one without it.

The reverse signal exists too. Material that lists only rising usage with no limitations section, or that ends on a claim that some percentage of adopting companies saw results without defining results, is closer to a marketing asset. Those numbers do not belong in an internal decision document.

5. When perception and measurement disagree

Answers diverge inside a single survey. In Stack Overflow's 2025 study, 51.6 percent reported a positive productivity impact from AI, while in the same study 46 percent said they actively distrust the accuracy of AI output, 66 percent reported hitting answers that are almost right but not quite, and 45.2 percent said debugging AI-generated code takes more time. Those four numbers are not fighting each other; they are measuring different axes. The first is satisfaction, the other three are friction.

The best-known head-on conflict between measurement and self-report is the randomized controlled trial the nonprofit METR published in July 2025. Sixteen experienced open-source developers worked 246 tasks in their own repositories, and in the condition where AI was allowed they took 19 percent longer — while the same people afterward estimated AI had made them 20 percent faster. The details of that trial are covered in the measurement-design article linked above.

There is a follow-up nobody should omit. In February 2026 METR published that it was acknowledging selection bias in its own experiment and changing the design. Developers who refuse to work without AI are increasingly missing from the sample, and between 30 and 50 percent of participants avoided submitting tasks they did not want to do without AI assistance. In other words, experiments are not free of bias either. Randomizing the treatment does not randomize who agrees to participate.

The call. When perception and measurement disagree, do not pick a winner — read it as two instruments measuring different things. Perception is a measure of cognitive load and satisfaction, which genuinely matters for retention and sustainability. It is simply not a substitute for elapsed time.

6. What grew is output, not value

The eye-catching figures in the Linear data are mostly output-class metrics. Pull requests are up 111 percent against a June 2024 baseline, and issues created by AI went from a negligible share in 2024 to close to half of all issues by August 2026. People outside engineering started shipping code too: the share of product managers attaching pull requests went from 3 percent to 10 percent in two years, and designers from 1 percent to 8 percent.

What these values say is clear: more things are being made. What they do not say is equally clear. Whether those artifacts are better, whether they survive longer, whether they reached a customer — none of that is in the data.

The passage to read alongside it is the observation that total time spent on product development went up rather than down. AI did not replace existing work; it stacked another layer on top of it. The old pattern where efficiency gains get consumed by more consumption shows up here too, and that dynamic has its own treatment in the article on the Jevons paradox.

The structural weakness of output metrics is that they have no denominator. For a doubling of pull requests to be good news, value per pull request has to hold, and that condition was never tested. The opposite result is common: split work into smaller pieces and the count rises while the size per change falls. By count it is an improvement; by total volume it is flat.

7. Read the splits, not the average

The genuinely useful part of usage research is rarely the total. It is where the groups diverge. Linear's data splits sharply by function.

The more interesting split is by team type. In the same report, teams using coding agents moved from 21 to 65 pull requests per week, while other teams moved from 8 to 10. This contrast gets quoted often, and most people quoting it drop the starting point.

The two groups were already at 21 versus 8 before the treatment. Agents may have raised output, or teams that already shipped a lot and leaned toward automation may have adopted agents first. Observational data cannot separate the selection effect from the treatment effect. That single sentence is the tool you will reach for most often when reading usage research.

A workable rule: if there is no evidence the comparison groups were alike before the treatment, do not quote the multiple. Carry the direction across and leave the size behind.

8. Three axes where gains and losses split

8.1 Type of work

Gains concentrate in work where the shape of a correct answer is fixed and repeated: scaffolding and boilerplate, test skeletons, format conversion, a regex or a shell one-liner, the first call against an unfamiliar API, a first draft of documentation. What these share is that you can verify correctness immediately.

Losses concentrate in judgment where right and wrong do not surface right away: boundary design, data model trade-offs, exchanging performance for readability, handling exceptions in domain rules, sequencing a migration. Here plausible-but-wrong output is easy to produce, and filtering it costs more than writing it would have. The almost right but not quite state that 66 percent of Stack Overflow respondents reported comes precisely from this territory.

8.2 Career stage

The common framing is gains for juniors, losses for seniors. In practice it is more accurate to say the direction of the gain is not what differs; the type of cost is.

  • People who hold the context pay a new cost to explain constraints they already carry in their heads. That METR's participants had worked their own repositories for years is not a detail you can drop from the interpretation.
  • People without the context pay less to explain but are less able to recognize when output is wrong. Verification shifts onto the reviewer.
  • Gains therefore tend to peak in the middle band: someone who knows the domain but is new to this particular stack.

8.3 Codebase maturity

A new project has no conventions to respect, so conventions the tool invents become the team's conventions. Less friction, but design nobody ever decided on hardens into place. A mature codebase carries piles of undocumented constraints the tool does not know, so violations appear constantly.

DORA's 2025 framing of AI as an amplifier is the same observation. Where the foundation is solid it acts as an accelerator; where processes are already misaligned, the misalignment spreads faster. That conclusion is more useful read backwards: this is the first reason a usage study's average does not apply to your team — nothing guarantees the surveyed organizations share your foundation.

9. Gains accrue to individuals, costs accrue to teams

Individuals reporting they got faster while team metrics stay flat is not a contradiction; it is the expected result. When authoring speeds up, volume piles onto the next stage.

  • Review capacity does not grow. Doubling pull requests does not double the number of people who can review or the hours of focus they have. A longer queue can push lead time up rather than down.
  • Review cost behaves differently from authoring cost. You know why you wrote your own code; with generated code you have to reconstruct the intent. The same line count takes longer to read.
  • Psychological ownership weakens. Changes you did not type attract less attachment, and an attitude of the reviewer will catch it creeps in. First-pass verification quietly transfers to the reviewer.
  • Non-engineer contributions blur boundaries. When product managers and designers start attaching pull requests, who owns that code and who gets paged for it becomes ambiguous.

The point that matters here is that this transfer barely registers in usage research. Studies count events, and reviewer fatigue and rubber-stamped approvals do not emit events. The closest traces are time from pull request open to merge, review round trips, and comments before approval — and usage reports generally do not report that segment.

10. New costs that appear at team scale

Scoping adoption cost to subscriptions and tokens will not give you a real unit cost. At team scale, new categories of maintenance appear.

  • Reserved review capacity — when output rises, review time has to be scheduled explicitly, or it slides into overtime.
  • Redefining standards — decide what counts as a team convention before tool-generated conventions become the de facto standard.
  • Security boundaries — automatically added dependencies, secrets that drift into prompts, and the credential scope an agent can reach all need to enter the review checklist.
  • Context assets — rule files, prompts, and external connection settings become maintained artifacts. When they go stale they quietly produce wrong results.
  • Contribution boundaries and on-call — settle responsibility in advance for cases where the person who shipped the code is not the person who gets paged.
  • Knowledge transfer — as fewer people can answer why this code is the way it is, onboarding cost for new hires arrives on a delay.

And what you actually watch should be outcome metrics, not usage. The minimum set is lead time from work start to deploy, change failure rate meaning the share of deploys that caused an incident or rollback, time to restore, and rework share meaning how often the same area is touched again after merge. How to define each and which traps to avoid is covered in the article on measurement traps and in the measurement-design piece linked earlier.

11. A checklist for reading usage research

Work through these in order when you open a report. If more than three come back without an answer, the material does not belong in an internal decision document.

Who is in the sample and how they were recruitedSelf-selection and paid customers are both specific populationsDo not generalize to all developers

Add a few questions for the moment you carry findings into your own organization, and the checklist becomes directly usable.

  • Do the surveyed organizations have a baseline capability similar to yours? Different pipelines, test coverage, and review culture produce different results.
  • Did the reported gains come from the kind of work you actually do most?
  • If you receive that gain, which stage grows a queue? Usually review and QA.
  • Could the same material support the opposite conclusion? If so, it is reference material, not a basis for a decision.
  • Can you carry the number across in one accurate sentence? If the qualifiers make the sentence too long to use, the number was never slide material.

What could not be verified

  • Raw data and aggregation code — the underlying dataset and aggregation logic for the Linear report are not public, so no reproduction check was possible. Figures here are reproduced as the publisher stated them.
  • How AI-created issues are classified — for the claim that AI authors nearly half of all issues, the definition separating automation rules and integration bots from actual agent judgment could not be confirmed.
  • Baseline comparability of groups — how teams using coding agents differed from other teams in company size, industry, or team size is not published. That is why the multiple is not treated as causal here.
  • DORA's recruitment method — whether the sample is random or self-selected is not stated in the public FAQ, and the methodology section of the report itself was not opened for this piece. Only respondent count and publisher are cited above.
  • Regional samples — no comparable usage telemetry restricted to a single national market was found. Every figure here comes from a global sample and does not transfer directly to any one country.
  • Output versus business results — none of the studies cited demonstrates that rising pull request counts translated into revenue, retention, or customer satisfaction.

Conclusion: a study is a questionnaire, not an answer

The most valuable part of a report like this is usually not the number on the cover but the stated limits buried in the body. It cannot see outside the product. An opened pull request says nothing about the value of the change. There is no way to know whether output turned into business outcomes. Those three lines are worth more in practice than the 111 percent on the cover.

Usage research is not a map showing where your organization sits. It is closer to a questionnaire telling you which questions to ask. Take direction from somebody else's data; measure magnitude in your own.

Three things to do immediately. First, next time a link like this arrives, open the methodology section before the conclusion. Second, when a number tempts you, write it out with its sample and definition attached in a single sentence; if you cannot, do not use it. Third, before you set any usage metric as a target, observe at least one outcome metric first. Without a baseline you will never know whether a later change was an improvement.
