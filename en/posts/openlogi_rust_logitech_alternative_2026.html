OpenLogi 2026 - A Rust, Local-First Alternative to Logitech Options+

Putting Mouse Settings Back in Your Hands - No Account, No Telemetry

Jun 8, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: Why Does a Mouse Setting Need an Account?

Say you buy a nice Logitech mouse. A model like the MX Master 4 has multiple buttons, lets you change DPI, and lets you toggle SmartShift (the switch between free-spinning and ratcheted wheel modes). But to touch those settings you have to install Logitech Options+, and you are often prompted to log into an account. All you wanted was to remap a single button - yet the install runs into hundreds of megabytes, a background process stays resident, and it is hard to know what is being sent where.

In June 2026, a project that topped GeekNews (news.hada.io) targets exactly this annoyance. It is called OpenLogi. Per the project, it is an open-source alternative to Logitech Options+ written in Rust that controls Logitech mice locally over the HID++ protocol, with no account and no telemetry. This article walks through what OpenLogi is trying to solve, what HID++ actually is, where it sits relative to existing open-source tools like Solaar, and the larger question lurking underneath: why has a mouse driver become so bloated in the first place?

1. The Problems with Logitech Options+

Let us be clear about the starting point. This is not to say Options+ is bad software. As the official tool, it offers everything through a GUI and integrates features like firmware updates and multi-device Flow. The problem is the cost that rides along with "one mouse setting." The commonly cited complaints fall into four buckets.

  • Account prompts: To use everything fully, you are often encouraged or required to sign into a Logitech account. Many users bristle at a cloud account inserting itself into local device settings.
  • A heavy, resident process: The install is large, and a background process runs constantly to detect devices. On macOS you get a notice that a background process was added to login items - and if you disable it, customization stops working.
  • Telemetry concerns: It is hard for users to see transparently what is collected and sent. The very fact that OpenLogi advertises "no analytics, no usage tracking, no crash phone-home" is evidence that this is a real user concern.
  • Fragility under OS policy changes: When Apple tightens input-monitoring and background-process security rules, there are reports of the official tool failing to keep up - getting stuck on a loading screen or having features partially blocked.

In short, there is a gap between the simple desire to "remap a mouse button" and the account, footprint, residency, and opacity you must accept to do it. That gap is exactly the opening OpenLogi slips into.

2. What Is OpenLogi - In One Sentence

OpenLogi in a sentence: "A Rust open-source tool that controls a Logitech mouse's buttons, DPI, and SmartShift locally over HID++, with no Logi Options+, no account, and no telemetry." Per the project, the core design principle is local-first. Everything happens on your machine, and bindings live in a plain TOML file.

The described mechanism is this. OpenLogi talks to Logitech HID++ mice over a Logi Bolt receiver, a direct Bluetooth connection, or a wired connection. Button presses are remapped through the OS event tap, and DPI and SmartShift changes are written straight to the device over HID++. In other words, it handles the device without running any resident Logitech software.

OpenLogi's message is simple: "No account required. Your data never leaves your machine." In the small domain of mouse settings, that simplicity turns out to be a surprisingly large differentiator.

2.1 Features (Per the Project)

According to the project's README and description, OpenLogi offers the following. Some numbers may shift between versions, so check the official docs alongside this.

  • Interactive GUI: A visual interface (a desktop app) where you click a mouse diagram to assign an action to each button.
  • Many built-in actions + custom shortcuts: Dozens of built-in actions, plus recorded keyboard shortcuts mapped to buttons.
  • DPI presets and a SmartShift panel: Adjust sensitivity steps and wheel behavior.
  • Per-application profiles: An overlay that swaps bindings depending on the running application.
  • CLI: A command-line tool for headless device inventory and diagnostics.
  • Localized UI: The settings window localized into several languages.

2. What HID++ Is - The Guts of Peripheral Control

To understand OpenLogi you need to know its foundation, HID++. The name is unfamiliar, but the idea is not hard.

The mice and keyboards we use follow a standard called HID (Human Interface Device) from the operating system's point of view. "Basic input" - moving the cursor, clicking, key presses - travels just fine over plain HID. That is why a mouse works the instant you plug it in, without a driver.

The trouble lies beyond that. Changing DPI across five steps, putting a macro on a button, switching the wheel into free-spin mode, reading the battery level - these "advanced features" cannot be expressed in the vocabulary of standard HID. So Logitech layered its own extension protocol, HID++, on top of standard HID.

Aspect Standard HID HID++ (Logitech extension)
Handles Cursor movement, clicks, key input DPI, button mapping, SmartShift, battery, firmware
Driver needed None (works on plug-in) Vendor software or a HID++ implementation
Example tools OS default input stack Options+, OpenLogi, Solaar

What is interesting is that HID++ 2.0 is designed as a "self-descriptive" protocol. That is, without knowing the device in advance, a tool can ask the device "what features do you have?" and read back a feature table. Each feature defines a set of functions, and the tool sends a request with the desired feature index and function ID. This structure is precisely why open-source tools like OpenLogi and Solaar can converse with a device without Logitech's proprietary software. Note that HID++ itself is a Logitech-proprietary spec; parts of it have been published externally, providing the basis for reverse engineering and open-source implementations.

3. Why Rust - What the Language Choice Means

The fact that OpenLogi is written in Rust is not a mere matter of taste. A peripheral-control tool sits in the background for long stretches, intercepts the OS's input events, and communicates with the device at a low level. There are good reasons Rust fits this work.

  • Memory safety: Without a garbage collector, Rust blocks a large class of memory errors (null pointers, buffer overruns) at compile time. For an always-on resident process, memory safety is directly tied to stability.
  • Lightweight and native: It ships as a small native binary without a heavy runtime - a fitting trait for a tool that markets itself as "lightweight."
  • Low-level control: For byte-level communication with HID devices, you get C-level control while being safer.

Interestingly, OpenLogi is one instance of a recent trend: rewriting old, heavy tools as lightweight Rust-native ones. A sibling example in the SSH-management space is covered in our piece on sshc, an SSH manager built in Rust. The desire to replace a "heavy, account-bound GUI" with a "lightweight, local native tool" is not confined to mouse drivers.

4. The Solaar Context - Where OpenLogi Fits

OpenLogi is not the first open-source Logitech tool. Its best-known elder is Solaar. Solaar is a Linux device manager for Logitech hardware, a mature open-source project under GPL v2. It manages many Logitech keyboards, mice, and trackpads connected over Unifying, Bolt, Lightspeed, or Nano receivers, direct USB, or Bluetooth, and it exposes settings like battery display, FN-key options, and smooth scrolling. Solaar, too, talks to devices using Logitech's proprietary HID++ (and Centurion) commands.

So how does OpenLogi differ from Solaar? Based on the descriptions, here is the picture. Note that both projects are evolving actively, so verify the current state in the official repositories.

Aspect Solaar OpenLogi (per description)
Primary platform Linux-centric macOS supported; Linux and Windows planned
Language Mostly Python Rust
Character Mature and stable, broad device support New and under development, GUI experience emphasized
In common Uses HID++, local and open source, aims for no-account and no-telemetry

So OpenLogi is a new entry in the lineage of "open-source Logitech tools." You could say it is re-walking the path Solaar has long laid in the Linux world, but from a different starting point: Rust and macOS-first. The two are less competitors than complements - the same philosophy implemented on different platforms and in different languages.

5. The Trend of Open-Source Peripheral Tools

Put OpenLogi and Solaar side by side and a larger pattern emerges. While peripheral makers increasingly push heavier "companion software" (Options+, various gaming-gear hubs), on the opposite side a steady stream of lightweight, local, open-source alternatives keeps growing.

Three motivations sustain this trend.

  • Privacy: Discomfort that input-device software for mice and keyboards demands accounts and telemetry. Input devices are inherently sensitive because they touch every action a user takes.
  • Fatigue with software bloat: Excessive install size and resident processes relative to a simple function. The question: "Does remapping a button really need hundreds of megabytes and a background daemon?"
  • Reclaiming control: Storing settings in plain files, with open source you can inspect to verify what it does. Your machine, not the cloud, becomes the source of truth.

These three motivations are really a software-wide theme, not a mouse-specific one. They also amount to a question of judgment - what you should build yourself versus what you should not. That principled angle continues from a different direction in the principle of not rolling your own browser.

6. Setup and Use - How to Start

Concrete install steps depend on version and platform, so here we sketch only the big picture from the description. For exact commands and the latest supported scope, always check the official repository (GitHub AprilNEA/OpenLogi) and docs.

  1. Check support: Per the description, the supported list includes MX Master 4, 3S, and 3, MX Anywhere 3, Signature M650, and Ergo M575, over a Logi Bolt receiver, direct Bluetooth, or USB. First confirm your device and connection are on the list.
  2. Check platform: macOS is supported today; Linux and Windows are described as "coming soon." Confirm your OS is in scope.
  3. Install a release: Grab a binary from the official releases and install it. On macOS you may need to grant permissions such as input monitoring (a common trait of HID++ tools).
  4. Map in the GUI: Click the mouse diagram to assign an action to each button, and adjust DPI and SmartShift. Settings save to a TOML file.
  5. Per-app profiles (optional): If you need different bindings only in a particular app, set up a profile overlay.

The point is that it all ends "without a cloud account, on your machine, in a plain-text config file." If you want to back up or move your config to another machine, you just copy that TOML file. You lose the convenience of cloud sync, but in exchange you gain simplicity and transparency.

7. Limits and Caveats - An Honest Assessment

For balance, let us be explicit about the limits. OpenLogi is appealing, but at this moment you should factor in the following.

  • No stability guarantee: The project itself states it is "under active development and not yet stable; features and config may change." A binding-breaking change could land.
  • Limited platforms and devices: It is macOS-first today, and the supported-device list is finite. If your mouse is not on it, behavior is not guaranteed.
  • Some official features absent: Certain official features like firmware updates or multi-device Flow may fall outside an alternative's scope. If you truly need them, the official tool is still required.
  • Trusting permissions: Because the tool intercepts input events and writes commands to the device, even as open source you should verify the source and release provenance before granting permissions.

In sum, for mission-critical gear, treat it as an "experimental tool" and evaluate carefully; but for power users who value privacy and lightness and can tolerate change, it is well worth trying. The safe habit is to always cross-check any "unconfirmed feature" against the official docs.

8. The Privacy Angle - Input Devices Are a Sensitive Domain

Viewing a mouse driver through a privacy lens may feel like overkill. But input devices are inherently in contact with every interaction a user makes. Which apps you use and how much, which buttons you press in which context - with intent, this information becomes a behavioral profile.

That is why OpenLogi's three promises - no account, no telemetry, local plain-text config - mean more than marketing copy. They are a design decision to pull the trust boundary inside your own machine. If there is no channel for data to leave in the first place, there is nothing to worry about regarding "how that data might be used." The strongest privacy guarantee is not "anonymized well" but "never collected at all."

9. The Software-Bloat Angle - The Value of Staying Small

Finally, let us widen the lens one more step. The deeper question OpenLogi pokes at is "why does software keep getting heavier?" A mouse-settings tool's job is, by nature, narrowly bounded. Yet official companion apps keep piling on accounts, upsells, notifications, analytics, and auto-update, growing bloated.

OpenLogi's approach runs the other way: a small, verifiable tool that does one thing well. That is an old virtue of the Unix philosophy and an increasingly scarce value today. Small tools are easier to understand, present a narrower attack surface, and age better in maintenance. Above all, they hand back the sense of control of "knowing what I am running."

The real cost of bloated software is not disk space. It is no longer being able to know what is happening on your own machine - the loss of a sense of control. The value of a tool like OpenLogi lies in returning that control, even in a small domain.

10. Who Should Use OpenLogi

Let us narrow to a practical recommendation.

  • A good fit: Privacy-minded developers and power users, people who want to avoid background residency and account prompts, those who want to version-control their settings as plain files, and anyone looking for a lightweight alternative on macOS.
  • Too early for: Work environments where stability is paramount, users of devices not on the supported list, people who genuinely need official-only features like firmware updates or Flow, and those for whom GUI convenience alone is enough and who have no motivation to switch.

If you are on Linux, it is reasonable to evaluate the mature Solaar first while you wait for OpenLogi's Linux support. The two share the same philosophy, so whichever you use, you reap the benefits of "no account, local, open source."

11. Conclusion + 3 Key Takeaways

OpenLogi is a small tool that carries a big story. It starts from the reasonable grievance - does a single mouse setting really need an account, telemetry, and a heavy resident process? - and offers a lightweight, local alternative in Rust, built on the open foundation of HID++. Per the description it is still under development and macOS-first, but its direction is unmistakable.

Three takeaways to close:

  1. OpenLogi is an account-free, local, open-source alternative. It controls a Logitech mouse's buttons, DPI, and SmartShift over HID++ while never sending data off your machine. Privacy is the core differentiator.
  2. HID++ makes it all possible. Logitech's extension on top of standard HID is the open foundation that lets tools like OpenLogi and Solaar control devices without proprietary software.
  3. Be cautious for now, but worth watching. It is pre-stable with limited support, so be careful with critical gear - yet as a movement showing "the value of staying small," it is well worth keeping an eye on.
