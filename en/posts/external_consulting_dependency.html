What External Consulting Leaves Behind

What accumulates in the organization, and what leaks out

Aug 20, 2026 Trends & Culture

The deliverable survived. The reasoning behind it did not

Sooner or later you pull the advisory report from two years ago back off the shelf. The conclusion is stated clearly enough. What is not in the document is which alternatives were considered, why this option was chosen over those, and which assumptions had to hold for the recommendation to work. Nobody still in the building can explain it either. At that point the options collapse to one. Call them again.

A short parable posted on the corporate history pages of the British retailer Iceland has been circulating again in developer communities. A boat crew with one rower and seven captains loses a race, hires consultants, receives a reorganized management layer, loses by a wider margin the following year, and ends by firing the rower and paying the managers a bonus. As satire it is well built. Its weakness is the weakness of all parables, which is that the conclusion is fixed before the story begins. Real decisions about whether to bring in outside help do not resolve that cleanly.

So this article does not take the parable's side. There are situations where outside advice genuinely earns its keep, and if you refuse to acknowledge those first, everything that follows is just venting. The question here is narrower and more useful. When the engagement ends, what accumulates in the organization and what leaks out? The difference is rarely produced by the quality of the firm. It is produced by the contract and by how the work was run.

First, the cases where outside eyes are actually required

There are roughly four situations where an outsider can do something an insider cannot.

  • When internal politics block the conclusion. The judgment that a business line should be shut down rarely comes from the executive who built it, or from anyone reporting to them. This is not a competence problem, it is a position problem. Some sentences can only be said by someone who is not on the org chart.
  • When an expertise gap is short-lived. Foreign tax treatment, a one-off regulatory response, a technology stack you are adopting for the first time. Filling a six-month knowledge need with a permanent hire is inefficient. Hiring is hard to reverse, engagements are easy to reverse.
  • When you are short of hands, not judgment. The decision is already made and you simply lack people to finish by the deadline. What you are buying here is throughput rather than insight, and contracting it that way keeps expectations and outcomes aligned.
  • When the organization has no basis for comparison. Whether your cost ratio is high or low is something only a person who has seen many organizations can tell you. That said, industry association data and public filings cover a good part of this need without a firm.

Conversely, if none of these four applies and you are still calling, what you are buying is probably not analysis. That case gets its own section below.

SituationWhy outside is betterWhen inside is better
Decision blocked by internal politicsNo stake in the outcome, so the conclusion can be said out loudThe decision maker has already decided and only wants supporting material
Short-term expertise gapFaster than hiring and easier to reverseThe capability will be needed continuously for three years or more
Insufficient headcountThroughput can be purchased for a fixed periodExplaining the context costs more than doing the work
External benchmarkThey have a sample drawn from many organizationsPublic filings and association statistics would do
Direction of the core businessAlmost neverAlmost always. Context is the raw material of the judgment

The rare cases where advisory effects were actually measured, and what happened nine years later

The argument over whether consulting works has run for decades largely because evidence is hard to build. Two studies from economics faced the problem head on. Both are randomized controlled trials, which is why they are worth citing.

  • Bloom, Eifert, Mahajan, McKenzie and Roberts ran a management field experiment on Indian textile firms, giving free consulting on modern management practices to randomly selected treatment plants. The journal version reports that adopting those practices raised productivity by 17% in the first year through improved quality and efficiency and reduced inventory, and that within three years it led to the opening of more production plants. They identified informational barriers as the main reason the practices had not been adopted earlier.
  • Bruhn, Karlan and Schoar ran a trial with 432 small and medium enterprises in Mexico, where one year of access to management consulting raised total factor productivity and return on assets. Using national social security records, they found a large and persistent increase in headcount and total wage bill five years on.

Read that far and consulting looks like an intervention that works. Then the same researchers went back to the Indian sites nine years later. Their 2018 report is the result. Roughly half of the adopted management practices had been dropped. The gap between treatment and control plants was still there, so the intervention had not been wasted. But the more interesting finding was the reason for the decay. The two most cited causes were managerial turnover and directors not having enough time.

Put those two findings side by side and the thesis of this article writes itself. Whether an advisory effect persists depends less on the quality of the advice than on where the knowledge was attached. Attached to a person, it leaves when that person leaves. Attached to documents, procedures and metrics, it stays. The same study noted that practices spread from the experimental plants to other plants inside the same firm, but barely spread to other firms at all. Knowledge does not cross organizational boundaries easily.

The real issue is not the contract, it is who owns the knowledge

No contract, however well drafted, transfers understanding automatically. A contract governs ownership of artifacts, not ownership of comprehension. Those are different things.

The format of the deliverable throws the reasoning away

The standard shape of an advisory deliverable is a document that compresses the conclusion. It has to be explainable to executives in forty minutes, so it gets built that way. Look at what the compression discards and the problem becomes obvious.

  • The alternatives that were considered and why each was rejected
  • The formulas behind the numbers and the source data they were computed from
  • The preconditions that must hold for the conclusion to be valid
  • The sensitivity of the conclusion if those preconditions break
  • Where in the analysis the team's confidence was lowest

None of the five survives into a conclusion deck. Yet when circumstances shift slightly, the thing you need is not the conclusion but exactly those five items. To check whether an assumption still holds, the assumption has to be written down. To refresh a number, you need the formula. Without them, the organization cannot update the conclusion, and because it cannot update, it buys again.

Rehiring is not evidence of failure

Clear one misconception out of the way. Hiring the same firm again is not itself a problem. Working with someone who already knows the context lowers ramp-up cost and usually produces better work. The problem is being in a state where calling them again is the only available option. The two look identical from outside and are completely different in bargaining position. A rehire with alternatives is a contract. A rehire without alternatives is dependency.

Incentives, and the second product called legitimation

There is no need to impugn anyone's character here. People who work at advisory firms are generally diligent and capable. It is still worth stating the structure plainly.

  • An advisory firm's revenue comes from engagements, and having a follow-on engagement is better than not having one. That is not a moral fact, it is an income statement fact.
  • Therefore making the client self-sufficient is not something a firm will optimize for on its own initiative unless it has been contracted separately.
  • The senior people who appear in the pitch are sometimes not the people who staff the work. This too is usually resourcing rather than deceit, which is exactly why names belong in the contract.
  • Large and complex recommendations attract implementation support more easily than small and simple ones. A recommendation that is cheap to execute is not commercially advantageous to sell.

Designing a contract with this structure in view is the professional move. Signing without knowing it and feeling betrayed afterwards is the amateur one. If incentives are misaligned, do not leave them misaligned. Name knowledge transfer as a deliverable and put it inside the acceptance criteria.

Separately from analysis, advisory work sells a second product. Legitimation. When leadership has already reached a conclusion, an external signature spreads the accountability and reduces internal resistance. Mariana Mazzucato and Rosie Collington argued in their 2023 book The Big Con that the consulting industry, by acting as advisor, legitimator and outsourced executor at once, obstructs the accumulation of in-house capability in firms and governments alike. The book is contentious and has drawn substantial pushback. But the existence of the legitimation function is broadly acknowledged even by those who defend the industry, and cushioning a hard decision is not always a bad thing in organizational life. The problem is narrower. If you book the cost of that cushion as analysis spend, you can never evaluate the analysis.

Unmeasurability, or the counterfactual you cannot see

Attempts to measure advisory performance usually fizzle out, and not because the methods are weak. The thing being measured is unobservable in principle.

  • If results improved after the engagement, you cannot separate a favorable market from effective advice.
  • If results worsened, you cannot tell whether the advice was wrong or whether things would have been worse without it.
  • If only half the recommendation was implemented, a poor outcome cannot be attributed cleanly to the advice or to the execution.
  • The longer the implementation horizon, the more other variables intrude and the harder causal attribution becomes.

This is exactly why the two experiments cited above went to the trouble of random assignment. Without artificially constructing a group that did not receive the intervention, the counterfactual is unavailable. Individual companies cannot run that experiment. What they can do instead is give up on outcome evaluation and fix process indicators at contract signing. Whether the conclusion was correct may never be knowable, but whether the reasoning was handed over, whether internal staff can reproduce it, and whether assumptions were documented are all verifiable on the closing day. Moving the measurement onto things that can be measured beats straining to measure what cannot.

Four things that separate organizations where it sticks from those where it does not

Two organizations spend the same money with the same firm and get different results. The divergence usually happens at four points.

ItemWhere it sticksWhere it does not
Internal staffingDedicated staff work in the same room from day oneThe designated contact acts as a mailbox and appears only at meetings
Deliverable scopeConclusion document plus calculation files, source data and assumption listPresentation deck only
Decision recordsWhy this option and what was discarded is written into internal documentsThe decision is announced and the reasoning scatters into hallway conversation
Closing verificationInternal staff rerun the analysis and reproduce the key numbersA final readout counts as acceptance

All four are variables the buyer controls, not the firm. Which is why a post-mortem that blames only the consultants is usually half right at best. The third item deserves special attention because it costs almost nothing and pays disproportionately. Leaving four lines after every decision meeting, options considered, option chosen, reason for choosing, condition that would reopen it, puts the organization in an entirely different position two years later.

One more thing. Knowledge transfer is a problem of continuity rather than headcount. Rotating a different person onto each engagement produces an organization where many people have participated and nobody understands the arc. The nine-year follow-up finding that managerial turnover was the leading cause of decay points at the same mechanism.

What to check before signing: people, deliverables, data, transfer

The items below are effectively non-negotiable once the contract is signed. Ask before, not after.

ItemWhat to askAnswer that should give you pause
StaffingNames and allocation percentages of the actual team, and the notice procedure for replacementThey answer at team level and avoid individual commitments
Deliverable formatBeyond the conclusion document, do you receive calculation files, assumption lists and interim analysisOnly the final report is defined as a deliverable
Data accessCan you keep using derived artifacts built from your own dataOutputs live only inside the firm's proprietary tooling
Knowledge transferAre internal participation hours and handover sessions written in as deliverablesThey will do it in good faith but it is not a contract line item
Intellectual propertyIs ownership and secondary-use scope of the output specifiedThere is no ownership clause, or it assigns sole ownership to the firm
Reproduction checkIs there a step where internal staff reproduce the key figures before closingThe final readout substitutes for acceptance testing

Know the default before you negotiate ownership

For work commissioned by the Korean government, a baseline already exists. Article 56 of the General Conditions for Service Contracts, a contracting directive issued by the Ministry of Economy and Finance, treats intellectual property in the contracted output as jointly owned by the ordering agency and the contractor, with shares deemed equal unless otherwise agreed. It also allows the parties to agree on a different allocation in view of development contribution or the special nature of the subject matter. This directive does not apply as-is to private contracts, but knowing that joint ownership with equal shares is a recognized default changes how you sit at the table. If the standard agreement handed to you has no ownership clause at all, that is not industry custom, it is negotiating room.

The AI variation: what gets replaced and what does not

The claim that large language models will replace consulting comes up constantly. Split honestly, the replaceable and the non-replaceable parts are reasonably clear.

  • Replaced: public-source research and synthesis, market overviews, first-draft competitor comparison tables, report structuring, getting up to speed on unfamiliar terminology. This is the part that used to be bought as junior hours.
  • Partly replaced: data analysis and scenario modeling. Input quality and assumption setting remain human judgments, and errors there scale with the sophistication of the model rather than shrinking.
  • Not replaced 1, context: which department is afraid of what, which past failure turned a topic into a taboo, who actually holds the decision. None of this is documented and none of it can be fed to a model.
  • Not replaced 2, accountability: sometimes a party must be answerable when the recommendation is wrong. A model cannot be a counterparty and carries no liability.
  • Not replaced 3, legitimation: the political cushion described above. This is not a question of technical substitutability but of whether purchasing it is the right thing to do.

There is an interesting inversion here. Raising in-house analytical capability with AI ought to reduce advisory dependence, but it can run the other way. If the structure that discards reasoning stays intact and only production speed increases, you accumulate documents nobody can explain, faster. The knowledge ownership problem is not specific to external consulting. It belongs to every arrangement that delegates judgment. Swapping the delegate from a person to a model leaves the question unchanged. Why is this the conclusion, and which of us can explain it?

What could not be verified

Collected here are the points this article could not put a number on.

  • No credible public tally of consulting project success or failure rates was found. The figures in circulation are mostly industry self-surveys or lack disclosed samples and definitions, so none were cited.
  • Official statistics on Korean corporate spending on external advisory work and on renewal rates were also unavailable. Market research estimates vary too widely by definition to serve as evidence.
  • The two randomized trials cited cover Indian textile manufacturing and Mexican SMEs. Transferring them directly to large-cap strategy work or to Korean organizations would be a stretch. Only the general implication was carried over, namely that where knowledge is attached determines whether it persists.
  • Article 56 of the General Conditions for Service Contracts is a directive governing state-commissioned services. Clause-level wording and the latest amendments should be read from the official text at the Korean Law Information Center, and private contracts are governed first by what the parties agree.

Frequently asked questions

Assigning internal staff means their regular work slips. Is it still worth it?

Generally yes, but a nominal assignment achieves nothing. If you genuinely cannot free up the time, spend the budget on widening deliverable scope instead. Calculation files and assumption lists leave a path to reconstruction even when the person is gone.

We are a small organization. Will these clauses kill the deal?

Deliverable format and knowledge transfer affect cost, so asking for them may raise the quote. Then compare on the raised quote. The failure mode is choosing the cheap quote without the clauses and then expecting the same outcome. What is not asked for does not arrive.

How do you compensate for the firm not knowing your internal situation?

Divide the roles rather than trying to compensate. Judgments where context is decisive stay inside. What you take from outside is benchmarks and method. Executing a context-free recommendation verbatim is the most common failure path there is.

Where should an organization with years of accumulated dependency start?

Start by reviewing the deliverables from past engagements. If there are conclusion documents but no calculation basis and no assumptions, that is itself the diagnostic. Changing one line of deliverable scope in the next contract is the cheapest possible starting point.

Conclusion: this is not an argument against consultants

External advice is a tool. In some situations it accomplishes what simply cannot be accomplished from inside, and when someone bothered to run randomized trials on it, the effect showed up. The problem is not the tool. It is whether you designed for what remains after the tool is used.

  • Receive only conclusions and you will buy again. Receive the reasoning and you will update it yourself.
  • Outcomes resist evaluation because the counterfactual is invisible, but handover of reasoning and reproducibility are verifiable on closing day. Move the evaluation there.
  • The incentive structure is a business model, not bad faith. Know it and write it into the contract.
  • The cheapest countermeasure is four lines after every decision meeting: options considered, option chosen, reason, and the condition that would reopen it.

Back to the parable. The real problem in that story was never that they hired consultants. It was that the organization could not work out for itself that it was short of oars. As long as that condition holds, it hardly matters who you call.
