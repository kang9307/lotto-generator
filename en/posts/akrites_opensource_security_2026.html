The Akrites Open Letter — A Call to Defend Open Source Security Together

AI-accelerated vulnerabilities, supply chain attacks, maintainer burnout — diagnosing the structural crisis in open source security and the conditions for genuine collective defense

2026-06-28 Dev & Infra

Introduction: One Letter That Exposed an Uncomfortable Truth

On June 25, 2026, a new initiative led by the Linux Foundation — Akrites — published an open letter to the technology industry. Under the title "We All Depend on Open Source. We Will Defend It Together," over 20 organizations signed: AWS, Google, Microsoft, Anthropic, OpenAI, Red Hat, Cisco, IBM, NVIDIA, JPMorganChase, Citi, and more.

The letter's sharpest line is brief: "Finding a serious vulnerability in a major open source project used to take weeks. This now takes minutes." The AI-powered weapon handed to attackers is the fundamental reason this letter was convened. This analysis goes beyond summarizing Akrites. It examines the structural crises the letter exposes — maintainer burnout, funding asymmetry, supply chain attack patterns — and asks what corporations, foundations, and communities must each actually do.

A caveat upfront. As of June 2026, Akrites is a newly launched initiative. The open letter and founding announcements have been verified, but operational results do not yet exist. Declaration and execution are different things. Naming that gap honestly is this article's job.

1. The Structural Crisis in Open Source Security — Three Layers

The context that assembled Akrites is not a simple technical problem. Three layers of structural vulnerability are deteriorating simultaneously across the ecosystem.

1.1 Maintainer Burnout — The Weakest Link Is Human

The most underappreciated factor in open source security is not code — it's people. Many of the critical libraries that underpin global financial infrastructure, cloud platforms, and telecommunications networks are maintained by one or two volunteers in their spare time. These individuals receive almost no compensation, their contributions are taken for granted, and bug reports and security requests never stop.

Why does this become a security problem? CISA's post-mortem on the xz utils incident stated it plainly: burned-out maintainers cannot give adequate attention to reviewing new contributors, and that gives malicious actors time to build trust. "The primary attack vector is not technical vulnerabilities but human and process vulnerabilities."

Akrites acknowledges this, committing to serve as "maintainer of last resort" when projects lack active maintenance. But the community is right to insist this cannot be a stopgap. The structural fix is sustained financial support — sporadic sponsorships do not prevent burnout.

1.2 Supply Chain Attacks — The Pattern xz and npm Revealed

The xz utils backdoor discovered in 2024 is the most dramatic illustration of this crisis. The attacker spent 2.6 years accumulating contributions and trust in the project. When the original, exhausted maintainer ceded primary control, malicious code was injected. Had it not been caught, this backdoor in liblzma — used in SSH authentication — could have affected millions of Linux servers worldwide.

The npm ecosystem shows the same pattern. In March 2026, a supply chain attack struck the Axios package: attackers compromised a maintainer's npm account and published malicious versions containing a postinstall script that executes automatically on every npm install. One package installation was enough.

Three elements are common to these attacks. First, they exploit trust relationships, not code. Second, fatigued maintainers weaken the review checkpoint. Third, vulnerabilities lie dormant for months or years before discovery. Where these three intersect, supply chain attacks breed.

1.3 Funding Asymmetry — Infinite Dependency, Zero Contribution

Open source's paradox is this: trillion-dollar corporations extract enormous value from open source infrastructure while the people maintaining that infrastructure receive almost nothing in return. According to Linux Foundation research, many libraries that play critical roles in global software infrastructure are maintained on an essentially unpaid volunteer basis.

No funding means no security audits. No audits means hidden vulnerabilities go undiscovered. Undiscovered vulnerabilities lie dormant until an attacker finds them first. This chain is what Akrites aims to break. The open letter calls on participating organizations to invest "real resources — engineering talent, security expertise, and funding."

2. What the Akrites Open Letter Actually Says

Let's examine the letter's substance directly — and distinguish what is actionable from what is aspirational.

2.1 AI-Accelerated Vulnerabilities — A New Threat Dimension

Akrites was convened now because of AI. As the letter states, discovering serious vulnerabilities in major open source projects has been compressed from weeks to minutes. This gives attackers an asymmetric advantage. Defenders must monitor thousands of packages simultaneously; attackers can run automated AI scans to build vulnerability lists first.

The deeper problem is the discovery-to-deployment gap. Even after a patch is published, it typically takes months for enterprise systems to apply it. In that window, AI-enabled actors exploit already-known vulnerabilities at accelerating speed. This is why Akrites measures success by "patch deployment, not patch publication" — a directionally correct principle, but one whose operational implementation remains unspecified.

2.2 Shared Response Structure — SIRT and CVD

Akrites' execution structure rests on two pillars. The first is a shared Security Incident Response Team (SIRT) to coordinate from vulnerability discovery through remediation and disclosure. The second is a standardized Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure (CVD) process built on confidentiality-first principles.

The core philosophy is providing maintainers with "a single trusted partner." One of the recurring nightmares for open source maintainers has been receiving separate, simultaneous contact from dozens of companies about the same vulnerability — each with different confidentiality requirements, disclosure timelines, and remediation preferences. Akrites promises to unify that point of contact.

This design is theoretically sound. But execution has a prerequisite: participating corporations must actually abandon their individual channels and route exclusively through Akrites. Incentives to maintain parallel bilateral tracks for short-term advantage have not disappeared.

2.3 What the Letter Demands — Accountability Beyond Declaration

The letter makes three demands: real resource investment (engineering talent, security expertise, funding); adherence to standardized disclosure processes; and direct maintenance of critical packages lacking active maintainers.

Taken together, the ambition is clear. This is not merely a pledge to write checks — it is a declaration to change how the entire ecosystem operates security. The community's reasonable question is whether this declaration translates into contribution, or ends as a PR event for large corporations.

3. A Sustainable Open Source Security Model — Funding, Audits, SBOM

If Akrites correctly identifies the problem, then the quality of solutions deserves scrutiny. A sustainable open source security model requires three interlocking elements.

3.1 Sustained Funding — Structure, Not Episodes

Platforms like Tidelift, GitHub Sponsors, and Open Collective have partially addressed open source funding, but they fall short for core infrastructure libraries. The Alpha-Omega program under OpenSSF — directly funding maintainers of the most critical and vulnerable projects — is the most realistic model currently in operation.

How Akrites connects to this structure is critical. The letter promises "funding," but one-time sponsorships do not prevent burnout. What is needed is compensation at a level that allows maintainers to sustain themselves professionally while managing critical software. Without this structural support, the ecosystem does not change.

3.2 Security Audits — Surfacing What Cannot Be Seen

No funding means no audits. But auditing is not merely a spending problem — it is a systemic one. The Security Incident Response Team Akrites proposes is strong at reactive response. But proactive auditing — finding undiscovered vulnerabilities before attackers do — requires a separate framework.

OpenSSF's Scorecard and SLSA (Supply-chain Levels for Software Artifacts) quantify security maturity across open source projects. If these metrics are integrated into Akrites' prioritization of target projects, resources can be concentrated where vulnerability is greatest. As of June 2026, the operational details of that integration have not been published.

3.3 SBOM — No Deployment Without a Dependency Map

Akrites' choice to measure success by patch deployment rather than publication is significant. A patch can be available for months while enterprises remain unaware that their stack includes the affected component. Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) is the direct answer to this problem.

U.S. Executive Order 14028 and the EU's Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) are already moving toward making SBOM a regulatory requirement. OpenSSF's SBOM Everywhere SIG is strengthening tooling around the SPDX and CycloneDX standards. A 2024 survey found 78% of organizations planned to increase SBOM use within 18 months.

Reality, however, lags. Generating an SBOM and actually maintaining it as a living asset used for vulnerability response are entirely different capabilities. Many organizations treat SBOM as a compliance checkbox. If Akrites genuinely intends to achieve patch deployment at scale, it needs standards that enforce SBOM utilization across all member organizations.

4. Community Response — Between Support and Skepticism

Reactions to Akrites across GeekNews, Slashdot, and open source forums are genuinely mixed — not a simple binary of praise and criticism, but specific concerns grounded in hard experience.

4.1 Reasons to Support

The case for welcoming this initiative is pragmatic. When large corporations commit engineering talent, that directly reduces the maintenance burden. Consolidating duplicate security reports into a single channel reduces maintainer chaos. Imperfect coordination beats no coordination, especially when AI is accelerating the threat.

4.2 Grounds for Skepticism

The critique is rooted in history. When large corporations have launched open source governance initiatives, power has tended to flow toward corporate interests. Existing community channels have been bypassed or absorbed, weakening independence. The concern that this becomes a coordination layer addition rather than "real funding for real maintainers" is legitimate.

The sharpest criticism is this: corporations extract trillions in value from open source while the people sustaining it receive almost nothing. If Akrites does not change that imbalance, the structural problem remains unsolved regardless of how many organizations sign the letter.

4.3 My Assessment

Akrites points in the right direction. It accurately identifies the intersection of AI-accelerated vulnerabilities, supply chain attack patterns, and maintainer burnout. The SIRT and unified CVD are actionable improvements. But the gap between declaration and execution is large, and success metrics are not yet defined. Two conditions are necessary for this initiative to produce results. First, sustained financial support flowing directly to maintainers. Second, participating corporations actually abandoning individual security channels and adhering to the unified process. Without both, Akrites becomes another well-intentioned declaration.

5. What Each Stakeholder Must Actually Do

Akrites calls for shared responsibility. What does that look like in concrete terms for each actor?

5.1 Corporations — If You Use It, Contribute to It

The responsibility of companies profiting from open source is clear. First, know your dependencies (SBOM). Second, provide sustained direct funding to maintainers of your critical dependencies (Alpha-Omega model). Third, invest engineers as upstream contributors. Fourth, route vulnerability reports through a single channel (Akrites). If any of these four is absent, "collective defense" remains rhetoric.

5.2 Foundations — Bridge Governance and Funding

The role of foundations like Linux Foundation, OpenSSF, and CNCF is not to launch initiatives but to ensure their sustained operation. Specifically, Akrites' declared unified CVD process must be designed to integrate with OpenSSF's existing tools — Scorecard, SLSA, SBOM Everywhere. The goal should be strengthening the existing ecosystem, not adding another disconnected layer.

5.3 Community Developers — Demand and Contribute

Community developers and maintainers are both beneficiaries and validators of this initiative. They must publicly track and demand: Is Akrites functioning as a single point of contact? Are funds actually reaching maintainers directly? Have corporations abandoned individual channels? Forcing transparency reporting is the community's essential role.

6. Open Questions — What Remains Unclear

As of June 2026, the Akrites open letter is a declaration, not an operations manual. The answers to these questions will determine the initiative's ultimate impact:

  • Which open source projects are prioritized first, and by what criteria?
  • How much funding flows directly to maintainers, and in what structure?
  • How is the SIRT staffed and empowered?
  • How are roles divided between Akrites, OpenSSF, and Alpha-Omega?
  • Will participating corporations actually abandon individual security channels?
  • What metrics will be used to measure success in one year?

When answers to these questions emerge, Akrites will have moved from declaration to execution.

Conclusion: What Comes After the Letter Matters More

The Akrites open letter represents the largest formal acknowledgment by industry of the structural crisis in open source security. The AI-accelerated threat reality, the fact that supply chain attacks target people rather than code, and the reality that maintainers sustain global infrastructure without pay — identifying all three simultaneously is genuine progress.

But the history of declarations is unforgiving. Well-intentioned initiatives have ended as corporate PR events too many times. For Akrites to achieve different results, measurable commitments — funds reaching maintainers, CVD compliance, audit completions — must be publicly reported starting now.

Defending open source is not resolved by a single initiative. Every corporation that uses it, every developer who contributes, every community that watches — all must play their actual role. The declaration "We Will Defend It Together" becomes real only when the commitment holds even when no one is watching.
