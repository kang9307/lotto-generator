Age Standards for Youth and Children in Korea: Cheongsonyon Bohobeop vs. Adong Bokjibeop

Understanding the Different Age Criteria Under Korea's Youth Protection Act and Child Welfare Act

2025-06-30 Life & Practical

Key Takeaway

In Korea, the age definitions for "cheongsonyon" (youth) and "adong" (child) differ from one law to another, which frequently causes confusion. Knowing the difference between the Cheongsonyon Bohobeop (Youth Protection Act) and the Adong Bokjibeop (Child Welfare Act) is genuinely useful in everyday life.

Legal information notice — This article summarizes publicly available South Korean statutes for general readers. Every age below reflects the standard set by a specific Korean law and is cited with its article number. These thresholds apply in Korea only and differ from country to country, so please do not assume they match the rules where you live. Statutes are amended from time to time, and the same provision can be read differently depending on the facts. Verify the current text at the Korean Law Information Center (law.go.kr), and consult a qualified attorney for any specific case, dispute, or administrative matter.

Why Do the Age Criteria Differ?

1. Different Legislative Purposes

The Cheongsonyon Bohobeop (Youth Protection Act) is primarily designed to shield young people from harmful social environments, so it covers a broader age range -- everyone under 19 (international age).

The Adong Bokjibeop (Child Welfare Act) focuses on ensuring basic welfare and healthy development for children, targeting the relatively younger group of those under 18 (international age).

2. Alignment with International Standards

The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child defines a child as anyone under 18, and Korea's Child Welfare Act follows this same standard.

Real-Life Examples

Gaming and Internet Access

  • 18-year-olds are still covered by the Youth Protection Act, meaning they are subject to game shutdown systems and harmful-site blocking.
  • While they are adults under the Child Welfare Act, they remain protected under youth-protection rules.

Movie Ratings

  • "Restricted" (cheongsonyon gwallam bulga) movies require viewers to be at least 18 (international age).
  • This follows the standard set by the Youth Protection Act.

Buying Tobacco and Alcohol

  • You must be 19 (international age) or older to purchase these products.
  • Both laws prohibit sales to anyone below this age.

Age Definitions Across Korean Law at a Glance

It is not only "cheongsonyon" (youth) and "adong" (child) that differ. Korean statutes use a whole family of terms — yeongyua (infants and toddlers), yua (young children), eorini (children), adong (children), cheongsonyon (youth), sonyeon (juveniles) and miseongnyeonja (minors) — and each term carries its own age range depending on the law. This is not a drafting error: it is a deliberate consequence of each law protecting a different group for a different purpose. What stands out is how much law is packed into the single year between 18 and 19. The Child Welfare Act and the Game Industry Promotion Act draw the line at 18; the Youth Protection Act, the Civil Act, the Juvenile Act and the child sexual abuse statute draw it at 19. Meanwhile the Framework Act on Youth extends all the way to 24, and the Single-Parent Family Support Act reaches 22 for students. The same person can simultaneously be a child under one law, a youth under another, and an adult under a third.

Adong, Cheongsonyon, Miseongnyeonja, Sonyeon: How the Terms Differ

Everyday Korean uses these words interchangeably, but in statutes they are four separate concepts serving four separate purposes.

  • Adong (child) — the language of care and welfare. The Child Welfare Act, art. 3(1) sets it at under 18, and the surrounding provisions are about abuse prevention, protective measures and parenting support.
  • Cheongsonyon (youth) — a word with two faces. Under the Youth Protection Act (under 19) youth are people to be shielded from harmful environments; under the Framework Act on Youth (ages 9 to 24) they are people to be supported through activities, welfare and capacity building. The same word powers two laws with almost opposite postures.
  • Miseongnyeonja (minor) — a question of civil capacity. Under Civil Act art. 4 majority arrives at 19; before that, legal acts generally require a guardian's consent. This is the concept that actually bites in contracts, bank accounts and phone plans.
  • Sonyeon (juvenile) — a criminal-procedure concept. Juvenile Act art. 2 sets it at under 19, and art. 4(1)(2) carves out those aged 10 to under 14 as chokbeop sonyeon. The emphasis is rehabilitation rather than punishment.
  • Yeongyua, yua and eorini — these are closer to service-domain labels: daycare (Child Care Act), kindergarten (Early Childhood Education Act), and traffic and product safety (Road Traffic Act, Special Act on the Safety of Children's Products).

In other words, "how old is a child in Korea?" has no single answer. The precise question is always "a child under which law?"

What the 2023 Age-Counting Reform Actually Changed

A second source of confusion is that Korea historically used several different ways of counting age. To settle this, art. 7-2 of the Framework Act on Administration was newly inserted and art. 158 of the Civil Act was amended, with the so-called "international age unification" taking effect on 28 June 2023.

The amended text provides that age "shall be counted in full years, including the date of birth, and expressed in years," with months used for children under one year old. Crucially, the administrative provision opens with "except where otherwise specifically provided in other statutes" — and that clause is the entire answer to "then why is year age still around?"

Method How it works Legal basis Where it is used
International age Counted from the date of birth; increases on each birthday. You are 0 on the day you are born Civil Act art. 158; Framework Act on Administration art. 7-2 (in force 28 June 2023) The default for administrative and civil matters: Child Welfare Act, Civil Act, Juvenile Act, Criminal Act and most others
Year age Current year minus birth year. Everyone gains a year on 1 January, regardless of birthday Only where an individual statute says so (the proviso in art. 7-2) Youth Protection Act, Military Service Act, school entry under the Elementary and Secondary Education Act, Framework Act on Civil Defense
Korean age You are 1 at birth and gain a year every 1 January No statutory basis — a customary convention only Casual conversation. Never used in legal documents or official forms
  • The reform did not invent a new age; it wrote down in plain terms the standard that already governed most statutes, removing room for argument.
  • So even when a provision omits the word "full," it should be read as international age unless a special rule says otherwise.
  • Conversely, when a statute spells out something like "1 January of the year a person turns X," that statute's own method wins.
  • Korean age carries no legal effect, so on government forms, medical intake sheets and contracts you should state your international age.

Where Korea Still Uses "Year Age"

Year age survives in areas where scattered birthdays would make nationwide enforcement impractical: school cohorts, conscription, and point-of-sale checks for restricted goods, where a clerk needs to decide instantly from a birth year alone.

Area Standard Legal basis
Elementary school entry Enrolment on 1 March of the year after the child turns 6 (a guardian may shift this by one year in either direction) Elementary and Secondary Education Act art. 13
Enlistment in the reserve pool Male citizens enter the "preliminary service" pool from age 18, reckoned from 1 January of that year Military Service Act art. 8
Buying alcohol and tobacco You leave the "youth" category — and may purchase — from 1 January of the year you turn 19 Youth Protection Act art. 2(1)
Coverage as "children and youth" for sexual abuse protection Coverage ends on 1 January of the year the person turns 19 Act on the Protection of Children and Youth against Sexual Abuse art. 2(1)
Civil defense corps Male citizens from 1 January of the year they turn 20 until 31 December of the year they turn 40 Framework Act on Civil Defense art. 18

Remembering just these five resolves most day-to-day age confusion. Alcohol and tobacco is the classic trap: people calculate their international age, conclude "I am not 19 yet," and assume a purchase is barred — but the proviso in the Youth Protection Act means the purchase is lawful from 1 January of that year, birthday or not. From the seller's side the rule is far simpler, because only the birth year on the ID matters.

Criminal Responsibility: Chokbeop Sonyeon and Beomjoe Sonyeon

No age threshold is debated more heatedly in Korea than criminal responsibility, and here too two statutes interlock.

  • Criminal Act art. 9 provides that "the act of a person who is not yet 14 years of age shall not be punished." In short, anyone under 14 is outside criminal punishment.
  • Juvenile Act art. 4(1)(2) makes a juvenile aged 10 or older but under 14 who commits an act violating penal law a subject of juvenile protection proceedings — the chokbeop sonyeon.
  • So a person aged 10 to under 14 escapes punishment but not consequences: the case is referred to the juvenile division of the family court, which may impose a protective disposition.
  • Those aged 14 to under 19 bear criminal responsibility as sonyeon, and depending on the facts the case may proceed either as a juvenile protection case or as a criminal case.
  • From 19, the person falls outside the Juvenile Act and ordinary criminal procedure applies.

Sexual offences carry their own separate thresholds. Criminal Act art. 305(1) punishes sexual intercourse or indecent acts against a person under 13 as though it were rape or indecent assault, and art. 305(2) applies the same treatment where a person aged 19 or older commits such an act against a person aged 13 or older but under 16. Layered on top, the Act on the Protection of Children and Youth against Sexual Abuse treats anyone under 19 as protected. A single incident can therefore engage three separate lines — 13, 16 and 19 — at once.

Where an incident happens at school, a separate school-level review may run in parallel with any criminal process. For that procedure, see our Korea School Violence Response Guide.

Situations That Trip People Up

When you are asked to prove your age

  • Alcohol and tobacco retailers only need the birth year, because the Youth Protection Act uses the year-age method.
  • By contrast, when civil capacity is at stake — opening a bank account, signing a contract — the benchmark is international age from the exact date of birth.
  • The same person can, on the same day, be able to buy alcohol yet unable to sign a contract without a guardian.

When you write your age on a form

  • Absent any other instruction, write your international age.
  • If the form is worded as "born in YYYY or earlier," it is a year-age form.
  • Korean age has no legal basis and should never appear on an official document.

When school year and age do not line up

  • School entry follows year age, so classmates in the same grade can differ by a full year in international age.
  • Some statutes, such as the Game Industry Promotion Act, also look at enrollment status, so outcomes can differ within a single classroom.
  • Where early entry or deferral has shifted things, first establish whether the rule keys off age or off grade.

Updates to note

  • The game shutdown system has been abolished. The rule that blocked online game access for those under 16 during late-night hours was repealed by an amendment to the Youth Protection Act promulgated in December 2021 and ceased to apply from 1 January 2022, replaced by a "game time choice" system in which the young person or their guardian sets the hours. The reference to the shutdown system earlier in this article describes the pre-repeal regime.
  • The "youth not admitted" film rating tracks 19, not 18. The word "youth" in film classification follows art. 2(1) of the Youth Protection Act, so the rating operates against those under 19 — excluding anyone who has reached 1 January of the year they turn 19. Actual admission checks are carried out by cinemas and distributors, so follow the notices posted on site.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. How exactly do the Youth Protection Act and the Child Welfare Act differ?

Youth Protection Act art. 2(1) defines youth as anyone under 19, excluding those who have reached 1 January of the year they turn 19. Child Welfare Act art. 3(1) defines a child as anyone under 18. The former exists to block harmful environments; the latter to advance welfare.

Q. Why do the ages differ from law to law?

Because the risk being guarded against and the support being offered are different. Shielding young people from harmful substances is safer with a wider net, while welfare benefits are more coherent when aligned with the international standard of 18 in the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child. The proviso in art. 3 of the Framework Act on Youth, which expressly allows other statutes to set different standards, assumes exactly this variation.

Q. If the age-counting reform took effect, why is year age still used?

Because art. 7-2 of the Framework Act on Administration begins with "except where otherwise specifically provided in other statutes." Areas where an individual law has set its own year-age method — the Youth Protection Act, the Military Service Act, school entry, civil defense — continue unchanged.

Q. Is an 18-year-old an adult or a youth?

Both and neither. Still a minor under the Civil Act, no longer a child under the Child Welfare Act, but still youth under the Youth Protection Act — while simultaneously being an eligible voter and old enough to marry.

Q. Does a chokbeop sonyeon really face no consequences?

Criminal punishment is excluded by Criminal Act art. 9, but Juvenile Act art. 4(1)(2) routes the case to the juvenile division, which may impose a protective disposition. "No punishment" and "no consequences" are very different statements.

Q. How do I confirm which standard applies to my situation?

The reliable method is to first identify which statute the service or program rests on, then read that statute's definition provision. Search the law by name on the Korean Law Information Center (law.go.kr) and open art. 2 or art. 3, where definitions almost always sit.

Where to Verify This Information

Age thresholds are amended often. Building the habit of checking primary sources directly will spare you a great deal of misinformation.

  • Korean Law Information Center (law.go.kr) — the Ministry of Government Legislation's official full-text database. Search by statute name and open art. 2 or art. 3 to see the definitions. Each provision displays its effective date and amendment history, so you can confirm you are reading the current version.
  • Easy Law (easylaw.go.kr) — the same material reorganized by everyday topic. Sections such as "working youth," "single-parent families" and "civil defense" gather the relevant provisions in one place.
  • Ministry guidance — youth policy sits with the Ministry of Gender Equality and Family, child welfare with the Ministry of Health and Welfare, and school matters with the Ministry of Education. Ministry press releases are usually the fastest signal that something has changed.
  • When you cannot confirm something — for programs whose eligibility age is being raised in phases, the applicable figure depends on the date, so always check the latest official announcement. Do not rely on the numbers in an old blog post.

Conclusion

Korea's age criteria for youth and children are set differently depending on the purpose of each law:

  • Cheongsonyon Bohobeop (Youth Protection Act): Under 19 (international age) -- focused on protection from harmful environments
  • Adong Bokjibeop (Child Welfare Act): Under 18 (international age) -- focused on welfare and healthy development

Age 18, in particular, sits right on the boundary between the two laws, so different standards may apply depending on the situation. Whenever you use a service in Korea, it is a good idea to check in advance which legal standard that service follows.

Understanding these differences in age criteria helps you grasp minor-protection policies and related regulations more accurately and reduces confusion in everyday life.

Three principles are worth carrying away. First, the question is never "how old is a youth?" but "a youth under which law?" Second, international age is the default, yet year age survives wherever an individual statute says so — youth protection, military service, school entry, civil defense. Third, these thresholds are amended frequently, so verify the current text at the Korean Law Information Center before acting on anything consequential. Those three habits resolve most of the confusion on their own.

A closing reminder — the ages set out here are general standards drawn from the text of Korean statutes, not legal advice, and they govern in South Korea only; equivalent rules differ substantially in other countries. Statutes are amended, and in any individual case exceptions, transitional provisions or case law may change the outcome. If you need to exercise a right, discharge an obligation, respond to an administrative measure, or handle a criminal or family matter, check the current provision at the Korean Law Information Center and consult a qualified attorney.
