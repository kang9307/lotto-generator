This article covers only what could be confirmed directly in the Microsoft Support document (help/10741), TP-Link FAQs 138 and 1556, and ASUS FAQ 1030641, and its scope is diagnosing connection failures, slow speeds and drops on a home router.

Starting Point - Split the Symptom Three Ways and Find the Boundary

Router trouble shows up as one of three things: the connection fails, it is slow, or it drops, and each symptom has a different first place to look. The three documents, however, begin with the same question. They ask whether the fault is on one device or on every device. Once that boundary is drawn, the suspect narrows to the device, the router, or the modem and the line behind it.

The ASUS document says that when a wireless device cannot reach the internet, one should open the router's web interface, look at the network map to see whether it shows the internet as disconnected, and check whether wired devices also fail to connect. If either is true, the reader is sent on to separate documents about failed internet access. The document also suggests that, if the ISP modem has its own Wi-Fi, a device should be connected to that Wi-Fi. If the device gets online through the modem's Wi-Fi, the next step is to look at the internet status on the router, and if it cannot get online that way either, the advice is to contact the ISP.

The Microsoft document offers two ways to compare. One is to connect another laptop or phone to the same network: if that works, the document says the problem is likely with the first device. The other is to run ipconfig, find the default gateway address, and ping it. A reply means the connection to the router exists, so the problem may be the modem or the ISP. With no reply, the PC is connected straight to the modem with an Ethernet cable, and if that connection gets online, the document concludes that the Wi-Fi router is the source of the problem.

  • Compare with another device: connect another laptop or phone to the same Wi-Fi and see whether the symptom repeats (Microsoft).
  • Check wired devices at the same time: if wired devices cannot get online either, treat it as an internet access problem rather than a wireless one (ASUS).
  • Ping the default gateway: a reply means the connection as far as the router is intact (Microsoft).
  • Test through the modem directly or through the modem's Wi-Fi: whether the modem gets online separates the router from the line (Microsoft and ASUS).

The five-step method for narrowing down only slow speeds is covered in the internet speed test guide. This article concentrates on the order for each symptom (no connection, slow speeds, drops) and on the points where the vendor documents say different things.

Power Cycling - Two Official Documents Give Different Orders and Waiting Times

The procedure for switching the router and modem off and on is often presented as a single standard, but the two documents checked directly differ in the order of switching on and in the waiting times. The Microsoft Support document (step 4) connects the modem first, while the TP-Link FAQ (step 5) powers on the router first. ASUS FAQ 1030641 does not cover a power cycle procedure. The table below reproduces what each document says.

Neither document explains why the two orders are opposite, and this article could not confirm the reason either. If the router is a TP-Link, the order in that maker's document is the one to follow, and if no maker document is at hand, the order in the Microsoft document, which is the Windows-side guidance, is the way to stay within the sources. Neither document gives evidence that one order is better than the other.

The Microsoft document adds a few conditions. If the equipment is a single cable modem and Wi-Fi router combination, only that one device needs the same restart. Restarting briefly disconnects everyone on the Wi-Fi network, and the stated purpose is to create a new connection to the ISP. Readiness is judged by the status lights on the two devices.

  • Combo device: a device that merges modem and router is restarted as a single unit (Microsoft).
  • Backup battery: if the modem lights stay on after unplugging, remove the battery, and put it back after the wait (Microsoft).
  • Everyone disconnects: while the restart runs, every device on the same Wi-Fi is cut off for a time (Microsoft).
  • When to check the result: TP-Link says to wait one to two more minutes after the last device is on before testing the internet.

Symptom 1 - Connected to Wi-Fi but No Internet

When Windows shows No Internet under the Wi-Fi name, the Microsoft document calls the state limited connectivity. The PC is connected to the local network and may be able to see other devices on it, but anything that needs the internet, such as browsing, email or streaming, does not work. Windows decides this by sending a small HTTP request to a server on the internet, and the icon appears when nothing comes back.

The sequence of checks the document gives is fairly simple. First, turn Wi-Fi off and on and reconnect. Next, in an administrator Command Prompt, renew the IP configuration with ipconfig /release and ipconfig /renew and clear the DNS cache with ipconfig /flushdns. Then turn off any manual proxy on the Proxy page in Settings. If this step fixes it, the connection icon changes within a few seconds, according to the document.

If that fails, look at the IP address and the DNS server directly. The document explains that an address of the form 169.254.x.x means Windows could not get an address from the router, and for a network that does not use static IPs it says to confirm in the router documentation that DHCP is enabled. For DNS, find the address with ipconfig /all | findstr /c:"DNS Servers" and ping it. If the PC has a valid IP but cannot ping the DNS server, the document says to change to another public DNS address and test again.

  • Reconnect Wi-Fi: turn Wi-Fi off and on and connect to the wireless network again.
  • Renew the IP: run ipconfig /release and then ipconfig /renew.
  • Clear the DNS cache: run ipconfig /flushdns.
  • Check the proxy: turn off a manual proxy under Network and Internet, Proxy in Settings.
  • A 169.254.x.x address: the router did not hand out an IP, so check the DHCP setting.

Checking the WAN IP - The TP-Link Branching

The TP-Link FAQ, written for a router that has already been set up and still cannot get online, says to log in to the management page first and read the Internet IP address under Advanced > Status. That value is described as the starting point that tells whether the problem is on the router side or the modem side. According to the document, most TP-Link routers use 192.168.0.1 or 192.168.1.1 as the default LAN IP.

In the document's wording, 0.0.0.0 means the router has not received a valid address from the modem. The document gives no basis for reading this straight away as a line fault. What it says to check first is the cable connection, a direct-to-modem test, MAC address cloning, a LAN IP conflict and the connection type, and its own summary says only that the problem is likely with the modem connection or the physical setup. The table below carries over the document's branches and the order of its steps.

The detailed steps in the 0.0.0.0 branch carry their own conditions. MAC address cloning is used because some ISPs tie internet access to the MAC address of one specific device. On a PC connected to the router by wired Ethernet, open the MAC Clone section under Advanced > Network > Internet, select Use Current Computer MAC Address and save. The LAN IP change comes from the explanation that if the modem, or a DSL modem and router combination, uses the same address range, the two devices conflict, and the router's LAN IP is changed under Advanced > Network > LAN to an address such as 192.168.2.1.

In the branch where a valid IP exists, the document explains entering 8.8.8.8 as the primary DNS and 8.8.4.4 as the secondary DNS under Advanced > Network > DHCP Server. It describes 8.8.8.8 as a public DNS server operated by Google. The connection type has to be confirmed with the ISP, and the document names Dynamic IP, PPPoE and Static IP as examples and says that a wrong setting will not connect.

  • What 0.0.0.0 means: the router did not get a valid address from the modem, and the cause has to be sorted out through the six items the document lists, in order.
  • Condition for MAC cloning: the document warns that it must be done over a wired connection.
  • LAN IP conflict: when the modem-side device uses the same range, change the router LAN IP to another range.
  • Manual DNS: the first action the document gives when a valid IP exists but the internet does not work.

Symptom 2 - Slow Speeds: Distance, Band and Channel Width

For a connection that is up but very slow, the Windows document names three things. Check signal strength by confirming that the device is close to the router with no obstructions, end background processes that use a lot of network bandwidth in Task Manager, and connect another device to the same Wi-Fi. If the other device is slow as well, the router may need maintenance or replacement. The general procedure in the same document also tells the user, when the router shows 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz as separate networks, to try connecting to the other one.

The ASUS document describes the bands this way. The 2.4 GHz signal travels farther but is slower than 5 GHz, and 5 GHz reaches a shorter distance but can be faster. When the connection is unstable, it says to first check whether the device is on 2.4 GHz or 5 GHz and how far it is from the router when the drop happens. It recommends placing the router close to the devices, and where possible in the center of the space, in an open area with less furniture and no walls in the way.

Channel bandwidth and channel are set per band under Wireless > General on the router's web interface. Channel bandwidth affects wireless speed and signal stability, and an ASUS router adjusts it automatically by default. The default bandwidths the document lists are 20/40 MHz on 2.4 GHz, 20/40/80 MHz on 5 GHz, and 20/40/80/160/320 MHz on 6 GHz. The control channel defaults to Auto, and because using the same channel as nearby wireless devices can cause interference and slow speeds, it can be switched to a channel with less interference. On 5 GHz, the documented method is to clear Auto select channel including DFS channels and then pick a different channel.

  • Signal strength: bring the device closer to the router and remove obstructions (Microsoft).
  • Background apps: end processes that consume a lot of bandwidth in Task Manager (Ctrl+Shift+Esc) (Microsoft).
  • Switch bands: if 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz appear as separate networks, try the other one (Microsoft).
  • Default channel bandwidth: 20/40 MHz on 2.4 GHz and 20/40/80 MHz on 5 GHz, adjusted automatically and changeable only on the web interface (ASUS).
  • Changing the control channel: switch from Auto to a less crowded channel, noting that supported channels vary by each country's regulations (ASUS).

Symptom 3 - Drops: The ASUS Checklist

ASUS FAQ 1030641 lists nine steps for a wireless device that disconnects often. The first six concern the router, the seventh the environment, the eighth the device, and the ninth the reset. The document states that some wireless functions differ by firmware version, and that the settings screens split into firmware 3.0.0.6.102_35404 and later versus earlier. Channel bandwidth and control channel can be changed only on the web interface, not in the app.

  • 1. Update the firmware: check the version by clicking it under Network Map in the web interface, or under Settings > Firmware Upgrade > Check Firmware Version in the ASUS Router app.
  • 2. Return the channel bandwidth to the default: the default is automatic adjustment, and it affects speed and stability.
  • 3. Switch to a control channel with less interference: the default is Auto, and it is set per band under Wireless > General.
  • 4. Turn off Smart Connect: this makes it possible to set a different Wi-Fi name and password for each band.
  • 5. Adjust the antenna angle: try angles such as 45 or 90 degrees, and see the user manual for the recommended layout of each model.
  • 6. Move the router closer to the devices: so that the distance the signal travels does not weaken its strength.
  • 7. Reduce environmental interference: the document names microwave ovens, refrigerators, cordless phones and power boxes as sources, and iron cabinets, iron doors, cement walls, closed cabinets, the floor and behind the sofa as barriers.
  • 8. Update the device driver or firmware: this applies to laptops, smartphones, USB Wi-Fi adapters and consoles, and the device maker is the contact for updates.

The ninth step, the reset, is covered in section 8. Among the environmental factors the document points out in the seventh step are dense building materials such as gypsum, planks and barbed wire. The text goes only as far as saying these materials can affect how Wi-Fi signals penetrate, and it gives no figure for the loss.

The Windows side also has settings tied to drops in the document. For a Wi-Fi connection that drops frequently, the Microsoft document says to choose the power plan under Power Options in Control Panel and, under Wireless Adapter Settings in the advanced power settings, change Power Saving Mode to Maximum Performance. A separate step in Device Manager is to clear the Allow the computer to turn off this device to save power box on the Power Management tab of the Wi-Fi adapter properties. The same item also advises changing the router channel to reduce interference.

Checking the Device Side - The Windows Document Sequence

If the router looks healthy, the device is next. The general procedure in the Microsoft document has thirteen steps arranged from the simple to the more disruptive. The document says that on Windows 11 the first move is to run the Network and Internet troubleshooter in the Get Help app, and on Windows 10 to run the Network troubleshooter under Network and Internet, Status in Settings.

The early steps are status checks. Confirm in the taskbar network icon that Wi-Fi is on and shows Connected, and that Airplane mode is off. Next, under Network & internet, Wi-Fi, Manage known networks in Settings, forget the network and reconnect by entering the password again. The document also covers an adapter that does not support the frequency the network uses. For example, if the adapter supports only 2.4 GHz and the network uses 5 GHz, the network does not appear. In the uncommon case where the driver filters the frequency out, the adapter properties in Device Manager have an Advanced tab where the Wireless Mode or Band setting should allow the network's frequency.

The later steps make larger changes. In an administrator Command Prompt, run netsh winsock reset, netsh int ip reset, ipconfig /release, ipconfig /renew and ipconfig /flushdns in that order. After that comes uninstalling the driver and restarting, and the document urges having a backup driver from the PC maker's site ready beforehand. It says this approach is worth considering if the connection stopped working properly after a recent update.

  • Wi-Fi status: confirm Wi-Fi is on and Connected appears below the name.
  • Airplane mode: confirm it is off under Network & internet in Settings.
  • Forget the known network: use Forget under Manage known networks, then connect again.
  • Command order: winsock reset, int ip reset, release, renew, flushdns, in that sequence.
  • Remove the driver: tick Attempt to remove the driver for this device, uninstall, and restart.
  • Disable power saving: clear the box that lets the computer turn the device off, on the Power Management tab in Device Manager.

Recovery - Resets Come Last

All three documents place a reset last. The ASUS document says to press the RESET button for about 5 to 10 seconds and release it when the power LED starts flashing. All the LEDs going dark at that point is normal because the router is rebooting itself, and the user waits until the power LED and Wi-Fi LED come on again before opening the management interface. A note says button positions differ slightly between models, so the manual should be consulted. A reset clears every setting, and Quick Internet Setup (QIS) is run again from the web interface or the app.

If a standard reset does not fix it, the ASUS document points to a Hard Factory Reset in a separate document with its own model list. In the TP-Link document too, a reset is the last item after the DNS setting and the restart, and after the reset the router is configured from scratch. The Windows network reset is the device-side equivalent of a last resort. On Windows 11 it is run under Network & internet, Advanced network settings, Network reset, and on Windows 10 under Status, Network reset.

Network reset has side effects. Installed network adapters and their settings are removed and then reinstalled with default values after the restart, so software such as VPN clients and Hyper-V virtual switches may need to be set up again. Known networks may all be switched to the public network profile, under which the PC is not discoverable by other devices. The document adds that this method can help with connection problems after upgrading from Windows 10 to Windows 11 and with the inability to reach shared network drives.

  • ASUS reset: RESET for about 5 to 10 seconds, release when the power LED starts flashing, and wait until the power and Wi-Fi LEDs are on again.
  • After the reset: every setting is gone, so set up the internet and Wi-Fi again with QIS.
  • Windows network reset: a last resort after the steps above, returning adapters and settings to their defaults.
  • Authentication problems: when the password is correct but rejected, the Microsoft document lists rebooting or resetting the router and updating its firmware.

What Could Not Be Confirmed

  • The restart-order guidance from KT and other carrier help desks could not be opened, so this article does not rely on any carrier recommendation.
  • The reason Microsoft and TP-Link give a different power-on order is not stated in either document.
  • Non-overlapping channel numbers on 2.4 GHz, the channel spacing, and the Korean rules for 5 GHz DFS channels could not be confirmed from an official source that opened. ASUS says only that supported channels differ by each country's regulations.
  • Figures such as the signal loss per wall or a ceiling on effective speed do not appear in the three documents.
  • ASUS FAQ 1030641 does not state the default admin address of ASUS routers, and documents from domestic makers such as ipTIME are not among the sources of this article.
  • The three documents say nothing about a relationship between automatic firmware update times and drops.
