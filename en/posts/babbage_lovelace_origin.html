What the First Programmer Title Is Actually Arguing About - Reading the 1843 Note G Against Its Sources

Establish first where the translation and the Notes were printed, then separate what each side of the argument leans on

2026-09-05 Trends & Culture

Accounts of the origins of computing almost always begin with two names. Charles Babbage (1791-1871) and Ada Lovelace (1815-1852). And with very few exceptions they stop at the same sentence: that Lovelace was the world's first programmer.

That sentence attracts a rebuttal. The rebuttal attracts a rebuttal of its own. The argument became sharp in the 1970s and has now run for more than half a century, with documentary evidence held up by both sides. The complication is that the two sides are holding up different piles of paper. That is the reason the question refuses to close.

What follows deals only with the 1843 printed article and with the scholarly literature that has accumulated around it. Assessments of character and matters of private life are outside its scope. Where the sources diverge, no verdict is offered on which side is right; instead each claim is set beside the evidence it depends on. Items for which the grounds could not be established are gathered with their reasons in a section near the end.

The occasion - a comic strip reopens an old argument

A comic series that renders the history of free software and open source in cartoon form picked Babbage and Lovelace for its opening installment. A note explains that the drawings were redone and the narration revised while the series moved from an older site to a new address, and the piece circulated through a Korean developer community.

The comic's own narration is not the evidence base for anything below. What is worth noting is what it chose to include. The first installment covers the structure of the Difference Engine and the Analytical Engine, the separation of store from mill, the punched cards, and the statement that the procedure for computing Bernoulli numbers is commonly introduced as the first published computer program. It also records that historians disagree about how much of that procedure originated with Lovelace and how much was joint work with Babbage.

As popular treatments go, that is balanced. But stopping at there is disagreement leaves the interesting question untouched. Which stack of paper does each side of the disagreement actually rest on? Of that material, what is a fact you can see with your eyes, and what is a judgement drawn from those facts? Without separating those two layers, the argument slides into a matter of taste.

The physical object printed in 1843

The first thing to nail down is physical. What was printed on paper in 1843, and in what order?

The starting point is not Lovelace but an Italian. Luigi Federico Menabrea (1809-1896) was at the time professor of mechanics and construction at the university of Turin; he would later become prime minister of the Kingdom of Italy, serving from 1867 to 1869. Babbage presented the design of the Analytical Engine in Turin in 1840, and Menabrea, who was in the audience, wrote it up in French.

Three things stand out. First, this is not a work authored solely by Lovelace; it is a translation. The body text is Menabrea's. Second, the notes appended to that translation are longer than the translation itself. In his 1864 memoir Babbage put the notes at roughly three times the length of the original memoir. Third, there is no author's name, only the initials A.A.L. That sits squarely inside the conventions under which women publishing in scientific journals of the period withheld their full names.

Taylor's Scientific Memoirs was a serial published in London between 1837 and 1852 which, in cooperation with the British Association for the Advancement of Science, printed English translations of significant Continental scientific papers. Bessel, Bunsen, Gauss and Ohm appeared in the same pages. These notes, in other words, were not a popular publication; they went into a venue that contemporary scientists read.

Notes A through G - what sits in which note

There are seven notes. The dispute concentrates almost entirely on the last of them, but you cannot see where that last one sits without knowing what the other six do.

NoteSubjectPosition in the dispute
AThe difference between the Difference Engine and the Analytical Engine. The distinction between operations and the objects operated upon. The Jacquard-loom analogy and a conditional remark about musicThe note Babbage specifically asked in a letter not to have altered
BThe store built from columns and discs, and the system of variable cardsMachine description. Little contested
CThe principle of the Jacquard loom and the technique of backing the cardsThe physical basis for repetition
DA table of the procedure for solving two simultaneous linear equationsSame lineage as the example in Menabrea's original. No repetition in it
EExtension of the computational examplesOne of the notes Babbage refers to in the correspondence
FThe practical question of how many cards would be requiredA question of scale
GThe statement that the engine originates nothing, and the diagram for computing Bernoulli numbersNearly the whole dispute lives here

Set Note D beside Note G and the difference is stark. The table in Note D uses six variables for constants, nine for intermediate values and two for results, and it finishes in eleven steps. There is no repetition and it fits on a single page. The diagram in Note G does neither.

The Note G diagram - twenty-five operations and two layers of repetition

The plate attached to Note G is headed 「Diagram for the computation by the Engine of the Numbers of Bernoulli」. It was larger than the page format of the volume, so it was printed on a folded sheet and interleaved. Its structure runs as follows.

Structure of the Note G diagram - the range the sources fix

  Variables    data 10 / working 3 / result 4
  Operations   25 in total

  01 - 12   outside the loops
  13 - 23   outer loop        printed on the plate: a repetition of operations 13 to 23 follows
    13 - 16   inner loop 1
    17 - 20   inner loop 2
  24 - 25   outside the loops

The decisive feature here is nesting. Two inner loops sit inside an outer loop, and that fact is printed on the plate as an annotation. The Difference Engine could not have carried out a calculation of this shape, because it had no mechanism at all for inspecting an intermediate result and branching on it. Nor does anything of this form appear anywhere in Menabrea's original paper.

The choice of Bernoulli numbers was not arbitrary either. The sequence was a common subject in nineteenth-century mathematics, used to compress sums of powers into polynomial form. Its decisive property is that the nth value is defined in terms of the values preceding it. Each result has to be fed forward into the next, so the computing procedure necessarily acquires a repeating structure. It would be hard to find a better example for exhibiting iteration and accumulation.

What the letters of summer 1843 preserve

Over the weeks in which the notes were being written the two exchanged letters almost daily. Lovelace wrote from the Ockham Park estate south of London; Babbage replied from his house on Dorset Street in the city. That correspondence survives, divided between the British Library, the Science Museum and the Bodleian Library at Oxford, and it was assembled into citable form in journal articles published in 1980 and 2003.

Here is what the letters settle. In a letter dated only with a weekday, Lovelace is the one who proposes putting Bernoulli numbers into a note. The reason is stated as well: she wants it as an illustration of how an implicit function might be worked out by the engine without having been worked out by human head and hands first. She then asks for the necessary data and formulae.

That single sentence carries a lot of weight in the dispute, because it contains both halves at once. The choice of the example and the purpose behind that choice come from her side; the mathematics itself is what she requests from him. Detach either half and you get the opposite picture.

The letters that follow preserve further detail. In one written on a Saturday evening she reports discovering that the table and diagram are seriously wrong in one or two points, says she has remade them, and adds that the new version improves considerably on the first. In the Sunday letter she notes that the diagram was done in pencil and that her husband is at that moment inking it over for her. In another letter dealing with repetition she explains that a recurring group or cycle would arise among the variable cards as well as the operation cards, and identifies the outer loop as the repetitions of thirteen through twenty-three.

Babbage's side survives too. In a letter dated 30 June 1843 he says he is delighted with Note D, calls it her usual clear style, and says he will himself make one trifling alteration. In another he says he is very reluctant to return Note A and asks her not to alter it.

The sentence Babbage himself wrote in 1864

Lovelace died in 1852 at the age of thirty-six. Twelve years later Babbage set down his own account of how the notes came about. The memoir was published in London in 1864 and the passage sits on page 136.

Its substance is this. They discussed together which illustrations might be introduced and he suggested several, but the selection was entirely her own. So too, he writes, was the algebraic working out of the different problems, with one exception: the one relating to the numbers of Bernoulli, which he had offered to do in order to save her the trouble. That one she sent back to him for an amendment, having detected a grave mistake he had made in the process. He then records that the notes extend to about three times the length of the original memoir, and that their author had entered fully into almost all of the very difficult and abstract questions connected with the subject.

The weight of that statement comes from its timing. He was writing for posterity and plainly cared about memorializing his engines, and he had no motive to inflate the share of someone already dead. At the same time the passage does not hand a clean win to either side, because the claim that he took on the algebraic working of the Bernoulli case and the claim that the selection and the working out were entirely hers stand in the same paragraph.

The skeptical case - what Bromley and Stein leaned on

The skeptical line has three strands. It begins with Bruce Collier's doctoral thesis, submitted at Harvard in 1970, which contains a sharply negative assessment. One frequently quoted and unpleasant sentence, however, carries a caveat. A magazine piece in 2010 presented that sentence as a quotation from the thesis, but a researcher who afterwards checked a printed copy recorded that he could not find the sentence in it. A quotation in wide circulation is not necessarily present in the original.

The most influential figure is Allan Bromley (1947-2002), a computer scientist at the University of Sydney whose meticulous reading of Babbage's drawings and notebooks is what made the Science Museum's Difference Engine reconstruction possible. His position hardened in two stages. In a 1982 paper he wrote that the notes were composed under Babbage's supervision. In a 1990 volume chapter he wrote that all but one of the programs cited in the notes had been prepared by Babbage three to seven years earlier, and that the exception, on Bernoulli numbers, had been prepared by Babbage for her. Even inside that sentence, though, a qualifier travels along: she did detect an error in it.

Dorothy Stein's 1985 biography is the second strand. One of its grounds is a typographical error. In Menabrea's French original the word meaning case was misprinted so that it read like an abbreviation for a trigonometric function, and Menabrea, Lovelace and Babbage all failed to catch it. Stein read that as evidence of a shallow mathematical grasp.

Something dropped out of that line as it entered the standard textbooks. A recent edition of a widely used history of computing states that all of the programs in the article were Babbage's work. Yet Bromley himself had written all but one. The qualifier vanished in one further round of paraphrase. Here you can watch retelling thin a source out.

One more thing belongs here. In later private correspondence with Maurice Wilkes, Bromley acknowledged that he had at times expressed his views too intemperately, and in the same exchange he set down his own verdict that the Analytical Engine was a very sophisticated calculator that had not crossed the watershed into stored-program computing. His assessment of Lovelace and his assessment of Babbage were being adjusted together, inside the same person.

What is settled and what is interpretation

Now the claims from both sides go into one table alongside the evidence each rests on. No verdict is rendered. Each row simply separates what is written on paper from the judgement drawn out of that paper.

Read the table down its columns and a pattern emerges. The third column almost never collides with itself. It is true that Babbage had drawn up tables earlier; it is true that Lovelace drew the plate in pencil; it is true that Babbage admitted his own mistake. Every collision happens in the fourth column. Which is to say this is not a dispute about facts but a dispute about units of accounting.

The title itself was attached in the 1950s

One point deserves to be made plainly. The title of first programmer is not an 1843 artifact. The word program appears nowhere in the notes. The very idea of programming a computer is vocabulary that arrives a century later. The path by which the title got attached reconstructs roughly like this.

  • 1950 - Alan Turing, writing on machine intelligence, quotes a passage from Note G and names it Lady Lovelace's objection. The quotation as he gives it drops one word from the original
  • 1953 - A symposium volume on digital computing machines introduces the content of the notes to the first generation of machine builders. Its text says that the plate is what we should now call a programme. Participants in the Ada language effort later recalled this book as the source from which they had all learned about Lovelace
  • 10 December 1980 - The United States Department of Defense reference manual for the language is approved and given the standard number 1815, which is Lovelace's year of birth, on a date that is her birthday
  • 1983 and 1987 - Revised editions become an American national standard and then international standard 8652, and the name of the language spreads widely

The order matters. The title did not emerge from archival research; it was attached during the 1950s, as the machine generation looked for its own lineage, and the 1980 naming of the standard hardened it. Only afterward did the documentary scholarship of the 1970s onward collide with it. Reverse the order and you misread the nature of the dispute. It is not that scholars ruled first and popular writing pushed back late; the naming came first and the checking followed.

Code for a machine that was never built

One fact frequently drops out of the argument. The Analytical Engine was never built. Not in Babbage's lifetime and not afterward. The diagram was written toward the specification of a machine that did not exist.

That condition changes everything. Without execution there is no arbiter of true and false. The way we adjudicate code today is mostly by running it. In 1843 that route was unavailable. All that remained was consistency on the page, which is why the two of them exchanged letters checking each other's indices and signs. It is why Lovelace asked Babbage to scrutinise the indices on particular sheets very carefully. Mutual review stood in for execution.

The machines themselves ended differently. The Difference Engine side eventually produced hardware. The Science Museum in London built Difference Engine No. 2 from Babbage's design of 1847 to 1849; the calculating section was completed in June 1991 and the printing mechanism was added in 2002. Doron Swade led the project. The Analytical Engine side has no physical object at all.

So verification of the diagram arrived by another route. In a 1979 paper published in a Russian-language journal, two researchers transcribed the Note G plate into a 65-line FORTRAN program. A handful of misprints turned up, along with one variable carrying the wrong sign, and once that was corrected the series of Bernoulli numbers came out correctly. A machine ran the procedure for the first time 136 years after it went to press.

There is a knot in the history of programming here. Design can run ahead of implementation, and the gap between them can exceed a century. And a procedure that never runs still leaves behind an object of inspection, namely internal consistency. Structurally this is not different from the problem of the gap between specification and implementation today. Who keeps the infrastructure running, which deals with the case where the spec survives while the implementation stops, is the modern version of the same axis.

Sentences that are in the Notes and sentences that are not

Writing about Lovelace tends to attract romanticised claims: that she foresaw artificial intelligence, that she anticipated thinking machines. Put the sentences actually printed in the notes on the table and they point elsewhere.

The passage in Note G opens like this. The Analytical Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform. It can follow analysis, but it has no power of anticipating any analytical relations or truths. That is an explicit denial of machine origination. It is precisely why Turing quoted the passage in 1950 and labelled it Lady Lovelace's objection. The sentence cited as evidence of prophecy is in fact the sentence that denies the prophecy.

Note A contains a different and often quoted sentence: the analogy in which the Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves. And the same note contains the passage about music. That passage, however, is a conditional. Supposing, it says, that the fundamental relations of pitched sounds in the science of harmony and of musical composition were susceptible of such expression and adaptations, the engine might compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music of any degree of complexity or extent. Strip the conditional clause and you have a prophecy; keep it and you have an argument that operations on number can be generalised to operations on symbols. The second is what is actually written.

So the inventory of what is absent runs as follows. There is no description of a learning machine. There is no concept corresponding to what we now call artificial intelligence. The words program and programmer do not occur. What is present is a discussion of the possible generalisation of symbolic manipulation, and an explicit denial that the machine originates anything of its own.

Why the same structure of argument keeps recurring

The table in section eight showed that the settled facts do not collide with one another. Every collision arose over what to count as a contribution. That question does not belong to 1843 alone.

  • Supplying an expression versus converting it into a procedure - If Babbage gave the formula and Lovelace turned it into twenty-five steps, what is each share? Is knowing an algorithm the same work as bringing it down into an instruction sequence for one particular machine?
  • Verification and error detection - Was finding the grave mistake Babbage himself admitted a contribution or an assistance? It is the same question as where a name goes today when a reviewer's remark is what caught the defect
  • Documentation and explanation - Note A is the first piece of writing that laid out what kind of object the machine was. In which column does a document that establishes the meaning of a design, in the absence of an implementation, belong?

All three questions repeat verbatim in present-day collaboration. Count by commits and design discussion, documentation and review disappear; count by name order and the actual hands disappear. What a project must decide when AI enters its contributions lands in exactly the same place. Without a rule for what counts as a contribution the argument happens after the fact, and an argument after the fact is decided by whichever kinds of records happened to survive.

Look at what kinds of records the events of 1843 left behind and the bias is easy to see. On Babbage's side there survives a large body of design drawings and notebooks. On Lovelace's side there survive a printed article and letters. That researchers reading the drawings and researchers reading the letters reached different conclusions follows naturally. The type of source pushed the conclusion. It is no different from the way an organisation that keeps design documents and an organisation that keeps only chat logs remember their own histories differently. On how to read design documents themselves, how to read open-source architecture covers that ground.

The useful part of this dispute, then, is not the verdict. It lies in the discipline of keeping settled facts and counting rules apart. Deciding who was first does not close by reading more sources; it closes only once the rule for counting is fixed first. What is written on the paper, by contrast, is fixed regardless of the counting rule. Not mixing those two layers is the most practical thing this subject has to offer.

What could not be established

The items below are stated without assertion in the body because the grounds could not be established. Nothing has been filled in by estimate.

  • The original sheet of the Note G folded plate - The account of variable layout, operation count and loop ranges rests on scholarly literature that reproduces and analyses the plate. The online transcriptions carry the body text only and do not reproduce the typography of the inserted sheet, so the fine markings on the plate itself were not verified here directly
  • The tables Babbage made between 1837 and 1840 - The statement that they numbered several dozen, and the question of whether any of them carried a loop structure, rest on Bromley's papers. The original notebooks and drawings in the Science Museum were not consulted, so whether those tables and the Note G plate belong to the same category is left undecided here
  • The disputed quotation from Collier's thesis - Records conflict over whether the harsh sentence in wide circulation actually appears in the thesis. Only the fact that a researcher who checked a printed copy reported not finding it has been stated in the body; the original thesis was not opened to settle the point
  • Page numbers of Menabrea's French original - The journal, month, year and issue number are printed at the head of the 1843 English version, but the page range of the French original could not be established here
  • The approval date of the revised Ada standard - Sources give January and February for the approval of the 1983 revision. Only the year appears in the body
  • Lovelace's work outside Note G - Whether she ever drew up another procedure or table cannot be resolved from the letter excerpts published in the journals to date
