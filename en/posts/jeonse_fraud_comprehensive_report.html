In the Shadow of Jeonse Fraud: A Comprehensive Report on Korea's Rental Deposit Scam Crisis

Understanding South Korea's Unique Lump-Sum Rental Deposit System and Its Exploitation

June 8, 2025 Finance

I. Introduction: An Unending Nightmare -- The Current State and Severity of Jeonse Fraud

A. Surging Statistics and Scale of Damage

In recent years, South Korean society has been deeply plagued by the nightmare of jeonse (lump-sum rental deposit) fraud. According to the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport's 2023 report, the number of jeonse fraud cases reported more than doubled compared to 2020, showing a steep upward trend. Specifically, approximately 5,000 jeonse fraud cases were filed nationwide in 2023 alone -- a more than 30% increase over the 3,800 cases in 2022. Damages have also skyrocketed, with total reported losses exceeding KRW 1 trillion in 2023, a more than 40% increase from the previous year.

Particularly alarming is the rise in high-value fraud: cases involving deposits of KRW 100 million (approximately USD 75,000) or more surged from 30% of all cases in 2020 to 45% in 2023. This indicates not only an increase in the sheer number of scams but also a growing scale of individual losses. The government officially recognized over 25,000 victims, and total jeonse deposit guarantee losses in 2023 are estimated at approximately KRW 4.3 trillion. A government-wide special crackdown identified 16,314 victims with combined losses of KRW 2.4963 trillion. By May 2025, a total of 30,400 cases had been formally classified as jeonse fraud, demonstrating the continuously expanding scope of the crisis.

These statistics make it clear that jeonse fraud is no longer a matter of personal bad luck or a problem confined to certain neighborhoods -- it has escalated to a national-level crisis. The increase in high-value fraud in particular suggests that scam methods have become increasingly organized and sophisticated, signaling not just quantitative growth but a qualitative deterioration of the problem.

B. Background: How Jeonse Fraud Became a Social Crisis

Multiple factors have converged to make jeonse fraud such a severe social problem. What was once limited to specific regions or demographics has spread nationwide, closely linked to structural instability in the real estate market. In recent years, South Korea's real estate market has experienced dramatic price swings driven by changing economic conditions and shifting government policies, causing tremendous confusion for tenants.

The sharp rise in jeonse prices and the broader housing price boom intensified the shortage of jeonse units, pushing tenants to rush into contracts even under unfavorable conditions. Government policy failures and resulting market distortions have also been cited as key drivers of the crisis. This market environment has further widened the information asymmetry between landlords and tenants.

Jeonse fraud has evolved beyond simple individual crimes into a threat that undermines society's entire trust system -- a consequence of these accumulated structural problems. This suggests that the issue cannot be solved through criminal punishment alone; a multifaceted approach including market stabilization and institutional reform is essential.

C. The Primary Victims: The Harsh Reality for Young Renters

The greatest victims of jeonse fraud are people in their 20s and 30s -- young adults who are relatively inexperienced and financially vulnerable. Statistically, young people account for approximately 63% of all victims, with some data sources reporting rates as high as 74.36%. The government crackdown confirmed that victims aged 30 and under constituted 62.8% of the total.

The disproportionate exposure of young people to jeonse fraud is inseparable from their socioeconomic position. With limited assets and less experience, young renters have lower access to critical information during the leasing process and tend to rush contracts to escape housing instability. For many of them, the jeonse deposit represents virtually their entire savings, making fraud not just a financial loss but a devastating blow to their entire lives.

For a generation that already struggles to afford homeownership due to skyrocketing housing prices, jeonse has served as nearly the only rung on the housing ladder. But even this option is now exposed to the risk of fraud, severely threatening housing stability for young adults. This transcends individual harm -- it undermines the economic independence and social stability of the very generation that will lead the country's future, potentially deepening intergenerational inequality and sapping the vitality of society as a whole.

II. Types and Methods of Jeonse Fraud: How They Target Tenants

Jeonse scammers employ a wide range of methods to target tenants' precious deposits. Their tactics are becoming increasingly cunning and organized, making it extremely difficult for ordinary citizens to detect them.

A. Analysis of Major Jeonse Fraud Types

Kkangtong Jeonse (Empty-Shell Jeonse; "Underwater" Deposits)

This is the most fundamental form, where the landlord has neither the intention nor the ability to return the jeonse deposit. It typically occurs when the combined total of the jeonse deposit and the mortgage exceeds 80% of the property's actual market value. It is closely associated with so-called "gap investment" (gaeptuja), where the landlord purchases property using only the tenant's deposit and bank loans, with no personal capital. This is especially common with newly built villas (low-rise apartment buildings) where accurate market values are hard to determine. Developers or landlords set the jeonse deposit at or above the property's market value, then transfer ownership to an insolvent shell landlord or straw buyer, absconding with the funds.

Forged Documents and Fake Landlord Scams

In this scheme, fraudsters use forged property registry documents (deunggibu deungbon) or fake identification during the contract process. They may also present a fake landlord who is not the actual property owner, then sign the contract and steal the deposit.

Double Contracts (Overlapping Leases)

This method involves signing jeonse contracts with two or more tenants for a single rental property and pocketing the deposits. In some cases, an agent tells the actual landlord they are arranging a wolse (monthly rent) contract while secretly signing a jeonse contract with a new tenant and embezzling the difference.

Trust Fraud (Sintaksagi)

This type exploits South Korea's real estate trust system. When a property is under a trust registration -- meaning ownership rests with the trust company -- the original owner (the trustor) may sign a lease without the trust company's consent, rendering the contract void and leaving the tenant's deposit unprotected. In some cases, the building owner transfers ownership to a trust company, takes out bank loans, then colludes with a real estate agent to falsely assure tenants that their claim has priority over the trust company's, signing jeonse contracts and embezzling the deposits.

No-Money-Down Gap Investment (Mujabongaeptuja)

In this scheme, perpetrators purchase large numbers of properties using jeonse deposits alone -- with zero personal capital. When housing prices decline or they fail to secure additional tenants, they become unable to return deposits. Cases have been uncovered in the Seoul metropolitan area where hundreds of homes were acquired this way, with total embezzled deposits reaching tens of billions of KRW. The Korea Housing and Urban Guarantee Corporation (HUG) jeonse deposit return guarantee system has sometimes been exploited in this process, and developers have been found to facilitate simultaneous purchase and jeonse contracts to deceive tenants.

The prevalence of no-money-down gap investment suggests that this high-risk investment method was tolerated or insufficiently restrained in parts of the market, raising a warning that institutional loopholes will continue to be exploited.

Jeonse Loan Fraud

In this type, the landlord and tenant collude to fabricate a jeonse contract, then use it to fraudulently obtain government-subsidized jeonse loans from financial institutions.
