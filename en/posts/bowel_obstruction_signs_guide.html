Bowel Obstruction Symptoms and Emergency Precautions

The signals that mean go now, and why home remedies make it worse

Jul 27, 2026 Health
⚠️ Medical information notice. This article is for general information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Do not use it to label your own condition or to postpone care. If you have the symptoms described below, seek medical attention immediately. In South Korea the emergency number is 119; if you are outside Korea, use your own country emergency number.

Introduction: pain in the belly, and nothing coming out

A bowel obstruction is a state in which food, digestive fluid and gas can no longer move down the digestive tract because something is blocking the way. As hospital patient references describe it, part of the tract running from the esophagus through the stomach, duodenum, small intestine and colon becomes partly or completely blocked, so contents cannot pass. Some episodes settle on their own. Others deteriorate very quickly, and that is the problem.

MedlinePlus, run by the U.S. National Library of Medicine, states plainly that a complete intestinal obstruction is a medical emergency and often requires surgery. Fluid, gas and digestive secretions build up above the blockage, pressure inside the bowel rises, the bowel wall swells, blood flow worsens, and the bowel can eventually die or tear. If it tears, contents spill into the abdominal cavity and can cause peritonitis and sepsis.

Yet early on, an obstruction can look like ordinary indigestion or constipation. That resemblance is why so many people decide to wait and see, and why the condition is often recognised late. This article is not written to help you diagnose yourself. It is written so you can recognise which signals mean you should get a clinician to look at you without delay, and so you know what not to do before you get there. The section on dangerous home remedies is the most important part.

1. Not one condition but two - mechanical blockage versus ileus

Two very different situations can look like a blocked bowel. Because the cause differs, so does the treatment, which is why clinicians try to distinguish them early.

1.1 Mechanical obstruction - the path is physically blocked

Here the passage is genuinely narrowed or closed off. Common culprits include adhesions (bands of scar tissue that stick loops of bowel to each other or to the abdominal wall after previous surgery), bowel trapped in a hernia, tumours, volvulus where the bowel twists on itself, and intussusception where one segment telescopes into another. According to StatPearls, published through the U.S. National Institutes of Health, adhesions are the leading cause of small bowel obstruction in developed countries, followed by hernias and then malignancy.

1.2 Paralytic ileus - the path is open but nothing moves

In ileus there is no physical blockage; the bowel simply stops propelling contents forward. Classic triggers include recent abdominal surgery, inflammation inside the abdomen, electrolyte disturbance such as low potassium, and certain medicines, notably opioid painkillers. The symptoms can look much like a mechanical blockage, but treatment focuses on correcting the underlying cause and supporting recovery.

1.3 Partial versus complete

The degree of blockage matters too. In a partial obstruction some contents squeeze past the narrowing, so a little gas or some loose stool may still come out. That is exactly why people wrongly conclude that diarrhoea rules out a blockage. A complete obstruction means neither stool nor gas passes, and as noted above that is an emergency.

2. What it feels like - four core symptoms

Medical references consistently list four hallmark features: abdominal pain, vomiting, distension, and the inability to pass stool or gas. Any one alone means little. Several together mean a great deal.

4. The danger that drives everything - strangulation

Every clinical guideline on bowel obstruction treats one concept with particular seriousness: strangulation. It means more than a blocked path. It means the blood supply to that segment of bowel has also been cut off.

4.1 Why it is so serious

Without blood flow, the bowel wall begins to fail. StatPearls describes the cascade: bowel above the blockage distends, venous return is impaired, the wall becomes oedematous, the mucosa turns ischemic, gut bacteria translocate across the wall, and necrosis and perforation can follow. Once perforation occurs, peritonitis and sepsis become life-threatening possibilities.

4.2 What makes clinicians suspect it

The findings reported as suggestive of strangulation or ischemia are listed below. Several can only be established with hospital tests; only the first few are things a person might notice themselves.

  • Pain that changes from intermittent waves to constant, severe, unrelenting pain
  • Fever
  • Rapid pulse (tachycardia)
  • Localised tenderness over one spot
  • Signs of peritoneal irritation - rigidity, rebound tenderness, guarding
  • Raised white cell count on blood tests
  • Metabolic acidosis and elevated lactate
  • Bowel wall thickening, mesenteric stranding or reduced perfusion on CT

4.3 In this case, treatment does not wait

For a simple obstruction, a period of conservative management is reasonable. The Bologna guidelines on adhesive small bowel obstruction recommend an initial trial of medical treatment for around 72 hours before proposing surgery. But the same guidance carves out a clear exception: when signs of severity such as strangulation, peritonitis or intestinal ischemia are already present, surgery goes ahead without that wait. In other words, when strangulation is suspected, time is bowel. That is precisely why waiting at home overnight is a costly choice.

5. Causes and who is at higher risk

5.1 Common causes in adults

  • Adhesions after abdominal surgery - the leading cause of small bowel obstruction in adults. Appendectomy, caesarean section, gynaecological and colorectal surgery can all lead to them, and obstruction can appear years or even decades later
  • Hernia - bowel pushing through a weak point in the abdominal wall and becoming trapped. It is the leading cause in people who have never had abdominal surgery
  • Tumours - colorectal cancer and other malignancies narrowing the lumen. Lesions in the descending and sigmoid colon in particular are often first discovered because they cause obstruction
  • Inflammatory bowel disease - repeated inflammation and stricture, as in Crohn disease
  • Diverticulitis - narrowing after recurrent inflammation
  • Volvulus - the bowel twisting on itself; sigmoid volvulus is recognised in older adults
  • Scarring after radiotherapy to the abdomen or pelvis
  • Gallstone ileus, swallowed foreign bodies and faecal impaction among less frequent causes

5.2 Causes that matter most in children

The pattern is different in infants and young children. Intussusception is a classic example: sudden severe pain makes the child draw the legs up and cry inconsolably, then settle briefly, in a repeating cycle. Over time green bilious vomiting appears, and Korean hospital references note that a majority of cases show a characteristic sticky redcurrant-jelly stool within about 12 hours of onset.

👶 With children, move faster. If a child vomits green or yellow-green material, passes bloody stool, develops a swollen firm abdomen, or becomes floppy and unresponsive, seek emergency care without delay. Children cannot describe what they feel, and their condition can deteriorate quickly.

5.3 Higher-risk groups - the same symptoms should move you faster

  • Anyone who has had abdominal or pelvic surgery, no matter how long ago
  • People with a hernia, especially if the bulge will not reduce
  • People with colorectal or other intra-abdominal cancer, treated or untreated
  • People on chemotherapy or with known peritoneal spread
  • People with Crohn disease or other inflammatory bowel disease
  • People who have had abdominal or pelvic radiotherapy
  • Older adults, in whom pain is often understated and diagnosis delayed
  • People with limited mobility or cognitive impairment who cannot report symptoms well
  • People on long-term opioid painkillers, who are at risk of reduced bowel motility

6. What not to do - why home remedies are dangerous

This is the section that matters most. The internet is full of advice on how to unblock a bowel at home, but when an obstruction is suspected, the actions below can make things worse. Pressure above the blockage is already elevated; adding contents or stimulation stretches the wall further, worsens blood flow, and in the worst case contributes to perforation.

🚫 Do not do these when a bowel obstruction is suspected
  • Taking laxatives - forcing material toward a blocked path is hazardous
  • Giving yourself an enema - adding pressure and stimulation without knowing the cause
  • Massaging or pressing the abdomen - no evidence of benefit, and potential for harm
  • Drinking water or other fluids, or eating - contents accumulate further and the risk of vomiting and aspiration rises
  • Self-medicating with painkillers, anti-diarrheals or anti-emetics - masks the picture and delays diagnosis
  • Deciding to wait a day and see - the most expensive option if strangulation is under way

What to do instead is simple: stop eating and drinking, note how symptoms are changing, and get to a medical facility. Emergency departments generally want patients fasted anyway, so arriving with an empty stomach usually speeds up assessment and treatment.

7. What happens at the hospital - knowing helps

One reason people hesitate to go is simply not knowing what will happen. Understanding the usual pathway makes the decision quicker.

7.1 Diagnosis - history, examination, imaging

  • History and physical examination - previous surgery, when symptoms started, when gas and stool last passed, palpation and auscultation of the abdomen
  • Blood tests - white cell count, electrolytes, kidney function and lactate to gauge dehydration and possible ischemia
  • Abdominal X-ray - the basic study, showing dilated loops and air-fluid levels. Its sensitivity is limited, so a normal film does not rule out obstruction
  • Abdominal CT - regarded as the standard investigation. It shows the transition point, the cause, bowel wall thickening, and whether ischemia or perforation is present
  • Ultrasound - useful where radiation exposure should be minimised, such as in children and pregnancy
  • Endoscopy - sometimes used to identify or treat the cause

7.2 Initial treatment - fasting, fluids, decompression

References consistently describe three pillars of initial care. First, nothing by mouth, so no further load reaches the bowel. Second, intravenous fluids to replace water and electrolytes lost through vomiting and third-space pooling. Third, a nasogastric tube passed through the nose into the stomach to suction out accumulated contents and gas. Medication for pain and nausea is added as needed. The tube is uncomfortable, but it reduces vomiting, lowers aspiration risk and relieves pressure on the bowel.

7.3 Surgery - when it is needed

Many cases improve with conservative care, but surgery is indicated in situations such as these.

  • Findings that suggest strangulation or bowel ischemia
  • Suspected peritonitis or perforation
  • Complete obstruction that does not resolve with conservative treatment
  • Bowel trapped in a hernia that will not reduce
  • A cause such as a tumour that requires operative management

The operation depends on the cause and the patient's condition: dividing adhesions, resecting a narrowed segment and rejoining the bowel, or in some circumstances creating a temporary stoma. In obstruction caused by tumour, a stent is sometimes placed to restore the passage before definitive surgery. The treating team decides which approach fits after reviewing imaging and test results.

8. Information to bring to the clinician

The list below is not a self-diagnosis checklist. It is a note to prepare in advance of the questions you will be asked. The more accurate this information, the faster the diagnosis. If the patient is too unwell to speak, a family member can prepare it instead.

  • The time gas last passed and the time of the last bowel movement (among the most valuable details)
  • When the pain began, and whether it comes in waves or is constant
  • Where the pain is, and whether it has moved over time
  • How many times vomiting occurred and the colour of the vomit (food, yellow, green, brown)
  • When the abdomen began to swell and by how much
  • Whether there has been fever or chills
  • Any previous abdominal or pelvic surgery and when
  • Any diagnosed hernia, or a visible bulge
  • History of cancer, chemotherapy or radiotherapy
  • Any inflammatory bowel disease such as Crohn disease
  • All current medicines, especially painkillers, opioids, laxatives and anticholinergics
  • Recent weight change and change in bowel habit
  • The last thing eaten or drunk and when (needed for surgical planning)

9. Correcting common misconceptions

Some widespread beliefs about blocked bowels are wrong, and a few are dangerous. The table draws on the references cited above.

Warm water will clear itWhat you drink cannot pass, so it accumulates and raises the risk of vomiting and aspiration. Fasting is the rule when obstruction is suspected

10. Recovery and preventing recurrence - follow your team first

For aftercare and prevention, instructions from your own treating team always take precedence. Causes differ from person to person, as do the treatments received, so applying generic advice can do harm. What follows are only widely accepted principles.

  • Restart eating only in the order and at the pace your team sets, which usually means starting with water and advancing in stages
  • Discharge instructions often say to make contact immediately if pain, vomiting, distension or inability to pass gas return. Do not treat that advice lightly
  • Where adhesions were the cause, understand that recurrence is possible. Knowing the warning signs is itself a form of prevention
  • If you have a hernia, discuss repair rather than postponing it. Neglected hernias can cause obstruction
  • Because a tumour can be the underlying cause, discuss colonoscopy and screening if you have changes in bowel habit, blood in stool or weight loss
  • If you take opioids, plan bowel management with your prescriber in advance. Do not stop or add medicines on your own
  • General advice about fluids, fibre and activity applies to everyday bowel health when you are well, not to a moment when obstruction is suspected

Frequently asked questions

Q1. Is there anything safe to do at home?

Trying to fix it at home is not advised. Laxatives, enemas, abdominal massage and forcing down food or fluids all add load to a bowel that is already under pressure. Stop eating and drinking and get medical care right away.

Q2. How do I tell it apart from constipation?

Constipation usually still allows gas to pass; obstruction typically stops both stool and gas and brings pain, vomiting and distension with it. Partial obstruction can still produce some loose stool, so symptoms alone are unreliable. Get it checked rather than deciding yourself.

Q3. Which symptoms mean calling emergency services immediately?

Constant severe pain, a rigid abdomen with rebound tenderness, fever or chills, vomiting blood or faeculent material, and cold sweats, pallor or reduced consciousness. Move faster if you have had abdominal surgery or have a hernia.

Q4. If it turns out to be nothing, was going to the ER a waste?

The cost of missing a bowel obstruction is very high. Finding a different cause is itself valuable information, and ruling things out with tests is legitimate medical care. When it is borderline, err upward.

Q5. If the abdominal X-ray is normal, am I in the clear?

An X-ray is a basic test and does not catch every obstruction. If symptoms continue or worsen, further imaging such as CT may be needed, so do not be reassured by a single result. Report any change in symptoms back to the clinician.

Conclusion: recognising the signal is your job, judging it is theirs

The mistake made most often with bowel obstruction is not misdiagnosis. It is missing the moment to go. When pain, vomiting, distension and the loss of both stool and gas arrive together, the question is not what disease this is; it is whether you need to be seen now. If you have had abdominal surgery, or live with a hernia, a tumour or ongoing chemotherapy, the threshold should be lower still.

And until you are seen, the right thing to do is not to try something but to put nothing in. Laxatives, enemas, abdominal massage, even a mouthful of water, are burdens rather than help when the bowel is blocked. Meanwhile, the details that sound trivial, such as when gas last passed and what colour the vomit was, are genuine clues for the clinician. One line is worth keeping from this article: if in doubt, do not eat, and go to the emergency department.
