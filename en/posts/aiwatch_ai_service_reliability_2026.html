AIWatch and the AI Reliability Illusion: The Gap Between Official Uptime and Real Incidents

What Real-Time Monitoring of 41 AI Services Reveals - Status Page Limits and How to Design for Multi-Vendor Resilience

Jul 17, 2026 IT/Tech

Introduction: We Are Already Standing on AI APIs

What happens when a single LLM API call fails in a production service? Coding agents stall, chatbots go silent, voice assistants lose their response. Over the past few years, that question has stopped being hypothetical and become a daily operational reality. As AI features move onto the critical path of products, "is the AI API reliable?" has effectively become the same question as "is this service alive at all?"

The problem is that we don't have a good standard tool to answer it. For a traditional web service, uptime alone gave a rough sense of reliability. AI services are different. A model can be technically up while its answer quality drifts, requests can complete while latency multiplies several times over, and problems can appear quietly in a single region or a single model version. Prompted by AIWatch, a monitoring service recently featured on hada.io (GeekNews), this piece works through why a gap opens up between "official uptime" and "reliability as it's actually experienced," and what practical system design looks like once you take that gap seriously.

1. What AIWatch Shows - 41 Services, and the Gap With Official Numbers

1.1 What It Monitors

AIWatch is an independent dashboard that tracks 41 AI services in real time (later expanded to 43) - LLM APIs such as Claude, OpenAI, and Gemini; coding agents such as Windsurf; voice tools like Deepgram; and inference infrastructure such as Modal and Groq Cloud. It collects its own uptime, incident, and latency data and rolls them into a composite score out of 100, reportedly weighted across uptime (40 points), incident impact (25 points), recovery speed (15 points), and responsiveness (20 points). The key distinction is that these are numbers a third party measured independently, not numbers a vendor chose to report about itself.

1.2 What the June Report Found

According to the AIWatch report featured on hada.io, 35 of the 41 monitored services experienced at least one outage, and their combined downtime added up to 712 hours and 26 minutes. The more interesting finding is about latency degradation: the same report detected 102 latency degradations, of which 99 never showed up on any official status page. A response that still arrives, just three or five times slower, doesn't get classified as "down" - so it never touches the official metric, even though it's exactly what users experience.

1.3 The Paradox of "99% Official Uptime"

Claude is the clearest illustration of this paradox. In the same report, Claude's official uptime was a very high 99.55%, yet AIWatch logged 45 incidents over the same period and gave it a composite score of 67 out of 100 - a "Fair" rating. A single uptime number says "it was up almost all the time." A count of 45 incidents says "it wobbled often." These are not contradictory. Uptime only measures the share of fully-down time, while an incident count also captures partial outages, degraded performance, and elevated error rates - so the two numbers are simply measuring different things.

Independent monitoring from around the same period shows a similar pattern elsewhere. The Claude API has been observed at roughly 99.32% uptime over a 30-day window (about 5 hours of downtime a month), with 114 incidents logged in a 90-day window - 30 of them major, with a median resolution time of 1 hour 9 minutes. OpenAI, for its part, reported 99.76% official uptime for the December 2025-March 2026 period (about 16 hours a year), while independent tallies from around the same window recorded 22 incidents in a single month. This is best read as a structural feature of the AI API ecosystem as a whole, not a problem specific to one company. In the same AIWatch report, Deepgram had the lowest stability - 6 outages and more than 45 cumulative hours of downtime, including a single 27-hour incident - while Windsurf (100), Modal (94), and Groq Cloud (91) held the highest scores.

2. The Structural Limits of Official Status Pages

2.1 The Built-In Problem of Self-Reporting

Almost every AI provider's status page is written by that same company. What counts as an "incident," how severe it's classified, and when it gets updated are entirely up to the provider's judgment. This isn't malice - it's structural. When the company selling a service is also the one defining that service's reliability metrics, there's a natural incentive to classify ambiguous cases conservatively, i.e., as "not an outage." A status page is a useful reference, but it's important to be clear that it isn't, by itself, an SLA-grade measurement.

2.2 What a Binary Uptime Metric Misses

Uptime is inherently a binary (up/down) metric. A situation where responses still arrive but latency has multiplied, where an error rate suddenly jumps from a fraction of a percent, or where timeouts spike in a single region, is not "down" - so it doesn't touch the uptime number. To users and developers, though, all of these register the same way: "the service is unstable." The "99 of 102 unreported" figure above is precisely the size of that blind spot.

2.3 Aggregation and Lag

Official uptime is usually an average blended across every tier, every model, and every region. An outage confined to one paid tier, one model version, or one region can get diluted into the overall average and become invisible. On top of that, status-page updates are frequently posted well after the actual incident, once a human has confirmed and written them up. The common complaint across many independent monitoring providers is that a status page alone rarely tells you, in real time, why your request is failing right now.

3. Reliability Problems That Are Specific to AI Services

3.1 Failure Modes That Look Nothing Like Traditional APIs

Reliability monitoring for a traditional web API is largely solved by asking "did I get a 200 OK?" LLM APIs are different. A request can return 200 while the answer is truncated by a token limit, get rejected by a content policy, run into a model version that was silently deprecated, or see latency multiply several times over depending on region. A simple health check misses most of this variety.

3.2 Model Changes and Quality Drift

A risk unique to AI services is a model that behaves differently from yesterday while remaining technically "up." When a provider quietly swaps a backend model version or updates a fine-tune, the API endpoint stays the same while output style, accuracy, or tone can shift underneath it. This is sometimes called prompt drift or context rot. In other words, uptime can read 100% while a quality SLO breaks silently.

3.3 Rate Limits and Regional Variance

Under heavy traffic, individual requests don't necessarily fail - they hit a rate limit and get delayed or queued instead. This often isn't logged as an "outage," but it can be fatal for anything latency-sensitive. Add geographic variance in latency and availability on top of that, and the same service can feel meaningfully more or less reliable depending on the user's location or the deployment region.

4. Why Independent Observation Matters

This is where the value of an independent monitor like AIWatch becomes clear. A provider's status page reflects "what we've decided to report." Independent monitoring instead sends real requests and measures latency, error rate, and partial outages from a third-party vantage point. The gap between the two - 99.55% official uptime versus 45 logged incidents - is exactly that value. Independent monitoring has its own limits, of course: results shift depending on the geographic location of the probe, the polling interval, and the detection threshold, and no method fully eliminates the lag between detection and confirmation. Even so, reading official metrics alongside independent observation gets you far closer to the real picture than trusting either source alone.

5. Practical Design: Architect for Failure, Not Around It

If official uptime can't be taken at face value, the conclusion is straightforward: systems should be designed on the assumption that an AI API will fail eventually.

5.1 Timeouts and Retries

The baseline is a sane timeout paired with exponential-backoff retries. Transient latency spikes and one-off errors resolve on their own a large share of the time if you simply retry. Unbounded retries, though, make outages worse, not better - retry storms. A capped retry count and jitter need to be part of the design from the start.

5.2 Multi-Vendor Fallback Chains

Persistent failures that retries can't fix call for a fallback. Define an ordered list - primary vendor, secondary vendor, tertiary vendor - so that when the primary stops responding, requests are automatically routed to the next one. This isn't a question of "which model is better"; it's an availability design meant to make sure the service never fully stops. Because output format, quality, and cost differ across vendors, you also need a plan for how to signal a quality drop to users when a fallback fires.

5.3 Circuit Breakers

When the same failure keeps repeating, retrying every single time is itself wasted effort and added load. The circuit-breaker pattern runs through three states: closed (normal), open (consecutive failures detected, calls blocked immediately), and half-open (after a cooldown, a single trial request checks whether the service has recovered). While open, requests to that vendor aren't sent at all - they're routed straight to a fallback, which prevents latency from piling up and load from compounding.

5.4 Caching and Graceful Degradation

Caching frequently repeated queries cuts down on API calls outright, which also shrinks your exposure surface to any given outage. And rather than making every feature 100% dependent on AI, designing "graceful degradation" - falling back to rule-based responses or a cached prior result when the AI doesn't respond - keeps a single vendor's outage from cascading into a full product outage.

6. Extending SLAs and SLOs Beyond Uptime

A traditional SLA often ended with a single uptime percentage. That's not enough for AI services. The emerging consensus in reliability-engineering discussions is that you need a separate SLO covering response accuracy, hallucination rate, content-policy rejection rate, and human-escalation rate. In practice, it's safer to define internal SLIs and SLOs first, track them on a dashboard, and only publish externally the level you can actually defend. Uptime answers "was it up?" A quality SLO answers "was it useful?" Those are different questions.

7. What This Means for Developers and Businesses

All of this converges on a single question: how dependent is your product on a single AI vendor? Building fast on one API makes sense early on, but once that API sits on the critical path, the cost of vendor lock-in needs to be part of the calculation. Designing around a single official uptime number, versus designing with the knowledge that "there were 45 incidents," leads to genuinely different architectures. There are three things worth doing right now: (1) check whether the AI calls on your critical path have timeouts, retries, and a circuit breaker; (2) wire up at least one fallback vendor, even experimentally; and (3) start logging response quality and latency distribution yourself, beyond whatever uptime number the vendor reports.

Conclusion: Design for Unreliability

The numbers AIWatch surfaced - 35 of 41 services experiencing an outage, 712 cumulative hours of downtime, and 99 of 102 latency degradations that never touched a status page - aren't an attack on any one company. They're a signal that, as of July 2026, AI infrastructure as a whole still hasn't matured to the level of traditional cloud infrastructure. Official uptime is still worth checking, but it isn't the whole picture. Real reliability comes from independent observation, and from designing around the imperfection that observation reveals. The gap between teams that build on the assumption "an AI API will fail, somehow, eventually" and teams that don't only becomes visible the moment the next outage hits.
