Plantar Fasciitis Symptoms and Causes

Why the first step hurts most - why it is not really an -itis, what to rule out, and when to see a clinician

Aug 16, 2026 Health & Wellness

Introduction: worst on the first step, easier once you walk

You put a foot down beside the bed and the inside of the heel feels like it landed on a nail. Ten steps later it has faded. Then you stand all afternoon, sit down for a few minutes, get up again, and it is as bad as the morning. That pattern is itself information. Worst right after rest, easier once you move is the shape most often reported in plantar fasciitis, and as you will see below, pain that departs from that shape points somewhere else.

The name carries a built-in misunderstanding. The suffix means inflammation, yet in tissue taken from chronic cases inflammatory cells are largely absent. This is not an academic footnote. What you expect, how long you are willing to wait, and which procedures rest on shaky ground all turn on it.

This article stays with the shape of the symptoms, the causes, and above all what you have to separate out before calling heel pain plantar fasciitis. Treatment protocols and the mechanics of each stretch are covered separately in the Plantar Fasciitis Complete Guide — Symptoms, Causes, Diagnosis, Treatment & Stretches 2026.

Medical disclaimer
This article is for information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. If symptoms persist, seek care from a medical professional. Nothing here is a basis for starting or stopping any medication on your own. Diagnosis and the treatment plan follow the judgement of a clinician who has examined you. In particular, do not obtain and inject anything yourself, do not use medication prescribed to someone else, and do not accept unvalidated procedures. If you have diabetes, peripheral neuropathy, a weakened immune system, or you are pregnant, the calculus may differ from the general adult case. Acute pain following an injury, severe pain at rest, fever, or altered sensation all mean seek care rather than wait.

1. What the plantar fascia is, and exactly where it hurts

The plantar fascia is a thick, tough fibrous band running from the medial tubercle of the calcaneus — the heel bone — forward along the sole, fanning out toward the bases of the five toes. It is not a muscle, and that matters: it is a structure built to resist tension, a tissue whose job is to refuse to lengthen rather than to shorten and generate force.

The painful area is usually narrow. Not the whole sole, but the inner side of the underside of the heel, the single point where the fascia attaches to bone. Press with a finger and many people can find that spot themselves.

Fixing this location in mind makes the later comparisons easier.

  • Inner underside of the heel - the fascial attachment. Pain here that spikes on the first step is the typical picture.
  • Dead centre of the heel pad - this is where the fat pad sits. Pain that is worst barefoot on hard floors tells a different story.
  • Back of the heel - where the Achilles tendon inserts. A different structure entirely.
  • Behind the inner ankle bone - where nerves and tendons pass through a tunnel. Accompanying numbness points here.
  • Squeezing the heel from both sides - that direction loads bone, not fascia. Pain on squeezing raises a bone question.

Functionally the fascia is the cable in the windlass mechanism. As the toes lift during walking, the fascia winds around the joint, the arch rises and the foot stiffens, and that stiff foot is what pushes the body forward. The clinical test in which the toes are extended to reproduce pain uses exactly this mechanism.

2. Why the first step of the morning is the worst

The distinctive feature is not how intense the pain is but when it arrives. StatPearls describes it as sharp heel pain that is particularly bad with the first few steps out of bed in the morning, typically decreasing once activity begins and worsening again as the day goes on.

The mechanism is simple. During sleep the foot mostly rests with the toes pointing down. In that position the calf muscles, the Achilles tendon and the plantar fascia all sit shortened for several hours, and the tissue stiffens at that length. Put body weight on it and the stiffened tissue is pulled out to full length at once, loading a single attachment point with a sudden spike of tension.

So the real trigger is not the morning but the first movement after any period of stillness. Standing up after thirty minutes in a meeting, stepping out of the car at the end of a long drive, getting out of a cinema seat — the same pain arrives. A few steps later it eases as the tissue lengthens again.

A second curve overlays the first. Even after the pain fades, a day spent standing or walking brings it back by evening. The first curve is pain from stiffness; the second is pain from accumulated load. Because they overlap, the typical day is bad in the morning, best in the middle of the morning, and bad again at night.

Knowing the shape has a use. Pain while sitting still, pain that wakes you at night, pain that intensifies rather than eases as you walk — these run against the curve. At that point the name plantar fasciitis should be set aside for a moment.

3. Why the -itis in the name is not accurate

A 2003 histology study in the Journal of the American Podiatric Medical Association reviewed 50 surgical specimens from chronic plantar fasciitis. What appeared was myxoid degeneration, fragmentation and degeneration of the fascia, and vascular ectasia in the bone marrow, and in every specimen reviewed there was no histologic evidence of inflammation. The authors framed this as a degenerative fasciosis without inflammation rather than a fasciitis.

StatPearls follows the same line: the condition is characterised by an absence of inflammatory cells despite its name, with granulation tissue, microtears, collagen disarray, calcification and a thickened fascia on examination. This is why more recent writing often prefers plantar fasciopathy.

The conclusion should not be stretched further than the sample allows. Those 50 specimens came from chronic cases that failed conservative care and went to surgery. An absence of inflammation in the chronic stage is not proof of its absence at onset. Reading the sample honestly keeps the claim the right size.

Even so, three things genuinely change.

  • The rationale for repeated corticosteroid injection weakens. That study argued directly that serial corticosteroid injection into a tissue without inflammation should be reconsidered, and noted the potential to induce fascial rupture.
  • The expected timeline shifts. Inflammation suggests days to weeks; remodelling of degenerative tissue runs in months. Still being sore in month two is not, on its own, a sign of failure.
  • Load management moves to the front logically. If the problem is tissue that has to rebuild rather than inflammation that has to settle, the first job is matching daily load to what the tissue can currently carry.

The name will stay as it is by convention, and your clinic will still call it plantar fasciitis. What changes is not the label but what you expect from it.

4. Risk factors — the ones with numbers, and the ones without

Risk-factor lists are everywhere. The problem is that they blend factors with a measured effect size and factors that sound plausible and get repeated. Separate the two and the list gets shorter.

At the centre of the measured side is a matched case-control study published in the Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery in 2003. Fifty patients with unilateral plantar fasciitis were each matched by age and sex to two controls. The findings:

  • Body-mass index above 30 carried an odds ratio of 5.6 (95% CI 1.9 to 16.6) against a referent group at 25 or below.
  • Spending the majority of the workday on one's feet carried an odds ratio of 3.6 (95% CI 1.3 to 10.1) against those who did not.
  • Reduced ankle dorsiflexion — a limited range when bending the foot up toward the shin — was the factor the authors identified as the most important of the three.

The limits belong in the same breath. A case-control design cannot fix the direction of causation, and dorsiflexion was measured after the condition had developed. Whether stiff ankles caused the problem or the sore foot restricted the ankle cannot be separated by this design. The sample is 150 people in total.

On population characteristics StatPearls covers wider ground. Peak incidence falls between ages 40 and 60, with higher prevalence in women, in people aged 45 to 64, and in those with a BMI above 25. The running relationship is more specific: the condition accounts for roughly 10 percent of running-related injuries, and prevalence among runners has been reported as high as 22 percent.

5. Not all heel pain is plantar fasciitis

This is the most important section here. Plantar fasciitis is the most common cause of heel pain seen in outpatient care, but most common and yours are two different statements. StatPearls lists fat pad contusion or atrophy, stress fractures, and nerve entrapments such as tarsal tunnel syndrome as things to distinguish, and adds calcaneal injury, infection, neuropathic pain, tendinitis, osteoporosis and malignancy to the wider differential.

In practice the dividing lines are few.

  • Calcaneal stress fracture - often follows a sharp rise in training volume. Squeezing the heel from both sides hurts, pain persists at rest and at night, and walking makes it steadily worse rather than better.
  • Nerve entrapment - tarsal tunnel syndrome and other compressions of nerves passing near the heel. Numbness, burning or reduced sensation come along, pain radiates into the sole or toes, and it often worsens at night.
  • Fat pad atrophy or contusion - the sore point is the centre of the heel, not the inner edge. It is worst barefoot on hard floors and lacks the sharp first-step signature. It becomes more common with age.
  • Plantar fascia rupture - starts with a sudden tearing sensation, sometimes followed by bruising and swelling. The onset is nothing like the gradual arrival of plantar fasciitis.
  • Nerve symptoms originating in the back - the pain does not stay in the heel; it runs up the calf, the back of the thigh, or into the lower back.
  • Involvement of a systemic condition - both heels beginning to hurt around the same time, in a younger person, alongside morning stiffness in other joints or the back, is not a situation for looking only at the foot.
  • Heel pain in children - the distribution of causes in a growing child differs from an adult. Applying the adult plantar fasciitis frame is not the safe default.

If even one item in the right-hand column is clearly present, you have left the territory of self-assessment. Night pain and altered sensation in particular are the two signals that stop a common problem from being treated as a common problem.

One more thing: a heel spur visible on X-ray does not establish that the spur is causing the pain. Spurs turn up in people with no symptoms at all. Seeing one is not a reason to jump to the conclusion that it has to be removed.

6. Warning signs, and how to get seen

If any of the following applies, this is not a situation for counting weeks. Get assessed.
· Acute pain that started immediately after an injury, a tearing sensation, bruising or swelling
· Severe pain even at rest, or pain that wakes you at night
· Fever, redness or heat over the heel
· Numbness, burning or reduced sensation and other nerve symptoms
· Pain that prevents you bearing weight
· Both sides starting around the same time, in a younger person, or alongside morning stiffness in other joints or the back
· A wound or redness on the foot in someone with diabetes, peripheral neuropathy or a weakened immune system
· Steadily worsening despite weeks of reduced load

There is a threshold for the non-urgent case too. If the pain has run past several weeks, or if you are cutting down how long you stand or how far you walk, an assessment is worthwhile — less to start treatment than to rule the other things out. The differential above has to be separated by examination and, where needed, imaging, and the diagnosis and plan follow the clinician's judgement.

Where to go depends on the health system you are in. In some countries a primary care physician or family doctor is the first stop and referral opens the door to orthopaedics, sports medicine or physiotherapy. In others a podiatrist or physiotherapist can be seen directly without a referral. Use the same rule of thumb wherever you are: whichever service in your system is the first point of contact for foot and musculoskeletal problems.

A few things sorted out beforehand shorten the appointment.

  • When the pain started, and what changed around then — training volume, shoes, job, weight, floor surfaces
  • Whether you can point to the sore spot with one finger, and where it is
  • Which time of day is worst and which is best
  • Whether there is any numbness, altered sensation or night pain
  • Roughly how many hours you stand and how far you walk in a day
  • What you have already tried, and what happened

7. Responses with evidence — and the level of that evidence

What follows covers conditions you can adjust yourself. Night splints, shockwave therapy, injections and surgery require clinical judgement and administration and fall outside this article's scope; what to do and when is for the treating clinician to decide.

The thickest strand of evidence is stretching. A randomised trial published in JBJS in 2003 took 101 patients with chronic proximal plantar fasciitis of at least ten months' duration, assigned them to a plantar fascia-specific stretching protocol or an Achilles tendon stretching protocol, and assessed them at eight weeks; the plantar fascia-specific protocol came out ahead. A two-year follow-up of the same cohort published in 2006, with 82 patients completing, reported broad improvement. The 2023 revision of the heel pain clinical practice guideline in the Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy recommends plantar fascia-specific stretching together with gastrocnemius and soleus stretching for short- and long-term pain reduction.

Insoles are a different texture. A systematic review and meta-analysis of foot orthoses for plantar heel pain found that in the short term the evidence was of very low quality and showed no reduction in pain or improvement in function, and in the medium term moderate-quality evidence of pain reduction whose clinical importance is uncertain. A review comparing custom-made with prefabricated devices reported no difference at six weeks, twelve weeks or twelve months.

Translated into practice, there are four adjustable axes: the daily total carried by the foot, how far the ankle bends upward, body weight, and the impact of that first step after stillness. Which to touch first is set by your own conditions. If you stand eight hours a day the total comes first; if you doubled your running distance over two months, that increment does.

8. What not to do

This section separates outcomes more than the previous one does. The behaviours that stretch recovery share a shape: they erase the pain signal and leave the load in place.

  • Running through the pain. If it grows session by session and lingers into the next day, load has outrun repair. This is also the most common route to missing a calcaneal stress fracture.
  • Obtaining and injecting anything yourself. Do not do this for any reason. The same goes for using medication prescribed to someone else.
  • Maintaining activity while painkillers mask the signal. Whether to use medication is a matter for a doctor or pharmacist, and silencing pain does not raise the load a tissue can carry.
  • Accepting a procedure that promises an instant cure without showing evidence. Where the natural course runs in months, an offer of resolution in days is itself a signal.
  • Heavy-pressure massage or rolling meant to break something up. Bearing down hard on the sore point can escalate symptoms. Responding by increasing pressure is not the safe direction.
  • Fixing on the heel spur as the cause. Spurs appear in people without symptoms. That one shows on an image is not, by itself, reason to target it.
  • Returning to the old training volume the moment the pain stops. The day pain disappears and the day the tissue tolerates the old load are not the same day.
  • Carrying your own diagnosis for months while only managing it. Much of the differential list gets harder to deal with the longer it waits.

9. Course, and what this article could not verify

Why no one can promise a recovery time

StatPearls states that about 75 percent resolve spontaneously within twelve months. Elsewhere in the same document it says 70 to 80 percent see symptom reduction between nine and twelve months, and on surgery it gives about 5 percent in one place and at least 5 to 10 percent in another.

The inconsistency is itself informative. Natural-history studies differ in whom they include, how long they follow and what counts as improvement, so the numbers wobble. Which is why a promise of a few weeks is hard to support in this condition. The conditions that create individual variation are real and numerous.

  • Time elapsed since onset — telling, given that the trial above studied cases already running ten months or longer.
  • Hours spent standing or walking each day — the condition carrying an odds ratio of 3.6. If the job does not change, neither does this number.
  • Body weight — the condition carrying an odds ratio of 5.6.
  • How far the ankle bends upward — the factor the authors called the most important.
  • Anything else going on alongside — where an item from the differential list is also present, the course is not explained by one name.

Instead of a duration, watch the direction of change. Compare week to week how many steps it takes for the morning pain to fade, and what time in the evening it comes back; that shows whether the total load is genuinely falling. If those indicators only move the wrong way for several weeks, the thing to revisit is the diagnosis, not the routine.

What this article could not verify

  • The exact odds ratios by ankle dorsiflexion category — the journal's abstract page could not be opened, so only the authors' summary conclusion was confirmed.
  • The standardised mean differences and confidence intervals in the orthoses meta-analysis, along with the number of trials and participants included.
  • The evidence-grade letters the JOSPT 2023 guideline assigns to each recommendation.
  • Any quantified relationship between shoe type, heel height or cushioning and the risk of developing the condition.
  • A consistent effect size for how much flat or high arches raise the risk.
  • Interventional research testing directly whether weight loss reduces pain that has already developed.
  • Country-level prevalence and annual consultation statistics outside the sources cited here.

10. Frequently asked questions

Q. It only hurts on the first step. Do I still need to be seen?

That pattern is indeed the most common one. Matching the pattern is not the same as having a diagnosis, though. If it has run past several weeks, or you are quietly walking less, an assessment mainly rules other things out and saves time. Night pain, altered sensation, acute pain after an injury or fever change the picture entirely.

Q. If it is not inflammation, are anti-inflammatories pointless?

Whether the tissue shows inflammatory cells and whether an analgesic reduces pain are separate questions. What to take, how much and for how long is a matter to settle with a doctor or pharmacist, not something this article can answer. What is worth saying is that masking the pain while keeping activity unchanged is among the most common mistakes in this condition.

Q. Are custom insoles better?

A review comparing custom-made with prefabricated devices reported no difference in pain or function at six weeks, twelve weeks or twelve months. The evidence for insoles in general is of very low quality in the short term and, in the medium term, shows pain reduction of uncertain clinical importance. There is no evidence that paying more buys a better result.

Q. There is a heel spur on my X-ray. Should that be dealt with first?

Spurs are found in people with no symptoms. Visibility on an image does not establish it as the source of pain, and what to target is decided by a clinician reading the image alongside the examination.

Q. Both heels hurt. Does that mean it is worse?

Less worse than possibly something else. If both sides started around the same time and you are younger, or there is morning stiffness in other joints or the back, this is not a situation for looking only at the feet. That combination calls for an assessment that considers systemic causes.

Conclusion: read the shape first, then change the conditions

First, check the shape of the pain. One point on the inner underside of the heel, worst on the first step after stillness, easing within a few steps, sensation normal — that is the common picture. If even one element departs from it — night pain, pain at rest, numbness, onset right after an injury, sudden bilateral onset — get assessed before attaching a name. Heel pain has one common cause; it does not have only one cause.

Second, match your expected timeline to the tissue. What shows up in chronic specimens is degeneration, not inflammation. That means this is not an event that finishes in days, and that pain still present in month two is not by itself a failure. It also means an offer to compress that timeline into days should be asked for correspondingly strong evidence.

Third, change the conditions you can change. The ones carrying numbers were body weight, hours spent standing, and how far the ankle bends upward. Stretching is among the few responses actually compared in trials; insoles are worth trying with adjusted expectations. And whatever else you do, avoid the one combination that reliably backfires: silencing the pain while keeping the load.
