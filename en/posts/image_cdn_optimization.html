Why One Image Outweighs Everything Else on the Page

Where format, resizing, and caching eat into each other between the original and the screen

Aug 27, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: The Script Is Slow, but the Images Are Heavy

Some pages feel exactly the same after the bundle has been split and the third-party scripts pulled out. Sort the transfer table on such a page by size and the top ten rows are almost always images. Teams argue for weeks over a few hundred kilobytes of code, while the twelve photographs sitting on a detail page take up several megabytes that nobody ever counted.

In August 2026, an image proxy called wsrv.nl was posted on GeekNews. It takes the URL of an original image, performs resizing, cropping, format conversion, and compression in real time driven by URL parameters, and caches the result on a CDN. Internally it uses nginx and libvips, and it is published under BSD 3-Clause, so self-hosting is possible.

What deserves attention is not the service but the shape of that one sentence. Take an original, change its size, change its format, cache it. Every image pipeline, whatever it calls itself, is some arrangement of those four actions, and none of them is free. Changing size multiplies variants, changing format drags in compatibility and encoding time, and caching splits keys. There are no configuration snippets here and no product recommendations. Pricing moves over time, so instead of amounts this covers only what gets counted on the invoice.

1. Where Images Sit in Page Weight

The Page Weight chapter of the HTTP Archive Web Almanac 2025 reports medians from the July 2025 crawl. Desktop home pages load 1,058KB of image bytes and mobile home pages 911KB. In the same crawl, median total transfer was 2.86MB for desktop home pages and 2.56MB for mobile.

One caveat belongs right here. You cannot divide one median by another and call the result a percentage. Those two medians come from different pages, so they neither sum to a total nor divide into a ratio. What the numbers give is an order of magnitude, not a share. Images are the single largest line item at roughly a third of page weight, and nothing in that table supports more precision than that.

What matters more is how weight translates into perceived speed. An <img> element does not block the parser and does not hold up first paint, so render blocking is the wrong frame for it. The reasons images still dominate the experience lie elsewhere.

  • The largest element is usually an image. The LCP candidates web.dev defines include <img>, <image> inside SVG, video poster frames, and background images loaded through url(). If the biggest thing on screen is a photo, the metric stays unsettled until that photo lands
  • The bar is tight. A good LCP is 2.5 seconds or less, judged at the 75th percentile and segmented across mobile and desktop
  • Bandwidth is shared. Not blocking the parser does not mean leaving the connection idle
  • Arriving bytes still create work. Decoding cost lands on device performance rather than the connection. That is where a sluggish list view on a low-end handset comes from
  • Unreserved space makes the page jump. Shrinking bytes does not fix that

2. One Original Cannot Serve Every Screen

The most common state of affairs looks like this. A single 3,000-pixel original is shipped to every screen as the same file. Even on a phone 360 CSS pixels wide, the browser downloads all 3,000 pixels and scales them down. The page looks fine, so nobody senses a problem. Nothing anywhere reports that most of the downloaded bytes were discarded. The required size is a product of three axes.

  • Viewport width — how wide the window is
  • Pixel density — how many physical pixels fill one CSS pixel. A 2x or 3x display needs two or three times the image in the same slot
  • Layout — the CSS width that image actually occupies. It might be the whole viewport, or one cell of a three-column grid

2.1 sizes Is a Promise the Developer Makes to the Browser

A width descriptor in srcset declares the intrinsic width of each candidate file, and MDN insists that value must match the intrinsic width of the referenced image. Pixel density descriptors exist too, but mixing the two inside one srcset is incorrect usage.

The difficulty is when the choice happens. The browser begins fetching images before CSS has been applied. At that moment the painted width of the image is not yet determined. That is the slot sizes fills, and leaving it out defaults to 100vw, the full viewport width. Describe one cell of a three-column grid as the entire viewport and the browser will faithfully choose a candidate three times too large.

This is not a theoretical trap. The Web Almanac 2024 Media chapter found srcset on 42% of mobile pages. Yet the same chapter puts the median error of sizes values at 16% on mobile and 43% on desktop. The markup is present and the number inside it is wrong. Those figures come from a crawl using a 360px mobile viewport at 3x density and a 1376px desktop viewport at 1x.

The rule is simple. When layout width varies with the screen, pair width descriptors with sizes; when width is fixed and only density varies, as with a logo, density descriptors finish the job. <picture> solves a different problem: format negotiation, and art direction that changes the crop itself per screen.

3. Generations of Formats — What Each Does Well and What It Charges

Formats arrived in generations, but no new generation has ever replaced its predecessor. Each is good at something different, and each newer one buys compression with some other resource.

FormatGood atWhat it chargesWhere it fits
JPEGLossy compression for photos. Opens everywhere, and progressive rendering shows a blurry version earlyNo transparency. Ringing around sharp edgesThe photo fallback
PNGLossless, full alpha channelFar heavier than lossy formats for photographsGraphics that need transparency
SVGVector, so resolution stops being a questionUseless for photos. Complex paths cost rendering timeIcons, logos, shapes
WebPPer MDN, on average 25–35% smaller than JPEG, and lossless WebP typically 26% smaller than PNGSupported by all major browsers, with the caveat of Safari 14 or later on macOSThe default for photos and graphics alike
AVIFAV1 in a HEIF container. Per MDN, roughly 50% smaller than JPEG at visually similar qualitySlow to encode, support not yet comprehensive so a fallback is required, and no progressive renderingLarge photos where bytes decide

The reasons not to stop at «then just move everything to AVIF» are scattered across that third column.

  • Compression is bought with search time. A smaller file is the result of the encoder spending longer on the same image. At build time the build pays; at request time the user pays
  • There is no progressive rendering. JPEG can show a blurry whole from a partial download; AVIF needs the entire file. Whether «small but all at once» beats «large but blurry early» depends on the role that image plays
  • Decoding is not free. Where hardware acceleration is unavailable, the CPU unpacks it
  • A fallback adds one more variant. Keep all three formats and the same picture becomes three files, splitting cache entries three ways

3.1 Do Not Read Field Distributions as Controlled Experiments

The same Almanac chapter lists median bits per pixel on mobile: GIF 6.7, PNG 3.8, JPG 2.0, AVIF 1.4, WebP 1.3. Read that table alone and the conclusion is that WebP is more efficient than AVIF, the exact opposite of the MDN statement above.

Both are correct, because they measure different things. The figure MDN cites comes from a controlled comparison encoding the same source set into several formats, while the Almanac number is the distribution of images actually sitting on the web. The latter has baked into it what people put in each format and at what quality. With per-format measurements, the first question is whether it is a controlled comparison or a field distribution. Skip that distinction and the same benchmark will confidently produce opposite conclusions.

Adoption is worth a look too. On mobile: JPEG 32.4%, PNG 28.4%, GIF 16.8%, WebP 12%, SVG 6.4%. JPEG has fallen from 40% in 2022 but still leads, while GIF holds a sixth of the total with the worst bits per pixel of any format. The largest untouched chunk is not new-format adoption but the persistence of GIF.

4. Where Does the Resizing Happen

Even producing the same eight widths, when that work happens makes it a different system. There are three options.

PointFits whenWhat you gainThe trap
Pre-generated at build timeThe source set is finite and fully known before deploymentNo computation on the request path. The edge treats everything as ordinary static filesVariants multiply. Five widths times three formats is fifteen files per original. Adding one width means regenerating everything, and user uploads do not fit at all
Transformed at request timeSources are dynamic or effectively unboundedStorage holds only originals. A new width or format is one parameter awayEvery cache miss puts encoding time straight into user-facing latency. Open parameters let anyone invent new combinations
Serve the originalThere are few images and their sizes already suit the screenNothing new to operate. No pipeline means no pipeline to breakIt rests on the discipline of a person shrinking each upload properly. That collapses when the person changes

The request-time trap deserves unpacking once more. A transformation endpoint that accepts arbitrary parameters is an externally exposed permission to spend your money. Allow any integer width and a requester can walk the values to manufacture unbounded cache misses, and on every miss the server genuinely decodes, resizes, and re-encodes. That the service behind this article is described as rate-limiting uncached requests per IP is a structural requirement of the approach.

  • Narrow it with an allowlist. Accept only the widths you chose and clamp or reject the rest
  • Sign the parameters. Requiring a signature over the parameter set makes only your own URLs valid
  • The write-back hybrid is the middle value. Generate on first request and store the result, and from the second request it behaves like a static file. In exchange, more objects need invalidating

5. The Cache Remembers URLs

MDN is explicit when it describes HTTP caching. Responses are distinguished from one another essentially by their URL, and adding a Vary header keys the cache on a composite of the response URL and the listed request headers.

What follows is that a cache key is designed rather than given. Every time the URL forks, one more entry appears at the edge, and each new entry must reach the origin at least once to be filled. Eight widths times three formats times three quality levels turns one original into 72 cache entries, and with unchanged visitor numbers each entry now sees one seventy-second of the requests.

That is where hit rate goes. Not because traffic fell, but because keys multiplied. The edge does not hold entries forever either; the least requested ones get evicted first. The finer the keys are sliced, the lower the odds any given entry is requested again before eviction, and those odds feed the next miss. Adding variants is simultaneously a decision to reduce bytes and a decision to erode hit rate, and the two effects carry opposite signs.

Vary carries its own snare. MDN warns directly that User-Agent has a very large number of variations that drastically reduce cache reuse, and suggests varying behavior on feature detection instead where possible. Negotiating format through Accept sits on the same axis.

  • Normalize parameters. ?w=800&q=80 and ?q=80&w=800 look identical to a person and different to a cache
  • Keep tracking parameters off image URLs. Let them attach and you create one key per visitor
  • Change the URL when the content changes. A content hash in the filename makes one URL mean one set of bytes forever, which is what finally allows max-age=31536000, immutable
  • Holding the URL fixed while changing content forces invalidation. Invalidation is an asynchronous job across every edge, so it is never instant
  • Revalidation saves bytes only. A 304 via ETag still costs the round trip. For images that barely change, changing the URL is the better move

6. Reserve the Space First, Then Fix the Order

Two things survive any amount of byte reduction: a page that jumps, and an image you need now arriving late.

6.1 Layout Shift

Per web.dev a good CLS is 0.1 or less, again judged at the 75th percentile and segmented across mobile and desktop. The first line of its cause list is images and videos with unknown dimensions. MDN notes that including height and width lets the browser calculate the aspect ratio before load and reserve the space.

The common worry that this fights responsive CSS is unfounded. Pair it with width:100%; height:auto and the HTML attributes stop dictating display size and serve only the aspect ratio calculation. Adoption is low regardless. Web Almanac 2024 found that 32% of mobile <img> elements carry both width and height. Two-thirds still wait for the image with the space left empty.

6.2 Lazy Loading and Priority

What MDN describes for loading="lazy" is deferring the load until the image comes within a browser-calculated distance of the viewport. For images further down, that is exactly the right tool.

The problem is applying it above the fold. Lazy loading means starting later, and an above-the-fold image is needed now. There is a nastier combination on top of that. MDN stresses that explicit dimensions matter especially for lazy-loaded images, and gives the reason: a lazy-loaded image never loads unless it intersects a visible part of an element, and an unloaded image has a width and height of 0. Apply lazy loading without dimensions and you can create an image that would intersect once loaded but never loads.

There is a measurement. In A/B testing by WordPress core, turning off lazy loading for above-the-fold images improved LCP by 13–15% on archive-type pages and only 2–4% on single post pages. That the same change lands more than five times differently by page type is the real content of the number. It is a magnitude to reason with, not a value to copy. And the anti-pattern really is deployed: the same Almanac reports lazy loading on a third of all sites, with about 9.5% of LCP images lazy-loaded.

  • Give every image dimensions or an aspect-ratio. Whatever you exempt is what jumps
  • Never lazy-load an above-the-fold image. What sits above the fold is needed now
  • Give the priority hint to exactly one image. fetchpriority is a relative hint, so marking everything high marks nothing high
  • Do not let a background image become the LCP element. Images loaded via url() are discovered only after the stylesheet applies, so they start late

7. Build It Yourself or Hand It Off

Once transformation is a given, the next question arrives. Do we operate it, or do we hand it off. Rather than recommending a side, it is more useful to see what moves in which direction. This is not a performance question but a question of where responsibility sits.

Start with what self-operation takes on. An image decoder is fundamentally code that parses untrusted bytes. The moment you open a user-uploaded file on your server, that parser's vulnerabilities become yours, and isolating conversion in a sandbox becomes the baseline posture. Resource bomb defenses come as a pair: without limits on pixel count, frame count, and job duration, a single file that is small compressed and enormous expanded can burn your memory. In exchange you control parameter policy and retention, and images never leave the organization.

An external service takes that burden and the global footprint wholesale. Things travel the other way too. The original URL, or the original itself, passes through a third party, so for images that must stay access-controlled this becomes the central design question. Availability also leaves your control, and if that service stops, your images stop entirely. There is only one real device for lowering migration cost: keep image URLs behind your own domain so the backend can be swapped underneath.

Pricing moves over time, so look at the axis rather than the amount. Commercial billing usually rests on at least one of three: bytes delivered, the count of unique transformation results, and the number of stored originals. Under bytes-delivered billing, adopting AVIF is a saving; under per-transformation billing, each added format adds results. Before opening a price sheet, know which axis your own traffic is large on. Free tiers are mostly made of rate limits and cache retention windows, and a short retention window increases requests falling back to your origin, which is load you carry.

  • Images are static and number in the dozens — building nothing is the right answer
  • There are user uploads — transformation is a premise, not a choice
  • The images themselves need protection — third-party transit usually settles the question
  • There is no standing operations capacity — self-hosting bills you not on the day you build it but on the day a vulnerability is published

9. What to Measure

Transfer size is an upstream indicator. It can drop with no change in perception, or stay flat while perception improves. Judgment belongs on the user-facing axis.

Two metrics sit at the center: when the largest element painted (2.5 seconds or less) and how far the layout moved (0.1 or less). Both are judged at the 75th percentile, segmented across mobile and desktop. They are defined on a percentile because averages erase the slow tail, and image problems live exactly there.

The axes worth splitting on are page type, connection, device tier, and region. The lazy-loading experiment above landing at 13–15% for archives and 2–4% for single posts shows why. Lab measurement gives a reproducible number on a fixed device and connection; field measurement gives the real distribution of visitors. When the lab improves and the field does not, you usually fixed a population that does not exist.

  • Synthetic tests measure a cold-cache first visit. Request-time transformation latency is overstated there while repeat-visit benefit never shows
  • Tools that emit one score emit a weighted sum. Raising the score and fixing the experience are not the same task
  • Edge hit rate is invisible from the browser. It has to come from cache status headers or CDN logs, and a report missing it has told half the story
  • The cheapest check is developer tools. Put the delivered file's width next to the painted width and a wrong sizes becomes obvious at a glance

What Could Not Be Verified

  • The hourly throughput and monthly transfer figures for the service behind this article come from the GeekNews summary, and the operator's own page would not open for comparison
  • Whether a 2025 edition of the Almanac Media chapter has been published could not be confirmed. Format adoption, lazy loading, and srcset measurements here are from the 2024 edition
  • Current pricing levels and free-tier conditions for commercial image services shift over time, so only the billing axes are covered
  • How widely AVIF hardware decoding is actually used across devices could not be found in public sources

Wrapping Up

Images are the heaviest thing on a page because they are the asset a person makes and uploads by hand. Tooling watches the size of code; nobody counts photographs. So the first step is not compression but knowing what stands between the original and the screen.

Four things stand there. Size is the product of viewport width, pixel density, and layout, which one original cannot satisfy, and a wrong sizes makes responsive markup pick the wrong candidate. Format gets smaller with each generation while charging encoding time and fallbacks. The resizing point decides whether you absorb multiplying variants or first-request latency. The cache remembers URLs, so the moment you add variants the hit rate erodes.

So there is no one-line answer of the form «do this and it gets faster». There are only trade-offs, and which way to lean is decided by what that image is on the page for. There is an order, though. Reserve the space, protect the order of the one image above the fold, and only then reduce bytes. Invert it and you arrive at the familiar ending where the transfer graph slopes down beautifully and users notice nothing.
