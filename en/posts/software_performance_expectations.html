How We Agreed That Software Can Be Slow - Why Faster Hardware Never Feels Faster

Where the extra performance went, what slow actually measures, and the order for winning it back

Aug 23, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: The Moment That Is Just How Long It Takes Became an Answer

You click a button and watch a spinner. Three seconds later the screen changes. Raise it in a meeting and the reply is usually the same one. That is just how long it takes. And nobody finds that answer strange. The discussion ends right there.

That answer is the subject of this article. The discussion ends not because one line of code is bad but because an agreement that this is good enough already exists. Yet nobody signed it. No meeting ever resolved that our response-time target shall be three seconds, and three seconds is nonetheless operating as the target.

If the agreement was not made by a decision, no single decision will undo it. So the order has to change. First look at how the agreement hardened, then pick what can be reversed. Following that order, this article covers where the extra hardware performance went, which four different things hide inside the single word slow, why performance quietly loses priority, and both how to win it back and what you should not try to win back. It does not name any app or service as slow. Naming products without their conditions and measurement method produces impressions rather than information.

1. The Argument That Prompted This - Optimization Got Cheap

In August 2026 an essay by Dan Luu circulated in Korean developer communities. The title is the argument: there is no reason for software to be slow anymore. The grounds are that the price of optimization has fallen. Trying different compiler settings or experimenting with multithreading used to cost an expert days or weeks, and coding agents have cut that cost sharply.

The examples given are a regular expression engine and a board game AI. The author reports roughly 7 percent on held-out queries and up to 2x to 4x on long-running ones. He also states the limits himself. Current models are weak at experimental design, prone to overfitting a benchmark, and short on judgement about what to try on open-ended problems. A human still has to stand up the benchmarking scaffolding.

Where this article sits. The original argues that we can now do it. My interest is the next question. Being able to do something does not make it happen. If the reason optimization kept getting deferred for decades was really that it was hard, the backlog should drain the moment it becomes easy. In most organizations it does not. Cost was only part of the reason.

So the question worth asking is not how do we make it fast but how did slow become acceptable. Technical documentation answers the first. Organization, measurement and incentives answer the second. Leave the second alone and no amount of better tooling changes the outcome.

2. Hardware Got Faster, So Why Does Nothing Feel Faster

The observation is not new. In the February 1995 issue of IEEE Computer, Niklaus Wirth published A Plea for Lean Software, which set down the idea that software is getting slower more rapidly than hardware is getting faster. It is now known as Wirth's law, and Wirth himself credited the observation to Martin Reiser. The same idea circulates under other names as well, including May's law, Gates's law and Page's law. The fact that it has this many names is itself evidence that it is a repeatedly confirmed phenomenon rather than one person's complaint.

But concluding that software got lazy is wrong. The extra performance was not discarded. It was spent. Laying out where it went looks like this.

2.1 What Abstraction Actually Bought

Plenty of writing calls abstraction layers waste, but a fair account also asks what those layers removed. Automatic memory management eliminates whole classes of memory-safety vulnerabilities. Type systems and runtime checks move errors that would fire after release to before it. Package ecosystems replace the areas you will almost certainly get wrong by hand, such as cryptography, time zones and character encoding, with code that has been exercised. Undoing that to save a few milliseconds per request is only rational on the paths where those milliseconds actually matter.

2.2 Round Trips Multiply Rather Than Add

One row in that table behaves differently from the others. Network round trips. The remaining layers are additive costs, while round trips multiply depending on structure. If drawing one screen calls service A, and A calls B, and B calls storage, each segment's latency stacks in sequence. Add retries and timeouts and the bad case becomes several times the ordinary one.

What makes this structure dangerous is that it is invisible in the development environment. On a laptop every service lives on the same machine and round trips are under a millisecond. Users are on another continent, in motion, behind two proxies. The same code behaves like two different products in the two environments.

2.3 Portability and Speed of Delivery Are Trades, Not Waste

Writing it natively is faster. True. But finish the sentence: it is faster if you staff a team per platform, fix each bug that many times, and hold that many meetings for every feature. For organizations that can absorb that cost it is the right call. For organizations that cannot, the product never ships at all.

So the axis of judgement is not that abstraction is bad. It is whether we are actually using what this layer bought. A layer adopted to cover several platforms while you ship to exactly one means you paid and never took delivery. Which brings this section to its conclusion. The real problem is not that layers exist, it is that each layer's adoption was approved on its own while the total after a dozen of them is reviewed by nobody. A decision to spend three more milliseconds per request always passes. The sum of twelve such decisions is on no agenda anywhere.

3. Slow Has to Be Split Into Four Things

Slow is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Four things with different natures hide inside that one word, and optimization that starts before you pick one usually digs in the wrong place.

DimensionWhat it measuresHow users experience itWhere you usually work
ThroughputWork completed per unit of timeUsually not felt, only indirectly under loadParallelism, batching, capacity
LatencyTime for a single request to finishThe waiting itselfRound-trip count, critical path, caching
ResponsivenessTime from input to the screen reactingI pressed it and nothing happenedFreeing the main thread, splitting work
Startup timeTime until it is usable at allWaiting before you get anything. Suppresses return visitsLazy initialization, precomputation, smaller bundles

3.1 Why Raising Throughput Changes Nothing

Throughput is the number server teams most enjoy reporting. Doubling requests per second looks excellent. But if a single request still takes as long as before, nothing in front of the screen changes at all. Throughput is closer to a cost metric: it means the same traffic on half the servers, not that users wait less. The two often move in opposite directions. Batching requests raises overall throughput while making the request that arrived first wait for the ones behind it.

3.2 Responsiveness Is a Different Problem From Latency

People tolerate long latency far better when responsiveness survives. If the button shows a pressed state, scrolling tracks the finger and cancel works, the same amount of waiting becomes a different experience. Conversely, a short delay during which the screen freezes completely reads as a malfunction. So they need separate budgets. A single goal of three seconds overall means nobody ever looks at responsiveness. Write two lines instead: input reacts within 100 milliseconds always, completion within three seconds.

3.3 Startup Time Decides Whether People Come Back

Startup time holds a different rank from the other three, because it is a cost paid while the user has received nothing yet. When it drags, that tool drops out of the category of things you open just to check something quickly. This is the one place where degraded performance cuts usage frequency itself. Startup also degrades in the least visible way. Every added feature grows initialization a little, and nobody files that as a regression.

3.4 Pairs That Get Confused

  • Throughput mistaken for latency. The classic case where server metrics look fine and user complaints do not move
  • Latency mistaken for responsiveness. Shaving total time by blocking the main thread longer, which feels worse
  • The mean mistaken for the experience. A service that is good on average and bad one time in ten is remembered as the product that sometimes freezes
  • Cold start mistaken for warm start. Developers always launch with a warm cache

4. The Gauge for Perceived Delay - The Tail, Not the Mean

4.1 Why Those Three Limits Have Held for Thirty Years

The reason they held is simple: they are grounded in people, not machines. Processors got several times faster and the window in which a person feels something is instant did not move. The practical skill is deciding which metric to place on which line of the gauge. What belongs on the 0.1 second line is usually input feedback, not total completion time. Even when the whole thing takes three seconds, if the reaction is immediate and progress is visible, what the user remembers is not a three-second screen but a screen that is working.

4.2 Why Percentiles Instead of Averages

Modern web metrics use the 75th percentile for a reason. The mean covers a bad experience with good ones. Nine out of ten at 0.2 seconds and one at 8 seconds averages under a second, while what the user remembers is that one eight-second wait, and it is also what makes them hesitate the next time. So a performance report showing only averages should be read as measurement that has not started yet. Line up the 50th, 75th and 95th and the conversation shifts from the average looks fine to why is the 95th so far out.

4.3 At Scale the Tail Takes Over

Jeffrey Dean and Luiz André Barroso addressed this directly in The Tail at Scale, published in Communications of the ACM in 2013. The point is this: systems that respond to user actions within about 100 milliseconds feel fluid, and as scale grows, rare episodes of high latency come to dominate overall performance.

The structure makes it obvious. If one screen is assembled from twenty sub-requests and each request occasionally slows down, the chance that at least one of the twenty is slow rises sharply. The slowest one sets the response time. That is why perfecting each service's average never improves the feel of the screen, and why the remedy comes from design rather than local optimization: send duplicate requests and take whichever returns first, or drop the late fragment and paint the screen with the rest.

5. Why Performance Loses Priority

Everything above answers what is slow. From here the question is why it does not get fixed, and that is the part that actually decides the outcome.

5.1 What Is Not Measured Is Not Managed

Features have tickets. An owner, a deadline, a definition of done. Performance usually has none of the three. It is slow exists as a mood rather than a ticket, and moods do not enter a sprint. Worse, even when the metric exists it sits on a dashboard nobody opens. Error rate pages someone; response time only draws a graph. A metric that never pages is the same as a metric that does not exist.

5.2 Features Sell Better

Release notes say three new features and never say the settings screen is 30 milliseconds faster. Performance work is work with nothing to show. Done well, nothing happens, and getting credit for nothing happening is hard. This is not individual laziness, it is an incentive structure. When evaluation runs on what you produced and what performance work produces is waiting that is no longer there, the rational individual picks the feature.

5.3 Regression Arrives Gradually

Performance decay has no culprit. No commit created three seconds. Each commit added three milliseconds, a thousand times. So there is nothing specific to revert, and without something specific there is no decision to revert. This property makes performance unusually expensive to handle after the fact. A bug has a moment it broke and you can look near it; performance has no such moment. You end up combing six months of history, by which time half the people involved are on other teams.

5.4 Developer Machines Beat User Machines

Developers mostly see the product on the best device, the best network and the warmest cache. Wired office network, local server, every asset already cached in the browser. In that environment the problem does not exist. So the highest-leverage measure is not code, it is picking one baseline device. Keeping a mid-range handset that is a few years old on the desk and opening the release on it catches a large share of regressions before they ship.

6. How the Agreement That Slow Is Fine Hardens

Let the conditions above run for a few years and the next stage arrives. Slow stops being a defect and becomes a specification. The transition is quiet, and once it has happened it is far harder to reverse.

6.1 Expectations Are Learned

Users adapt. Live through three seconds twenty times on the same screen and it becomes normal. And nobody complains about what has become normal. This is dangerous because it is easy to read the disappearance of complaints as the problem being solved. In reality inquiries went down and churn went up, and the first number is on a dashboard while the second is not.

6.2 Rising Specs Hide the Symptom

When devices improve, the same code looks faster. Performance debt becomes the unusual kind of debt whose metrics improve without repayment. The catch is that the improvement reaches only people on new hardware. On older handsets it stays exactly as bad, and those users tend to leave quietly, so they barely register in the data either.

6.3 Without Alternatives, the Signal Arrives Late

Internal tools, mandatory systems in regulated industries, business software with high switching costs: people keep using them while slow because they have no choice. Performance is abandoned first exactly where market signals never arrive. And that is precisely where people spend eight hours a day.

6.4 What Optimize It Later Actually Means

Ship first and optimize later is usually sincere. The trouble is that later has no budget. After launch the next feature is already scheduled, the codebase has grown, and fixing performance now needs agreement across several teams. A decision that would have taken a day before launch becomes a quarter-long project after it.

So this section concludes as follows. The agreement is not made by decisions. It is made by decisions that were never taken. Nobody said let us make it three seconds; it became three seconds because nobody installed anything that would stop three seconds. The way back sits in the same place. Not making the decision again, but installing something you cannot pass without deciding.

7. Winning It Back - Set a Budget and Watch for Regression

The highest-leverage measure is not a code change, it is writing down a number you may not exceed. The performance budget concept documented by the Google Chrome team fills that role. The web.dev budget article splits budgets three ways: quantity-based metrics you can literally count, such as image size, font count and total script weight; milestone timings attached to moments in the user journey, such as First Contentful Paint or Time to Interactive; and rule-based metrics that use an automated tool's score as a floor.

The same document offers as a starting point under 5 seconds to interactive and 170KB or less of critical-path resources (compressed and minified) on a 3G network and a mobile device. That is not a call to adopt those exact values. What matters is the form: a number written down with its baseline environment stated. A performance goal recorded without an environment is not a goal.

7.1 The Order for Setting a Budget

  1. Start from a metric users live through. Not server response time but the moment the screen becomes usable
  2. State the baseline device and network. Without which handset and which connection, the numbers do not compare
  3. Write it as a percentile, one number per screen. The 75th or worse, not the mean. Ten metrics means nobody reads any
  4. Start from today's value. Write an ideal number and you are in violation from day one, and permanent violation kills the alarm
  5. Write down what happens when it is exceeded. Without that it is a wish, not a budget

7.2 Regression Watching Is Entirely About Automation

The budget's counterpart is regression watching. Performance decays gradually, so catching it by eye is impossible in principle. Measure automatically on every merge and block when the budget is exceeded.

  • If it does not block, it is ignored. A check that only warns goes unread within two months
  • Noise kills it. If the measurement environment wobbles and fails at random, the check is the first thing switched off
  • Wide thresholds, tight trends. Keep the blocking line generous and watch a weekly trend separately. One instance of three milliseconds is invisible; a hundred of them show in the trend
  • Compare only under identical conditions. Different device, data or cache state is not a regression, it is a different measurement

A judgement about order. Open a profiler without a budget and there is always more to improve. Nobody knows where done is, so performance work gets classified as the kind of task that never ends and stops being scheduled. The real job a budget does is not starting optimization but creating a place to stop.

8. The Order of Profiling, and When Not to Optimize

The most quoted and most frequently misquoted sentence in this subject comes from Donald Knuth's Structured Programming with go to Statements, published in ACM Computing Surveys in 1974, on page 268.

We should forget about small efficiencies, say about 97% of the time: premature optimization is the root of all evil. Yet we should not pass up our opportunities in that critical 3%.

Usually only the first half gets quoted. Read the second sentence and the meaning turns close to its opposite. Knuth did not say do not optimize. He said leave the 97 percent alone and be sure to catch the critical 3 percent. That is not a prohibition, it is an instruction about order, and honoring the order means finding out where the 3 percent is first. Optimization that starts from this looks slow without measuring is premature optimization.

8.1 The Order of Profiling

  1. Build a scenario that reproduces. Measuring something different each time makes improvement indistinguishable from noise
  2. Start from the user-facing metric. From the moment the screen becomes usable. Function-level comes after
  3. Split the segments. Decide first whether the time went to network, server, transfer, parsing or rendering
  4. Open the profiler on that segment only. Opening everything at once produces a graph nobody can read
  5. Change one thing at a time. Change two together and you will never know which one worked
  6. Measure again and update the baseline. An improvement you did not record will not be evidence at the next regression

8.2 When Not to Do It

  • When you have not measured. That is not optimization, it is guessing
  • When you already meet the budget. Going further here is the same as not doing something else
  • When it is not a hot path. Doubling the speed of code that runs twice a day changes nothing
  • When correctness or safety has to be shaved. Speed bought by removing validation is repaid later as an incident
  • When you must buy complexity you cannot undo. Unreadable code cannot be fixed by the next person
  • When the requirement is about to change. Few things are as reliably wasted as polishing a feature that is going away

8.3 The Exception - Irreversible Decisions Are Made Up Front

There is territory where measure later and fix it does not apply: decisions whose later change cost is effectively unbounded. The shape of the data model, how many round trips one screen needs, whether data lives on the device or the server, whether synchronous or asynchronous is the default. Those are design, not premature optimization. The test is simple: does reversing it take days, or a quarter? If days, later is fine. If a quarter, decide now.

How keeping data next to the user changes the feel is written up in How Some Web Apps Feel Instant - Local-First Sync and In-Browser Databases, and how far the server-side segment can actually be trimmed is covered in the Web Server Performance Tuning Complete Guide.

9. The Other Side, Honestly - What the Performance Bought

If you read this far and land on so older software was better, you missed half the argument. Work that was simply not done back then is now baseline. Remove it and things certainly get faster, and most of it must not be removed.

Runtime checks and automatic memory management eliminate whole classes of memory-safety vulnerabilities. Maintaining a tree for assistive technology and managing focus adds computation, but removing it creates people who cannot use the product at all. Unicode normalization, bidirectional text and locale-aware collation are a burden, yet fast ASCII handling is the wrong answer for most of the world. Transport encryption and per-request authorization are not free, and nobody wants to return to the era without them. Logs and traces attach a cost to every request and in exchange let you find the cause when something breaks. And one more: a slow product that shipped today sometimes beats a fast one that shipped three months later.

So the honest diagnosis reads like this. The problem is not that layers exist, it is paying a price without knowing you are paying it. The items above make clear what they buy. By contrast, a layer adopted for several platforms while you ship to one, a service boundary drawn for reuse that nobody reuses, and tracing switched on for debugging that nobody reads are cases of paying and never taking delivery. What can be won back mostly sits in the second group.

The same story is even clearer at the tooling layer. How small delays attached to everyday work accumulate over a day is worked out in Terminal Performance and Developer Productivity 2026 - The Compounding Latency of Daily Work, and how to split a newly created source of delay such as a model call is covered in Where the Faster Model Wins - Splitting Latency Into Four Parts.

10. What Tips the Balance in the Korean Context

In Korean services the principles above tilt in particular directions. By the Internet Usage Survey run by the Ministry of Science and ICT and the National Information Society Agency, internet usage among people aged 3 and over is 94.5 percent (as of July 2024). Access itself is effectively saturated, which means what separates users is conditions rather than access.

10.1 Mobile Is the Default and Desktop Is the Exception

Open the logs of most Korean consumer services and mobile dominates. Yet the QA baseline and the performance measurement baseline are often still desktop. When the environment you measure and the environment that is used diverge, better metrics do not produce a better experience. Moving the budget's baseline environment to mobile is the first move.

10.2 Device Spread Decides Execution Time

The newest phone and a mid-range handset several years old look at the same screen. Even on the same network, time to paint diverges sharply here, because the connection decides download while the device decides parsing and execution. That is exactly why a total script weight budget matters so much.

10.3 The Problem Is Round Trips, Not Bandwidth

It is tempting to conclude that domestic connection quality means performance needs less attention, but real usage says otherwise. Subway cars, deep inside buildings, in transit, at peak hours. What degrades there is not bandwidth but round-trip latency and reconnection. And round-trip latency is not solved by making files smaller. It is solved by reducing the number of round trips.

10.4 What to Do First in Practice

  • Pick one baseline handset. Keep a mid-range device a few years old on the desk and open every release on it
  • Move the budget's baseline environment to mobile. A number that passed on desktop guarantees nothing
  • Count the round trips. Simply counting the sequential round trips needed to paint the first screen surfaces the fixes
  • Give the first screen its own budget. Cap the initially visible area rather than the whole page

12. Conclusion: Changing the Default

That is just how long it takes sounds like a statement of fact but is actually a quotation of an agreement. And that agreement was not made in a meeting. It was made because each layer was approved separately, because there was no forum that looked at the total, and because no line existed that stopped you when crossed. So reversing it is not a grand decision either. It is changing the default.

  1. Split slow into four. Without deciding whether it is throughput, latency, responsiveness or startup, everything after is guesswork
  2. Measure from the user's side, as a percentile. A server average decides nothing in this conversation
  3. Write one line of budget with its baseline environment stated. One per screen is enough
  4. Attach something that blocks when it is exceeded. A check that only warns is gone in two months
  5. Then open the profiler and separate what you bought from what you did not. Cost paid for safety, accessibility and internationalization is not on the list to reclaim

If only one practical measure survives, let it be this. The next time you hear that is just how long it takes, ask for numbers. How long exactly, on which device, at which percentile. In most teams it emerges that there is no answer to those three questions, and the absence of an answer is itself the start of the conversation. News that the tools got cheap only becomes useful after that.
