Choosing Among Korea-Listed ETFs - Why the Same Index Produces Different Results

Total cost, tracking error, premium and discount, and liquidity, set out from primary sources as of July 2026

July 29, 2026 Finance & Economy

Introduction: not which ETF is best, but why results diverge

Scope note. This article is about exchange-traded funds listed on the Korean market, and every tax statement in it describes the position of a Korean resident. Rules elsewhere differ. Several Korea-listed ETFs may follow the same index. The names differ and the managers differ, but the index they track is identical. Open the account a few years later, however, and the outcomes are not identical. The usual explanation is that the one with the lower headline fee wins, and most comparison tables settle the matter in a single column. This article argues that the explanation is only half right.

Fee gaps are typically a few hundredths of a percentage point per year. Meanwhile how far the fund failed to keep up with its index (tracking error), how far the traded price sat from net asset value at the moment you dealt (the premium or discount), and the bid-ask spread you actually crossed can be several times to several dozen times that gap, depending on circumstances. On top of which, the headline fee is not the whole of what an investor pays. Other expenses and brokerage commissions sit above it.

So the question here is not which product is good. No product name appears anywhere in this article. Instead it breaks why results diverge for the same index into separate components, then asks which component dominates under which conditions. The conclusion, stated up front: the right answer differs between small and short and large and long. Fees accumulate in proportion to time, while spreads and deviations accumulate in proportion to the number of trades.

What an ETF is and how the categories are drawn was covered in the ETF investing primer, so it is not repeated here. The contrast between overseas-listed and Korea-listed routes was set out separately in the Nasdaq-100 investing article. This piece deals only with the procedure for choosing among ETFs listed on the Korean market. Where something was verified, the source is named, and figures that could not be verified were left out.

1. Total cost is not the headline fee - three layers stacked

The first thing this article has to settle is the definition of cost. The number printed large on a product page is usually the total management fee. What an investor actually bears is wider than that.

As explained by the Korea Financial Investment Education Institute, the total management fee is the sum of the fees paid to (1) the asset manager, (2) the distributor, (3) the trustee and (4) the general administration company. In other words, that headline number is already a bundle of four items. And yet running a fund costs more than fees.

  • Other expenses - audit fees, index licensing, overseas custody, settlement-related charges and similar items that are actually spent in the course of running the fund. Unlike a fee schedule set in advance, these are tallied after the fact, so they move.
  • Brokerage commissions - to track an index faithfully the fund has to buy and sell constituents whenever the index changes. This is the resulting trading cost. It grows when the index turns over often or when the underlying assets are expensive to trade.
  • The investor own trading cost - the three items above come out of the fund, and on top of them sit the commission paid to a brokerage and tax. These are not created by the product but by the person dealing in it.

There is a trap in the terminology. The abbreviation TER is used with different scopes in different documents. The Korea Financial Investment Education Institute uses it to mean the sum of the four fee items. Disclosure screens and market practice, by contrast, use the label total expense ratio to mean the headline fee plus other expenses, and show the brokerage commission rate as a separate line beside it. The safe habit is therefore to ignore the abbreviation and read the line names directly.

For context, distribution-stage charges on funds carry statutory ceilings. Under the Ministry of Government Legislation plain-language service, a sales fee may not exceed 2 percent of the subscription or redemption amount, and a sales commission may not exceed 1 percent of the annual average value of the collective investment property. Listed ETFs are bought on the exchange rather than through the ordinary public fund channel, but the fact that fees and charges are a regulated matter is worth remembering.

One common misconception deserves correcting. Competing to cut the headline fee is not the same as cutting the real burden. A very low headline fee can be overtaken once large other expenses and brokerage commissions are added, and a slightly higher headline fee can end up with a lower total because its other expenses are small. Comparison therefore has to be done by checking the three lines separately and adding them.

2. Tracking error and premium or discount - two similar names, two different things

After cost, the pair most often confused is tracking error and the premium or discount to net asset value. Both sound like they answer the question of whether the fund follows the index, but the things being compared are different.

Start with net asset value. As the Korea Financial Investment Education Institute explains it, net asset value is total net assets after deducting liabilities, divided by the number of shares in issue, and it is calculated once a day. Because it does not refresh in real time during the session, an indicative net asset value is published intraday, precisely so that investors do not buy above or sell below the real worth.

  • Tracking error - expresses how widely the movement of the fund net asset value is dispersed against the underlying index. A smaller value is read as following the index well. It is a metric about the quality of the management.
  • Premium or discount - expresses the gap between the price actually traded on the market and the indicative net asset value, as a percentage. The formula given by the same institute is (market price minus net asset value) divided by net asset value, times 100. It is a metric about the moment of the trade.
  • What widens tracking error - fees and other expenses, the cost of rotating constituents, cash holdings, the timing of dividend handling, hedging costs and the limits of the replication method all contribute. Note that cost is itself a component of tracking error.
  • What widens the premium or discount - concentration of supply and demand is the classic driver. When buy orders crowd into one window, the book is pushed up and the price rises above net asset value. Deviations also open up easily when the underlying sits overseas and cannot be marked live during the Korean session.
  • Disclosure thresholds - according to the same institute, the Korea Exchange requires mandatory disclosure once the deviation exceeds a set level, and that level is 1 percent for domestic-investment ETFs and 2 percent for overseas-investment ETFs.
  • How the two relate - low tracking error does not save you from buying expensively at a moment when the deviation has opened. A price sitting on the indicative value does not save you from drifting away from the index over a long holding period. Neither substitutes for the other.

For the sake of market transparency the Korea Exchange has the creation basket, net asset value, index constituent details, the tracking error between index and net asset value, and semi-annual management reports published. In other words tracking error is something to look up in a disclosure, not to estimate by feel.

3. Liquidity and liquidity providers - when you order changes the price

The advice that a product with large turnover is preferable is heard often. But understanding why, as a matter of market rules, changes the judgement. Korea-listed ETFs operate with a liquidity provider system.

The Korea Exchange guidance on the system runs as follows. A liquidity provider must be a designated participating company licensed for investment dealing and brokerage of securities that has signed a liquidity provision contract with the asset manager and is a settlement member of the exchange. It must post two-sided quotes within five minutes whenever the market spread ratio exceeds the declared spread ratio.

  • The obligation window - per the exchange guidance, it runs from five minutes after the opening single-price auction ends until the closing single-price auction begins. In clock terms it is shown as 09:05 to 15:20.
  • When the obligation is waived - when the market spread ratio is within the declared spread ratio, during single-price auction sessions, when five minutes have not yet passed since such an auction ended, and when the exchange judges intervention necessary because of abrupt shifts in quoting or trading conditions.
  • The declared spread ratio - per the same guidance, it is a ratio filed with the exchange by the asset manager within 2 percent for domestic underlyings and 3 percent for overseas underlyings, and is described as typically around 1 percent in practice.
  • The deviation obligation - the same guidance states that keeping the closing deviation within 3 percent for domestic underlyings and 6 percent for overseas underlyings is a legal obligation.
  • Minimum quote size - according to the Korea Financial Investment Education Institute, a liquidity provider posts two-sided quotes of at least 100 shares so that investors can deal near a sensible price.
  • What this means in practice - outside the obligation window the book can thin out. A large market order placed then can be filled well away from net asset value.

One caution belongs here. The exact clock times attached to single-price auction sessions are written differently across documents and have been revised before. The guidance above shows the opening auction session as running from 08:00 to 09:00, but that window should be re-confirmed with the exchange and your brokerage before you place a real order. What is certain is that there are windows around the open and the close where the liquidity provider obligation does not apply. Korean trading hours and the auction mechanism generally were covered in the Korean stock market rules guide.

This is also where turnover matters. The presence of a liquidity provider means a minimum quote is maintained, not that any size fills at any price. In genuinely busy products, ordinary participants stack orders beyond the provider quotes, so spreads are narrow and fills are stable. Where trading is thin, a book sitting out near the declared spread ratio can be the normal state of affairs.

4. Distributions - take the income, or leave it to compound

Even for the same index, when income is handed to the investor changes the outcome. Per Korea Exchange guidance, ETFs pay distributions monthly or quarterly, and the purpose of distributing includes reducing tracking error.

The essential thing to know is the category that defers distribution and reinvests instead, often called a reinvesting or total-return structure. What matters is that this structure is not available to every product.

  • The rule - Article 26-2 of the Enforcement Decree of the Income Tax Act, as published by the Korea Law Information Center, includes among the requirements for a collective investment vehicle that it shall settle and distribute at least once a year from its establishment date. Annual settlement and distribution is therefore the baseline.
  • The exception - the same article contains a provision covering a listed index collective investment vehicle whose purpose is to track, as such, an index based solely on the prices of shares traded on the securities market. The path to deferring distribution, where dividend income is reinvested in line with constituent weights, opens there.
  • What that means in practice - a reinvesting structure is not an option available across all categories. Whether a given product reinvests, and whether reinvestment is permitted for its category, has to be confirmed in the prospectus and the manager notices. The article has been amended before, so read the version in force when you invest.
  • The advantage of reinvesting - no withholding occurs at a distribution date, so the tax payment point moves later. Meanwhile the reinvested amount keeps working, so the compounding window lengthens.
  • The limit of reinvesting - the tax does not vanish, it relocates. The amount subject to tax on disposal grows accordingly. And it does not suit anyone who needs cash flow.
  • The advantage of distributing - regular cash arrives, and taxation is spread across several years. For someone managing annual financial income so it does not pile into one year, this can be preferable.
  • The limit of distributing - each distribution is taxed as dividend income, and putting it back to work means paying trading costs again. Distributions also accumulate directly into the annual financial income total.

Rates come later, but one point belongs here. Under Article 129 of the Income Tax Act as cited by the Ministry of Government Legislation plain-language service, dividend income tax is computed by applying a rate of 14 percent to the dividend income amount, with local income tax added separately. The commonly quoted 15.4 percent comes out of that structure.

5. Replication method - physical, synthetic, and the counterparty

Tracking the same index does not mean tracking it the same way. There are broadly two routes.

First, physical replication. The fund actually buys and holds the assets that make up the index, either in full or in a sample sufficient to follow it. Second, synthetic replication. Material published by the Financial Services Commission on 26 February 2013 defines a synthetic ETF as an ETF that replicates and tracks an index by using over-the-counter swap transactions and similar means, and distinguishes it from the traditional ETF that holds shares, bonds and the like.

  • Why synthetic exists - some underlyings are difficult or expensive to hold physically. The same material explains that the method allows a wider range of products based on overseas and commodity indices.
  • Counterparty risk - a synthetic fund receives the index return under a contract with a counterparty. If that counterparty fails to perform, the outcome is affected. This is a category of risk that physical funds do not carry.
  • The regulatory apparatus - the same material states that managers must build an entry, operation, exit and disclosure regulatory framework for managing counterparty risk, and that the counterparty credit rating is restricted to a level of AA− or above. A collateral management framework and risk disclosure obligations were presented alongside.
  • When the name reveals it - some products carry a suffix in the name indicating the synthetic structure. Even so, do not infer the structure from the naming alone; confirm the replication method in the prospectus.
  • Currency hedging - a suffix marking a hedged share class is widely used. The design aims to reduce the effect of currency moves, and hedging costs money, which shows up in tracking error. It does not follow that a hedged version is always the safer choice.
  • Order of judgement - the replication method is something to check before the headline fee, because a lower fee does not help if the kind of risk is different.

One addition: the quality of the index itself is part of the product choice. Per Korea Exchange guidance, an index underlying a securities-based ETF must comprise at least 10 constituents, no single name may exceed 30 percent of the index, and the constituents making up 85 percent of market value must each meet requirements of at least 15 billion won in market capitalisation and at least 100 million won in average daily turnover over the preceding three months. Since similar names can conceal different indices, matching the formal index name is the starting point of any comparison.

6. Tax category - domestic equity, or everything else

This is the item that most decides the after-tax outcome, and again it describes Korean resident taxation. Under the ETF tax guidance published by the Korea Exchange, Korea-listed ETFs fall into two groups.

  • Domestic equity ETFs - those tracking a Korean stock index directly. Capital gains are not taxed, and only distributions bear dividend income tax of 14 percent plus local income tax, giving 15.4 percent.
  • All other ETFs - those tracking overseas indices, commodities and similar assets. They are subject to holding-period taxation, and withholding applies to the smaller of the realised capital gain and the increase in the taxable base price.
  • Securities transaction tax - because an ETF is classified as a fund rather than a share, securities transaction tax is not levied. This differs from selling an individual stock.
  • The derivative categories - the same guidance notes that for leveraged and inverse products the taxable base increase barely arises, so actual taxation is extremely rare. That means little tax, not little risk.
  • Aggregate financial income taxation - per National Tax Service guidance, where an individual interest and dividend income for the year exceeds 20 million won, aggregate taxation applies. At or below that level withholding by the financial institution settles the liability, and a spouse financial income is not added to your own.
  • What the taxable base increase means - it is a separately computed reference price used for tax purposes. It can differ from the return you saw on screen, and tax attaches to whichever of the two is smaller. That is why the headline return and the taxable amount can fail to line up.

7. The account changes the answer - general, ISA and pension

Deciding which account will hold the position before choosing a product narrows the field, because what can be held and how it is taxed vary by account.

  • General brokerage account - any listed ETF can be held. Taxation follows the table above, and there is no tax relief. Freedom of choice is greatest here.
  • ISA - the defining feature is that gains and losses inside the account are netted before tax is applied. Where several positions are run together and results are mixed, this can be structurally favourable. Limits and eligibility conditions are affected by amendments, so confirm them when opening the account.
  • Pension savings accounts and IRP - only products listed on the Korean securities market can be held, and gains arising inside the account are deferred until withdrawal. Because no withholding occurs on each disposal, the longer the compounding window, the larger the benefit.
  • Eligibility limits inside pension accounts - the range of products that may be held is restricted, and higher-risk categories such as leveraged and inverse products are generally excluded. However, the precise exclusions and permitted range differ by account type and by institution, so this must be confirmed individually. No specific figure is asserted here.
  • The IRP risk-asset cap - only a set proportion of the balance may sit in risk assets, so equity-type products cannot fill the whole account. The precise cap and how it applies should be confirmed against supervisory rules and institution guidance.
  • What deferral really does - when tax is deferred, the money that would have gone to tax keeps working. Over long horizons that means the account choice moves the result more than a few hundredths of a percentage point in fees.

Contribution limits and the tax treatment on withdrawal were set out in the pension savings and IRP guide. The point to take from here is simple: fixing the account first automatically narrows the product shortlist. Choosing the product first and then hunting for an account leads to positions that cannot be held.

8. Which factor dominates, and when

Now to the central question. Which is larger, the fee gap or the combination of tracking error, deviation and trading cost? The answer is that it depends on the conditions. A comparison that does not state its conditions is not saying anything.

The principle is simple. Fees and other expenses accumulate in proportion to time. They are deducted from net assets every single day, so the longer you hold, the more they compound. Spreads and deviations accumulate in proportion to the number of trades. They only arise when you deal, so buying once and holding means paying once. Tracking error sits between the two: it is partly the result of cost and partly the result of management.

Every number above is a simulation using hypothetical figures and does not describe any actual product. Compounding and tax were assumed to be zero to keep the arithmetic simple. Even so, the point at which the ranking flips comes through clearly.

  • Short holdings with frequent dealing - comparing headline fees is close to pointless. Spreads and deviations dominate. In this range, choosing a product with a deep book and ordering inside the liquidity provider window are far more practical remedies.
  • Long holdings with large amounts - the spread is paid once and done, while fees and other expenses repeat every year. Here the sum of the real cost lines and the stability of tracking error are decisive.
  • Tracking error is worst over long horizons - a 0.3 percentage point annual tracking gap is six times a 0.05 percentage point fee gap. And yet tracking error is not printed large on product pages, so it drops out of comparisons easily.
  • A different tax category makes the whole discussion moot - whether capital gains are brought into charge is a difference measured in percent, not in hundredths of a percent. Where categories differ, align the category before comparing costs.
  • Very small funds carry a separate risk - a small asset base tends to inflate the ratio of other expenses, and the possibility of a delisting process cannot be excluded. Specific delisting criteria are affected by rule amendments, so confirm them in the exchange rules directly.
  • What never changes - the volatility of the underlying itself. However finely costs are optimised, the possibility of losing principal remains. In terms of sequence, this item belongs first.

11. Frequently asked questions

Q. Is it enough to pick the ETF with the lowest headline fee?

The headline fee is part of the burden. It is the sum of the fees paid to the asset manager, distributor, trustee and administrator, with other expenses and brokerage commissions stacked on top. The disclosed total expense ratio covers the first two, and the brokerage commission rate is a separate line. Check the three lines separately, add them, then compare.

Q. How is tracking error different from the premium or discount?

They compare different things. Tracking error is the gap between the index and net asset value; the premium or discount is the gap between the market price and the indicative net asset value. The first is a management matter, the second a timing matter. Small tracking error does not save you from buying at a wide deviation, and a price on the indicative value does not save you from drifting over a long hold.

Q. Is it safe to trade right at the open or just before the close?

Those are windows in which the liquidity provider obligation does not apply. Per exchange guidance the obligation runs from 09:05 to 15:20, with single-price auction sessions and the five minutes after such a session exempt. When no obligation applies, the book can thin and the price can move well away from net asset value. The clock times have been revised before, so confirm them before dealing.

Q. Is a category that reinvests instead of distributing better?

For many categories the choice does not exist. Article 26-2 of the Enforcement Decree of the Income Tax Act requires settlement and distribution at least once a year from the establishment date, with a provision covering listed index vehicles tracking an index based solely on the prices of shares traded on the securities market. Reinvesting moves the tax point later, but the tax does not disappear.

Q. Are capital gains on Korea-listed ETFs untaxed?

It depends on the category. A domestic equity ETF has untaxed capital gains, with dividend income tax on distributions only, whereas ETFs tracking overseas indices, commodities and similar assets fall under holding-period taxation and are withheld on the smaller of the gain and the taxable base increase. Where annual financial income exceeds 20 million won, aggregate taxation applies.

Conclusion - the answer comes from line items and conditions, not product names

Three sentences compress this article. First, the headline fee is only part of the burden; the real figure appears once other expenses and brokerage commissions are added. And because the abbreviation TER covers different scopes in different documents, read the line names rather than the acronym. Second, tracking error and the premium or discount sound alike but are separate metrics, and neither substitutes for the other. Tracking error works a little at a time throughout the holding; deviation and spread work at the instant you deal.

Third, and therefore, the right answer changes with the conditions. Fees and other expenses scale with time; spreads and deviations scale with the number of trades. For someone running a small amount briefly and dealing often, the spread governs; for someone parking a large amount for years, real cost and tracking error govern. A comparison table that hands both people the same answer is wrong for one of them.

There is one more layer that can render the whole cost discussion irrelevant: the tax category. Domestic equity ETFs have untaxed capital gains while other categories fall under holding-period taxation, and that difference is measured in percent, not hundredths of a percent. Then there is the account. Deferral inside a pension account means the money that would have gone to tax keeps working, which over long horizons can outweigh any fee difference. The practical sequence is purpose, then account, then tax category, then index, then costs and metrics, with fee comparison as the final fine-tuning.

A closing word on the nature of this field. Fees, expenses, rules and tax law keep changing. So rather than numbers to memorise, this article leaves behind the routes for checking: costs at the Korea Financial Investment Association fund disclosure service, tracking and deviation disclosures at the Korea Exchange, the tax category in the exchange tax guidance and with the National Tax Service, and the distribution requirement in the decree at the Korea Law Information Center. The habit of checking outlasts the habit of remembering. If the structural difference between index funds and ETFs is the open question, the index fund versus ETF comparison covers it.
