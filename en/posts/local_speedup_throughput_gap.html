What Actually Speeds Up When You Speed Up a Non-Bottleneck - Where Local Speed and Total Throughput Diverge

The slowest station sets the total time. When an upstream step gets faster, what shrinks is not lead time but the amount of work waiting in line

Aug 31, 2026 Trends & Culture

The time it takes to build something has halved, and the release cadence has not moved. Tickets close faster, and the moment a change reaches users looks about the same as before. Most people who hit this gap suspect the tools or suspect each other. It is neither. The structure the work flows through was always shaped this way.

This piece does the arithmetic on how much the whole shortens when one step gets faster. The conclusion up front: the total time is set not by the step that got faster but by the slowest station, and speeding up only the front end shrinks not the lead time but the pile that accumulates in front of the constraint. Whether more people or fewer are needed, and what any job title's future looks like, are not questions this piece answers. It answers one question about flow.

This is not an assessment of any particular organization or occupation. Every study cited comes with its publisher, year and sample, and what could not be confirmed is collected in one section near the end. The lead-time budget in the tables is constructed to show the arithmetic and is not measured data from any organization.

1. The trigger - a multiplier that left a question

In late August 2026 a public three-part playbook circulated in developer communities. Its headline sentence read Why 10× faster coding doesn't translate into proportional organizational productivity, and its final part argues that requirements, review and approval become the new bottleneck. The claim compresses to one line. What deserves automating is not the act of writing code but the time from request to deployment.

The multiplier in that headline, though, cannot be carried over as is. What it is measured against does not appear on the public landing page. There is no statement of what kind of work formed the sample, what the baseline was, or whether the thing measured was typing speed or time to finish one item. Summaries putting the organizational gain in a specific percentage range also circulate, but their origin and sample could not be confirmed. So neither number is used here.

The argument does not need the size of the multiplier anyway. If anything, the larger the multiplier, the stronger the argument. Whether a segment gets twice as fast or a hundred times as fast, and even in the extreme where the segment drops to zero seconds, there is a ceiling on how much the whole can shrink. Section 3 works out where that ceiling sits, and everything after it describes what happens underneath.

2. The slowest station sets the total time

Work that passes through several stages in sequence shares one property. The throughput of the whole flow cannot exceed the throughput of its slowest stage. This is closer to a definition than a discovery. Put a stage handling twenty items a day behind one handling a hundred, and the flow produces twenty. Raise the front stage to two hundred and it still produces twenty. What changes is not the output but the amount piling up between the two.

The theory of constraints, popularized by Eliyahu M. Goldratt in The Goal in 1984, is about this property. Three points carry over.

  • Every flow has one constraint. If two appear, usually one of them has not been measured yet
  • Improving anywhere but the constraint does not raise the output of the flow. Only that station's numbers look better
  • Relieve the constraint and the next slowest station becomes the new one. This is not a one-time job but a continuing measurement

The third gets dropped most often. When results fall short after a bottleneck is relieved, people conclude the fix was wrong, when in fact the bottleneck moved. In software this is hard to see for a simple reason. In a factory anyone can see boxes piling up, while changes waiting for review sit inside a tool and almost nobody counts that line daily. Invisible inventory does not get managed.

The ratio need not be exactly this, but the shape - hands-on time in the minority, waiting in the majority - shows up in most work that carries approvals.

3. What share the building time held - the arithmetic ceiling

Find the part a tool shortens. Of the eight hours in design and implementation, six are hands-on, and if half of that is actual typing, that is three hours, 3.6 percent of 83.5. The other three hours go to deciding what to build, reading existing code, retracing and revising the plan.

Attach the calculation Gene Amdahl set out in a 1967 conference paper. If a segment holding fraction p of the total gets s times faster, the overall reduction is p × (1 − 1/s), and no matter how large s grows it never exceeds p. If typing is 3.6 percent, even reducing it to zero shortens the lead time by 3.6 percent.

The objection that a tool shortens more than typing is fair. Time spent reading and retracing, time spent drafting, all of that shrinks too. Then it is fairer to take the whole design and implementation stage, which is 10 percent of the lead time, so erasing all eight hours puts the ceiling at 10 percent. Be generous and halve the hands-on time in requirements and testing as well, and 83.5 hours becomes 75. Erase all 13.5 hours of hands-on work and the ceiling is 16 percent.

How many times faster a segment got means nothing without knowing what share of the total it held. A multiplier states a numerator and says nothing about the denominator. And in the table above, most of the denominator was waiting, not hands-on time.

Even this calculation is optimistic. It assumed the waiting time in each stage stays the same, and when the front end speeds up, downstream waiting grows. Why, is the next section. The version of this arithmetic extended toward tool count and coordination cost lives in The Productivity Mirage.

4. What a faster front end produces - work in progress

John D. C. Little published a proof in Operations Research in 1961 for a relation stating that, in a stable queueing system, average work in progress equals average arrival rate times average time in system. In symbols, L = λW. The assumptions are loose enough that it holds regardless of arrival distribution or service discipline.

Turn it around and you get W = L / λ. Throughput λ is set by the slowest station, so when the front end speeds up and arrivals rise, throughput stays put. What grows is L, the amount standing in line, and as L grows so does W. In words, speeding up a non-bottleneck front end leaves total throughput unchanged and makes each item's lead time longer. That is arithmetic, not a side effect.

If the review queue usually held four items and now holds twelve, a newly submitted change waits three times as long for its turn. The person who made it feels that the work took thirty minutes, and the change reaches users later than it used to. Both statements are true at once. This is exactly where individual perception and flow output separate.

A longer line carries costs of its own, often more expensive than the waiting itself.

  • Items collide - the longer a change waits, the likelier it conflicts with what changed meanwhile. Merging costs more time, and that time lengthens the line again
  • The maker's memory cools - feedback three days later means rebuilding the context from scratch
  • Queue jumping appears - every urgent item pulled forward lengthens the rest of the line. Reprioritizing becomes work in itself
  • Status checking becomes a job - the time spent answering where is that now shows up in no metric
  • Changing direction gets expensive - the more built and unshipped, the more gets thrown away when plans change. Work in progress becomes sunk cost

In a factory this inventory is visible and therefore managed. In software it is not. Dashboards usually show items completed, not items waiting.

5. Why reading speed stays where it was

Downstream fails to keep up because downstream work has a different nature. Building can be split into parallel branches. Reading and judging cannot. One person reads one thing at a time.

  • Judgment requires context - whether a change is right is settled by its relationship to its surroundings, and loading those surroundings does not scale with the size of the change
  • Approval carries a name - work that carries a name is structurally hard to do fast. That is a matter of where accountability sits, not of caution
  • Verification cost does not track production cost - a five-line change can consume five hours, and five hundred lines can be done in ten minutes
  • Reviewers usually have other work - review is fitted in rather than done full time, and fitted-in work arrives irregularly, which enlarges waiting

One caution. Reading this as the reviewers are slow breaks the diagnosis entirely. The line grew not because reading slowed but because the arrival rate rose. Read at the same speed with more coming in and the line necessarily lengthens.

There is also something people do when the line grows. A preprint posted to arXiv in May 2026 (Bartolucci and Vivo, Queue & AI: When Faster Tasks Slow Down the Workflow) sorts it into two effects. One is a variance wedge, the divergence in which mean completion time falls while errors that slipped past review return as costly rework and workflow-level performance degrades. The other is adverse selection under congestion: as the line grows, reviewers relax their acceptance threshold, so scrutiny drops exactly when it is most needed. That it has not been peer reviewed is worth keeping in mind.

6. What happens past ninety percent utilization

Waiting time does not grow in proportion to utilization. Past a point it jumps. Without knowing that shape, removing slack at the constraint looks like an improvement.

An approximation J. F. C. Kingman produced in 1961 explains it. Written as a product of variability, utilization and service time, its utilization term takes the form ρ / (1 − ρ), where ρ is arrival rate divided by service rate, that is, how full the station is.

90%9.009.0×
95%19.0019.0×
98%49.0049.0×
  • This table is a shape, not a prediction. Actual waiting multiplies this by the variability term and the service time
  • Going from 50 to 70 percent and going from 90 to 95 are entirely different acts. The first makes waiting 2.3 times, the second moves it from 9 to 19
  • So a small amount of extra work sent to a station with no slack raises waiting a great deal. That is what happens at the constraint when the front end speeds up
  • Conversely, pinning a constraint's utilization to 100 percent is not efficiency but queue production. A station with no idle time is not a well-running station, it is one where the line is growing

The other terms are worth noting. The variability term measures how irregular arrivals and service are. At the same utilization, lumpy and irregular arrivals make waiting longer. A flow that sees zero items and then suddenly twenty waits far longer than one receiving a steady four a day at the same utilization. That term connects to the next section.

7. Batch size - why sending in lumps takes longer

A batch is the unit sent at once. Twenty changes reviewed and released as one lump, versus one at a time twenty times. The total is identical and the result is not.

  • The first item waits - while twenty accumulate, item one simply waits for the rest to be produced. That waiting shows up in no stage's work time
  • Review cost grows faster than the count - untangling what touches what inside a lump of twenty costs more than twenty single reviews summed
  • Rollback becomes all-or-nothing - one bad item cannot easily be peeled out, so people hesitate, and the problem stays while they hesitate
  • Cause isolation gets harder - twenty candidates when something breaks. Finding it takes longer, and the flow stops meanwhile
  • Arrivals get bursty - this inflates the variability term from the previous section, which is why waiting grows at the same utilization

Small and frequent wins on arithmetic, not diligence. Halve the batch and the accumulation wait halves, and the four items above shrink with it.

There is a condition. If the fixed cost of each send - build, approval process, announcement - is high, small and frequent costs more. Ten sends means paying that cost ten times. So reduce the fixed cost of a single send before reducing batch size. Doing it in the reverse order fails, and when it fails people conclude that small batches are bad.

A faster front end also pushes batches larger. More produced in the same time means more naturally packed into each review request. Nobody decided it, and it happens. The 2025 report from Google Cloud's DORA puts the point this way: AI accelerates software development, but that acceleration can expose weaknesses downstream. Acceleration does not create the weakness, it exposes one that was already there.

8. Where measurement goes wrong - upstream metrics

Whatever is easy to count becomes the metric. Things produced come in clean counts, while things waiting have to be defined before they can be counted. So metrics drift upstream.

What is countedWhat it tells youWhat it misses
Lines of code writtenUpstream production volumeHow many lines shipped, and how many were reverted
Tickets closedThroughput of one stageHow long that ticket waited in the next line
Change requests openedActivity levelHow far the pending pile has grown
Tool usage rateWhether it was adoptedWhich part of the flow adoption changed
Perceived individual speedThat person's experienceThe relationship between that experience and lead time

What should be counted instead is what made it all the way through. The software delivery metrics DORA maintains have that property. Change lead time is defined as the amount of time it takes for a change to go from committed to version control to deployed in production, and it is used alongside deployment frequency, failed deployment recovery time, change fail rate and deployment rework rate. The first three sit on the throughput side, the last two on the instability side.

  • The endpoint is production - not whether an intermediate stage was cleared but whether it reached users. That is where these part ways with upstream metrics
  • Speed and stability are read together - look at one alone and it can be improved by selling the other. Waving reviews through cuts lead time and raises failure rate
  • They attach to the flow, not to people - they do not count how many each person did. Start counting that and section 11 happens

There is no need to memorize a list. One principle is enough. Upstream metrics speak only about upstream. An upstream metric improving after an upstream speedup proves nothing.

9. What speed may have increased

So far the story has been that upstream speed carries poorly into the whole. Being honest means looking at the other side. Speed may have increased some things rather than reduced them.

  • Rework - items that come back after passing. The same item queues twice
  • Rollbacks - cancelling after release. The rollback itself creates a new line
  • Incident response - cleaning up after something ships. This stops planned lines outright
  • Status checking - a longer line means more queries and reports. Captured by no metric
  • Discards - built but never used. The share rises with the amount of work in progress

On this question there is material whose publisher, year and sample can all be confirmed. Two annual reports from Google Cloud's DORA, and the fact that their results differ is the important part. The 2024 report found that adoption clearly raised individual productivity, flow and job satisfaction while relating negatively to software delivery stability and throughput. The 2025 report - fielded from June 13 to July 21, 2025, with responses from nearly 5,000 technology professionals worldwide plus more than 100 hours of qualitative data - says something different: Unlike last year, we observe a positive relationship between AI adoption on both software delivery throughput and product performance. The same report adds that AI adoption does continue to have a negative relationship with software delivery stability.

The throughput side reversed direction in a year and the stability side did not. The difference between the two years needs careful reading. It could be better tooling, or a different mix of adopting organizations, and since these are survey correlations they cannot be read as causation.

One thing is clear regardless. That throughput sometimes rises means the path from upstream speed to whole-flow output is not closed. There are conditions, and the same report states them: Teams working in loosely coupled architectures with fast feedback loops see gains, while those constrained by tightly coupled systems and slow processes see little or no benefit. So the argument here is not speed is pointless. It is that for speed to pay, downstream has to be open.

10. How to measure where the waiting happens

From here it is a question of what to observe. No tool names required. Most collaboration tools already record state-change timestamps, and where they do not, one table will do.

  1. Draw the flow as observed - not the documented process but the path taken by ten items that actually reached production last month. Include the arrows that loop backward. Those are usually the most expensive segment
  2. Record two timestamps per stage - entry and exit. Two is enough, and asking for three breaks the record-keeping
  3. Separate waiting from hands-on time - stage total minus hands-on time is waiting. Putting those two numbers side by side is the point of this whole piece
  4. Count the line once a day - how many items sit in front of each stage. A single reading is not enough, you need the trend
  5. Find where the line does not shrink - that is the bottleneck. Not the longest stage but the one where the line keeps growing
  6. Estimate that station's utilization - arrivals against capacity. Past ninety percent you are on the curve from section 6, and there a small increase comes back large

A few things are easy to confuse while measuring.

  • The longest stage is not the bottleneck - a stage whose work is simply long may accumulate no line. Service time and waiting time are different stories
  • The busiest person is not the bottleneck - busyness signals utilization, not queue. If a station looks unhurried while its line grows, that is the bottleneck
  • Average lead time hides the distribution - if half ship in a day and the rest take three weeks, the average explains nothing. Read the median with the upper range
  • Less than a month of observation wobbles - release cadence, holidays and quarter-end come through untouched. Include at least one full cycle

Settle one thing before measuring. Write down what will count as improvement first. Decide after measuring and the flattering number gets picked.

11. Look at the flow, not at people

The most common way bottleneck hunting goes wrong is that the question changes. Where does it stop becomes who is slow. They sound alike and produce opposite results.

  • The queue hides - people stop leaving pending items in a pending state and hold them instead. An invisible line cannot be measured. Measurement destroying measurement
  • Local optimization starts - everyone improving their own stage's number makes the whole worse. Waving reviews through cuts review time and raises rework
  • A bottleneck is a property of a station, not of a person - it is set by the relation between arrival and service rates. However high the service rate, more arrivals make it a bottleneck
  • Concentrate work on a capable person and that station becomes the bottleneck - the cause is concentration, not incompetence. Change the person and if the concentration stays, so does the bottleneck

Borrowing DORA's 2025 phrasing, AI's primary role is as an amplifier, magnifying an organization's existing strengths and weaknesses. An amplifier does not create direction. It enlarges the shape already there. Where the flow is open, upstream speed becomes output. Where it is blocked, the same speed becomes queue. That is why the same tool produces opposite results in two organizations.

The frame has one more practical advantage. Fixing a flow can be agreed on. There are no sides in the fact that review waiting is twenty hours. A conversation about who is slow creates sides, and once sides exist the data starts bending.

What could not be confirmed

  • What the multiplier in the trigger's headline is measured against - the sample, baseline and measured quantity could not be confirmed on the public landing page, so it is not used as evidence here. Summaries putting the organizational gain in a specific range also exist, but their origin could not be traced and they were left out
  • The often-quoted coefficients from the 2024 DORA report tying adoption increments to throughput and stability changes - specific figures circulate in secondary summaries, but the interval and sample behind them could not be confirmed on the public summary page, so the 2024 result is stated by direction only
  • The lead-time budget table in section 2 is constructed to show the arithmetic. It is not measured data from any organization, and there is no reason the proportions must be these
  • The utilization table in section 6 calculates only the utilization term of an approximation. Actual waiting also multiplies variability and service time, so waiting cannot be estimated from that table alone
  • The arXiv preprint cited in section 5 has not been peer reviewed, and the threshold conditions it presents are results inside a model
  • Lead time and waiting time aggregates sampled from organizations in any one country - no material with publisher, year and sample all confirmable was found, so every figure here is either constructed or derived from a formula

Summary

Speed up one step and that step gets faster. The whole gets faster only when that step is the bottleneck. Otherwise total throughput holds and the pile behind it grows instead. As the pile grows, each item waits longer for its turn, and the result is a state where individuals feel faster while lead time gets longer. The two sensations are not a contradiction but two faces of the same arithmetic. Since hands-on time is only a fraction of lead time in multi-stage work and the rest is waiting, erasing all of the hands-on time still leaves a ceiling on how much the whole can shrink. A multiplier means something only alongside the share its segment held.

The work sits before adding tools. Draw the flow as observed, record entry and exit timestamps per stage, separate waiting from hands-on time, and count the line in front of each stage daily. The station where the line keeps growing is the bottleneck, and if its utilization is past ninety percent a small increase comes back large. Smaller batches cut waiting, but the fixed cost of a single send has to come down first.

What remains is the frame. A bottleneck is a property of a station, not of a person. The moment who is slow is asked, the queue hides and measurement dies. The question worth asking is where does it stop, and that question has no sides. A question without sides gets to a more accurate answer.
