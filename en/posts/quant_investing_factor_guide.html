From the Sources of Systematic Excess Return to Smart-Beta ETFs and the Real Limits

Introduction: Factor Investing, a Rules-Based Attempt to Beat the Average

Every investor has, at some point, asked whether there is a reliable way to beat the market average. Factor investing is the academic answer to that question. Rather than relying on a single company's story or on gut feeling, it is rooted in empirical research showing that specific characteristics (factors) shared across many stocks are linked over the long run to excess return, the so-called premium. Cheaply priced stocks (value), recent relative winners (momentum), less volatile names (low volatility), financially strong firms (quality), and smaller companies (size) are among the most cited factors.

This guide explains why factors are discussed as sources of excess return, what the logic and risks of each major factor are, how the performance rotation known as factor cyclicality works, how individuals can access factors through smart-beta ETFs, and the limits you must understand. It is organized around decision criteria and comparison. That said, this article is informational and recommends no specific product. A factor premium is a story about long-run averages, not a return guaranteed every year, and backtests do not secure the future - a point worth stating up front.

1. What Is Factor Investing? The Sources of Systematic Excess Return

The traditional Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) explained a stock's expected return with a single sensitivity to the overall market (beta). Yet real data repeatedly showed return differences that market beta alone could not explain, and researchers called the shared characteristics behind them factors. The Fama-French three-factor model is reported to have sharply improved explanatory power by adding size (small caps) and value (cheap stocks) to the market, and the five-factor model introduced in 2015 added profitability and investment. Momentum, meaning trend, was left out of the original model and is typically handled separately, as in the Carhart four-factor model.

So where does this excess return come from? Three broad explanations are cited. First is a risk premium: holding a factor means bearing a risk others avoid, and the premium is the compensation. Second is behavioral bias: investors chase popular names or avoid losses, so certain characteristics stay persistently mispriced. Third is institutional constraints: rules like leverage limits or benchmark-tracking pressure leave certain factors in place. Academics disagree on which explanation holds, and this debate over causes is central to judging whether a factor will persist.

2.1 Value and Momentum: Two Opposite Axes

Value is contrarian by nature - buy cheap and profit as prices revert - while momentum is trend-following, riding what is already rising, so the two often move in opposite directions. Holding both is therefore discussed as a way to offset each other's droughts. But value is vulnerable to the value trap, where a stock is cheap for good reason, and momentum to the sharp reversal when a trend breaks.

2.2 Low Volatility and Quality: Defensive by Nature

Low volatility leans on the low-volatility anomaly: contrary to the textbook idea that risk and return move together, less volatile stocks have historically delivered market-like returns with lower risk. Quality holds firms with strong profitability and balance sheets and, like low volatility, tends to be defensive in downturns. In strong rallies, though, these defensive factors are easily left behind.

2.3 Size: The Most Contested Factor

The size factor (the small-cap premium) was strongly observed in early research, but there have long been counterarguments that it weakens once liquidity is adjusted for, or disappears in some markets. Size is more often discussed as a complement to other factors than as a standalone strategy.

2.4 A Closer Comparison: Defining Metrics, Theory, and Past Droughts

Even within a single factor, the metric used to define it changes which stocks are held, and whether the theoretical basis is risk compensation or behavioral bias changes how likely the premium is to persist. 3. Why Do Factors Pay? Risk Premium vs. Behavioral Explanations

To judge whether a factor will keep working, you have to ask why the excess return exists at all. If the cause is compensation for risk, no amount of market learning removes the payment for bearing that risk. If the cause is investor error, the premium can disappear the moment the error is corrected. That is why the same data leads some to optimism and others to skepticism.

3.1 The Risk-Premium Explanation

The first reading is that a factor premium is payment for bearing a risk others avoid. Cheaply priced firms often really do carry more financial leverage or less stable earnings, leaving them more exposed in downturns, and the value premium is the compensation for accepting that exposure. Fama and French introduced the five-factor model on the evidence that the three-factor model missed much of the variation in average returns tied to profitability and investment. In it, the profitability factor RMW (Robust Minus Weak) is defined as the return difference between diversified portfolios of stocks with robust and with weak profitability, and the investment factor CMA (Conservative Minus Aggressive) as the return difference between firms that invest conservatively and those that invest aggressively.

3.2 The Behavioral Explanation

The second reading is systematic investor bias. Slow reaction to good news - underreaction - is offered as the basis for momentum, while excessive pessimism about bad news - overreaction - is offered as the basis for value. If this explanation holds, the premium is a market mistake, which implies it can shrink once the mistake becomes widely known and gets corrected.

3.3 The Institutional and Funding-Constraint Explanation

The third points to structural constraints on market participants. Frazzini and Pedersen's "Betting Against Beta" describes investors who cannot use leverage, or whose leverage is capped by margin requirements, overweighting high-beta assets to reach their return targets - with the result that high-beta assets trade at a premium and low-beta assets at a discount. It is a frequently cited account of why the low-volatility anomaly survives even after becoming well known.

These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and their weight differs by factor. What matters in practice is that you should be able to state, in one sentence, why the factor you hold earns an excess return. If you cannot, what you have is not a rationale but an expectation borrowed from past returns.

4. Factor Cyclicality: Factors Take Turns

This is exactly where factor investing is easy to misunderstand. A factor premium is a long-run average, not a steady annual excess return. Research from MSCI and others notes that while factor indexes have shown excess risk-adjusted returns over long horizons, over short horizons they exhibit significant cyclicality and have underperformed the market for years at a stretch. In early economic recoveries, value tends to lead; in uncertain downturns, low volatility and quality tend to gain relative strength.

Because of this cyclicality, a common practical approach is multi-factor diversification - holding factors with opposite behavior or low correlation (for example, value and momentum) so that when one lags, another holds up. Note, however, that factor rotation - switching to whichever factor is currently favored - requires forecasting regimes, faces rapid signal decay, and raises trading costs, making it a demanding strategy. For individual investors, diversification and long holding are generally discussed as more realistic than timing.

5. Factor Cycles and How to Sit Through a Long Drought

In factor investing, the variable that decides your outcome is often not which factor you picked but whether you were still holding it during the bad stretch. MSCI's long-horizon work on factor indexes reports that every factor index has experienced at least a two-to-three-year consecutive period of underperformance, that value and size each showed maximum drawdowns of 35% or more, and that value, size, and yield have seen drawdowns lasting longer than a decade. Over the past decade specifically, momentum and quality outperformed while value, low volatility, equal weight (low size), and dividend yield lagged the broad market by roughly 260-350 basis points annually.

Helpfully, the same research notes that these periods of underperformance have not coincided across factors. That is the empirical case for holding several factors with different behavior. Factor timing - rotating into whichever factor is currently cheap - sounds more appealing, but Asness finds that timing strategies built on the factors' own valuation spreads were historically very weak, and that some tests claiming to demonstrate their long-run power were exaggerated or inapplicable. The practices below build the drought into the design instead.

  • Write down your tolerance first: deciding before you buy how many percentage points below the market, and for how many years, you are willing to sit leaves less room for an emotional exit later.
  • Do not judge it against the market index alone: compared only with a cap-weighted index, ordinary cyclicality looks like failure. Track the relevant factor index alongside it and separate "the fund failed to track its index" from "the factor itself is out of favor."
  • Combine factors whose droughts do not overlap: pairing opposites such as value and momentum gives one a chance to hold up while the other lags. Correlations do shift by regime, however.
  • Match the time horizon of the money: betting capital you need within two or three years on a decade-scale premium means your cash-flow schedule, not the factor, determines the result.
  • Cap the allocation: setting a ceiling on how much of total assets the strategy can occupy limits how much a long drought can affect your life.
  • Rebalance by rule, not by forecast: fixing a calendar trigger such as once a year lets you reset drifting weights without predicting regimes.

None of this prevents losses, and no combination guarantees excess return over the market.

6. How to Access Factors Through Smart-Beta ETFs

The main route for individuals to access factor strategies is the smart-beta ETF. Instead of traditional market-cap weighting, a smart-beta ETF tracks an index that selects and weights stocks by predefined factor rules such as value, low volatility, or quality. Factor investing is technically a subset of smart beta. Its appeal is that an individual who cannot practically pick hundreds of stocks and rebalance by rule can obtain rules-based exposure at low cost.

Still, two funds claiming the same factor can produce different results. The decision criteria to check are generally:

  • Index methodology: even within value, the metric used (P/E, P/B, cash flow, etc.) changes which stocks are held.
  • Rebalancing frequency: switching more often keeps factor exposure fresh but raises turnover and trading costs.
  • Expense ratio: the factor premium itself may be modest, so fees can erode the excess return.
  • Purity vs. diversification: decide between concentrating in one factor or blending several in a multi-factor fund to cushion cyclicality.

In short, rather than choosing on the smart-beta label alone, it is reasonable to compare methodology, total fees, and rebalancing together. This article recommends no specific ETF.

7. Common Traps When You Backtest It Yourself

Writing your own rules and testing them on historical data is a good way to understand factors. The problem is that a backtest almost always looks better than reality. This is structural rather than a matter of skill, and knowing the traps in advance is what lets you distrust your own results. CFA Institute's material on backtesting likewise treats data biases and overfitting as central issues in strategy validation.

TrapHow it arisesConsequenceMitigation
Survivorship biasTesting the past using only the securities that still exist, so delisted, bankrupt, and acquired names drop out of the sampleFailed investments vanish entirely, systematically overstating returnsUse survivorship-bias-free datasets and state explicitly how delisted names are handled
Look-ahead biasUsing information not knowable at the time - for example, first-quarter results stamped in the database as of April 1 when the actual release came much laterProduces returns that could never have been executedUse point-in-time data and build in the delay from data availability to trade
Omitted trading costsAssuming zero commissions, taxes, bid-ask spread, and market impactThe higher the turnover, the wider the gap between paper and realized returnsCalculate turnover first, then re-evaluate after deducting conservative costs
Overfitting and data snoopingRepeatedly tuning parameters against the same dataThe model learns noise and collapses out of sampleOut-of-sample validation, fixing hypotheses and rules in advance, logging the number of attempts

On trading costs, Novy-Marx and Velikov examined the after-cost performance of a broad set of anomalies and reported that, when designed to mitigate transaction costs, most anomalies with less than 50% turnover per month generated significant net spreads, while few with higher turnover did. Average trade execution costs for mid-turnover strategies were estimated at roughly 20 to 57 basis points. In other words, turnover is the first number to check in any backtest. On the overfitting side, Harvey, Liu, and Zhu counted at least 316 factors published in the literature and argued that, given that volume of testing, the customary t-ratio of 2.0 is not enough and a newly discovered factor should clear a t-ratio above 3.0. The flashier the backtest, the higher the bar it should be asked to clear. Use the checks below on your own work.

  • Did you validate out of sample? Separate the period used to build the rule from the period used to test it, and where possible verify with walk-forward analysis that rolls through time.
  • How many times did you change the parameters? Logging the number of attempts lets you gauge for yourself how likely a good result is to be luck.
  • Does performance hold when parameters shift slightly? If it only works at one specific number, you may have found a coincidence rather than a rule.
  • Did you compute turnover? Work out how often the portfolio is replaced per year, then re-evaluate with costs applied on top.
  • Could you actually buy these names? If thinly traded stocks are producing the performance, implementation is unlikely.
  • Did you adjust for dividends, splits, and issuance? Without adjusted prices, the return series itself is distorted.
  • Did you account for taxes and account structure? The more you trade, the wider the gap between pre-tax and after-tax results.
  • Is the data a later revision? Financial statement data is sometimes restated after the fact, so what you see now may differ from what was visible then.

8. The Limits of Factor Investing: Traps You Must Know

8.1 Factor Decay

The most fundamental concern is that a known factor can weaken. Once an anomaly is widely published and capital crowds in, the room for excess return may shrink. The point is raised especially for factors explained by behavioral bias: as the market learns the bias, the premium can fade.

This concern has been measured. McLean and Pontiff assembled 97 variables reported to predict cross-sectional stock returns and compared performance before and after publication, finding that portfolio returns were 26% lower out of sample and 58% lower after publication. The 32-percentage-point difference between those figures is interpreted as the effect of trading informed by the published research. The realistic picture is less that a factor disappears outright and more that it thins out in proportion to how well known it becomes - which is why a historical backtest return should never be adopted as your expected return.

8.2 Data Mining and Overfitting

Academia has reported so many factors that it is dubbed the factor zoo, and a strong critique is that many of them may be the result of fitting noise in historical data by chance. The CFA Institute and others warn that backtest overfitting - p-hacking, or overfitting - produces performance that looks convincing only on paper. The flashier the backtest, the more scrutiny it deserves.

8.3 Trading Costs and the Implementation Gap

Backtests generally understate trading costs. High-turnover factors (momentum especially) accumulate fees, taxes, and market impact from frequent trading, widening the gap between paper returns and executable returns. Factor strategies tend to work most robustly when trading is restrained enough that costs do not overwhelm the expected benefit.

8.4 The Patience to Endure Droughts

The final limit is psychological. A factor can underperform the market for years, and an investor who abandons it during that stretch misses the long-run premium that the eventual rebound provides. Ultimately, factor investing is not a free lunch, and it must be approached on the premise that every factor goes through droughts.

9. A Practical Checklist for Factor Investing

The items below are not a scoring system for ranking products. They are questions that test whether you can explain your own choices. If many of them go unanswered, the safer reading is that you are not yet ready to decide.

9.1 When Examining a Factor ETF or Fund

  • Have you actually read the methodology document for the index the fund tracks?
  • Do you know which metrics define labels like "value" or "quality" in this particular product?
  • Have you checked the rebalancing frequency and the annual turnover it implies?
  • Do you know the total expense figure, management fee and other costs combined?
  • Have you checked tracking error against the index and the premium or discount to net asset value?
  • Are assets under management, average daily volume, and the bid-ask spread adequate?
  • If it is multi-factor, have you distinguished between combining separate single-factor sleeves and selecting names by an integrated multi-factor score?
  • Have you looked at the holdings for unintended sector or industry concentration?
  • For overseas assets, is the exposure currency-hedged or unhedged, and what does that cost?
  • Are distributions paid out or reinvested, and how does that interact with your tax situation?
  • In the performance chart shown, have you separated the simulated period before the product launched from live track record?
  • Does this overlap with factor exposure you already hold elsewhere?

9.2 Questions to Put to Yourself

  • Can you explain in one sentence why this factor's excess return exists?
  • Is that explanation risk compensation, behavioral bias, or an institutional constraint?
  • Could you hold through three consecutive years of underperformance? Have you written that limit down as a number?
  • Have you decided, before buying, under what conditions you would sell?
  • When do you need this money, and does that date match the strategy's time horizon?
  • Have you set a ceiling on this strategy's share of total assets?
  • Is the evidence you have seen anything more than marketing material from the seller?
  • Have you chosen in advance the benchmark you will judge performance against?
  • Do you have a rule to follow when the urge to rotate into whatever factor is winning arrives?
  • Including the possibility of losing principal, is this an amount whose worst case you can absorb?

This checklist is an aid to judgment only; clearing every item guarantees no return.

10. Conclusion: How to Approach Factor Investing

Factor investing is a systematic attempt to reach the sources of excess return through rules rather than instinct. First, understand that value, momentum, low volatility, quality, and size each have different logic and risk, and hold factors with opposite behavior to diversify cyclicality risk. Second, access factors via smart-beta ETFs while comparing index methodology, total fees, and rebalancing. Third, keep the limits - factor decay, overfitting, trading costs - always in mind, and be wary of dazzling backtests. Fourth, because factors are a long-run-average story, they matter only when you have the capital and time to endure droughts. Fifth, if you intend to build your own rules, check for the four traps - survivorship bias, look-ahead bias, omitted trading costs, and overfitting - before you trust any result.
