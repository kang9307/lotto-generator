Workplace Harassment in Korea: How to Respond - From Evidence to Reporting

The three legal elements, what counts as evidence, the recording question, and the in-house and Labor Ministry routes, in Q&A form

Jul 26, 2026 Law & Legal

This article is general information based on the Labor Standards Act of the Republic of Korea, not legal advice on any individual case. Identical-looking conduct can be assessed differently depending on the workplace and the evidence, and the law and case law can change. For a concrete plan, consult a lawyer or a certified labor attorney. Other countries define and handle this very differently.

What to do right now (quick answer)

  • Start a written record today. Date, time, place, what happened, who was present, in plain factual language. Memory fades; a record does not.
  • Do not decide alone whether it counts. The test is set by statute, not by how tough you think you should be. Ambiguity is a reason to ask, not a reason to wait.
  • Free advice: the Ministry of Employment and Labor call centre, 1350 (dial without an area code, from within Korea). You can ask anonymously.
  • If your workplace has a reporting channel, you can use it. Once a report is received, the employer has a duty to investigate without delay.
  • If the employer does nothing, or the employer is the one harassing you, you can file a complaint with the competent regional labor office.

One more thing worth saying plainly: being harassed is not evidence of a shortcoming on your part, and there is no need to blame yourself for not acting sooner. Start from where you are.

When someone is living through repeated humiliation, exclusion, or unexplained removal from their own work, the first thought is almost always the same one: am I just being oversensitive? Counsellors say it is the single most common opening question. But the question is back to front. Sensitivity is not the test. Since Korea's workplace harassment provisions took effect in July 2019, the test and the employer's obligations have been written into the Labor Standards Act, and that is what an assessment actually turns on.

This article is written on the assumption that you are reading it in a hurry, so it runs as a series of questions and answers: what the statutory definition is, where the line sits between firm management and harassment, how to preserve evidence, what the employer must do once a report lands, and what routes exist when the employer does nothing. It remains general information. Real cases turn on context and evidence, which is why professional advice is flagged at each decision point rather than only at the end.

Q1. Does what happened to me legally count as workplace harassment?

Article 76-2 of the Labor Standards Act defines it as conduct in which an employer or worker uses superiority of position or relationship at work to go beyond the appropriate scope of work, causing another worker physical or mental suffering or worsening the work environment. That definition is usually broken into three elements.

  • 1. Use of superiority — not only rank. Age, tenure, weight of numbers (a group against one person), and relationships of work dependence are described as qualifying too. That is why harassment by peers, or even by a junior, can be established.
  • 2. Exceeding the appropriate scope of work — either there is no genuine business need, or there is one but the manner used is not socially acceptable. This is where most disputes live.
  • 3. Suffering or a worsened work environment — the person must actually have been harmed, or the environment must have become one in which normal work is difficult.

The point that matters most: if any one of the three is not made out, it is generally not treated as harassment under the statute. Read the other way, organising your situation around those three headings makes consultations and reports considerably smoother. You do not have to reach a verdict yourself; that is the job of whoever investigates.

Q2. Where is the line between a strict boss and harassment?

This is the hardest boundary. The common formulation is that where there is a genuine business need and the manner stays within socially accepted bounds, an instruction counts as legitimate management even if it is unpleasant. The table below sets out the direction of the usual analysis; it does not guarantee the outcome of any particular case.

AxisMore likely legitimate managementMore likely beyond the appropriate scope
Work relevanceInstructions within the person's role and dutiesPersonal errands, intrusion into private life
PurposeImproving work or managing performanceIsolating or subduing a specific individual
MannerCriticism of the work, with a direction for improvementRemarks that demean the person, humiliation in front of others
Even-handednessThe same standard applied to others in the same situationApplied repeatedly to one person without reasonable basis
Work allocationDifficulty adjusted to role and capabilityRemoval of work without reason, or repeated impossible tasks
PersistenceA one-off point, resolved on the spotConduct that repeats and accumulates over time

In practice, repetition and accumulation often weigh more heavily than a single loud incident. That is precisely why records matter. Individual episodes can look trivial in isolation and reveal an unmistakable pattern once laid out in date order.

Q3. How do I gather evidence?

Nothing elaborate is required. The most useful material, by a wide margin, is the note you write on the day.

3-1. The record (the foundation)

After each incident, write down the date and time, the place, what happened, who was present, and the effect it had. Factual language carries more weight than emotional language. Writing it the same day on a personal phone or personal email preserves a timestamp, which supports the consistency of your account later. Do not keep it only on a company device or company account. If access is cut off, the material goes with it.

3-2. Messages, email, and work records

Capture or export messenger threads, emails, and assignment records so the counterpart and the date remain visible. Narrow crops invite arguments about context, so include what came before and after. If the issue is exclusion from work, meeting invitation lists, duty allocation sheets, and changes to approval chains can all show the shape of the situation.

3-3. Witnesses

Confirmation from a colleague who was present is valuable. But colleagues still have to work there. Approaching them in a way that creates pressure can damage the relationship, so starting with a simple confirmation of what happened is usually the realistic move.

3-4. Medical and counselling records

If you have sought treatment or counselling for sleep problems, anxiety, or low mood, those records can support the harm side of the case. More importantly, looking after your health comes first. The point is not to see a doctor in order to build a file; it is that if you are already struggling, there is no reason to put it off.

Q4. Can I record a conversation without saying so?

This is the most frequently asked question and the most widely misunderstood. Article 3 of the Protection of Communications Secrets Act prohibits a person who is not a party from recording or listening to a private conversation between other people. The operative words are between other people.

  • A conversation you are part of (a meeting, an interview, a phone call) recorded without telling the other side — as a party to the conversation, the common reading is that this does not violate the Act.
  • A device left running while you are absent, capturing a conversation among others — this is third-party recording and can be unlawful, with criminal exposure discussed in commentary. Safest not to do it.

One further caution. Even a lawfully obtained recording is a separate question from evidentiary weight. Courts are described as weighing the circumstances and purpose of the recording, whether it was edited, and the credibility of what was said. So keep the unedited original. Some workplaces also restrict recording through security rules or work rules, which raises a separate internal-discipline risk. If you are weighing whether to record, ask a labor attorney or lawyer before you do it.

Q5. What happens after I report internally?

Article 76-3 of the Labor Standards Act sets out what the employer must do once a report is made. In sequence:

  1. Reporting — anyone who becomes aware of workplace harassment may report it to the employer. It is not limited to the person harmed.
  2. Duty to investigate — on receiving a report or becoming aware of harassment, the employer must investigate objectively and without delay.
  3. Protection during the investigation — where necessary, the employer must take appropriate measures such as a change of work location or paid leave for the affected worker, and must not impose measures against that worker's wishes. This is the basis for objecting to being the one who gets moved out of the way.
  4. Measures once confirmed — if harassment is confirmed and the affected worker requests it, the employer must take steps such as changing the work location, reassignment, or paid leave.
  5. Action against the perpetrator, with consultation — before disciplining or relocating the perpetrator, the employer must hear the affected worker's views.
  6. No detrimental treatment — the employer must not dismiss or otherwise disadvantage the reporter or the affected worker.
  7. Confidentiality — those who take part in the investigation must not disclose what they learn against the affected worker's wishes.

It is reported that a separate administrative fine applies where the employer or a relative of the employer is the perpetrator, and a further fine where the employer fails to carry out the investigation duty. Exact amounts and triggers should be checked against the law in force at the time.

Q6. What if they retaliate because I reported?

This is the part people fear most, and the statute treats it separately. Dismissal or other detrimental treatment of a reporter or an affected worker is prohibited, and an employer in breach is reported to face criminal penalties (the provision is commonly described as up to three years' imprisonment or a fine of up to 30 million won). The fact that this is framed as a criminal matter rather than an administrative fine says something about how the provision is weighted.

Practically, the key is to record the before-and-after. A sudden transfer, a shrinking of duties, a sharp drop in an evaluation grade, exclusion from meetings: note the timing and whatever reason was given. Where the detriment takes the form of dismissal or discipline, a separate route exists through a remedy application to the Labor Relations Commission, and that route has time limits, so it is worth seeking advice early rather than late. Our companion piece on wrongful termination and workers' rights covers that process.

Q7. What if the company will not investigate, or the boss is the problem?

Internal channels failing is not rare. Where the perpetrator is the employer or the person who controls personnel decisions, an internal report is often unrealistic. In that situation you can skip the internal step and go straight to the external route.

  • Ministry of Employment and Labor call centre, 1350 — useful for checking the procedure and your options before committing to anything.
  • Complaint to the competent regional labor office — a complaint can be filed online through the ministry's labor portal, or in person at a nearby district labor office. There is no fee.
  • Labor inspector review — once filed, an inspector is assigned and the facts are examined through a formal process.

It is reported that in April 2026 the ministry revised its guidance on handling harassment complaints, strengthening the position so that cases where the employer is the perpetrator, cases involving severe harm, and cases where a company refused or plainly botched an investigation are examined directly by labor inspectors. How the guidance applies still varies case by case, so confirm where your situation sits during the consultation stage.

Q8. It has made me ill. Can that be an industrial accident?

Possibly. It is commonly explained that depression, anxiety disorders, and similar conditions arising from workplace harassment can be recognised as occupational illness. Recognition opens access to medical care benefits and temporary disability benefits through the Korea Workers' Compensation and Welfare Service.

That said, recognition of mental illness turns on establishing the link between work and the condition, and the process is not simple. The records and medical documentation discussed above become the core material here as well. You can apply yourself, though many people work with an experienced labor attorney or lawyer.

Q9. We are a small company. Does this apply to us?

The Labor Standards Act applies in full to workplaces with five or more regular employees, while workplaces below that threshold have historically been covered by only part of the Act. However, as policy has moved toward phasing in fuller application of the Act to workplaces with fewer than five employees, the harassment provisions have been discussed and applied as a priority category, according to reports.

Timing and scope can shift with each amendment, so treat this article as orientation rather than a settled rule and confirm your own workplace's position through the 1350 line or your regional labor office. Even where the statutory duties do not apply, conduct such as assault, threats, defamation, or insult may be actionable under other laws, so there is no reason to abandon the idea of getting advice.

Where to get help

  • Ministry of Employment and Labor call centre — 1350 (general labor law consultation, within Korea)
  • Ministry labor portal — online filing of complaints covering unpaid wages, workplace harassment, and other violations; no fee
  • Competent regional labor office — in-person or postal filing, and labor inspector review
  • Korea Workers' Compensation and Welfare Service — advice on occupational illness claims, including mental health conditions
  • Certified labor attorney or lawyer — case strategy, Labor Relations Commission applications
  • Local government workers' rights centres — free labor consultation in many municipalities
  • If things feel overwhelming, Korea's mental health helpline is 1577-0199 and the suicide prevention line is 109. Both operate around the clock.

Conclusion: take it in order

To recap. First, start recording today — date, place, what happened, who was there, factually, on a personal device. Second, do not judge it alone. The three elements (superiority, beyond the appropriate scope, harm or a worsened environment) are a frame for thinking, not a verdict you have to reach. Third, get advice before acting. The 1350 line is free and can be used anonymously. Fourth, decide whether the internal or external route is realistic in your situation — if the employer is the perpetrator, the external route is open from the start. Fifth, keep recording after you report. Detrimental treatment is a separate breach in its own right.

The single thing most worth avoiding is holding out alone for too long. The longer the enduring goes on, the blurrier the record gets and the worse the health toll becomes, and people often find that the material they need no longer exists by the time they need it. There is also no requirement to wait until the evidence is perfect; what you have now is enough to start a consultation.
