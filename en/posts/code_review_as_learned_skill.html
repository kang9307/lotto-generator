Code Review Does Not Improve on Its Own

A reading order, five lenses, a three-tier comment notation, and the drills that actually build reviewers

Aug 16, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: approvals got faster and the same thing broke again

A change request is approved in thirty minutes. It carries three comments, all of them about variable names and line breaks. Two weeks later there is an incident, and the cause is a single condition that lived inside that change. Review happened. It just did not catch anything.

The usual diagnosis in teams where this repeats is 'let us be more thorough'. But thoroughness is not a quantity you can dial up. What you can change is what you look at and in what order, and that is a procedure rather than a temperament. People who catch things in review are not more conscientious; they read in a different order. Order can be taught.

That argument resurfaced recently. A post that made the rounds on GeekNews in August framed review as a skill you can learn, and backed it with three real bugs that current models missed. In all three the code itself was fine. One was a performance-versus-safety trade-off in a file locking design, one was whether a newly used command-line flag actually existed in the deployed tool version, and one was what survives when a checksum and a body are uploaded separately and the upload is interrupted halfway. All three are visible only if you carry context that is not on the screen.

This article does not argue about the purpose of review or which tool to buy. Purpose is covered in review as a maintainability check, and the tooling landscape in agentic code review. What follows is only about deliberately building the skill in one person.

1. Symptoms of leaving review to happen by itself

1.1 Style survives, design passes through

Collect the review comments from a team that runs without any training and the distribution is lopsided. Names, formatting, whether a comment block exists. Almost nothing that asks why a boundary was drawn where it was drawn.

This is a cost structure, not laziness. A style comment is nearly free: you only need one line of context, you cannot be wrong, and the author cannot easily push back. A design comment requires understanding the whole change, embarrasses you when it is wrong, and usually starts an argument. When two options differ that much in price, review drifts toward the cheap one. That is why an activity left to individual diligence gets worse over time rather than better.

1.2 Approvals get slower while fewer defects are caught

Once review becomes a rite of passage, reviewers defer in front of large changes. As waiting time grows, authors batch more into each change, since if you are going to wait anyway you may as well send it all at once. That is individually rational and collectively fatal: the next review is harder, so it is deferred longer.

When that loop spins up you get the worst pairing available, slower approval with fewer defects caught. Why cap rules rarely fix it is worked out separately in the dynamics of the review queue. The short version is that arrival rate against service rate governs the outcome, not the rule.

1.3 Reviewers look in different places and leave holes

One person opens the tests first, another starts with naming. Two people reviewing the same change double up on the overlap and leave the rest untouched. Adding reviewers does not close that hole. Without a shared norm, coverage is determined not by headcount but by the number of non-overlapping viewpoints.

Google's published reviewer guidance lists its checks in a fixed order precisely to plug that hole: design, functionality, complexity, tests, naming, comments, style, consistency, documentation. Nine items in sequence, with style at number seven.

2. The reading order, from intent to notation

Nine items are hard to recall under time pressure. Compressed into five stages you can hold in your head, it looks like the table below. The arrangement is not taste; it is an order of information acquisition. Each stage needs the answer from the one before it.

StageWhat you checkWhat you readWhat it catchesWhat it cannot catch
1. Intent of the changeWhat is changing and why, was there another wayDescription, issue, decision recordMis-defined problems, unnecessary workEvery implementation defect
2. InterfaceHow the shape of what crosses the boundary changedSchemas, signatures, exported typesCompatibility breaks, misplaced responsibilityInternal logic
3. Data flowWhere values enter and leave, who owns themCall paths, storage accessState mismatch, trust boundary violations, leaksNotation issues
4. Edge casesBoundaries, errors, concurrency, retriesTests, exception handling, lockingMost production incidentsReadability
5. NotationNames, comments, formattingStyle guide, linterCost of understandingEverything else

2.1 Why intent comes first

Open the code without knowing the intent and every judgement collapses into 'does this implementation work'. A perfect solution to a badly defined problem passes in that state. The grammar is fine, the tests are green, and there is nothing to point at.

There is a more practical reason. The most valuable comment available, 'does this change need to exist', realistically only appears in the first five minutes. After twenty minutes of reading, the reviewer has invested time in that code and stops proposing that the direction be reversed. So confirming intent is not merely first in sequence; it should be finished before the code is opened. If the description does not answer it, sending the change back right there is a legitimate review outcome.

2.2 Why notation comes last

Notation is automatable. Every minute a human spends on what a formatter or linter could catch is a minute not spent elsewhere, and the total is fixed.

The worse effect is anchoring. If a variable name grates on the first screen, the next twenty minutes turn into a conversation about names while the design problem slides past. Google's guidance classifies any purely stylistic point that is not in the style guide as personal preference and asks reviewers to prefix it with Nit: for exactly this reason: to detach that lane from the main one. The same document states flatly that software design is never a matter of pure style or personal preference. Design and taste do not carry the same weight.

2.3 What actually changes when you keep the order

  • Time allocation shifts. Someone who spent half their budget on stage five and moves it to stage four changes the kind of defect they find
  • You gain a stopping point. Blocked at stage one, you return the change without reading the code. That is procedure, not laziness
  • Comments stop colliding. Two people reading in the same order stall at different depths, so their attention spreads out
  • Review becomes predictable in duration. With a rough budget per stage there is less reason to defer it

One distinction is worth keeping sharp. This is an order for building understanding, not an order for triaging risk. What to look at first when there is no time to read everything is a different axis, and it is laid out as contract, invariant, then permission in moving the control point outside the code. Blend the two and you get neither training nor triage.

3. What to look at: five lenses

Checklists die as they grow. Twenty items and nobody uses it; five and it becomes a habit. These five were chosen around the places where real incidents keep recurring.

3.1 Boundary values

  • Zero, one, maximum, over. An empty list, exactly one item, exactly the page size
  • An empty string is not null. Treating them the same means something diverges later
  • Sign and rounding position for numbers. With money, one digit becomes an accounting problem
  • Time boundaries: midnight, month end, daylight saving transitions, and whether the zone attaches at storage or at display
  • Ordering among equal keys when the sort is unstable. Paging then duplicates or drops rows
  • Whether length is measured in bytes or characters. Non-Latin scripts and emoji split the two immediately

3.2 Error paths

  • The happy path has tests. The failure path usually does not. That asymmetry is the reviewer's territory
  • Code that swallows exceptions: an empty catch, or one that logs and continues
  • Partial failure. Two of three succeeded, so what remains and what disappeared
  • Whether a timeout exists, and whether it is shorter than the caller's timeout above it
  • Whether retrying is safe. You can only retry when sending the same request twice yields the same result
  • Whether internal paths or identifiers ride out inside error messages

3.3 Concurrency

  • Read-then-write windows. What gets overwritten if another request lands in between
  • Whether a transaction boundary wraps an external call. If it does, the connection is held for someone else's latency
  • Whether cache invalidation happens before or after the write. One ordering pins a stale value permanently
  • Whether locks are acquired in the same order on every code path
  • Whether batch jobs and online requests touch the same rows in the same window
  • This lens is nearly invisible to tests, which is why a human reading it is still the cheapest option

3.4 Reversibility

  • How you undo this after deploying. If the answer does not fit on one line, that is the finding
  • With a schema change mixed in, reverting the code alone is not enough. Does compatibility hold in both directions for one release
  • Changes that delete data or reshape it cannot be reverted. Those need a roll-forward procedure, not a rollback
  • Anything already sent does not come back: mail, payments, external notifications
  • If it can be wrapped in a feature flag the review burden itself drops. Ask about the flag removal date at the same time

3.5 Observability

  • If this goes wrong, who finds out and how. If the answer is 'the customer tells us', that is the defect
  • Whether the newly introduced failure modes have a log line or a metric behind them
  • Whether personal data enters the logs. A log line written once is not recalled
  • Metric cardinality. Use a user identifier as a label and the bill explodes a few months out
  • Whether the trace left by a failure is enough to identify the cause, and whether a request identifier runs the whole path

Once running the five in order becomes routine, the time it takes goes down rather than up, because you are no longer inventing the list each time. Until then, keep it visible beside the diff.

4. How to write feedback: blocking, suggestion, taste

The most common place review breaks is not reading but writing. Ten comments delivered in a uniform tone leave the author unable to tell what must be fixed. So they fix everything, argue about everything, or silently comply and quietly start resenting review.

The fix is trivial. Put a grade in front of every comment. No tooling and no policy document, just a prefix.

GradePrefixMeaningRelation to approvalSentence shape
BlockingBlocking:This must not merge as isNo approval until resolvedWhat + under which input or state + with what result
SuggestionSuggestion:I think there is a better wayAuthor decides, may keep with reasonsAlternative + why it is better + your call
TasteNit:Fine to ignoreUnrelated to approvalOne line, short
QuestionQuestion:I am asking to understandClosed by an answerState exactly what you do not know
MarkerPraise:Record what went wellUnrelatedWhat is good and why

Nit: is not an invented convention. It is written into Google's guidance, which asks you to attach it to an improvement that is not in the style guide so that the developer knows it is not mandatory. The other four extend the same principle.

4.1 The sentence shape that carries evidence

A blocking comment has a minimum of three parts: what, under which input or state, with what result. Drop any one and the author cannot reproduce it, and from there it becomes an argument.

  • Bad — this part looks a bit risky to me
  • Good — Blocking: this dereferences the first element when the list is empty, so every new user fails on first entry
  • Good suggestion — Suggestion: hoisting this branch into the query cuts it to one query per call. Not urgent at current volume, so your call
  • Evidence ranks as incident history > specification > principle > personal experience > taste. The higher you sit, the shorter the argument
  • State your own confidence. 'I am certain' and 'I am guessing, please verify' are different comments

4.2 The question form is not always the right one

Advice to phrase things softly is everywhere, but a question form applied to a blocking issue does harm. 'What if we did it this way' reads as an option, the author legitimately declines it, and after the incident all that remains is 'I did mention it'. That helps nobody.

Conversely, an assertive form where you actually needed to check a fact produces a wrong finding, and after two of those the reviewer's blocking comments lose weight entirely. The criterion is not tone but confidence. If you know the answer, assert it and attach evidence; if you do not, ask and say what you do not know. The principle of pointing at the code rather than the person still holds. 'Why did you do it this way' and 'what remains if this path fails' are entirely different sentences. In distributed teams reviewing across time zones this matters more, not less, because a misread comment costs a full day of round trip rather than a turn of conversation.

4.3 Agree on the approval bar as one sentence

Google's guidance sets the bar at the point where a change definitely improves the overall code health of the system, and pairs it with the line that there is no such thing as perfect code, only better code. Without that one sentence, an individual reviewer's perfectionism becomes the team's throughput.

It is worth fixing the disagreement path too. The same guidance says to seek consensus first, move to a conversation if that fails, and escalate if it still fails, while not letting the decision sit. In practice the most expensive state is neither rejected nor approved for three days.

5. The drills that build reviewers

This is the part that matters. The order and the notation above can be read today and used tomorrow. But the sense of what looks dangerous does not transfer through documents. It lives in the head of someone who has been through an incident, and getting it out requires a mechanism. The four below are arranged by cost.

5.1 Pair review: compare the comments

  • Two people review the same change separately, then compare comments. The non-overlap is the teaching material
  • The senior must not speak first. Hearing the answer first turns the rest into confirmation and hides the junior's own viewpoint
  • It costs double, so pin it at one change per week rather than every change. This is a training budget, not an operating procedure
  • Sharing a screen and narrating 'why am I opening this file right now' transfers the order directly
  • Pick an ordinary change, not a hard one. Hard changes have exactly one conclusion, so differences in attention never surface

5.2 Defect retrospectives and sharing near misses

  • After an incident there is one question: should review have caught this, and if so, at which stage
  • It is a question that looks for a stage, not a person. Break that distinction and nobody speaks up at the next retrospective
  • 'Review could not have caught this' is a legitimate conclusion. It then moves to a test or a gate rather than onto a checklist
  • The richer material is what did not blow up: defects caught in review, things spotted right before release. The original author proposes exactly this, keeping a short record of each bug found during a sprint and circulating it in the team
  • Fix the record at three lines: what it was, why it was invisible, what would have revealed it. Longer and nobody writes it

5.3 Scatter review assignment on purpose

  • If one person always reviews one directory, that directory gets safe and everything else gets risky. And when they leave, nobody knows anything
  • Put one owner plus one rotating reviewer into the assignment rule. The second seat is the training seat
  • Expect something different from the rotating reviewer: not 'is this correct' but 'does this read'. Naming the places that do not parse is valuable on its own
  • The book Google published states that most of its internal reviews are handled by exactly one reviewer. It separates approval into correctness, ownership, and language readability, and one person usually holds all three
  • So adding reviewers is not the answer. That design rests on the premise that one person's skill is the review quality, which is precisely why the money goes into training

5.4 Extract the tacit knowledge of whoever catches the most

  • Every team has someone who catches an unusual amount. Ask how they do it and the answer is usually 'I just see it'. That is tacit knowledge
  • Getting it out means mining why their attention moved, not their conclusions. Open a real change together, have them read aloud, and ask at each pause why they stopped
  • The original author frames this as Socratic dialogue and cognitive analysis of experts. Different packaging, same point
  • What you extract survives longer as a list of questions than as a checklist. 'What remains if this path fails' gets reused far more than a checkbox does
  • An hour a quarter is enough, but it has to be written down, or it leaves when the person leaves

6. What changed once AI started writing the code

6.1 Surface quality rose and intent verification got heavier

Generated code has tidy names, has comments, and is consistently formatted. From the reviewer's chair that means the signals that used to fire at stages one and five are gone. Messy code used to be a danger flag: tangled code meant the author was confused too, so digging around it usually produced something. That correlation weakened, and clean-but-wrong code became common.

So the time budget has to shift forward. Move what was spent on notation into intent and data flow. The three cases the original author cites are exactly this type. The code itself was syntactically and structurally fine; what was wrong was the tool version in the deployment environment, a transaction boundary, and a design trade-off. All three live off-screen.

6.2 There is nobody left to ask

With human-written code, asking 'why did you do it this way' produces an answer. With generated code the author frequently does not have one. Half of review used to be conversation, and that half is now empty.

The response is to relocate the requirement. Make the change description record the decision and the alternatives that were dropped, and decline to read the code without it. The broader movement of review from code toward decisions is covered in moving the control point. Compressed into one working rule: code the author cannot explain does not get approved. That is not a rule against tools; it is a rule that pins where responsibility sits.

6.3 The training path gets cut

Review skill used to grow by having your own code picked apart. Write less and that path narrows. At the same time the volume you have to read goes up. You are judging before you have learned, and no amount of individual diligence fixes that shape.

Which is why the drills in section five have to be introduced with a budget rather than left to emerge. One more thing holds its value: understanding one level of abstraction below where you work. Judging what a generator produced requires knowing what happens underneath it. The observation that understanding became the bottleneck shows up most sharply in the problem of growing reviewers.

7. Organisational metrics: what to measure to make review worse

7.1 What is easy to count is usually easy to game

Organisations that decide to manage review start with what is easy to count. Almost without exception, easy-to-count metrics are gameable through behaviour. And in an activity where quality is the entire product, the distortion arrives fast.

MetricIntentBehaviour it actually inducesWhat is left
Approval countIncrease participationSkim and approve, leave a one-line commentReview becomes ceremonial
Shorter review turnaroundRemove the bottleneckAvoid hard changes, pick up easy onesRisky changes wait the longest
Comment countMeasure diligenceMass-produce taste commentsSignal to noise degrades
Minimise round tripsReduce frictionSwallow uncertain findingsDefects pass through
100% review coveragePrevent gapsFill it with formal approvalsGreen dashboard, no substance

7.2 So what should you look at

  • Treat waiting time as a distribution, not a target. Look at the tail rather than the median: how many changes sat untouched for more than three days
  • The book Google published records an expectation of first feedback within 24 working hours. That is better used as a line past which something is wrong than as a target to hit
  • The size distribution of changes. The same book notes that about 35% of its internal changes touch a single file. Review only works when large changes are the exception. Splitting them is covered in stacking changes in layers
  • Rework rate, meaning how often the same files are edited again shortly after merge. It is an indirect signal of what review missed
  • The split of the stage at which defects were caught: found in review versus found after release
  • More valuable than any number is the retrospective question. Of last quarter's incidents, how many could review have caught, and why did it not

7.3 Never measure it per person

If you keep only one rule, keep this one. The moment review metrics enter individual performance evaluation, review becomes politics. Hard-to-rebut findings multiply, difficult changes get avoided, and people start choosing whom to point at. Defect detection rate per person breaks fastest of all, because it penalises whoever was handed the difficult code.

Use metrics at team level only, and give individuals training resources instead of a scorecard. An hour of pair review and a three-line retrospective note are far cheaper and far more effective than a per-person dashboard.

8. A ninety-day training plan

8.1 For an individual

  • Weeks 1-2 — keep the reading order beside your screen and follow it every time. Notation comments only in the last five minutes
  • Weeks 3-4 — prefix every comment with a grade. What you have actually been commenting on becomes visible within that week
  • Weeks 5-8 — pick your weakest of the five lenses. Usually concurrency or observability. Apply that one deliberately on every review
  • Weeks 9-12 — revisit defects that came out of changes you approved and mark which stage let them through. This is the highest-yield step in individual training
  • Ongoing — read the comments of whoever catches the most, in chronological order. What surfaces is not their conclusions but what they opened first

8.2 For a team

  • Month 1 — put the three grade prefixes into the conventions. One agreed line, no tooling required
  • Month 1 — settle the approval bar as a single sentence. 'Approve once it definitely improves overall code health' is enough
  • Month 2 — put a weekly pair review on the calendar. Left to volunteering it does not happen
  • Month 2 — create a channel for three-line near-miss notes. It survives only if the format is fixed
  • Month 3 — add a rotating reviewer seat to the assignment rule
  • Month 3 — attach checklists per path. Do not build a global checklist
  • Quarterly — make 'could review have caught this' a standing item in incident retrospectives

9. What could not be confirmed

  • No figure for what percentage of defects code review catches appears here. Statements circulating in secondary sources, such as reviewed code carrying 20-30% fewer defects, could not be confirmed against a primary source
  • Google's internal case study on review (ICSE 2018) and Microsoft's study on review expectations and outcomes (ICSE 2013) could not be opened as full text, so no comment-type distributions or median latency figures are cited from them. The Google-related statements here stay within the published review guidance and the book Google released
  • Widely quoted thresholds such as 'keep pull requests under 400 lines' are also absent, since the primary source could not be confirmed
  • No study measuring reviewer training programmes against a control group was found. The mechanisms in section five are reasoned design, not measured prescriptions
  • No authoritative survey comparing review practice across Korean, Japanese, Chinese and Western teams was confirmed either. Where practice is described here, it is judgement rather than statistics

Conclusion: review is a procedure, not a personality

What separates people who catch things from people who do not is not conscientiousness. They have an order, they have lenses, they grade their comments, and they revisit what they missed. All four can be learned and all four can be taught. Which means a team with bad review does not have a people problem; it has a missing-procedure problem.

The organisation's job is not to say 'be thorough' but to turn those four into procedure and attach a budget. One hour of pair review a week, three lines per near miss, one rotating reviewer seat. That is the whole cost, and in most teams it is cheaper than a single recurring meeting.

With generated code in the mix, the return on that investment went up rather than down. There is more to read, the surface signals are gone, and the path where you learned by having your own code criticised has narrowed. Deliberate training is what is left.

If you change one thing tomorrow, change this. On your next review, prefix each comment with blocking, suggestion, or taste. It needs no tooling and no agreement, and it shows you on the spot which side your reviews have been leaning on.
