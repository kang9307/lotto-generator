Vitamin D Deficiency Complete Guide — Symptoms, Causes, Testing and Supplementation

Understanding vitamin D — what it does for bones, immunity and muscles, how deficiency develops, how to test for it, and how to safely correct it

2026-06-24 Health & Wellness
⚠️ Health information notice — This article is for general information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Consult a clinician if you have symptoms or need a decision.

Introduction: Why Vitamin D Deficiency Matters

Vitamin D is unlike most vitamins — our bodies can produce it from sunlight rather than relying entirely on diet. Technically, it functions as a prohormone and participates in hundreds of biological processes. Yet despite this capacity for self-production, a significant portion of the global population maintains insufficient blood levels. Modern indoor lifestyles, widespread sunscreen use, high-latitude winters, and diets low in fatty fish all contribute to this widespread shortfall.

The challenge is that vitamin D deficiency often produces vague, non-specific symptoms — fatigue, muscle aches, low mood — that overlap with dozens of other conditions. Without deliberate testing, it is easy to miss. This guide covers what vitamin D does in the body, the symptoms and risk groups associated with deficiency, how blood testing works, and evidence-based approaches to correction. All information is based on authoritative sources including the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, the Endocrine Society, and the National Academy of Medicine, current as of June 2026.

1. What Vitamin D Does — Bones, Immunity, and Muscles

1.1 Bone Health

Vitamin D's most established role is facilitating calcium absorption from the gut. When blood levels of the active form (calcitriol) are adequate, the intestine absorbs a much higher fraction of dietary calcium. Without sufficient vitamin D, calcium absorption drops, parathyroid hormone (PTH) rises to compensate by drawing calcium from bones, and bone density falls over time. In children, severe deficiency causes rickets — a softening and weakening of developing bones. In adults, prolonged deficiency leads to osteomalacia (bone softening) and increases the risk of osteoporosis and fracture.

1.2 Immune Function

Immune cells — macrophages, T cells, B cells — express vitamin D receptors, and the vitamin modulates both innate and adaptive immune responses. Observational research has associated lower vitamin D levels with higher rates of respiratory infection and certain autoimmune conditions. However, the causal relationship is still being studied, and evidence that supplementation reliably prevents infections is not yet definitive. The immune role of vitamin D is real but should not be overstated.

1.3 Muscle Function

Muscle tissue also contains vitamin D receptors, and vitamin D contributes to muscle protein synthesis and neuromuscular function. Severe deficiency is associated with proximal muscle weakness, gait instability, and increased fall risk — particularly in older adults. Multiple observational studies have linked low vitamin D status to muscle pain and fatigue, though supplementation trials show mixed results when deficiency is not confirmed first.

1.4 Other Roles

Vitamin D is being investigated for roles in cardiovascular health, insulin secretion, mood regulation, and cancer prevention. The evidence in these areas is less established than for bone and calcium metabolism, and findings are often observational. It is accurate to say vitamin D is important beyond bones, while acknowledging that the science is still evolving.

2. Symptoms of Vitamin D Deficiency

Most symptoms of vitamin D deficiency are non-specific, meaning they can be caused by many other conditions. The following are commonly reported associations, but none are diagnostic on their own. Blood testing remains the only reliable confirmation.

2.1 Fatigue and General Weakness

Persistent fatigue — feeling tired even after adequate sleep — is one of the most frequently reported symptoms in people with documented vitamin D deficiency. If fatigue persists without clear explanation, checking vitamin D status alongside other common causes (anemia, thyroid dysfunction, sleep disorders) is reasonable.

2.2 Bone and Joint Pain

Deep, aching bone pain — particularly in the lower back, hips, legs, and ribs — can be a sign of osteomalacia in adults. Pressing on the sternum or shin bone and experiencing tenderness is a classic clinical indicator. This is frequently misattributed to arthritis or fibromyalgia without vitamin D testing.

2.3 Muscle Weakness and Cramps

Difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs, or lifting moderately heavy objects may reflect proximal muscle weakness linked to deficiency. Muscle cramps can also occur, partly because vitamin D plays a role in calcium regulation within muscle cells.

2.4 Low Mood and Depression

Several brain regions express vitamin D receptors, and lower vitamin D levels have been associated with depressive symptoms in population studies. Seasonal affective disorder (SAD), which worsens during low-sunlight months, has also been linked to vitamin D. Evidence for supplementation improving mood is promising but not yet conclusive.

2.5 Frequent Infections

Given vitamin D's role in immune modulation, deficiency may increase susceptibility to upper respiratory tract infections. People who notice they get sick frequently, especially in winter when sun exposure is minimal, may benefit from vitamin D testing.

2.6 Hair Loss and Slow Wound Healing

Some research has linked vitamin D deficiency with alopecia areata (patchy hair loss), though the evidence is not strong enough to establish causation. Wound healing involves immune and proliferative processes in which vitamin D may play a supportive role; deficiency has been associated with slower recovery in some studies.

3. Who Is at Risk — High-Risk Groups

3.1 Indoor-Oriented Lifestyles

Since 80–90% of vitamin D is synthesized through skin exposure to UVB radiation, people who spend most of their day indoors — office workers, remote workers, hospitalized patients — have structurally limited synthesis. Modern working arrangements make indoor deficiency a population-level concern, not just an individual one.

3.2 People Living at Higher Latitudes

Above approximately 35 degrees latitude (which includes most of Europe, the northern United States, Canada, Korea, Japan, and northern China), UVB radiation is insufficient for vitamin D synthesis during autumn, winter, and early spring — regardless of how much time is spent outside. For residents of these regions, supplementation during low-sun months is a practical necessity for many.

3.3 Older Adults

Skin efficiency in vitamin D synthesis declines with age. Kidney activation of vitamin D to its active form also decreases. Older adults additionally tend to spend less time outdoors and eat less, compounding the risk. Low vitamin D in the elderly is directly associated with falls and hip fractures — a major cause of morbidity and mortality.

3.4 Individuals with Darker Skin

Melanin in the skin absorbs UVB radiation and reduces vitamin D synthesis. People with darker skin require substantially longer sun exposure to produce the same amount of vitamin D as those with lighter skin. At higher latitudes, this puts darker-skinned individuals at significantly higher risk.

3.5 People with Obesity

Vitamin D is fat-soluble and distributes into adipose tissue. Higher body fat effectively dilutes circulating 25(OH)D levels. Research consistently shows an inverse relationship between body mass index (BMI) and blood vitamin D levels.

3.6 Those with Malabsorption Conditions

Conditions that impair fat absorption — Crohn's disease, celiac disease, gastric bypass surgery, cystic fibrosis — also impair absorption of fat-soluble vitamins including vitamin D. Certain medications (anticonvulsants, corticosteroids, some cholesterol-lowering drugs) can also interfere with vitamin D metabolism.

3.7 Exclusively Breastfed Infants

Human breast milk typically contains insufficient vitamin D. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends 400 IU/day of vitamin D supplementation starting shortly after birth for breastfed infants. Formula is typically fortified and usually provides adequate amounts.

4. Causes of Vitamin D Deficiency

4.1 Insufficient Sunlight Exposure

This is the primary cause for most people. UVB radiation triggers vitamin D synthesis in the skin, but many factors limit this: latitude, season, cloud cover, air pollution, glass windows (which block UVB), clothing, and sunscreen use. An SPF 15 sunscreen reduces UVB transmission by more than 90%, although the actual impact on vitamin D status in practical use is debated.

4.2 Low Dietary Intake

Foods naturally rich in vitamin D are limited: fatty fish (salmon, herring, mackerel, tuna), egg yolks, and UV-exposed mushrooms. Fortified foods — milk, plant-based milks, cereals, orange juice — vary widely by country and product. Strict vegan diets avoiding fortified foods are at particular risk. Food alone rarely provides adequate vitamin D without either sun exposure or supplementation.

4.3 Impaired Absorption or Metabolism

Fat malabsorption conditions reduce absorption of dietary vitamin D. Liver dysfunction impairs conversion to 25(OH)D (the storage form). Kidney disease impairs conversion to active calcitriol. In these cases, deficiency may persist even with seemingly adequate intake.

5. Blood Testing — 25(OH)D Reference Ranges

The standard test for vitamin D status is serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], also written as calcidiol. This test measures the body's primary storage form of vitamin D, which reflects both sun exposure and dietary intake over weeks to months. Results are reported in ng/mL (U.S.) or nmol/L (Europe and most other countries).

6. How to Correct and Maintain Adequate Vitamin D

6.1 Sunlight Exposure

Exposing the arms, legs, and face to midday UVB sunlight (roughly 10 a.m. to 2 p.m.) for 15–30 minutes on clear days generates significant vitamin D. After this period, applying sunscreen or seeking shade reduces UV damage risk without substantially impairing synthesis that has already occurred. Indoor sunlight through windows provides no UVB and therefore produces no vitamin D.

6.2 Dietary Sources

Key food sources include:

  • Wild-caught salmon: approximately 600–1,000 IU per 100 g
  • Herring, mackerel, tuna: approximately 200–600 IU per 100 g
  • Fortified cow's milk or plant milk: approximately 100–120 IU per 240 mL
  • Egg yolk: approximately 40 IU per egg
  • UV-exposed mushrooms: approximately 400–450 IU per 100 g
  • Fortified cereals and orange juice: varies by product

Diet alone is rarely sufficient to reach optimal vitamin D levels, especially for those in high-risk groups or living at northern latitudes.

6.3 Supplements

Vitamin D supplements come primarily in two forms: D3 (cholecalciferol, from animal sources) and D2 (ergocalciferol, from plant/fungal sources). D3 is generally considered more effective at raising and sustaining blood levels, but D2 is functionally similar and suitable for vegans.

For deficiency correction, physicians commonly prescribe higher doses — often 50,000 IU once weekly for 8–12 weeks — followed by maintenance dosing. This should only be done under medical supervision with blood monitoring. For general prevention in otherwise healthy adults, 1,000–2,000 IU daily is widely used and remains within the safe upper limit.

Vitamin D is fat-soluble and absorbs better when taken with a meal containing fat. Adequate magnesium is also important as a cofactor in vitamin D metabolism.

6.4 Toxicity — When Too Much Becomes Harmful

Unlike water-soluble vitamins, excess vitamin D accumulates in body fat and can reach toxic levels. Vitamin D toxicity causes hypercalcemia (elevated blood calcium), which produces nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, excessive thirst, frequent urination, weakness, and kidney stones. Chronic hypercalcemia can damage kidneys and blood vessels. Toxicity is caused by supplement over-use, not by sunlight or natural food sources. Doses consistently exceeding 4,000 IU per day without medical oversight carry risk and should be avoided.

7. Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Should I test my vitamin D levels even without symptoms?

Routine screening in the general population is not universally recommended by all health authorities, as evidence that treating asymptomatic deficiency prevents disease outcomes is still being evaluated. However, testing is appropriate for high-risk individuals: those with malabsorption disorders, older adults, people with limited sun exposure, those on interfering medications, and those with unexplained bone pain or fatigue. Discuss with your healthcare provider whether testing makes sense for your situation.

Q. Do I need to take calcium with vitamin D?

Vitamin D enhances calcium absorption, so the two nutrients work synergistically. However, calcium supplements carry their own considerations — some research suggests high-dose supplemental calcium (not dietary calcium) may be associated with cardiovascular risk. The general approach is to prioritize dietary calcium intake from foods (dairy, leafy greens, fortified foods) and supplement only the remaining gap if needed. Your doctor or registered dietitian can help determine what is appropriate for you.

Q. How long does it take for vitamin D supplements to work?

Blood 25(OH)D levels rise gradually over weeks to months with supplementation. The time to reach sufficiency depends on baseline levels, dose, body weight, and individual metabolism. It is common practice to recheck blood levels 8–12 weeks after starting supplementation to assess the response and adjust the dose if needed.

Q. Is vitamin D3 or D2 better?

D3 (cholecalciferol) is generally more effective at raising blood 25(OH)D levels compared to D2 (ergocalciferol) at equivalent doses. D3 is derived from animal sources (lanolin). D2 is derived from yeast or fungal sources and is suitable for vegans. Both are used in clinical practice; D2 at higher doses can achieve similar outcomes. For routine supplementation, D3 is the most commonly recommended form.
