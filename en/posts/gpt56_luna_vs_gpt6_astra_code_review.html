This article is based on the figures in a measurement report published on 15 September 2026 by Entelligence AI, a code review tool company. No figure absent from the original is invented; estimates derived from two published values are marked as derived. Model prices are the OpenAI list prices the report cites.

What was measured — 50 public pull requests and two models

The first question a team faces after adopting AI code review is which model to run it on. The flagship is expensive; the budget tier is cheap. Vendor benchmarks do not tell you the quality gap between them, because vendor score tables do not measure the task of reviewing. The measurement Entelligence AI published on 15 September 2026 aims squarely at that gap. It was written by Aditya Jha of the same company, and its character is clear: a company that builds a code review tool running an experiment in its own product domain.

The subjects are the two ends of OpenAI's line. GPT-5.6 Luna is the cheapest tier currently offered, at $0.20 per million input tokens and $1.20 per million output tokens. GPT-6 Astra is the flagship at $10 and $50. That is a 50x gap on input and roughly 42x on output. Those are multiples of list price, though; the real cost multiple depends on how many tokens each model uses — in this measurement it was about 28x per review (section 5).

The material is 50 real pull requests from public repositories: ten each from Cal.com, Sentry, Discourse, Keycloak and Grafana, collected under an organization named AI-Code-Review-Evals. Each PR was reviewed by both models, the findings were verified, and only verified bugs were counted. Astra took part in the verification alongside humans — a point revisited below as a limitation.

The results in one table — bugs, precision, cost, speed

Gathered into one table, the report's figures look like this. The two models shared 44 bugs, Luna alone found 25, Astra alone found 48, so the union is 117.

The author's one-line summary is "Luna found 75% as many verified bugs as Astra for 3.6% of the money." Dividing 69 by 92 gives 75%, and $0.20 by $5.66 gives 3.5%, so the sentence matches the table. The figure for running both — 117 bugs for $5.86 — comes from the same table. Everything up to here is what the report states; everything below is interpretation of how to read those numbers.

The composition of the union deserves a look of its own. 44 bugs were caught by both models, 48 by Astra alone and 25 by Luna alone. That Astra missed 25 breaks the assumption that one expensive model is enough; that Luna alone would miss 48 breaks the assumption that the cheap model can substitute. The two sets disagree on more than half of their contents, and that is the information to read from this table before any ranking.

What a 22-point precision gap means in practice

The first number to look at is not the bug count but the precision. Only 74% of Luna's findings passed verification: for every four it raised, one was wrong. Astra's 96% means one in twenty-five. Deriving the total number of findings from verified bugs and precision, Luna raised about 93 findings of which 24 were wrong, and Astra about 96 of which 4 were wrong. The two models raised a similar volume of findings, but the amount of useless material inside it differs sixfold.

The cost of a wrong finding does not appear on the model price list. People pay it. That is why the GeekNews discussion of this article produced the reaction that a 22-point gap is felt heavily in practice. The cost that false positives leave with a team splits into four parts.

  • Verification time. The reviewer reads the finding, opens the code and confirms it is wrong. Even a few minutes per finding is half a person-day across 24 of them.
  • Erosion of trust. Once wrong findings recur, reviewers start skimming past AI comments, and from then on the correct findings are ignored with them. A low-precision tool disarms itself.
  • The author's defence cost. The PR author replies to and explains away the wrong findings. In many teams one extra review round-trip delays a merge by a day.
  • Burial of the real bugs. With 69 real findings mixed among 24 false ones, separating the two becomes the review.

So the cheap model's "3.6% of the cost" is a statement about the API invoice, while the human time created by the precision gap accrues outside that invoice. What this measurement shows is not that the cheap model is bad, but that a cost comparison that leaves out human verification time is not a comparison at all.

Attaching a scale shows how large that difference is. Carrying the false-positive rate derived from the 50 PRs over to 2,000 PRs a month, Luna would produce roughly 960 wrong findings a month and Astra roughly 160. Assuming three minutes to check and close each one, that is 48 hours against 8. The 40-hour gap turns the API price difference ($218) into one person's weekly wage. This is, of course, an assumption that transplants the ratio from 50 public-repository PRs, and a team's own values will differ. The direction is nonetheless clear: precision is a cost line, not a quality line.

The gap on security and concurrency bugs

Luna's 75% relative score on total bugs collapses when narrowed to security bugs. Of 24 verified security bugs, Luna found 9 and Astra found 19 — 37.5% against 79.2%. The author writes that Luna also fell behind on concurrency problems and was "substantially weaker" on security vulnerabilities. The missing 25% is not spread at random; it is concentrated in the most expensive class of bug.

The report does not explain why the distribution looks like this, but the nature of review work suggests some of it.

  • Security bugs are often invisible inside a diff. A missing input check or an absent authorization step is a problem of what is not there, which is hard to catch from changed lines alone. The stronger model differs in the breadth of assumptions it makes.
  • Concurrency bugs require holding two or more places in mind at once. Lock ordering and read-write races on shared state are not decided within one hunk.
  • The cheap model is good at surface patterns. Missing null checks, unused return values, near-typos — it catches these well, and because such bugs make up a large share of the total, the 75% headline emerges.
  • So the file matters before the model does. Sending changes to authentication, cryptography, serialization, input parsing and concurrency paths to the side with higher precision and higher security recall is the most direct rule this measurement yields.

The cost structure — $0.0041 versus $0.113 per review

Per review, Luna cost $0.0041 and Astra $0.113, roughly 28x. The multiple is smaller than the list-price ratio (50x on input, 42x on output) because Astra uses far fewer output tokens. Converted to cost per verified bug it becomes $0.0030 against $0.061, a factor of 20 — because verified bugs diverge 92 to 69, the per-bug multiple (20x) is smaller than the per-review multiple (28x).

Scaling it makes the numbers tangible. For an organization reviewing 2,000 PRs a month, the derived monthly cost is about $8 for Luna, about $226 for Astra, and about $234 for both. Either is less than a single reviewer's daily wage. At this scale the model fee is not the decision variable. The variables are the human time consumed by false positives and the cost a missed security bug will exact later.

  • "75% for 3.6% of the money" is a statement about totals. Luna caught only 9 of the 24 verified security bugs against Astra's 19, so what was traded for the 96.4% saving is not the average bug but the most expensive one.
  • Running both costs 3.5% more than Astra alone. $5.66 becomes $5.86, and in exchange the 25 bugs only Luna found are added. The range where parallel execution makes sense is wide.
  • Cost per bug varies by team. These are values for 50 public-repository PRs. Twenty PRs from your own repository will yield different precision and a different cost per bug.

2,104 versus 688 output tokens — the cheap model talks more

A figure that is easy to overlook in the table but touches daily practice directly is output tokens. Luna spent an average of 2,104 tokens per review; Astra spent 688. The cheap model says three times as much. Yet the times were 23 seconds for Luna and 36 for Astra — the flagship is slower. Producing less while taking longer means thinking more before writing, and the 96% precision can be read as the result.

  • The reviewer reads three times as much. Longer findings cost human reading time and raise the burden of picking the wrong ones out. Output tokens are the reader's cost.
  • In this measurement the more talkative model was the less accurate one. That cannot be generalized, but it is clear that the length of a finding must not be read as a signal of its quality.
  • Latency decides the mode of use. Both 23 and 36 seconds are too long to wait for synchronously when opening a PR. With either model, the review belongs on the PR-creation event, run asynchronously in advance.

Running asynchronously means the review arrives on the PR before the human does, and from that moment the length and precision of the findings set the first impression. A PR that opens with a 2,000-token list of findings and one that opens with 700 terse tokens are read in different frames of mind. That is why, when choosing a tool, "how many does it find" and "how much does it cost a person to read it" belong in the same table — and this measurement is close to the first case that puts both columns there.

Four limits of this measurement

The report records its own limits. They are not there to discount the result but to mark how far it can be carried.

  • One run per PR. Only ten were re-run, and on those Astra re-found 10 of 15 bugs every time while Luna re-found only 7. A good part of a single-run score sits inside the variance; 69 and 92 would move on another run.
  • Astra was both judge and contestant. Astra took part in verifying findings. There is a structural chance that Astra passes its own findings, and the 96% precision stands on that condition.
  • Every PR predates the training cutoffs. These are PRs from public repositories, so they may have been in both models' training data. This design cannot distinguish finding a bug from remembering where one was.
  • Only the diff was provided. The review saw the change without the repository. That is why the discussion included the experience that diff-only review performed poorly, and with context both models' numbers would change.
  • Reproducibility materials are still an open request. An issue on the measurement repository (Entelligence-AI/code_review_evals) notes that the link to the Luna versus Astra materials is missing. No third-party reproduction is among the references.

Applying it to a team — routing rules and a verification loop

What can safely be taken from this measurement is not a ranking but a structure: the cheap model catches surface bugs broadly, the expensive one catches security and concurrency bugs precisely. Assigning the two by file path and change type is the most direct application.

Nature of the changeAssignmentBasis in this measurement
Docs, tests, config files, simple feature additionsBudget model (Luna class)High surface-bug recall at $0.0041 per review
Authentication, cryptography, sessions, input parsing, serializationStrong model (Astra class)19 versus 9 of 24 security bugs
Concurrency paths — locks, queues, schedulersStrong modelThe concurrency weakness the author reports for Luna
Final review before a releaseRun both117 bugs for $5.86, against a single-model maximum of 92
CI merge gateNeither model as an automatic blocker74% and 96% precision both require human confirmation
  • Do not block in CI. Even 96% is wrong once in twenty-five. Post findings as review comments; a person makes the merge decision.
  • Provide repository context. Diff-only input is both this measurement's weakness and a common default in practice. Include related files and call paths and both models perform differently.
  • Measure twenty PRs from your own repository first. Precision and cost per bug differ by codebase. Do not transplant public-repository numbers; run a small sample the same way and get your own.
  • Record cost per verified bug. That, not cost per review, is the metric for choosing a model. Log the human time spent on false positives next to it and the real cost appears.
  • Re-run to see the variance. Run the same PR two or three times and separate the findings that appear every time from the ones that waver. Mark the wavering ones as lower confidence.

One principle runs through all five. The model produces findings; the team keeps the record of verifying them. After a month of noting which findings were right, which were wrong, and which files needed the strong model, the team has a precision table of its own. That table is what makes it possible, when the next model arrives — and the two models in this article will be a generation behind within months — to decide whether to switch on your own data rather than on a vendor announcement. The point of reading a measurement is not to copy its conclusion but to become able to repeat it on your own team.

What could not be verified

  • The criteria for selecting the 50 PRs. Whether they were random or chosen for known bugs is not in the original.
  • The number of human verifiers, the judging procedure, and the size of Astra's influence on the verdicts.
  • The run configuration of the two models. Reasoning effort and temperature are not stated.
  • Input tokens per review. Only output tokens are published, so why the cost multiple settles at 28x cannot be fully reconstructed.
  • Reproducibility materials. An open issue notes the link is missing from the repository, so no third-party reproduction is confirmed.
  • Comparison with other vendors' models. The measurement covers two OpenAI models only.
