This article centers on the Chrome for Developers post "Shipping JPEG XL in Chrome" (Luca Versari, Moritz Firsching and Philip Jägenstedt, published October 6, 2026) and cross-reads it against the Chromium Dash schedule, Can I use, MDN and the Interop 2026 JPEG XL Investigation repository. Browser support states are the values each source lists, and each source has its own reference date.

The Announcement — JPEG XL Decoding from Chrome 155

The Chrome for Developers blog announced that Chrome is shipping decoding support for the JPEG XL (.jxl) image format starting from Chrome 155. The post introduces JPEG XL as a next-generation image format designed to meet the needs of modern web developers and photographers, and says it offers 30-50% better compression than JPEG, lossless compression, built-in HDR support, lossless JPEG transcoding and more.

The recommendation the blog gives developers fits in one sentence: to get the best results, try both AVIF and JPEG XL. The Chrome team then writes that it expects JPEG XL to be most helpful for high-fidelity or lossless compression, especially of photographic images, or in cases in which fine-grained progressive decoding is preferred. Because the wording is "expects," this is the Chrome team's outlook and not a measured result.

The blog also states how the post is organized: why JPEG XL was brought to Chrome, how Rust was used to ensure memory safety first, the extensive performance work that makes the decoder fast, and what the journey says about developer feedback and the web standards ecosystem. Its closing section, titled "Try it out," says that with JPEG XL officially landing in Chrome the web becomes faster, richer and safer. It encourages developers, content creators and platform owners to start using .jxl images and animations in their pipelines, and asks readers to file bugs they find. That closing sentence is the blog's own assessment of the benefit, and it is not backed by a figure in the post.

Three kinds of statement are mixed in the post, and they carry different weight. The release fact ("shipping decoding support starting from Chrome 155") can be checked against a schedule. The format claims ("30-50% better compression than JPEG") come from the Chrome team and are given without conditions. The engineering account (a pure Rust decoder, SIMD abstraction, fuzzing and AI review) describes what the team says it did. The sections below keep these three apart so that each can be weighed on its own.

What "From 155" Covers — The Schedule and Support Entries

Which date and which environments "starting from Chrome 155" refers to is not fixed by the blog text alone. The post gives a version number and does not give a date, a flag state or a per-platform scope. Public data fills in part of the picture from three directions.

  • The Chromium Dash milestone schedule. For 155 it lists branch_point 2026-09-14, earliest_beta 2026-09-16, early_stable 2026-09-23, stable_date 2026-10-06 and the next stable_refresh 2026-10-13. The stable_date of the preceding milestone, 154, is 2026-09-22. On the schedule, the stable channel date is therefore the same day as the blog post.
  • The Chrome column on Can I use. Chrome 152, 153 and 154 are marked as disabled by default with a note that the feature can be enabled at chrome://flags/#enable-jxl-image-format, and 155 onward is marked as supported (y). In other words, a feature that sat behind a flag in earlier versions is marked as supported without a flag from 155. The Chrome entries on Can I use for 156 and 157 are also marked as supported (y), which matches a feature that stays on after its first stable release.
  • MDN wording. MDN's image types guide says Chrome 145 and later supports JPEG XL behind the #enable-jxl-image-format flag. That sentence reflects the situation before the 155 announcement and should not be read as the current state.
  • Android scope. The blog does not distinguish platforms. The Chrome for Android column on Can I use ends at 154, marked as not supported and disabled by default, so no 155 entry exists. Whether desktop and Android shipped together cannot be settled from these sources.

JPEG XL Characteristics — The Blog Claim and MDN

The performance figure for JPEG XL sits in a single sentence of the Chrome blog. "30-50% better compression than JPEG" is a sentence written in the Chrome for Developers blog, and the comparison conditions, such as image type, quality metric and measurement method, are not presented in the post. The figure is best read as the blog's claim rather than generalized without conditions. The same sentence lists lossless compression, built-in HDR support and lossless JPEG transcoding as further features, and none of those is accompanied by a number either.

The feature scope of the format, on the other hand, is summarized separately by MDN. MDN describes JPEG XL as a royalty-free raster image format standardized as ISO/IEC 18181 that supports lossy and lossless compression, progressive decoding, high bit depths, wide color gamuts, HDR, transparency and animation, and can losslessly transcode existing JPEG images so that the original JPEG file can be reconstructed.

  • How the blog positions the format. The Chrome team presents JPEG XL not as a single replacement for JPEG but as an option to try alongside AVIF. The blog contains no statement that JPEG XL is superior to AVIF.
  • MDN's general advice. MDN recommends WebP or AVIF first for raster images and says to also consider JPEG XL for large, high-resolution raster images.
  • By image type. MDN recommends lossless encoding for screenshots, diagrams, logos and line art, and says photographs and other continuous-tone images can usually tolerate lossy compression. This is general guidance about image types and not about any one format.

The Rust Decoder jxl-rs — Safety First

The blog's second section is titled "Safety first: Reimplementing the decoder in Rust (jxl-rs)." It describes image decoders as one of the most critical and targeted attack surfaces in any modern web browser. Decoders process complex, untrusted binary structures directly from the network inside the renderer process, and the post says decoders written in memory-unsafe languages like C++ have historically been prone to vulnerabilities such as out-of-bounds reads, heap overflows and use-after-free bugs.

  • Security model. According to the blog, Chrome's security model relies on sandboxing and defense-in-depth, guided by the rule of two. The blog adds that sandboxing is a secondary layer of defense.
  • The choice. To eliminate those risks at the source, the blog says Chrome integrated jxl-rs, a pure Rust implementation of the JPEG XL decoder.
  • What the Rule of 2 says. The Chromium document linked from the blog describes a rule to pick no more than two of three properties: untrustworthy inputs, an unsafe implementation language, and high privilege. Since an image decoder receives untrustworthy input, the reading is that it should avoid at least one of the other two.
  • Verification. The blog says jxl-rs was verified with several state-of-the-art techniques, including fuzzing and AI review of the code, and that no memory safety bugs were found throughout the entire implementation history. This is the Chrome team's own report and not the result of a third-party audit, and the blog presents it as further validation of what Rust brings to memory safety.

Designing for Speed — target_feature_11 and jxl_simd

The blog is explicit that safety alone does not make adoption easy. It says a memory-safe decoder that is approximately as fast as the best non-memory-safe alternative is a much more obvious choice than one with a significant performance compromise. That sentence states a design principle, and the post body does not present a measured speed comparison between jxl-rs and libjxl. The same section is titled "Design for speed, without compromising safety," which frames speed and memory safety as goals to be met together instead of as a trade-off the team chose between.

  • Using SIMD safely. The blog says a fundamental part of the performance of modern codecs is making full use of the SIMD hardware on modern devices, and that doing so safely required the target_feature_11 Rust feature to be stabilized, which allowed SIMD instructions to be used without requiring unsafe code.
  • The Rust side of the record. The rust-lang/rust pull request 134090 linked from the blog is titled "Stabilize target_feature_11," and GitHub shows it as closed on 2025-02-13.
  • jxl_simd. The next step was a SIMD abstraction layer, jxl_simd, inspired by the C++ Highway library. The blog explains that Highway was itself originally developed for libjxl, the C++ reference implementation of JPEG XL.
  • The result. Together, the blog says, these developments allowed writing a multi-platform library that does not compromise on SIMD performance optimizations while restricting unsafe operations to a small number of highly-vetted locations.
  • Inheriting from libjxl. Performance optimizations in jxl-rs build on those in libjxl. They include a generic processing pipeline for steps that cross region borders, while minimizing data copies to maximize hardware performance.
  • Tracking. The blog says the performance of the Rust reimplementation across different hardware platforms has been tracked on the jxl-rs performance dashboard.

Developer Feedback and the Interop 2026 JPEG XL Investigation

The last body section of the blog covers the basis of the decision. The Chrome team says it considers web developer feedback from a wide range of channels such as bugs, surveys, the Developer Signals Project and the Interop Project. The decision to ship JPEG XL was based on consistent feedback and requests from web developers, most visible in the Interop process, where it was a popular proposal in 2026 and several years prior.

For interoperability, the blog says the Chrome team participated in the Interop 2026 JPEG XL Investigation. The aim is to ensure there is test coverage for all of JPEG XL's features in browsers and that those tests pass in Chrome. The investigation's repository (web-platform-tests/interop-jpegxl) lists its scope as follows.

  • Color and bit depth. It lists 8, 10, 12 and 16-bit depths, wide gamuts such as Display P3, Adobe RGB, Rec.2020 and ProPhoto RGB, exotic uses of ICC profiles, and CMYK conversion as test targets.
  • HDR. It lists tone mapping (PQ/HLG) and gain maps (ISO 21496-1).
  • Coding modes. VarDCT at the largest and smallest block sizes, Modular mode in both lossy and lossless forms, and alpha channels with both VarDCT and Modular mode are to be tested.
  • Progressive rendering. The scope covers aligning on how browsers should render just the DC data, and whether to support progressive_dc, which is downsampled even further.
  • Other items. Lossless transcoded JPEG, orientation (the codestream orientation is respected and EXIF orientation is ignored), animations, layers and JPEG XL art made from minimal modular-mode files are also in scope.
  • Web integration points. img, picture and source, video poster, input type=image, embed, object and iframe, link rel=preload as=image, SVG image, and the CSS properties background-image, content, image-set() and list-style-image are listed for testing.

Per-Browser Support Entries on Can I use

The Can I use JPEG XL entry carries markings from after Chrome 155 support. The table below transcribes the columns and notes of that entry, and the markings follow the site's data at the time. Can I use classifies the status of this format as "other."

MDN's image types guide records a similar picture for Safari: Safari 17 and later supports JPEG XL, and Safari does not support progressive download of JPEG XL files, rendering them only after the complete download. Both sources agree that Safari does not decode progressively. This means the progressive-decoding benefit the blog highlights may show up differently across browsers. The Can I use notes also distinguish the two kinds of limitation: note 4 says only still images are supported and animated image sequences are not, and note 5 says partial support refers to not supporting progressive decoding. A page that depends on animation or on progressive display therefore needs to check each target browser separately, because the blog speaks only for Chrome.

Fallback Markup and Checks Before Adoption

MDN says to provide an alternative format such as AVIF, WebP or JPEG with the picture element when using JPEG XL, and explains that the standard img element does not support compatibility fallbacks while the picture element does. Below is the example from the "Providing image fallbacks" section of the MDN image types guide, unchanged. It prefers SVG and falls back through WebP, PNG and GIF, and MDN adds that you can specify as many source elements as you wish but typically two or three is all you need.

<picture>
                      <source srcset="diagram.svg" type="image/svg+xml" />
                      <source srcset="diagram.webp" type="image/webp" />
                      <source srcset="diagram.png" type="image/png" />
                      <img
                        src="diagram.gif"
                        width="620"
                        height="540"
                        alt="Diagram showing the data channels" />
                    </picture>

How to apply this structure to JPEG XL is interpretation. In the example the first source has the highest priority, so putting a source whose type is image/jxl first makes the browser try JPEG XL first; that follows from the structure MDN describes. The MDN document does not contain a sample that includes JPEG XL.

  • Fallback order. The sources inside a picture element are evaluated from top to bottom, so the priority among image formats depends on the goals of the service. The blog only advises trying both AVIF and JPEG XL.
  • Image type. The conditions under which the blog sees JPEG XL as most helpful are photographs that need high-fidelity or lossless compression and fine-grained progressive decoding. The blog says nothing about superiority for other image types.
  • Basics. The basics of resolution, compression quality and format conversion are covered in the image compression primer, so they are not repeated here.

What Could Not Be Confirmed

  • Comparison conditions. The image types, quality metric and measurement method behind "30-50% better compression than JPEG" are not in the blog.
  • Android status. Whether JPEG XL is enabled by default in the Android build of Chrome 155 cannot be settled from the blog or Can I use.
  • Speed figures. Absolute numbers comparing jxl-rs speed with libjxl or other formats are not in the post body; only a link to the performance dashboard is given.
  • Encoding plans. The blog has no statement on built-in encoding or on producing .jxl through Canvas or WebCodecs.
  • Firefox 158 release date. Can I use marks Firefox 158 as supported, but the stable release date of that version cannot be settled from these sources.
  • Earlier support history. The blog has no account of Chrome's earlier JPEG XL support history, so it is not covered.
