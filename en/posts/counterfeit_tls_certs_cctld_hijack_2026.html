This post separates, by source, the October 6, 2026 Google Chrome security blog post and the Ars Technica article published the same day. Every fact is attributed in the form “Google said” or “Ars reported”, and nothing that neither source states is included.

Overview: who said what

On October 6, 2026, Google’s Chrome Secure Web and Networking Team published “Chrome's Response to Recent ccTLD Registry Hijacks”. It says that in the previous week the team became aware of a series of domain hijacks in the .gh (Ghana), .sl (Sierra Leone) and .as (American Samoa) country-code top-level namespaces (ccTLDs). The same day, Dan Goodin of Ars Technica reported the story under the headline “Hackers obtain counterfeit TLS certificates for Google and other large services”. The article’s subhead says that the compromise of three domain registries let hackers walk off with unauthorized certificates.

Almost all of the facts in this episode come from Google’s statement. Ars writes that Google said it on Tuesday, repeats the content, and adds background on what TLS certificates do and a 2011 precedent. Ars’s headline uses “counterfeit”, while Google’s sentences use “unauthorized”. The wording used here follows Google and says unauthorized issuance.

  • Announcing party. The Chrome Secure Web and Networking Team, in a post dated October 6, 2026.
  • Report. Ars Technica, by Dan Goodin (Senior Security Editor), published the same day.
  • When Google learned of it. Google wrote only that it became aware “last week”, and gave no date for when the compromise began or how long it lasted.
  • What is left out. Neither source describes the attackers’ identity, motive or national attribution, and this post does not address them either.

  • Outreach. Google said that where possible it reached out to impacted organizations to alert them to its findings and actions.

What Ars added: changed delegations and passing validation

How the attackers got through validation is summarized in two sentences of the Ars article. Ars explains that with control of the three ccTLDs the attackers were able to change the IP addresses of a selected list of websites, and that once they could send and receive traffic on those sites they modified authoritative DNS records and nameserver delegations for selected domains, which let them pass industry validation checks that require an applicant to prove control of the domain. The Google post does not spell this path out and says only that authoritative DNS records were modified and unauthorized certificates were obtained.

  • Level of detail in Ars. The article contains no step-by-step procedure and no tools, and this post does not go beyond that level.
  • Meaning of “control”. Ars uses “registries” in the subhead and “hijacked three top-level domains” in the text. How the attackers obtained the ability to change delegation data is in neither source.
  • Ars’s judgment sentence. Ars writes that with all known unauthorized certificates now blocked the risk is mitigated, but that, as Google noted, any certificates that remain undiscovered pose a threat.

Issuers that followed the rules: what domain control validation trusts

The CA/Browser Forum Baseline Requirements set out in section 3.2.2.4, “Validation of Domain Authorization or Control”, how a CA validates that an applicant owns or controls a domain. The section corresponds to the domain control validation (DCV) that Google mentions (interpretation). The table below summarizes the relevant parts of that document. Neither source says which method was used in this incident.

Provision What the document says Relation to this incident
3.2.2.4.7 DNS Change Confirms control over the Authorization Domain Name by confirming the presence of a Random Value or Request Token in a DNS CNAME, TXT or CAA record returned in a query —
3.2.2.9 Multi-Perspective Issuance Corroboration Corroborates, from other network perspectives and before issuance, the domain validation and CAA determinations made by the primary perspective. The document says this can improve protection against equally-specific prefix BGP attacks or hijacks Not mentioned in either source
DNSSEC validation An item requiring the primary network perspective to perform DNSSEC validation on all DNS queries used for domain authorization or control is listed with a compliance date of 2026-03-15 Not mentioned in either source
Reuse of validation data The maximum reuse period for domain name and IP address validation data shrinks on a schedule (next section) Background to Google’s advice (CAA section)
  • What validation depends on. The DNS Change definition makes the result of a DNS query the evidence of validation. If the query result is the answer from the properly delegated server, the CA decides as the rules say (interpretation).
  • Google’s summary. Google wrote that, due to the nature of the attacks, it has no reason to believe the CAs did anything wrong. That issuance following the rules and unauthorized issuance could both hold is the shape of this report (interpretation).
  • Purpose of multi-perspective checks. The purpose the document states is protection against BGP attacks. Neither source says what part multi-perspective checks or DNSSEC played in this incident.

Chrome blocking and revocation through CAs

Google said that, as part of its usual incident response process, it immediately blocked the use of unauthorized certificates for Google properties in Chrome via CRLSets. It then worked with the issuing CAs to have the certificates revoked, to protect users of clients other than Chrome. After the initial mitigation, CT log data revealed additional organizations believed to have been impacted by the same attacks, and Google says it proactively blocked those certificates in Chrome so that users of those sites were kept safe as soon as possible.

  • Scope of the blocking. Google described CRLSets blocking for the certificates for Google properties, and for the other organizations’ certificates wrote only that it blocked them in Chrome. The sentence does not say whether CRLSets was used for the latter.
  • Scope of revocation. In the Google post, revocation through CAs is described alongside the certificates for Google properties. Whether the other organizations’ certificates were revoked is in neither source.
  • Background from Ars. Ars writes that the process for officially revoking certificates is slow and cumbersome, so browser makers have devised quicker methods to block specific certificates at the browser level.
  • Chrome users. Google said Chrome users do not need to take any action to be protected.

Google’s warning not to rely on browser-side blocking

At the start of its advice to domain owners, Google wrote the following. Chrome took steps to identify and block suspected unauthorized certificates across the affected ccTLDs, but browser-side intervention should not be relied on to protect users. Because of the complexity of DNS hijacks Google cannot guarantee that its analysis identified every affected domain, nor do Chrome interventions reliably protect non-Chrome users. As the reason for the advice, Google said domain owners are in the best position to know which certificates and CAs are authorized for their namespaces.

  • Limit of the guarantee. The original is “we cannot guarantee that our analysis identified every affected domain”. Ars relayed the same point, that undiscovered certificates would remain a threat.
  • Non-Chrome users. The original is “nor do Chrome interventions reliably protect non-Chrome users”. How other browsers responded is in neither source.
  • Who the warning is for. The sentence is addressed to domain owners and organizations, not to ordinary users.

Certificate Transparency log monitoring

Google’s first recommendation is to monitor Certificate Transparency continuously for all of one’s domains. Google’s explanation is that every trusted-by-default certificate relied upon by Chrome must be disclosed in public CT logs, so monitoring the logs gives a near real-time alert whenever a certificate is issued for the owner’s domains. Monitoring should cover the entire domain portfolio, including parked or regional ccTLD properties, and anyone operating a domain in .gh, .sl or .as should review recent CT log entries for unexpected issuance.

  • How the CT project describes it. The Certificate Transparency site explains that logs are built on Merkle trees, are publicly verifiable and append-only, and calls them “tamper-proof”. Domain owners, browsers and others can analyze and monitor logs to see which CAs issued which certificates, when, and for which domains.
  • Role of monitors. The same site says that if you subscribe to a CT monitor for your domain, you get updates when precertificates and certificates for those domains are included in any of the logs that monitor checks.
  • Why CT exists. The site says that before CT there could be a significant time lag between a certificate being wrongly issued and a CA doing something about it.
  • Use in this incident. Google said that after its initial mitigation, CT log data revealed other impacted organizations.

CAA records and reuse of validation data

Google’s second recommendation is to publish restrictive CAA records with ACME account bindings. Google explains CAA as DNS records through which a domain owner declares which CAs may issue certificates. It says CAA cannot prevent issuance during an active DNS hijack, but that it provides a critical safeguard after DNS control is restored and can prevent some routing-based and HTTP attacks entirely. The reason is that CAs are permitted to cache and reuse completed domain control validation checks for later issuance, and restoring a restrictive CAA policy that limits issuance to specific accounts and validation methods prevents an attacker from using cached validation state to mint new certificates after a hijack ends.

  • Limit of CAA. The original is “CAA can not prevent certificate issuance during an active DNS hijack”.
  • Long-term work Google named. Google said it is committed to improving the HTTPS ecosystem by reducing certificate validity and DCV reuse, through the Chrome Root Program and the new Chrome Quantum-resistant Root Program.
  • The schedule in the Baseline Requirements (reuse of validation data). The compliance table lists the maximum reuse period of domain name and IP address validation data as 200 days from 2026-03-15, 100 days from 2027-03-15 and 10 days from 2029-03-15. The maximum validity of subscriber certificates in the same table is 200 days, 100 days and 47 days.
  • Account binding rule. The same table lists, with a date of 2027-03-15, the item requiring CAs to process the accounturi and validationmethods parameters as specified in RFC 8657.
  • Scope. The schedule above is what the document lists, and neither source says what reuse period each CA applied in this incident.

The 2011 DigiNotar case and related reading

Ars says this is not the first time threat actors have obtained unauthorized certificates and cites the 2011 hack of the Netherlands-based certificate authority DigiNotar. According to Ars, that hack allowed attackers to mint counterfeit certificates for Google.com and more than 200 other high-traffic domains, and the certificates were used against at least 300,000 people with ties to Iran as they browsed the sites impersonated by the forged certificates. Ars adds that there have been many similar incidents since, most often through failures by certificate authorities but also by domain holders.

  • The difference as described. The DigiNotar case is described as a failure at a CA, while this incident is one in which Google said it has no reason to believe the CAs did anything wrong.
  • Related reading. The general point that a domain is delegated, not owned, is covered in the country-code domain risk post, the shortening of certificate lifetimes and revocation after OCSP in the post on what was learned about SSL that is now wrong, and the background on browser root stores and Certificate Transparency in the post on Korea’s GPKI root and the Firefox root store.
