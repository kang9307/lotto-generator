Introduction: the verdict lands before the reading starts

When a page opens, the eye does not start by reading. It scans. Title, the length of the first paragraph, and one picture. If that picture is a translucent brain floating under violet lighting, or the composition where circuit traces stream past somebody's face, the thumb pauses. Not because reading stopped, but because judging started.

File that reaction under taste and nothing usable survives. What is really happening is an inference. The reader reads, off that image, how much the writer spent on this page. If the image looks like it cost nothing, the parts that are not visible probably cost nothing either. That is not aesthetics. That is cost estimation.

So here is the judgment up front. There are four kinds of friction, and only one of them is an actual factual error. The other three are impressions. Refuse to separate them and you land either on the useless conclusion that AI images should never be used, or on the equally useless one that it is all subjective anyway. Both are wrong.

This piece splits those four apart, explains the structure by which trust erodes, marks the slots where generated images legitimately belong, and gives the order for deciding whether to include one. Which tools produce what is covered separately in the AI image generator comparison, and the move toward open models in the open-weights image model piece. What is on the table here is not how to make them, but whether to attach them.

1. The friction, broken into four kinds

The sentence AI images bother me contains four things of entirely different natures. Left blended, the response gets blended too.

1.1 Decoration unrelated to the body

The most common case. An article about database index tuning opens with a figure in a suit touching a hologram in front of a server rack. That picture corresponds to no sentence in the text. The reader gains zero information and the scroll gets a screen longer.

The loss has two layers. One is direct: attention was spent and nothing came back. The other is informational, and it is about the standards of the person who filled that slot. Choosing to fill an empty space with anything at all, rather than leave it empty, says something about the editing. Stock photography had the same problem, but at a different scale, because picking a stock image took time and that friction filtered a lot of it out. Generation removed the friction.

1.2 The same style, over and over

Homogeneous within one site, more homogeneous across sites. A given model in a given period has habits in lighting, saturation and composition that work like a fingerprint. That gradient where teal meets magenta. Skin rendered too smooth. Backgrounds that fall away into an unusually soft focus.

The consequence is that the image stops being specific to this page and becomes something seen everywhere. A visual element earns its keep by distinguishing the page it sits on, and once that function is gone the value does not fall to zero, it goes negative, because the image now transmits an additional fact: this person ran that pipeline too. The reason the term AI slop settled into English usage has less to do with the quality of any single image than with this sameness.

1.3 Charts that only look like they carry data

This is the one that is not about taste. Things shaped like bar charts. Axes and legends. Architecture diagrams with arrows. A generative model does not compute these. It reproduces the learned appearance of a chart, so bar heights do not match the tick marks, legend text is alphabet-shaped rather than being words, and arrows land wherever.

That is not an impression, it is showing the reader wrong values. And unlike the other three, this one spreads. A reader who notices that the numbers inside the picture do not add up starts by asking whether the numbers in the body were produced the same way. It is the one path by which a single image turns into a verdict on the factual standing of the whole article.

Worth adding: text embedded in an image is not just unreadable to people. To a machine it is pixels rather than text. How that property plays out in cost accounting is taken apart in the piece on rendering code as an image.

1.4 The classic breakdowns: hands, letters, physics

Six fingers. A smeared shop sign. Glasses whose arm passes through an ear. A shadow falling away from the light source. What defines this category is that the verdict is instant and final. The first three leave the reader suspecting. A visible breakdown converts suspicion into confirmation.

Better models made these rarer, which is not the same as solving the problem. What filled the space they left is the sameness from 1.2, so the confident verdict got replaced by a vague sense of having seen this before. From the reader's side, the wrong picture became the familiar picture.

Kind of frictionWhat the reader actually readsNatureResponse
Unrelated decorationThis slot got filled with anythingImpression, editorial standardsRemove the image
Repeated styleThis is not specific to the pageImpression, failed identityFix a style or reduce frequency
Generated chartsThe numbers do not add upFactual errorNever generate, draw it
Visible breakdownsThis is definitely generatedImpression, confirmed verdictHuman final review

Only the third row is different in kind. The rest can be tuned inside the range of editorial preference, but charts are not a tuning target, they are a prohibition. Holding that single line stops the expensive failure.

2. How trust erodes: readers estimate cost

The path from seeing an image to doubting the text is inference, not emotion. Unpacked:

  1. The reader cannot verify the accuracy of the body directly. Verifying means reading, and reading is precisely the decision being made
  2. So they look for a cheap signal: something visible immediately, requiring no domain knowledge to judge
  3. An image satisfies exactly those conditions. The verdict takes about a third of a second
  4. If little effort went into the image, they infer that the other effort on the same page by the same person was similarly little
  5. That inference decides whether to read

Step four is the load-bearing one. What gets judged is not the image's quality but its marginal cost. A photograph taken for the piece required someone to pick up a camera and go outside. A hand-drawn diagram required deciding what to draw. Even a well-chosen stock photo required the choosing. A generated image requires none of the three, which is why it functions as evidence for the hypothesis that this writer takes the cheap path when one exists.

The piece that pushed this argument back into circulation lands on exactly that point. Nelson Figueroa's AI generated images discourage me from reading your blog, posted 1 October 2024 and updated 18 February 2026, says that AI images in a blog make him wonder whether the text is AI-generated to some extent as well. He notes it is expected on corporate blogs and hits differently on an independent one, and adds that he would rather look at a crudely drawn picture.

Be clear about what that piece is. It is a personal essay. No experiment, no sample, no numbers. It records one person's reaction and presents itself as exactly that. It gets cited widely not because it has evidence but because the reaction is widely shared. So the question of how readers actually behave has to be answered elsewhere. Section 4 is that place.

3. Is that inference fair?

Here is the judgment on no effort in the image means no effort in the text. It is partly fair and low in precision. Both halves are true, so saying only one of them gets it wrong.

3.1 Where it holds

  • Same person, same page. Editorial standards tend to be consistent at the level of a person. Someone sloppy in one place is rarely fastidious in another
  • Removing friction exposes standards. Doing a hard thing well is a question of ability. How someone handles an easy thing is a question of standards, and filling an empty slot with whatever is exactly the latter
  • Chart errors really do correlate with body errors. The probability that someone who did not check the numbers in the picture checked only the numbers in the text is not high
  • Signals that cost nothing get used in bulk. And an article carrying a bulk-used signal is statistically more likely to be bulk-produced. The reader's prior is not mistaken

3.2 Where it misfires

  • It cannot tell no budget from no care. An individual who cannot afford an illustrator and picked generation over paid stock gets caught in the same net
  • It fires on good writing too. One thoughtless decorative image on a technical document that took months sets the first impression of that document. Correlation, not causation
  • The yardstick keeps moving. An image that would have been flagged as a breakdown three years ago passes now, and the styles that read as obviously generated today will be different in two years. The measuring stick itself has an expiry date
  • It collides with what is already standard. Background removal, outpainting and denoising have had generative models in them for years and nobody objects. Was generation involved stopped being a binary question some time ago

Put plainly: the reader's inference is a high-recall, low-precision filter. It catches careless work well and sweeps up innocent work with it. And that is the practical fork. Talking readers out of running the filter is not an option. The only available move is to change how images are attached so the filter does not fire.

4. What the research says and does not say

This is where the direction changes. The proposition that AI images reduce credibility is less solidly supported than it looks. The results actually flip depending on conditions.

Familiarity Matters (Eom, Middleton, Luo, Li and Brossard, 2025), published in Science Communication, ran an online survey experiment with 742 US adults randomly assigned to view Instagram posts about CRISPR gene editing. The question was what a label indicating AI involvement does to the perceived credibility of the information.

The result has two layers. First, the AI disclaimer on its own did not significantly move credibility across message frames. Second, and more usefully, the direction split by the participant's familiarity with AI. Those more familiar with AI rated AI-disclaimed posts as more credible; those less familiar rated them as less credible.

Translated into working terms: a disclosure is not a neutral device, it is a device whose sign flips with the audience. To a technical reader the label reads as honesty. To a general reader it reads as a warning sticker. The same sentence, working in opposite directions.

Adjacent work points the same way. A study of audience perceptions of AI-driven news in AI & SOCIETY reports that trust in AI-generated news sits close to neutral overall and varies sharply with demographics such as age, with younger respondents more receptive where transparency and readability were prioritised. On the preprint side, work on how the level of detail about AI use affects reader trust asks directly whether more disclosure buys more trust, and a study on willingness to read AI-generated news argues that willingness is not driven by perceived quality alone.

4.1 The gap between those studies and this topic

Honestly stated, there are three steps between that literature and the question of putting generated images on a blog.

  • The stimuli are mostly news and science information, a different context from an individual's editorial choices
  • The manipulated variable is mostly the disclosure, not the presence or style of the image
  • The measure is a credibility rating, not a behavioural metric like bounce rate or time on page

Which is why no figure of the form AI images raise bounce rate by N percent appears here. The numbers circulating are mostly one site's after-the-fact observations or citations whose source has evaporated. What this article offers instead of a number is a mechanism, and mechanisms last longer in practice.

5. AI text versus AI images

They are not the same problem. Reaction speed, mode of judgment and actual harm all differ.

AxisWhen the image is generatedWhen the body is generated
Time to verdictA third of a second, from scanningMinutes, requires reading
Skill required to judgeNone, count the fingersDomain knowledge, ear for prose
ConfidenceCertain if a breakdown is visibleAlmost always circumstantial
Model fingerprintStyle is homogeneous and obviousPrompting blurs it considerably
Actual harmUsually none, it is decorationFalse statements persist
Reader responseImmediate, affectiveDelayed, retrospective
Disclosure practicePlatform labels emergingEffectively none

The strange row is actual harm. The side that does real damage is the text, and the side readers react to first and hardest is the image. That mismatch is arguably the whole subject.

It arises for the reason already given. Readers judge what is easy to judge and infer what is not. The image is used as a proxy for the body. So it is only an image is half right. The harm from the image itself really is small. What it does is issue the verdict on the body in advance.

The structure by which one small visual inconsistency spreads into judgment of an entire product was worked out on the interface side first. The logic in why UI quality determines user trust transfers here almost unchanged. When forced to judge something unverifiable, people grab the nearest verifiable thing.

6. Where generated images legitimately belong

Ending on so do not use them would make this article wrong. The other side, stated properly: there are slots where a generated image beats the alternatives.

6.1 Concept illustration

When the thing to depict cannot be photographed. A cache hierarchy. Distributed consensus. An immune response. What used to occupy that slot was either a hand-drawn metaphor or an unrelated stock photo. A generated image is clearly better than the latter and usually worse than the former. For someone who cannot draw, it is a genuine step up.

6.2 Abstract backdrops and dividers

Category card backgrounds, list thumbnails, chapter breaks in a long piece. Slots whose purpose is not to convey information but to create visual rhythm. Whether the pixels were generated is not really the operative question here, because a gradient or a set of shapes would have done the same job.

6.3 Replacing stock photography

Paid stock always had problems. The same model appears on thousands of sites, likeness rights and usage scope have to be checked each time, and licence changes create retroactive exposure. Generated imagery swaps those problems for different ones. Training-data provenance walks in as a replacement concern, but the risk of using a real person's face without permission goes down. The call depends on the situation, but the tidy story where stock is safe and generation is risky does not hold.

6.4 Drafts and composition exploration

Using the output as an intermediate artefact rather than a deliverable. Setting direction before handing off to a designer, comparing compositions quickly, filling layout slots with placeholders. This never reaches the reader, so it sits outside the argument of this article. It is also where the most value actually shows up.

The dividing line in one sentence — does the image make a claim, or set a mood? If it makes a claim (numbers, structure, evidence) it is not a generation target. If it sets a mood (metaphor, backdrop, rhythm) generation is fine. That one line explains all four items above.

7. The order for deciding whether to include one

The step people usually get wrong is the order. Starting from should I generate an image here means walking in with the answer already fixed. The first question is whether this slot needs an image at all.

  1. Write one line describing what the reader cannot do without the image. If the line will not come, that slot does not need an image. Most candidates get filtered right here
  2. Pin the role to exactly one thing. Evidence, data, concept or mood. If it is two, it should be two images
  3. Evidence and data are excluded from generation. To show a screen, capture the real screen. To show numbers, draw the chart. No exceptions
  4. Concept and mood are where generation works. Open the tool only here
  5. If it was generated, a person looks at it once. Hands, letters, shadow direction, and anything that contradicts the body
  6. If the same style appears three times running, cut one. The repetition itself creates the signal described earlier
  7. Decide whether the caption says so. Section 4's finding, that the sign flips with the audience, applies here
  8. Record the decision. Knowing what went where and why is what keeps the standard from drifting later

7.1 When no image is the better answer

Concretely, these are slots where removing the image is almost always better.

  • A meaningless hero image at the top — it eats the entire first screen and pushes the body down. The title already does that job
  • One illustration per h2 — the regularity itself signals that slots were being filled
  • Diagram-shaped output containing text — a factual error, per 1.3
  • Generated portraits standing in for photographs — faces are what readers judge most sharply, and the cost of being caught is high
  • A picture of content already given as a table or list — the same information twice, losing on both search and accessibility
  • Imitations of brands, logos or product exteriors — if it differs from the real thing, it is misinformation

7.2 There are more substitutes than people assume

An empty slot does not have to be refilled with another image.

  • A table — the reason this article has several. Comparison reads faster as a table than as a picture
  • Code blocks and real output — the most honest visual element in technical writing
  • Actual screen captures — the one thing generation cannot imitate
  • A diagram you drew — vector editor or diagram syntax, either way. It does not have to be pretty, it has to be correct
  • Pull quotes and callouts — enough on their own where only rhythm was needed
  • White space — not filling is also a choice

8. Disclosure and provenance, as far as it is established

Just say it was generated is the right answer, but each way of saying it leaks somewhere different. Three layers.

MechanismHow it worksStrong atWhere it breaks
C2PA Content CredentialsSigned creation and edit history in the fileForgery-resistant, verifiableNothing left once metadata is stripped
Automatic platform labelsApplied by the platform on uploadVisible without the reader lookingInherits the weakness above when credential-based
Invisible watermarksA signal embedded in the pixelsComparatively survives re-encodingPer-model and per-tool, hard to verify across
A line in the captionA person writes a sentenceNo pipeline strips itAbsent if the author does not write it

8.1 Why credentials break

C2PA is the standard for attaching a signed history to an image file recording who made it with what tool and how it was edited. Cameras that sign at capture exist, editing tools chain their edits onto the record, and some platforms read and display credentials on uploaded files. On design alone it looks like the answer to provenance.

What it runs into in practice is whether the metadata survives. Plenty of pipelines discard ancillary data wholesale while a file is uploaded, resized and re-encoded. Sending it through a messaging app often drops it. And decisively, taking a screenshot of it ends the chain. A file freshly captured from pixels on a display has no way to carry the original signature.

That property makes credentials an asymmetric device: their presence is grounds for trust, their absence is grounds for nothing. Neither no credential, therefore generated nor no credential, therefore real follows.

8.2 What platforms are doing

On video and social the disclosure norm is already institutional. YouTube has required uploaders to disclose realistic altered or synthetic content since 2024, and Meta applies AI-related labels across Facebook and Instagram. Note where those requirements point, though: at content depicting people, events or places as if real. Concept illustration and abstract backdrops were never the target.

Which leaves the fourth row, a line in the caption, as the layer that actually functions at the scale of an individual blog. Until automated provenance is complete end to end, the sentence a person wrote is the part that does not get stripped. The format is not hard. Concept illustration, generated is enough, and that one line removes the cognitive cost the reader was spending on the verdict.

9. Copyright: how much of it is yours

This section is not about using someone else's image. It is about what rights you hold in a generated image you made, which is a frequent point of confusion.

Start from the definition. Korean copyright law defines a work as a creative production expressing human thought or emotion, so a raw output produced by entering a prompt is hard to register on its own. The generative AI copyright guidance published by the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism and the Korea Copyright Commission addresses that boundary.

The registration guidance that followed uses human creative contribution as the test. In summary:

  • Editing that treats outputs as material — a finished piece assembled by selecting, arranging and editing multiple outputs may be recognised for creativity comparable to a compilation work
  • Where a human sketch comes first — a result built from the creator's own drawing, with generated elements chosen, placed and adjusted on top, leaves room for creative contribution to be recognised
  • Value of process records — the guidance notes that keeping a record of the generation and creation process, on video for example, serves as material for registration and for later disputes
  • Untouched output — conversely, an image posted exactly as generated gives weak grounds for objecting when someone copies it

Applied to running a blog the judgment gets simple. A generated image used as a header decoration is safest treated as effectively public. If it reappears on another site there is almost no card to play. If you want a signature image managed as an asset, generation belongs in the material layer only, with human work layered on top.

10. What search engines and platforms actually look at

One common misconception to clear up. The search engine's criterion is not whether AI was used.

Google's guidance about AI-generated content starts from the premise that AI can be useful for researching a topic and structuring material, while stating that using it to produce many pages that add no value for users falls under the spam policies. The policy in question is scaled content abuse, and its definition carries the phrase that it applies no matter how the content was produced. Mass production by hand gets the same treatment.

So there is no mechanism by which one image loses ranking for having been generated. What matters is when that image shows up as corroborating evidence of mass production. The unit of judgment is a body of pages, not a graphics file.

10.1 The routes by which images do affect search

  • Alt text — a generated decoration has nothing to describe, so alt attributes end up empty or filled pro forma. A loss on both accessibility and search
  • Page weight — a large meaningless image delays first render. That loss shows up in measured metrics
  • Duplicated visual assets — similar images from the same pipeline scattered across pages cannot do the job of distinguishing pages
  • Image search traffic — only images containing something people would actually look for bring traffic. Metaphors are not searched

11. How the argument runs in Korea

In Korea this subject is not debated as a question of personal blog aesthetics. It moves first through regulation and platform policy, for structural reasons.

First, platform-hosted blogs outweigh self-hosted ones. Disclosure practice is therefore settled by platform policy rather than by an individual's editorial choice. That is a different starting point from the English-speaking web, where whether to put generated images on a personal blog is discussed as an individual matter.

Second, statutory disclosure is further along. The legislative notice for the AI Framework Act enforcement decree covers notification of generative AI outputs alongside criteria for high-impact domains, confirmation procedures and training compute thresholds. Meaning there is an open path for disclosure to arrive as a rule rather than a voluntary norm.

Third, the community-side argument shifted away from editorial quality and onto filtering obligations. What a duty on operators to screen generated images actually touches is set out separately in the debate over mandatory AI image screening in Korean communities. That sits on a different layer from this article. This one is about the standard a writer sets for themselves; that one is about a standard imposed from outside.

In working terms the local conditions split like this. On a platform blog, the disclosure format will probably be decided for you, so the effort is better spent on making the body verifiable. On a self-hosted domain, disclosure is entirely yours, so settling a caption rule early saves retroactive work later. Either way the rule about never generating a chart is the same.

12. What could not be established

The following were left out of the body as figures or assertions because no primary source could be pinned down.

  • The effect of generated images on bounce rate and time on page. No public study measuring that relationship directly turned up. The percentage figures in circulation could not be traced to a source
  • How accurately readers actually identify generated images. No measurement of identification accuracy in a blog context exists. The studies in section 4 test conditions where a label is present
  • The magnitude of the effect from repeated style. The mechanism described in 1.2 rests on observation and is not an experimentally verified quantity
  • The exact scope of per-platform disclosure requirements. Which images are covered varies by platform and is revised often. Check that platform's help documentation at the time you are operating
  • Metadata loss rates. No current comprehensive survey of how well individual services preserve credentials on upload was confirmed. That loss occurs is certain from the standard's design; the rate is left blank
  • Internal image criteria at Korean platforms. Nothing here goes beyond what public documents establish

Conclusion: drop the image that was added to fill a gap

This argument gets summarised as AI images are bad, but what actually works is a much narrower set of rules. Three of them.

One. Do not generate an image that makes a claim. A picture carrying numbers, structure or evidence is drawn or captured, not produced. The only one of the four frictions that is a genuine factual error comes from here, and holding this line alone prevents the failure you cannot walk back.

Two. Do not add an image to fill a slot. If you cannot write one line about what the reader loses without it, leaving it empty is better. What readers read is not the quality of the image but what was put there and why, and the trace of something being filled in carries more reliably than almost anything else on the page.

Three. Decide disclosure by audience. The same label reads as honesty to a technical reader and as a warning to a general one, which is what the experiment showed. Neither labelling everything nor hiding everything is the answer. That said, a caption line on a concept illustration costs approximately nothing, and it removes the judging work the reader was doing.

One last inversion worth keeping. What readers react to is not the image, it is the editing. The same image, from the same tool, at the same quality, causes no problem in one article and costs trust before the first paragraph in another. The difference is not the model, it is the slot the image was put in. So the thing to audit is not the choice of tool but what each image currently on the page is doing. Ask one at a time and about half of them turn out to be doing nothing.
