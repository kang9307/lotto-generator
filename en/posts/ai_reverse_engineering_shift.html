What Changes When a Machine Reads the Binary - Which Parts of Reverse Engineering Move and Which Do Not

Making it readable got faster. Saying it is correct did not.

Aug 24, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: being able to read it and being able to say it is right are two different things

An unfamiliar executable lands on your desk. There is no source. There is no documentation. The company that built it is gone, or does not answer. And you still have to work out how it stores its data, because otherwise your own system cannot read that data. This is roughly what the starting position looks like when reverse engineering begins.

The subject came around again recently. A locally run language model read machine code and produced an explanation of its structure. Reactions split into two camps. One said anyone can do this now; the other said it was never that impressive to begin with. Both, I think, miss the same point.

What actually changed is the time it takes to turn a binary into something readable. What did not change is the evidence that lets you claim the readable version does the same thing as the original. These are different jobs, and the second one is still filled in only by observation.

This article does not cover procedure. It does not describe which tools to use, how to use them, or how to open any particular target. What it covers is the principle: why the work is hard, which of the missing pieces a machine can fill in, and what tells you when a filled-in piece is wrong.

1. What was reported - the confirmed scope and its conditions

On 22 August 2026, Adam Conway, an editor at the technology outlet XDA, published the result of a test he ran himself on his own workstation. The reported conditions were these.

  • Model - Qwen 3.8 27B, an open-weights model
  • Machine - a Lenovo ThinkStation PGX with an Nvidia GB10 Grace Blackwell chip, 128GB of unified memory and 273 GB/s of bandwidth
  • Resources - roughly 17GB of memory in use; generation at 15 to 30 tokens per second by default and around 50 after tuning
  • Environment - a single machine with no external connection, fully offline
  • Method - static analysis, without running the target at all. The work was reading arm64 machine code
  • Elapsed - about 30 minutes
  • Conditions - one target, one run, no third-party reproduction. The author himself wrote that he could not say whether the difficulty of this target was representative

What that task was aiming at, and what came out of it, is not covered here. Anything in the category of defeating a protection mechanism on a product is outside what this blog writes about. The part that remains is all the argument needs. On one disconnected machine, without ever running the target, turning machine-level text into an explanation a human can read took about 30 minutes.

A single case proves existence and nothing more. That this happened once and that this generally works are different sentences. Still, existence proofs count for what they are. A few years ago a job of this shape would have implied a cluster and a network connection, and neither appears in the reported conditions.

2. What reverse engineering actually does - retracing what compilation threw away

People new to this tend to picture it as breaking a code. It is closer to the opposite. What you are dealing with is not hidden information but erased information.

Turning source into an executable is closer to discarding than to translating. Anything the machine does not need in order to run falls away.

What the source hadWhat survives in the executableNature of the retracing
Variable and function namesAddresses and offsetsNot recoverable. They are invented anew
Comments and design docsNothing at allNot recoverable. Replaced by inference
Type declarationsAccess width, alignment, kind of arithmeticEstimated from indirect evidence
Structs and field boundariesBase address plus constant offsetsRebuilt from usage patterns
Control structuresA graph of branches and jumpsRetraceable from patterns
Module boundaries and intentCall relationships onlyMostly gone

So the product of this work is not the original. It is a second description believed to behave the same way. The C-like listing a decompiler emits is not original source either; it is a plausible candidate rebuilt from machine code, in which every name was invented, every type is an estimate and every struct boundary is a hypothesis.

Miss that distinction and everything downstream drifts. The moment a readable listing on screen is treated as the original, every name inside it hardens into fact. What we are reading is not a restoration. It is a hypothesis.

3. Why it is hard - the nature of a one-way transformation

The difficulty is not one thing. It comes in layers.

  • It is many-to-one. Source written in different ways compiles to the same machine code. Going backwards there are several right answers, and no way to tell which one was the original
  • Optimisation erases boundaries. After inlining, loop transformation, common subexpression elimination and tail-call conversion, the original function boundaries are simply not there. Ten lines a human wrote in one place can end up scattered across three
  • Code and data share the same space. There is always a region where nothing mechanically decides where instructions end and a table begins
  • Intent was never written down. Why this value is 3 is not in the code. That 3 might be a protocol version, a retry count, or meaningless padding
  • Each confirmation is expensive. Settling one judgement usually means running something, and setting up the conditions to run it takes time. The number of hypotheses testable in a day is capped by that

Put together, the difficulty is not that there is little information but that information was erased. An erased slot is not filled by reading harder; it is filled by guessing. And a guess stays a guess until it is verified. That sentence is the spine of this whole article.

4. Where a language model is genuinely strong - making things readable

Look at the table again and it becomes clear where a language model fits. Most of what went missing is names, intent and structure, and filling those in is a judgement about what something looks like. That is exactly the sort of work pattern recognition is good at.

  1. Name proposals. Read what an anonymous function does and call it input length check or header parse. Tedious and slow for a human, and cheap to get wrong
  2. Known-algorithm identification. Look at a constant table combined with rotations and note that it resembles a standard hash family. The more widely used the algorithm, the more often it appeared in training data, and the better this tends to land
  3. Narrowing candidates. Out of tens of thousands of functions, pick out the ones that touch file input and output, compare strings, or read a clock. That triage takes a person days
  4. Explanatory prose. Summarise scattered branch structure into sentences, which lowers the reading load and doubles as a first draft of whatever the team will need in writing

All four share a property. They are all work that makes things readable, not work that declares things correct. And in this band, being wrong is cheap: a bad candidate name is replaced by the next one, and a summary that drifts sends you back to the listing.

That is also why the reported 30 minutes is interesting. What those 30 minutes cut was not judgement time but reading time, and reading was the largest single block in this kind of work.

5. Where it is not - the problem of the plausible wrong answer

This is the most important section in the article.

Filling in erased information has one nasty property: you cannot tell whether what you filled in is wrong by looking at what you filled in.

When a person guesses badly it usually shows. The names read awkwardly, the explanation stops mid-thought, and a not sure about this here is left behind. That awkwardness is a signal to the next reader. A language model smooths exactly that surface. The names are plausible, the comments are natural, the explanation is internally consistent. A wrong conclusion reads just as well as a right one.

In practice it shows up in shapes like these.

  • Struct fields get names while the real boundary is off by one slot, and yet the names are all consistent with each other
  • A constant is read as a timeout in seconds when it is milliseconds, and every later statement is stacked on top of that
  • Two functions merged by the optimiser are read as one, and an original design that never existed is explained in detail
  • Code that resembles a well known algorithm but was modified in one place is declared to be the standard algorithm. That one place is frequently the place that matters
  • Confidence is decoupled from accuracy. In the weakest-evidence stretches the tone of the prose is exactly as assertive as anywhere else

The real risk is not that wrong answers appear. Wrong answers always appeared. What changed is how fast they appear, and how likely they are to pass review.

When something that took two days to read takes 30 minutes, the freed time does not automatically go into verification. In practice it usually goes into producing more hypotheses, all of them written in good prose. When a team reviews the result, awkward writing invites suspicion and smooth writing does not. The same line showed up in how a hard kernel bug gets narrowed down: shrinking the candidate set and confirming the cause are different jobs, and that is where the role of a tool is decided.

One sentence: the side that got faster is generation, and the side that became the bottleneck is verification.

6. Where verification belongs - observation decides, not explanation

The principle is simple. A hypothesis is not true because the explanation is plausible; it is true when observation supports the explanation. So the question to ask is not whether this reading makes sense, but what would have to look different if this reading were wrong.

Sort the work by its nature and the placement of verification decides itself. The only criterion is the cost of being wrong.

TaskCost if wrongWhere it belongs
Naming a functionMove to the next candidateCan be handed to a tool
Picking out relevant codeOne wasted passCan be handed to a tool
Structural summary and draft docsRead it againCan be handed to a tool
Declaring that this field is a lengthEvery later reading collapsesNeeds dynamic comparison
Declaring that this routine is algorithm XA broken interoperable implementationNeeds test vectors
Declaring the whole formatThe entire output is discardedNeeds comparison against the original

There are three places to attach verification.

  • Dynamic comparison. Confirm in a real run that values flow the way the static reading claimed. Static analysis can say how something looks; execution is what says how it behaves
  • Test vectors. If you concluded it is a known algorithm, feed the published inputs for that algorithm and check the published outputs come back. A variant that differs in one place fails right here
  • Behavioural comparison with the original. Feed the same inputs to the original and to the reimplementation across a wide range, boundary values and malformed input included. Mostly matching is not a pass

Order matters as well. Try to falsify first. Setting out to confirm surfaces only confirming examples. Writing down in advance which input would break the hypothesis, and then running that input, is far cheaper. That single ordering is close to the only device that catches a smoothly written wrong answer.

7. What changes for the defending side, and what does not

Read from the defending side, this shift actually simplifies the conclusion.

First, what does not change. A secret placed on the client was never a secret. Code that runs on a user device can be inspected by that user; that is a property of the execution model, not a consequence of better tooling. Keys, decision logic, pricing rules - anything kept only on the client will eventually be read. That statement was true thirty years ago and is true now.

What changes is the distribution of time until it is read. Obfuscation was never about making analysis impossible; it was about making it expensive. A defence holds while the cost of reading exceeds the attacker expected gain. When reading gets broadly cheaper, one side of that inequality drops. Obfuscation does not become pointless, but any design that survived on obfuscation alone now has a shorter shelf life. The same line runs through the piece on wrapping shell scripts into binaries, obfuscation is not encryption.

So the practical direction settles like this.

  • Make decisions inside the trust boundary. The client asks; the server decides
  • Keep only values on the client that cost nothing if read. If a value would hurt when read, the design is already the problem
  • Treat obfuscation as bought time and design what that time buys: key rotation, anomaly detection, incident response
  • Do not count our binary is hard to analyse as a security control. Delay is time, not control

8. The legitimate uses - four purposes

The connotation attached to the phrase hides a plain fact: a large share of this work is work that causes trouble if nobody does it.

PurposeWhy it is neededThe condition attached
Malware analysisThe other side ships no source. You must read what it does to detect, block and recoverIsolated environment, no sample distribution
Vulnerability research and disclosureIf only the vendor knows about a flaw, users have nothing to decide withA coordinated disclosure process
InteroperabilityUndocumented formats and protocols must be read before data can move or systems can connectPurpose and scope kept narrow
Repair of abandoned devices and softwareWhen the vendor is gone, the code is the only remaining basis for a fixLawful ownership and use

Malware analysis is the least contested item on the list. The other side publishes no source and provides no documentation. You have to read what the code does in order to extract indicators, write detection rules and work out what changed on an infected system. Without this work, defence responds to symptoms only.

Vulnerability research exists to reduce information asymmetry. If the only people who know about a flaw are the vendor and whoever is exploiting it, users have no material for a decision. A process comes attached: the international standards for vulnerability disclosure (ISO/IEC 29147) and for vendor-side handling (ISO/IEC 30111) describe the shape, and the gist is to tell the vendor first, allow time for a fix, and publish enough for users to decide. How flaws are found is outside the scope of this article.

Interoperability is the purpose that law has recognised most explicitly. Getting data out of an old system, reading an undocumented file format, connecting a discontinued device to a new system - all of that lives here. The conditions under which that purpose is recognised are covered in the next section at the level of statutory text.

Repair has grown in weight over the last few years. When a vendor shuts a service down, hardware that still works becomes unusable. That is the background to the US Copyright Office triennial proceeding, where exemptions for diagnosis, maintenance and repair have come up repeatedly and where the 2024 cycle again added and expanded items.

9. Law and contract - start from the fact that jurisdictions differ

What follows is a general explanation and not legal advice. What is actually permitted depends on the jurisdiction and on the specific contract, so a concrete situation should be checked with a qualified professional in that jurisdiction.

On that footing, three general points can be made.

First, purpose and scope carry the analysis. Korean Copyright Act Article 101-4 addresses program code reverse analysis head on. A person entitled to use the program, or someone authorised by them, may perform it without the rightholder permission when the information needed for interoperability cannot be readily obtained and obtaining it is unavoidable, limited to the parts necessary for interoperability. The second paragraph then excludes using the resulting information outside the interoperability purpose or providing it to third parties, and excludes using it to build a program substantially similar in expression.

The EU software directive (2009/24/EC) Article 6 has the same shape. Where reproduction and translation of the code are indispensable to obtain the information necessary to achieve interoperability of an independently created program, the rightholder authorisation is not required, provided the acts are performed by a lawful user, the information was not previously readily available, and the acts are confined to the parts necessary for interoperability. Using the information for other goals is not permitted.

The United States prohibits circumvention of access controls separately, in section 1201 of its copyright law, and places an interoperability exception inside it. A person who lawfully obtained the right to use a copy may circumvent for the sole purpose of identifying and analysing the elements necessary for interoperability of an independently created program. On top of that, a separate proceeding reviews temporary exemptions every three years.

The wording differs in all three, but the axes are the same: indispensability, limited purpose, limited scope, restricted use of the result. Whichever jurisdiction you start from, the question is whether you can account for those four.

Second, even where copyright law permits it, the contract remains. Terms of service and licences frequently contain clauses forbidding analysis. Which one prevails when a statutory exception collides with a contract term differs by jurisdiction, and even within one jurisdiction it varies with the character of the clause. The law has an exception so the contract can be ignored is not a safe assumption.

Third, the target adds another axis. The moment requests go to someone else infrastructure, computer network legislation applies; trade secret law operates separately; and distributing the output pulls in rules about distribution. Reading something you lawfully acquired on your own machine and handing the result to someone else are entirely different questions.

The practical standard that survives all this is simple. Write the purpose down first, tie the scope to that purpose, and decide in advance where the output may be used. An analysis with none of that on paper is hard to explain afterwards. The faster the tooling gets, the more that habit of writing the record first matters.

What I could not confirm

  • I could not confirm what level of difficulty the reported 30 minutes represents. The author himself stated one target and one run, and I found no third-party reproduction
  • There is no control group for how long the same work would have taken by other means. So a figure of the form it is N times faster cannot come out of this case at all
  • I could not find a public evaluation measuring that model accuracy across reverse engineering tasks in general. The ratio of the bands where it lands to the bands where it misses is unknown
  • How Korean courts have applied Article 101-4 in practice was not examined here. This article stays at the level of statutory wording
  • Failed attempts are mostly never written up. Collecting only published successes tilts the sample, and the case cited here is not exempt from that

Conclusion: three sentences

  1. What changed is the cost of making a binary readable. That really did drop, and I expect it to drop further
  2. What did not change is the evidence that the readable version does what the original does. That evidence still comes only from execution, test vectors and comparison
  3. So the thing to fix is not the generation side but the verification side. Without a process that catches a smoothly written wrong answer, you get wrong exactly as fast as you got faster

What to watch next

  • Whether work of this kind reproduces across many targets. One case and a distribution are different claims
  • Whether public evaluations of recovered-output accuracy take hold. Right now only anecdotes of things going well circulate
  • Whether verification tooling speeds up alongside. If only generation gets faster, the bottleneck merely moved
  • Whether designs that decide on the client actually decline. The principle is old, but practice always follows late

And all of this comes after the question of what you are reading it for. An analysis without a settled purpose is not a technical problem but a judgement one.
