Will Validity Requirements Guide — 5 Legal Forms and What Voids a Will

From testamentary capacity to each form's requirements, statutory content, forced heirship, revocation, and probate

Jul 4, 2026 Law & Legal

Introduction: A Will Only Counts Once It Meets Its Requirements

Most people think of a will as simply writing down what they want. Korean civil law treats it far more strictly. A will is a formal legal act: even a will written with complete sincerity can be voided entirely if it fails even one of the statutory formal requirements. The reverse is also true — satisfying the formalities does not automatically mean every sentence in the document carries legal force. Some content simply falls outside what a will can legally accomplish, and other content runs into the separate limit of forced heirship.

This article focuses less on the practical steps of writing a will and more on the skeleton of requirements that decide when a will is legally valid and when it fails. It walks through testamentary capacity, the requirements of each of the 5 statutory forms, what a will can legally cover, common mistakes that void a will, its relationship to forced heirship, and revocation and probate, in that order. Everything here reflects Korean civil law and published case law as of July 2026; statutes are amended and precedents accumulate, so specifics can change over time.

1. Testamentary Capacity — the First Precondition for Validity

Before checking form, confirm capacity. A will is a legal act, but unlike a property transaction, it does not require full legal capacity.

1.1 Age 17, and Mental Capacity

Under Korean civil law, anyone who has turned 17 may make a will. Even a minor who has passed 17 can make a valid will alone, without a legal representative's consent. However, the general rule that a person must have the mental capacity to understand the meaning and consequences of the act still applies to wills.

1.2 The Exception for Persons Under Adult Guardianship

A person under adult guardianship due to illness or disability may still make a will while their mental capacity has been restored. In that case, a physician must note the testator's restored mental state on the will at the time, and sign and seal it. Omitting this step can put testamentary capacity itself in dispute even if every other formal requirement is satisfied.

In short: testamentary capacity (age 17+ and mental capacity) and the formal requirements are two separate pillars. Satisfying only one does not produce a valid will.

2. The 5 Statutory Forms of a Will at a Glance

Korean civil law limits wills to five forms: holographic, audio-recorded, notarized, sealed-secret, and oral-dictation. This is known as strict formalism. Courts have consistently held that these requirements exist to make the testator's true intent clear and prevent disputes after death, so a will that departs from the required form is invalid even if it genuinely reflects what the testator wanted.

3. The Holographic Will's 5 Requirements — Miss One, Void the Whole Thing

A holographic will needs no witness or notary, making it the easiest form to use — and also the one most often voided for formal defects. Korean civil law sets out five requirements.

3.1 The Full Text, Handwritten

The testator must write the entire content by hand. A document typed and printed, or one dictated by the testator and written down by someone else, does not qualify as handwritten even if the testator approved it.

3.2 The Date

The year, month, and day must be handwritten. If the day is missing, the will is void. The date is the sole way to determine which of several wills controls, and it also evidences the testator's capacity at the time — so this formality carries real substantive weight despite looking like a technicality.

3.3 The Address

The testator's address must also be handwritten. Courts have held that the address does not need to appear on the same page as the main text — an envelope will do — but an incomplete address, such as one that stops at the neighborhood level, has been held void even when it does not actually prevent identifying the testator.

3.4 The Name

The testator must write their own name in their own distinctive handwriting so a third party can recognize it. This effectively functions as a signature.

3.5 The Seal

Finally, the will must bear a seal (a stamp or a fingerprint). A fingerprint counts as a valid seal, but courts have consistently held that a holographic will without any seal is invalid even if it is signed.

A holographic will requires all five of the full text, date, address, and name handwritten, plus a seal. Missing even one voids the entire will. The formal requirements allow no exceptions or flexibility.

4. The Other Four Forms — Requirements and Trade-offs

4.1 Audio-Recorded Will

The testator states the intent, name, and date aloud, and at least one witness states that it is accurate along with their own name, all recorded together. This helps when writing is difficult, but audio or video files carry a risk of damage or corruption, and the will still requires probate.

4.2 Notarized Will

The testator dictates the intent to a notary, who transcribes and reads it aloud; the testator and 2 witnesses then confirm accuracy and sign or seal it. Because a notary is involved, the risk of formal defects is low, the original is kept at the notary's office, and — crucially — no probate is required. The trade-offs are needing 2 witnesses and paying notary fees. Witnesses also face disqualifications: minors and anyone who would benefit from the will, along with their spouse or lineal blood relatives, cannot serve as witnesses.

4.3 Sealed-Secret Will

Used when the testator wants the content kept secret until death while still establishing its existence and authenticity. The testator writes the document with their name on it, seals it in an envelope, and presents it to 2 or more witnesses to declare it as their will; the date of submission is written on the envelope, and the testator and witnesses sign or seal it. It must then be submitted to a notary or family court clerk within 5 days of that date to receive a fixed date. Notably, if a sealed-secret will has a formal defect but otherwise satisfies the holographic will's requirements, it can still take effect as a holographic will.

4.4 Oral-Dictation Will — the Emergency Exception

This form is recognized only when illness or another urgent circumstance makes the other four forms unavailable. The testator dictates to one of 2 or more witnesses, who transcribes and reads it aloud; the other witness and the testator confirm accuracy, and all sign or seal it. Because this is an emergency exception, control is strict: a witness or interested party must petition the family court for probate within 7 days of the emergency ending, or the will loses effect.

5. What a Will Can Legally Cover — the Statutory List

Satisfying every formal requirement does not mean everything written in a will carries legal force. Korean civil law limits what a will can legally accomplish to a defined list.

5.1 Property Matters

This includes bequests (giving property benefits without consideration), establishing a foundation, creating a trust, designating how the estate should be divided (or delegating that designation to a third party), and prohibiting estate division for a set period.

5.2 Status Matters

Acknowledging a child born outside marriage, and appointing a guardian or guardianship supervisor for a minor child, can also be done by will.

5.3 Execution Matters

Appointing an executor to carry out the will, or delegating that appointment to a third party, is likewise within the statutory list.

Anything outside this list — a wish for siblings to stay close, or personal preferences about the funeral — is a moral expression, not a legally binding provision. When drafting a will, it helps to distinguish what carries legal force from what does not.

6. A Checklist of Common Mistakes That Void a Will

Most invalid wills fail on form, not content. Here are the mistakes seen most often in practice.

  • Not handwritten: a holographic will typed and printed, or written by someone other than the testator
  • Incomplete date: missing the day, or writing something vague like "an auspicious day in such-and-such year"
  • Incomplete address: no address at all, or one that stops at the neighborhood level
  • No seal: signed but not stamped or fingerprinted
  • Disqualified witness: a minor, or a beneficiary and their spouse or lineal blood relative, serving as a witness
  • Lack of testamentary capacity: under age 17, or lacking mental capacity at the time of drafting
  • Misreading the statutory scope: mistaking a personal wish with no legal force for a binding provision
Bottom line: the formal requirements allow no exceptions. Choosing a notarized will, despite the cost, removes much of this formal risk.

7. A Will's Relationship to Forced Heirship

7.1 The Basic Structure

Even a will that is fully valid in form can run into a separate problem if it infringes an heir's forced-heirship share (legal reserve). This is a minimum share guaranteed to certain heirs regardless of a will or lifetime gift: a spouse or descendant is guaranteed half of their statutory inheritance share, and an ascendant one-third. An heir whose forced-heirship share is infringed can claim the shortfall within 1 year of learning of the inheritance and the infringing gift or bequest, and within 10 years of the inheritance opening.

7.2 The 2026 Amendment — Forfeiture and Cash Payment

In April 2024, Korea's Constitutional Court struck down the forced-heirship provision for siblings and found the absence of forfeiture grounds unconstitutional. Amendments effective March 2026 accordingly let a family court declare an heir's inheritance rights forfeited for serious abuse or neglect of the deceased, which also forfeits that heir's forced-heirship claim. The amendments also clarified that forced-heirship shortfalls are returned in cash in principle. 8. Revoking and Changing a Will

8.1 Voluntary Revocation

A testator may freely revoke, in whole or in part, a will at any time while alive, and cannot waive this right to revoke.

8.2 Statutory Revocation

If a later will conflicts with an earlier one, or a lifetime disposition after the will (for example, selling or gifting property that was to be bequeathed) conflicts with the will's content, the conflicting part of the earlier will is treated as revoked. If a testator deliberately destroys the will document or the bequeathed property, that portion is likewise treated as revoked. Because several wills may surface and their order must be determined, the accuracy of the date discussed earlier becomes decisive here too.

9. Probate — a Confirmation Procedure, Not a Validity Requirement

After a testator dies, the holder or finder of any will except a notarized one (holographic, audio-recorded, sealed-secret, or oral-dictation) must petition the family court for probate without delay. A sealed will is opened in court with the heirs present. However, Korean courts treat probate as a procedure to confirm and preserve the will's form and condition, not a determination of whether the will's content is valid. In other words, probate is a separate procedural safeguard from the validity requirements discussed above. Skipping it does not undo formal requirements already satisfied, but neglecting the duty can delay execution or trigger separate penalties.

10. Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Do I need both testamentary capacity and formal requirements?

Yes. You need testamentary capacity (age 17+ with mental capacity) and full compliance with one of the 5 statutory forms. Satisfying only one does not produce a valid will.

Q. Can a formally perfect will still be challenged?

Yes. Formal validity and forced-heirship infringement are separate issues. A formally valid will can still trigger a forced-heirship claim and resulting dispute.

Q. Does skipping probate void a will?

No. Probate is a confirmation and preservation procedure, not a validity requirement. Neglecting the duty to petition can, however, delay execution or trigger separate penalties.

Conclusion — No Exceptions on Form, a Defined Scope on Content

A will's legal validity rests on two pillars: the formal pillar of testamentary capacity plus full compliance with one of the 5 statutory forms, and the content pillar of staying within the statutory list while respecting the limit of forced heirship. For a holographic will, always check that none of the five — handwritten full text, date, address, name, and seal — is missing; if you want to reduce formal risk, a notarized will, which needs no probate, is worth considering. Content that infringes forced heirship can still seed a dispute even when the form is valid, and every requirement and figure here can change with time and amendment.
