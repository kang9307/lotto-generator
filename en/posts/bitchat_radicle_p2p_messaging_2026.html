Bitchat Published on Radicle - What Bluetooth Mesh and Nostr P2P Messaging Really Delivers, and Where It Breaks

A messenger that runs without the internet: what it actually solves, and what it cannot

Jul 27, 2026 Tech

Introduction: Why an Internet-Free Messenger Is Back in the Conversation

A messenger that works without the internet is not a new idea. The notion of hopping a message across nearby devices over Bluetooth has been around for well over a decade, and in that time a series of apps have appeared, drawn attention and then quietly disappeared. Yet in July 2026 Bitchat was published on Radicle, a peer-to-peer code collaboration network, and this long-standing topic became a talking point in developer communities all over again. The interesting part is that the focus of the discussion is not the technology itself but the distribution path the code travels on.

This article separates three questions and treats each on its own terms. First, how Bitchat is actually built and how it behaves. Second, how much a physical layer like Bluetooth mesh can realistically carry, and how wide the gap is between the marketing line and measured performance. Third, and most importantly, the fact that being decentralized guarantees nothing about being safe. To state the conclusion up front: Bitchat is an interesting experiment and it is genuinely practical in specific situations, but it has not received an external security audit and the developer himself warns about exactly that, so it is not a tool to lean on when personal safety is on the line. The rest of this piece unpacks the evidence for that judgement one step at a time.

1. How Bitchat Is Built - A Dual-Transport Design

Bitchat is a peer-to-peer messaging app published in July 2025 by Jack Dorsey, a co-founder of Block. Its headline pitch is that it runs with no account, no phone number and no central server at all. The core of the design is a dual-transport architecture: a single app swaps between two transport layers of completely different character depending on the conditions it finds itself in.

1.1 The Offline Path - Bluetooth Low Energy Mesh

When there is no internet, Bitchat uses Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) to connect directly to nearby devices. The concept that matters here is the mesh. Even if my phone cannot reach the other person's phone directly, a third phone in between can receive the message and rebroadcast it, and the message arrives. Each such relay is called a hop, and Bitchat is documented as supporting relaying up to seven hops. No mobile carrier, no Wi-Fi router and no server is involved anywhere in that chain, which is undeniably an appealing picture on paper.

1.2 The Online Path - Nostr Relays

When the internet is alive, the app switches over to the Nostr protocol. Nostr is an open protocol built around broadcasting signed messages, called events, to a set of relay servers. A relay merely stores and forwards signed events; it cannot forge their contents, because doing so would break the signature. Bitchat offers global and local channels through roughly 290 relays, and uses geohashes to slice those channels into block, neighbourhood, city, regional and national tiers. The effect is that people who are physically close to one another naturally end up in the same room.

1.3 Encryption and Extra Features

Encryption differs by layer. On the Bluetooth leg the app prefers encryption based on the Noise protocol framework, and where that is not possible it falls back to Nostr's NIP-17 scheme. NIP-17 uses the XChaCha20-Poly1305 encryption defined in NIP-44 and wraps events in several layers, a technique known as gift wrapping, designed to reduce metadata exposure. Beyond that there are IRC-style slash commands and an Emergency Wipe feature that erases stored data the instant you tap the screen three times. The code is released into the public domain, and the iOS and macOS builds can be produced with Xcode or with the just command.

2. What Publishing on Radicle Signifies

2.1 What Radicle Is

Radicle is a code collaboration network that replicates and synchronises Git repositories peer to peer with no central server. It positions itself as a decentralized alternative to GitHub, and version 1.0 has shipped as a formal release. Its mechanics can be summarised as follows.

  • Each participant runs a node on their own machine, and the nodes connect to one another over a peer-to-peer network
  • Identity is defined not by signing up for an account but by a public key pair generated at the CLI - there is no company whose approval you need
  • Peer and repository discovery is handled by a purpose-built gossip protocol, while data replication uses the standard Git protocol
  • Collaboration artefacts such as issues, patches (the equivalent of pull requests) and code reviews are replicated along with the repository itself
  • The day-to-day flow resembles GitHub - you clone, you make changes locally, and you push to the network

Because there is no central server, there is simply no entity in a position to take a repository down or suspend an account. What you get in exchange is the mirror-image weakness: if nobody seeds a repository, it quietly ceases to exist. Responsibility for availability has moved from a company to a community; it has not been eliminated.

2.2 Why This Is Happening Now

The publication only makes sense with its context attached. On the night of 23 July 2026, India's Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C) sent GitHub a notice to block access to three Bitchat repositories within three hours. Large-scale protests had been running in India since 20 July, and there were reports of repeated internet shutdowns around protest locations. Earlier, during the 2025 protest period in Nepal, Bitchat downloads were recorded spiking past 48,000 in a single day. It was against that backdrop that the repository blocking request arrived, and immediately afterwards the community mirrored the code onto Radicle.

The point worth extracting here is not a political verdict on any particular country's action but the engineering lesson. Most open-source projects have, in practice, stacked their repository, issues, CI, releases and package registry entirely onto GitHub alone. GitHub is a single business, operated by a US company, and it sits in a position where it can restrict access in response to legal demands from various jurisdictions, its own terms of service, or commercial judgement. In other words, we use Git, a distributed version control system, while in practice standing on top of centralized infrastructure. This launch made the gap between Git's original design philosophy and the way it is actually operated unusually visible.

At the same time, over-reading the episode should be resisted. Putting code on Radicle does not raise its quality or its safety by any amount. It is a duplication of the distribution path and nothing more. Radicle itself also carries real constraints: it depends on node operators and seeders showing up, its ecosystem is still thin, and discoverability is far lower than on GitHub. The event is worth recording as an example of a broader trend toward diversifying code hosting, but that is not the same thing as GitHub being replaced.

3. The Physical Limits of BLE Mesh - Coldly Stated

This section is the most important one in the article, because software can be fixed and the physics of radio propagation cannot. Nearly all of the gap between what people expect from a Bluetooth mesh messenger and what they get originates right here.

3.1 The Paradox of People as Infrastructure

The row in that table that deserves the closest attention is dependence on device density. The fundamental paradox of Bluetooth mesh is this: it is a technology built to be used when infrastructure is absent, yet it only functions when the infrastructure made of people is packed tightly around you. In a rally with thousands of attendees, or at a music festival, the mesh forms well. Conversely, for a hiker stranded on a mountain, a rural village in a blackout, or an empty road at night - precisely the isolated situations where communication is most desperately needed - there are no devices nearby, so nothing works. That condition is the one most frequently omitted when this app is presented as an all-purpose disaster preparedness tool.

3.2 The Battery and Background Wall

For a mesh network to hold together, a large share of participants must burn their own battery relaying other people's messages. Users have no direct incentive to do that. Not many people will leave an app running when the battery is down to 20 percent. On top of that, iOS and Android both aggressively restrict background BLE activity to protect battery life. The inevitable result is a wide gap between laboratory numbers and real field performance. This structural problem is not a defect unique to Bitchat; it is a limitation shared by every phone-based mesh messenger ever built.

4. Decentralized Is Not a Synonym for Safe

Important: Bitchat has not received an external security audit. The developer himself has stated this as an explicit warning in the repository. You must therefore not rely on this app where personal safety or life is at stake, or in any situation that carries legal risk. What follows is technical analysis and is not a recommendation to use the app for any particular purpose.

4.1 The Developer's Own Warning

Jack Dorsey personally attached a warning to the Bitchat repository to the effect that this software has not received an external security review, may contain vulnerabilities, and does not necessarily meet its stated security goals. In press interviews he also said not to rely on it for secure communication until third-party review has taken place. Taking that warning at face value is the most accurate posture available. There is no basis on which a user can unilaterally promote a tool to safe status when the developer is stating that it is not yet verified.

4.2 The Specific Defects Experts Identified

The security firm Trail of Bits published a balanced assessment in July 2025. The problems it identified were as follows.

  • Broken identity authentication - a man-in-the-middle (MITM) attack allowing an attacker to impersonate a trusted contact was confirmed. It was assessed as a design flaw that undermines a core security promise
  • No per-message forward secrecy - because static keys are used within a session, there is no protection at the level of the Double Ratchet used by Signal and others
  • Buffer overflow in signature parsing - a defect that could lead to crashes, information disclosure or denial of service was reported (and fixed promptly)
  • Insufficient data authenticity verification - signature and authentication verification issues in the CWE-345 and CWE-347 families were raised

At the same time Trail of Bits noted positive signals too: that the code was published openly, that the security warning was placed prominently, that the buffer overflow was fixed within four hours, and that adoption of the Noise protocol framework was promised. Their conclusion leaned toward do not use it for sensitive communication right now, but help a young project mature rather than mocking it. That is a fair-minded view, yet for an ordinary user the first half of the sentence - do not use it right now - is the part that matters more.

4.3 Bridgefy, a Painful Precedent

The case that absolutely must be raised at this point is Bridgefy. Bridgefy, a Bluetooth mesh messenger, was in fact widely used during large-scale protests in Hong Kong, India, Iran, Lebanon, Zimbabwe, the United States and Myanmar. Then in August 2020, an analysis published by researchers at the Information Security Group of Royal Holloway (University of London) and the Applied Cryptography Group at ETH Zurich concluded that its claims about confidentiality, authentication and resilience did not hold. Users could be tracked, broadcast messages were unauthenticated so impersonation was possible, and confidentiality could be broken with a man-in-the-middle attack.

What came next is the more painful part. The vendor accepted the findings, adopted the Signal protocol (libsignal), and continued to market the app as suitable for high-risk users. But the follow-up study Breaking Bridgefy, again, presented at USENIX Security in 2022, showed that this was not sufficient on its own. Active man-in-the-middle attacks were still possible, impersonation on broadcast channels remained, and most of the privacy problems were still unresolved.

The lesson to take from this is unambiguous. Picking up a verified cryptographic library and designing a secure protocol are two different jobs. A library is only a component; if the assembly - identity binding, key verification, replay protection, metadata handling - is wrong, the finished product is still broken. And in that precedent, the people who actually paid the price were ordinary users who trusted the app and used it in dangerous circumstances. That history must not be forgotten when assessing Bitchat.

5. Metadata, the Blind Spot

Even with message contents perfectly encrypted, something is left behind: the metadata of who communicated with whom, when and where. This is exactly why intelligence agencies have long valued metadata above content. Relationship graphs and behavioural patterns say a great deal without any conversation text at all. In a Bluetooth mesh architecture, this problem is if anything harder to handle.

  • Physical proximity is itself a signal - Bluetooth only works by broadcasting to its surroundings, so anyone with scanning equipment can observe which devices were present at a given time and place
  • Re-identification risk on device identifiers - even with address randomisation applied, re-identification can be attempted from the combination of communication patterns, timing and signal strength
  • Information exposure inherent in relaying - a neighbouring node that relays a message sits in a position to observe traffic flow
  • The Nostr relay side - a relay operator can see connecting IP addresses and timing patterns. NIP-17 reduces exposure by wrapping events and randomising timestamps, but a user has no ready means of verifying for themselves whether a relay is censoring or tampering
  • Structural exposure from geohash channels - the very act of joining a local channel implies approximate location information

To put it plainly, decentralization contributes to censorship resistance but does not automatically confer anonymity. These are separate properties, and they are routinely conflated.

6. Comparing Similar Tools - What Actually Differs

Bitchat is not the only offline messaging tool. Each uses a different transport and sits at a different level of maturity, so a side-by-side comparison helps in forming a judgement.

BridgefyBLE meshTens of metres, hundreds with relayingNot needed (smartphone)Two academic analyses, problems confirmed both times

MeshtasticLoRa radio2-5km, 1-2km in a cityRequired (dedicated radio module)Open-source community review. Its purpose is closer to communication itself

What to read out of that table is that the axes of the trade-off differ from tool to tool. Bitchat and Bridgefy have accessibility on their side, working on a phone alone with no extra kit, but they are weak on range and on verification. Briar is relatively more mature on security but is Android-only and less convenient. Meshtastic is overwhelming on range but requires buying dedicated hardware and carries a real configuration learning curve. There is at present no option that satisfies convenience, range and verified security at the same time.

7. Where It Is Actually Useful in South Korea

Now let us narrow the lens to the Korean context. South Korea has, by global standards, unusually dense mobile coverage and public alerting systems. The CBS-based emergency text system operated by the Ministry of the Interior and Safety pushes alerts region by region through cell towers, and after the 2016 Gyeongju earthquake the system was improved so that the Korea Meteorological Administration issues earthquake early warnings directly. A separate national disaster safety communication network (PS-LTE) is also in place. In short, if the goal is simply for ordinary citizens to receive disaster information, a substantial system already exists and there is not much room for Bitchat to add to it.

ScenarioUsefulnessReason
Large concerts, festivals and stadiumsRelatively highEven when data is dead from cell tower overload, people are dense enough for a mesh to form
Large indoor events and exhibition hallsRelatively highFits the use case of exchanging short text with many people in the same space
Subways, underground malls and other dead zonesLimitedPlenty of people, but they move fast so the mesh keeps breaking. Korean subways also have good coverage, so the need is low
Urban blackouts and network outagesConditionalThe people around you must have the same app running. Prior adoption rate is effectively the whole story
Mountain rescue situations and being stranded at seaUnsuitableNo devices nearby, so no mesh forms. Satellite communication or a dedicated radio is the right answer
Sensitive information or communication carrying legal riskUnsuitableThis is an unaudited cryptographic implementation. A verified alternative should be used

One more condition deserves to be spelled out: prior adoption rate. A mesh messenger is of no use whatsoever if it is installed after a disaster has already struck, because with the internet down you cannot even reach an app store. It is a tool that only works at the moment of a disaster if enough people installed it well in advance, and yet in ordinary times there is no reason to use it, so it does not get installed. This chicken-and-egg problem is the real reason a succession of mesh messengers have failed over the past decade or more, and Bitchat is no exception to it. That structural barrier weighs more heavily than the polish of any individual app.

8. So Who Can Use It, and Who Should Not

A technology assessment only becomes useful when it converges into recommendations by user type. Divided out clearly, it looks like this.

8.1 Cases Where Trying It Is Fine

  • Developers pulling the design apart out of technical curiosity - the code is public and it is a good specimen for learning BLE mesh and Nostr integration
  • Casual chat at a festival or event when the network is dead - if the content would be harmless if exposed, there is genuine practical fun in it
  • Mesh network experimentation and research - for measuring hop counts and delivery rates in a real environment
  • Anyone diversifying their disaster preparedness - but strictly as one of several fallbacks, never as the only one

8.2 Cases Where It Must Not Be Used

  • Anyone whose personal safety is endangered by exposure - entrusting your safety to an unaudited cryptographic implementation is a gamble. The Bridgefy precedent shows precisely this
  • Whistleblowing and other communication carrying legal risk - confirmed impersonation and man-in-the-middle risks exist. It is categorically unsuitable
  • Handling corporate trade secrets or customer data - with no audit history, it cannot meet compliance requirements
  • As the sole communication channel when life is at stake - delivery is probabilistic and hostage to device density. In a rescue situation, satellite communication or a dedicated radio is the right answer
  • Ordinary users installing it because someone said it is safe - the developer himself is warning that it is not yet verified

Conclusion

The publication of Bitchat on Radicle is an event in which two different stories overlap. One is that the long-standing problem of single-point dependence in code hosting surfaced as a concrete case. The other is that the old idea of offline messaging has been put back on the test bench. The first story carries a lesson valid for developers generally: it is an occasion to re-examine the habit of using Git, a distributed system, while in practice betting everything on one location.

The second story has to be handled far more carefully. Bitchat is a well-executed experiment and the dual-transport design is clever. But the limits of the physical layer cannot be crossed with software, and an unaudited cryptographic implementation is itself a risk. It is worth remembering that the reassurance the word decentralized provides has nothing to do with actual safety, and that the price for that confusion was already paid once, with Bridgefy. When the developer himself says do not rely on this until external review has happened, taking him at his word is the most rational choice a user can make.

Summed up as of July 2026, it comes to this. Bitchat is a tool worth playing with at a festival and an excellent piece of study material, but it is not something to stake your life on at the moment safety is on the line. The instant that distinction is blurred, the technology stops protecting people and starts putting them in danger. If it one day passes a proper external audit and the identified authentication defects are resolved, the assessment can change. Until then, the accurate place to leave it is in the category of an interesting experiment.
