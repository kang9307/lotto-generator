This article is based on the sentences written in the October 6, 2026 post by Mihai Dinculescu, the author of the tapo library, in the repository's README, CHANGELOG, TROUBLESHOOTING and SUPPORTED_DEVICES files, in TP-Link FAQ 4416, in RFC 9383, and on the public page of the 2023 research team. tapo is an unofficial client, not a TP-Link product, and the descriptions of how KLAP and TPAP behave come from the library author, not from TP-Link documentation.

The Trigger: Nothing in the Code Changed, Yet 403 Forbidden Comes Back

The author's post opens with a single scene. A script has been switching a Tapo plug on and off for months, the plug quietly updates its firmware, and the next request comes back with 403 Forbidden. Not one line of the code has changed. The cause the post points to is a switch called "Third-Party Compatibility", located in the Tapo app under Me > Third-Party Services. Since firmware 1.4.0, a plug only talks to third-party clients the way it used to while that switch is on. The post says at least nine issues in the repository tell the same story and names #441, #449 and #473 as examples.

tapo is an unofficial Rust and Python client for TP-Link Tapo devices. The README's introductory sentence begins with "Unofficial Tapo API Client", and the author likewise introduces it in the post as his own unofficial client. According to the post, as of v0.11.1 the switch can stay off, with a few minor exceptions. This is the outcome of nearly four months of work, and the three releases landed within a week of each other: v0.10.0 on September 28, v0.11.0 on October 2 and v0.11.1 on October 4. GeekNews carried the story on October 7.

The Python package is a thin wrapper around the Rust crate, so every change lands in both at once under the same version number. The examples in the post are written in Rust, and the Python calls mirror them. The Rust usage example printed in the README is shown below. There is no argument that selects a protocol.

let device = ApiClient::new("<tapo-username>", "tapo-password")
                        .p110("<device ip address>")
                        .await?;
                    
                    device.on().await?;

On TPAP support, the post says there is nothing to change in user code. The client works out which protocol the device speaks and logs in over that one. What is worth looking at, then, is not how the library is called but how a single switch changes the protocol a device speaks and how far the library has followed it. General account and network settings for smart home devices are covered separately in the smart home and IoT device security guide.

One Switch Decides the Protocol: Device by Device

According to the post, on recent firmware the Third-Party Compatibility switch decides which protocol a device speaks. A light, plug, power strip or H100 hub speaks KLAP when the switch is on and TPAP when it is off. Cameras never spoke KLAP. Their older protocol is AES SSL, and some cameras refuse even that one when the switch is off. Before v0.11.1 the library did not speak TPAP, so it could only reach a device whose switch was on.

Where the switch lives is written in TP-Link FAQ 4416. The path is Tapo App > Me > Third-Party Services > Third-Party Compatibility, and the Me button sits in either the bottom right corner or the top left corner depending on the app version. In the Kasa app it is found under Me > Settings. The repository's TROUBLESHOOTING document adds that on older app versions the option sat under "Tapo Lab" and that toggling it there has no effect. The FAQ, last updated on October 28, 2025, says the following about the feature.

  • Purpose. The FAQ says the feature "was designed to balance users' demand for third-party apps with the security of product use."
  • When enabled. In the FAQ's words, "By enabling this feature, you may continue to manage Tapo/Kasa devices using third-party apps like Home Assistant." The verb it uses is "may".
  • Security. The FAQ asks users to keep in mind that enabling it "may reduce the security of your devices", and it also states that "some security vulnerabilities previously addressed may be reintroduced".
  • Default. "This feature is disabled by default to ensure security", and choosing to activate it "will be considered as acceptance of any associated risks".
  • Scope of support. "We have not officially advertised support for Home Assistant or other third-party apps, so enabling this feature does not guarantee that your devices will work seamlessly with them." The FAQ strongly recommends using the official Tapo/Kasa app.

Three Transitions: KLAP in 2023, Cameras in 2024, TPAP in 2025

The author sums it up as the third time in three years that a security change has quietly locked third-party clients out of Tapo devices. The switch is what the second transition left behind. Laid out as a table, the order the post describes looks like this.

PeriodDevicesWhat happened, per the postWhat was left for third parties
2023Lights and plugsIn February three researchers reported four flaws. A few months later firmware updates began swapping the devices' original AES protocol for KLAPEvery third-party client, this library included, had to implement KLAP
November 2023 to December 2024CamerasThe maintainer of a Home Assistant integration for Tapo cameras reported a vulnerability. By April 2024 cameras on new firmware had stopped accepting the integration's login. A toggle was added to the app in December 2024Turning the switch on brings the old local login back
October 2025 to the first half of 2026Plugs, then lightsFirmware 1.4.0 brought TPAP and put plugs behind the same switch. Lights followed on firmware 1.4.1 to 1.4.3Either turn the switch on, or have the client implement TPAP
  • 2023. Three researchers from the University of Catania and Royal Holloway, University of London, starting from an L530E bulb, reported four flaws in the way Tapo devices talked to the app. The post summarizes the impact as follows: someone within range could take over the victim's Tapo account and learn their Wi-Fi password. The author writes that TP-Link never said this report and the move to KLAP were connected, and did not announce the change either. One forum thread, noting that the latest firmware had changed the encryption type from AES to KLAP, asked "Is this an error or intentional? If intentional, WHY?", and that thread is now locked.
  • 2024. Juraj Nyíri, who maintains a Home Assistant integration for Tapo cameras, reported a vulnerability to TP-Link in November 2023, and TP-Link fixed it. The integration's 6.0.0 release notes give the report date as November 5 and say that users began reporting in April 2024 that their cameras had stopped working with the integration. Nyíri built a workaround that went through TP-Link's cloud and asked for permission to release it, and according to the post TP-Link reviewed the code and declined. What shipped in December 2024 was a toggle that turns the old local login back on. The post places that eight months after the first reports, and April to December 2024 is eight months. The heading of the release notes reads "Victory for Local Control: TP-Link Enables Third-Party Compatibility".
  • 2025. This time, the post says, the way out existed before the door closed, because the switch had been in the app since December 2024. It was, however, off by default. When owners of plugs that had stopped working asked on the forum, the reply they received included the sentence "please note that Home Assistant is not an officially supported third‑party platform for Tapo products." The author writes that TPAP itself was never documented.

The course of the 2023 research is recorded, with dates, on the page the research team published.

  • Start. Research began in May 2022 on the Tapo L530E.
  • Report. On February 25, 2023 the team started the disclosure process through TP-Link's Product Security Advisory channel. The report covered the lack of authentication of the smart bulb with the app, a hard-coded short shared secret, the lack of randomness during symmetric encryption, and insufficient message freshness.
  • Fix and publication. On June 16, 2023 TP-Link confirmed a valid solution and said it was releasing new firmware. On July 12 the paper "Smart Bulbs can be Hacked to Hack into your Household" was published through SECRYPT 2023.
  • Wider scope. The work later extended to the P100, C200, L510E V2, L630 and L900. The page summarizes four vulnerabilities enabling five potential exploits across six devices, and lists the L530E's vulnerable version as 1.1.9 and its fixed version as 1.2.4.
  • Vendor response. The page states that TP-Link acknowledged the reported issues and released the necessary fixes, and that the team actively collaborated with TP-Link by testing the fixes and confirming that the attack scenarios are no longer exploitable or do not give the attacker any advantage.

The KLAP and TPAP Logins: The Difference as the Author Describes It

The author calls the switch a security downgrade with a friendly name. Turning it on brings back the old login in place of the new one, and the new one is better, the reasoning goes. The table below carries over what the author explains in the post; it is not taken from TP-Link documentation.

AspectKLAPTPAP
Proof that both sides know the credentialsExchange of hashes built from the credentials and from two random values sent in the clearSPAKE2+ (RFC 9383), a password-authenticated key exchange
A single recorded loginCan be taken off the network and used to test password guesses as fast as the hardware allowsNothing in the exchange can be checked against a candidate password offline. A guess has to be tried against the device, one attempt at a time
Session keysThey come from the same ingredients, so a correct guess also decrypts everything that followedThey depend on secrets that both sides make up for that login and never send. Someone who learns the password later still cannot decrypt sessions captured before
Repeated attempts—After too many failed logins the device refuses every login for a while

The post also grants KLAP one point. Because it exchanges hashes, the password itself stays off the wire. The problem it raises is what comes next. Someone who records a single login on the same network can take it away and check guesses against it, and because the session keys come from the same ingredients, one correct guess also opens the traffic that followed. On the TPAP side the only way to test a guess is to try it against the device itself, and the lockout described above is what stops that.

RFC 9383 is titled "SPAKE2+, an Augmented Password-Authenticated Key Exchange (PAKE) Protocol". It was published in September 2023 and its category is Informational. The abstract introduces SPAKE2+ as a PAKE protocol run between two parties for deriving a strong shared key with no risk of disclosing the password, and calls it an augmented PAKE because only one party has knowledge of the password. The abstract also states that the document was produced outside of the IETF and IRTF, was published in the Independent Submissions Stream, and does not imply endorsement by the IETF or IRTF.

One condition applies. While the switch is on, a light or plug still advertises KLAP and the library logs in over that. The TPAP properties in the table therefore hold only once the switch is off. The author writes that if nothing else on the network needs Third-Party Compatibility, there is now a good reason to switch it off.

What v0.11.1 Adds: Automatic Protocol Selection, Error Kinds, Per-Device Exceptions

The v0.11.1 entries in the CHANGELOG are dated October 4, 2026 for both Rust and Python. The protocol-related changes are as follows.

  • Coverage. ApiClient supports TPAP, which lights, plugs, power strips, hubs and cameras on recent firmware require when the switch is off. This applies both when connecting by IP address and through discover_devices.
  • First request on an IP connection. Connecting to a light, plug, power strip or H100 hub by IP address now starts by asking the device which protocol it speaks. This takes the place of the legacy protocol probe removed in v0.11.0, so connecting sends as many requests as it did before that release.
  • Cameras. Connecting to a camera by IP address logs in over TPAP whenever the camera takes it, and sends one request more to a camera that does not.
  • Camera hubs. On an IP connection a camera hub is still logged in to over AES SSL. An H200 or H500 is logged in to over TPAP only through discover_devices, which logs in to a camera or camera hub over the protocol it announces.
  • Discovery results. discover_devices now returns a TPAP device like any other device instead of reporting it with Error::UnsupportedProtocol. That error is reserved for a device that speaks TPAP in a way the library cannot handle yet, and its description names what the device announced.
  • Expired sessions. For cameras and camera hubs, a request on an expired session now returns an Unauthorized error of kind SESSION_TIMEOUT instead of an HttpError with status code 401. As for every other device, refresh_session recovers from it.

The TPAP login comes with three new error kinds. Both the post and the CHANGELOG say the first two should not be retried in a loop, because a locked device refuses every login for a while.

  • TPAP_CREDENTIALS — the password is wrong.
  • TPAP_AUTH_ATTEMPTS_LIMIT — the device has locked itself after too many failed logins.
  • TPAP_HASH_MISMATCH — the device accepted the login but could not prove that it knows the password as well.

What the post calls "minor exceptions" sit in the camera family. Camera hubs do not speak TPAP yet. An H200, newly supported in v0.10, on firmware 1.7.5 announces AES SSL whether the switch is on or off, and the library logs in over that. For cameras it depends on the model and its firmware. A C220 and a C510W on firmware 1.3.4 speak TPAP, so they work with the switch off. A C210 on firmware 1.5.2 does not, so the library logs in to it over AES SSL, and the camera refuses that login while the switch is off. Placed side by side, the protocol-related changes across the three releases look like this.

Itemv0.10.0 (September 28)v0.11.0 (October 2)v0.11.1 (October 4)

While one protocol arrived, another left. The original AES protocol, the one KLAP replaced in 2023, was still in the library, which probed every light and plug it connected to by IP address to find out whether it wanted that or KLAP. The post says no firmware has shipped with that protocol for a long time, which is why v0.11.0 removes it. According to the CHANGELOG, a device whose firmware predates KLAP needs a firmware update in the Tapo app before it can be used, and because the client no longer probes, connecting by IP address sends one request fewer. AES SSL, the protocol cameras and camera hubs speak, is in the post's words a close relative: the same kind of encrypted envelope, but over HTTPS and with a different login. That one stays.

When the 403 Persists: Symptoms and Steps in the Troubleshooting Document

The repository's TROUBLESHOOTING document has a section titled "Handshake fails with 403 Forbidden (Third-Party Compatibility)". It says the problem was reported in #441, #449, #473 and #643 after plugs (P100, P110, P115) were upgraded to firmware 1.4.0 or newer. The document lists three symptoms.

  • Connecting to the device fails with Unauthorized: FORBIDDEN: Make sure Third-Party Compatibility is turned on in the Tapo app....
  • With debug logging enabled, the log shows Using KLAP protocol followed by Handshake1 error: 403 Forbidden.
  • The device worked previously and stopped after a firmware update. Devices still on older firmware keep working.

The document explains the cause this way. The library supports both protocols and uses the one the device announces, so the option can be left either way. If this error still appears, it means the device did not announce TPAP and then refused KLAP. The setting is stored per account and pushed to the devices, and that push does not always reach a device that was updated after the option was changed. The steps the document recommends, in order, are these.

  • First, switch the option off and back on again. Under Me > Third-Party Services > Third-Party Compatibility, switch it off, leave the settings screen, wait about a minute, go back in and switch it on. Wait another minute before retrying.
  • Second, update the Tapo app. On older app versions the option sits under Tapo Lab, and toggling it there has no effect. After the app is updated, the option moves to Third-Party Services and toggling it there works.
  • Third, as a last resort, factory reset the device. Remove it from the app, set it up again, and then toggle the switch once more.

The general advice at the top of the document comes in two parts. Try the latest release first, since it is the version that gets tested against current device firmware. Then enable debug logs, which show which protocol the library negotiated and where the connection failed. In Rust that is the environment variable RUST_LOG=trace; in Python it is a call to logging.basicConfig(level=5) before the client is created.

What Else Landed in the Same Cycle: Camera Hubs, Plug Schedules and Timers, the MCP Server

Until v0.10 the only hub the library could talk to was the H100. The H200 and H500 pair with sensors and switches as the H100 does, but they also pair with cameras and store their recordings. A handler for either hub is created with h200 or h500 on the ApiClient, and discover_devices now returns them ready to use instead of reporting an error. The CHANGELOG lists the following capabilities for CameraHubHandler.

  • get_general_device_list — lists the cameras paired to the hub.
  • get_timezone — reads the hub's timezone.
  • get_recording_dates and get_recordings — find the days that have recordings and the recordings stored for a camera within a time range.
  • download_recording — saves a recording as a playable MPEG-TS clip. In Rust it writes to any AsyncWrite; in Python it takes a file path.
  • get_child_device_list and the typed child handlers (t100, t31x and the rest) — the sensors and switches paired to the hub, which work the same way as on the H100.

All the times are UTC: DateTime<Utc> in Rust and timezone-aware datetime values in Python. The author states that he does not own either hub. H200 support is the result of one user testing against their own device over roughly thirty rounds, and the H500 was tested by another user.

Plug schedules and timers arrived in v0.10 on PlugHandler and PlugEnergyMonitoringHandler. They correspond to "Schedule" and "Timer" in the Tapo app. A schedule rule fires at a time of day, or at an offset from sunrise or sunset, either once or on a set of weekdays. The point the post stresses is that the rules live on the plug and fire on its own clock. They keep working when the script, the server or the internet connection is down.

  • Building rules. ScheduleRule::clock_weekly, sunrise_weekly and sunset_weekly, plus clock_once, sunrise_once and sunset_once for rules that fire a single time.
  • Adding and editing. add_schedule_rule and edit_schedule_rule. Rules read back from the device are read-only, and to_editable turns one into a rule that can be changed and sent back.
  • Reading. get_schedule_rules, and get_max_schedule_rules, which reports how many rules the device can store in total.
  • Removing. remove_schedule_rule and remove_all_schedule_rules.
  • Timer. set_timer arms a single countdown, between one second and 24 hours, after which the plug switches on or off. get_timer reads it back and clear_timer cancels it. A plug holds one armed timer at a time, so set_timer replaces whatever was armed before.
  • Error codes. The schedule rule error codes -1802, -1803 and -1805 now report as SCHEDULE_FULL, SCHEDULE_CONFLICT and SCHEDULE_INDEX instead of UNKNOWN.

In the repository's SUPPORTED_DEVICES table, the schedule features, add_schedule_rule among them, are marked in the P100/P105 column and the P110/P110M/P115 column. The power strip columns are empty.

tapo-mcp is the MCP server in the same repository that exposes Tapo devices to AI agents. According to the post, as of v0.5.3 it lists H200 and H500 camera hubs and the sensors paired to them, and it works with devices that have Third-Party Compatibility switched off. The MCP v0.5.3 entry in the CHANGELOG says TPAP support was added to list_devices, check_device, get_device_state, control_device and take_snapshot. Plug schedules, timers and recording downloads are library-only for now. What MCP is in the first place is covered in the MCP guide.

What Changes When Upgrading From v0.9, and What Is Planned Next

The "Before you upgrade" section of the post says to expect a few breaking changes when coming from v0.9. The legacy AES protocol is gone, trigger logs and temperature records have new field names, and Python enum values no longer compare equal to integers. The corresponding parts of the v0.11.0 entries in the CHANGELOG read as follows.

  • Legacy AES. Removed. Lights, plugs, power strips and the H100 hub now always connect over KLAP, and discover_devices reports a device whose firmware predates KLAP with Error::UnsupportedProtocol.
  • Trigger logs (Rust). logs in TriggerLogsResult is now a Vec<TriggerLog<E>>. Each TriggerLog carries the id, the triggered_at time as a DateTime<Utc> and the event. Previously every event variant repeated an id and a Unix-seconds timestamp.
  • Type names (Rust). S200Log, T100Log, T110Log and T300Log are renamed to S200Event, T100Event, T110Event and T300Event.
  • Temperature and humidity records. The datetime field of TemperatureHumidityRecords is renamed to retrieved_at, and the datetime field of TemperatureHumidityRecord to recorded_at.
  • Python enums. Values no longer compare equal to integers. DeviceType.Light == 0 is now False. Code should compare against the enum variant instead, and int(value) still works.
  • Python trigger logs. start_id in get_trigger_logs is now optional and defaults to 0, so get_trigger_logs(5) returns the five most recent logs.

Among the fixes in v0.10.0 are several that close paths by which credentials ended up in logs.

  • Logging in to a device no longer writes the password, a value derived from it, or the session token to the debug and trace logs.
  • The message and Debug output of Error::Http now show REDACTED in place of the session token in the request URL. This is where a failed request to a camera, camera hub or AES-protocol device used to reveal the token.
  • A device reply too short to be valid now returns an error instead of panicking.

The "What's next" section of the post lists five plans. No dates are given.

  • The MCP server. Energy usage and caching of discovery results come first. Recording downloads could follow if there is interest, which is as far as the wording goes.
  • The H110 hub. This is the hub that doubles as an infrared remote control. A pull request that adds it is in progress.
  • Cameras. Today the library only has a handler for cameras that pan and tilt. The plan is to add one for fixed cameras, starting with the C120.
  • A device simulator. Most of the library can only be checked against real hardware, and some of that hardware, such as the camera hubs, the author does not own. The idea is a simulator that answers the way the devices do, so that a broken login or a misread response is caught earlier.
  • A Node.js wrapper. The post describes this as long term and not a promise.

The post closes with conditional advice. If this library was the only reason for keeping Third-Party Compatibility on, upgrade and switch it off. If a device then refuses to log in, the author asks for an issue with its model and firmware, and notes that reports like that are how the C210 got its caveat and how the H200 got supported at all. TP-Link's FAQ, for its part, recommends the official apps when it discusses the same switch, so the two documents start from different positions. For keeping IoT devices on a separate network at the router, see the guest network section of the home router security guide.

What Could Not Be Verified

  • The TPAP specification. No TPAP document published by TP-Link was found, and the author also writes that it was never documented. The description of TPAP in this article is the author's account, and the post does not say by what method the author worked the protocol out. Nor do the sources state with which parameters Tapo firmware implements RFC 9383.
  • The link between the 2023 research and the move to KLAP. The author places the two events in chronological order and writes that TP-Link never said they were connected. The name KLAP does not appear on the research team's page either. No source states a causal link.
  • The empty cells in the table. The post does not say which protocol a C220 or C510W speaks with the switch on, nor what firmware version an H500 runs or how it behaves in each switch position. The H200 is the only camera hub for which the post gives a version.
  • Other cameras. The cameras the README lists as tested are the C210, C220, C225, C325WB, C520WS, TC40 and TC70. Of these the post describes TPAP behavior for the C210 and C220 only, and the C510W appears only in the post. The behavior of the remaining models is not in the sources.
  • The length and threshold of the lockout. Neither the post nor the CHANGELOG gives a number for how many failures trigger the lock or for how long "a while" is.
  • The list of nine issues. Of the "at least nine" issues the post numbers three, and the TROUBLESHOOTING document numbers four. The issue threads themselves are not among the sources used here.
  • The history of the cloud workaround. That TP-Link reviewed the code and declined is stated in the author's post and in the integration's release notes. No account from TP-Link's side was found.
