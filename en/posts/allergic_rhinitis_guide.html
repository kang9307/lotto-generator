Allergic Rhinitis Complete Guide

How it differs from a cold, where you sit on the severity scale, and what to reach for first

Jul 28, 2026 Health & Wellness

Introduction: one in five people has it, and half of them think it is a cold

Almost everyone knows someone who sneezes ten times each morning, gets through a small mountain of tissues, and sleeps with their mouth open because their nose is blocked. That person usually explains it as a cold that drags on every time the seasons change. But if the cold begins in the same month every year and ends in the same month, or if sneezing erupts every single time the bedding gets shaken out, it is not a cold.

According to the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency's national health and nutrition survey, 21.2% of adults aged nineteen and over reported having been diagnosed with allergic rhinitis by a physician as of 2023 (age-standardized to the 2005 projected population). Compared with 16.7% in 2018, the upward trend is clear. The 2023 allergic rhinitis guideline from the Korean Academy of Asthma, Allergy and Clinical Immunology similarly describes roughly one in five adults and about one in three adolescents in Korea as affected. In other words, roughly one reader in five of this article is likely to have the condition personally. Prevalence figures differ by country and by survey method, so treat these numbers as Korean data rather than universal values.

The trouble with a common condition is that inaccurate information hardens into common sense. People who have used a decongestant spray like a household staple for months and ended up more blocked than when they started are common. So are people who refuse first-line therapy because the word steroid frightens them, and people who have spent a fortune on bedding after believing an advertisement that one product solves everything. This article organizes the confusion in the order that actually matters: telling it apart, classifying it, testing, treating, controlling the environment, and recognizing warning signs. The environmental control section in particular separates measures with solid evidence from measures where the evidence is genuinely mixed, because advertising copy and clinical evidence often disagree.

One framing point before we begin. Allergic rhinitis is a condition to be controlled rather than cured. Like blood pressure or atopic dermatitis, the goal is to reduce exposure and suppress inflammation so that daily life is not disrupted. Judged against that goal, the priority order of treatment choices becomes much clearer.

Medical disclaimer
This article is general health information and does not replace medical diagnosis, prescription, or treatment. Nothing here should be used as a reason to start or stop a medication on your own. Decide the choice, dose, and duration of any medicine in consultation with a doctor or pharmacist. Recommendations differ for children, pregnant and breastfeeding people, older adults, and those with chronic illness, so avoid self-medicating and seek specialist care. If symptoms persist for more than two weeks or keep getting worse, see a clinician. If shortness of breath or wheezing develops, seek medical attention without delay. Emergency numbers and drug availability differ by country; in Korea the emergency number is 119.

1. What allergic rhinitis actually is: an IgE reaction in the nasal lining

Allergic rhinitis is an IgE-mediated inflammatory disease of the nasal mucous membranes caused by inhaled allergens, which is essentially how the Korean allergy society guideline defines it. Korea's national health information portal puts it in plainer terms: the lining of the nose reacts excessively to a particular substance, producing bursts of repeated sneezing, clear runny discharge, blockage, and itching.

The mechanism, very briefly, works like this. On first exposure the body produces IgE antibodies against that substance and parks them on the surface of mast cells in the nasal lining, a process called sensitization. When the same substance arrives again it is caught by those antibodies, and the mast cells immediately release histamine and other mediators. What you feel at that moment is sneezing, itching, and watery discharge. This is the early-phase reaction, occurring within minutes of exposure.

Several hours later, something different happens. Eosinophils and other inflammatory cells migrate into the tissue and make it swell. The signature symptom of this late-phase reaction is congestion. That two-stage structure is the key to understanding treatment choices. Antihistamines, which block histamine, work well on the early-phase sneezing, itching, and runny nose but are comparatively weak against blockage. Intranasal corticosteroids, which suppress the influx of inflammatory cells itself, cover congestion as well but take time to reach full effect. A large share of the familiar complaint that the medicine does not work comes not from a bad drug but from a mismatch between the symptom and the drug.

Causative substances divide broadly into perennial and seasonal. House dust mite leads the perennial group, followed by pet dander, cockroach, and mold. Pollen leads the seasonal group. Pollen calendars published by Korea's National Institute of Meteorological Sciences and the national weather service show that tree pollens such as oak, alder, birch, and cedar are generally airborne from April to early June on the mainland and from February to early April on Jeju Island, while weed pollens such as mugwort, ragweed, and Japanese hop run from July through November. Marking on a calendar which window your symptoms cluster in is a surprisingly powerful clue to the cause. If you live outside Korea, look up the pollen calendar for your own region, since the plants and timing differ.

This is the question that comes up most often. A definitive answer belongs to examination and testing, but four axes alone will point you in the right direction: itching, fever, duration, and trigger situation. The two most valuable rows in practice are itching and decisive clue. The family physicians' material specifically notes that nasal and palatal itch, sneezing, and conjunctival irritation are less prominent in nonallergic rhinitis. Under the otolaryngology criteria, acute rhinosinusitis requires purulent discharge together with either obstruction or facial pain and pressure; facial pain alone, without purulent discharge, is not enough to make the diagnosis. Two further patterns suggest bacterial sinusitis: symptoms that do not improve beyond ten days, or a double sickening in which things improve between days five and ten and then get worse again.

Vasomotor rhinitis is the type in which allergy testing is negative yet the nose reacts to nonspecific triggers such as cold air, strong smells, tobacco smoke, temperature change, and spicy food. Discharge and blockage occur, but itching and sneezing are not nearly as prominent as in allergic rhinitis. This distinction matters in practice because the treatment approach differs.

One more nuance is worth knowing. There is a form called local allergic rhinitis, also referred to as entopy, in which skin prick tests and serum specific IgE are both negative yet an allergic reaction occurs locally in the nasal lining. In a meta-analysis covering about 3,400 patients who were negative on both tests, roughly 24.7% had a positive nasal allergen provocation test. In other words, a negative test does not by itself rule allergy out. If your test results and your symptoms disagree, say so at your appointment.

3. Placing yourself on the ARIA classification: duration and severity

Allergic rhinitis used to be split only into seasonal and perennial. That split turned out to be of little use in deciding how hard to treat, because some people with perennial disease sail through it while some with seasonal disease cannot sleep for two months. ARIA (Allergic Rhinitis and its Impact on Asthma), which began as a World Health Organization collaborative initiative, reorganized the picture around two independent yardsticks: duration and severity.

AxisCategoryCriterion
DurationIntermittentSymptoms on fewer than 4 days a week or for fewer than 4 consecutive weeks
PersistentSymptoms on more than 4 days a week and for more than 4 consecutive weeks
SeverityMildNo to all four items below
Moderate-severeYes to one or more of the items below

The four questions that determine severity are listed below. Write them down and answer them for the last two weeks, and you will know where you sit.

  • Is your sleep disturbed, for example waking because your nose is blocked or sleeping with your mouth open?
  • Are daily activities, leisure, or sport affected?
  • Is your performance at school or work affected?
  • Are the symptoms themselves troublesome enough to bother you?

Crossing the two axes produces four classes: mild intermittent, mild persistent, moderate-severe intermittent, and moderate-severe persistent. The reason this classification is practical is that it sets the intensity of treatment and the interval for reassessment. Broadly, the direction of travel is as follows. Actual prescribing, however, is decided by a clinician who also weighs comorbidities, age, and occupation such as whether you drive.

  • Mild intermittent: avoidance measures and symptomatic medication as needed usually suffice, and managing only on symptomatic days can work.
  • Mild persistent: symptoms occur daily, so steady control is needed and avoidance measures carry more weight.
  • Moderate-severe intermittent: when the exposure window is predictable, such as pollen season, starting treatment ahead of it is advantageous.
  • Moderate-severe persistent: inflammation control becomes central, with reassessment after two to four weeks to adjust the step.
  • All classes: the principle is stepwise adjustment, stepping up when control is inadequate and stepping down when it is good.

The real value of classifying yourself is that your consultation gets shorter and sharper. Saying my nose is bad conveys almost nothing. Saying I have had symptoms more than five days a week for two months and I wake every night because I cannot breathe through my nose conveys a great deal.

4. Testing: what it can tell you and what it cannot

The biggest misconception about allergy testing is the expectation that a test reveals the cause. What a test actually tells you is whether your body has produced IgE against that substance, which is sensitization, not whether that substance is producing your symptoms right now. Plenty of people are sensitized and have no symptoms at all. That is why every guideline insists that test results must be interpreted alongside the symptom history.

Choosing between skin prick testing and serum specific IgE is a matter of circumstance rather than superiority. The two are largely interchangeable in practice; European standards documents on skin prick testing and various comparison studies credit skin testing with better sensitivity and faster turnaround, and blood testing with freedom from the constraints of antihistamines, skin disease, and pregnancy. For someone who cannot stop antihistamines, or who has urticaria or severe eczema, the blood test is the realistic option.

When you receive your results there is one question worth asking yourself: do symptoms actually get worse when you are exposed to the substance that came back positive? If dust mite is strongly positive but shaking out a duvet does nothing to you while spring reliably wrecks you, your seasonal pattern is more informative than the strong positive on the report.

5. Step one of treatment: why intranasal corticosteroids come first

The most misunderstood medicine in allergic rhinitis is the intranasal corticosteroid. The word steroid alone creates resistance, but international guidelines put this class first for good reason. The 2020 ARIA guidance favors intranasal medication, either an intranasal corticosteroid or a fixed combination of intranasal corticosteroid and antihistamine, over oral treatment in moderate to severe disease, and the 2020 United States practice parameter for allergic rhinitis likewise places intranasal corticosteroids as first-line. The rationale is a larger effect size and a faster onset of action.

It is important not to confuse these with systemic steroids. Intranasal corticosteroids are formulated to act locally on the nasal lining with low systemic absorption. That does not make them limitlessly safe, and common adverse effects include dryness, irritation, and nosebleeds. Nosebleeds in particular are largely a matter of spray direction.

5.1 Technique: it is the angle, not the drug, that causes nosebleeds

The central point in the family physicians' guidance on nasal spray technique is avoiding the septum, the wall down the middle of the nose. Blood vessels sit close to the surface there, in an area known as Kiesselbach's plexus, so repeated contact with the spray leads to dryness, erosion, and bleeding.

  • Blow your nose gently first to clear the passage.
  • Hold the bottle with the hand opposite the nostril you are treating, so the right hand for the left nostril.
  • Aim the tip up and outward, toward the inner corner of the eye on that side, and never straight at the middle wall.
  • Do not sniff hard while spraying, since forceful inhalation sends the drug down the throat, reducing effect and producing a bitter taste.
  • Avoid blowing your nose forcefully immediately after use.

In one observational study, only 56.67% of patients performed the aim outward step correctly, and failing that step was associated with a statistically significant increase in adverse events. Checking your angle is worth doing before concluding the drug has failed.

5.2 Time to effect: do not give up after two days

Intranasal corticosteroids are not fast-acting rescue medicines. Published sources put the beginning of some effect somewhere between thirty minutes and seven to twelve hours, but maximum effect generally takes two to four weeks. That is why some guidance recommends starting about two weeks before pollen season for seasonal allergic rhinitis. The 2023 Korean allergy society guideline also lists pre-seasonal treatment beginning two weeks ahead of the pollen period as a recommendation, noting that the recommendation is conditional and the certainty of evidence low.

6. Step two: antihistamines, combinations, and leukotriene modifiers

6.1 First versus second generation is a safety distinction, not a convenience one

Dividing antihistamines into generations is not a marketing device; it rests on the practical difference of whether the drug crosses the blood-brain barrier. First-generation agents enter the brain readily and cause sedation, drowsiness, and impaired cognitive and psychomotor function, along with anticholinergic effects such as dry mouth, urinary retention, constipation, and blurred vision. In older adults they can precipitate confusion.

The Canadian Society of Allergy and Clinical Immunology has published a position statement holding that newer-generation H1 antihistamines are safer than first-generation agents and should be the first-line antihistamines for allergic rhinitis and urticaria. The literature documents first-generation antihistamines being linked to car, plane, and boating accidents. Because first-generation ingredients frequently appear in combination cold remedies and over-the-counter sleep aids, checking the ingredient list and asking a pharmacist before driving or operating machinery is the safe move. One survey found that while most pharmacists mentioned sedation, explicit advice against driving was given in only a single case. The practical lesson is that you may have to ask first.

6.2 Combination therapy when single agents fall short

The recommendations of the 2023 Korean allergy society guideline can be summarized as follows. It matters that all four are stated as conditional recommendations with low to very low certainty of evidence, because it would be wrong to relay as certainty what the guideline itself flags as uncertain.

  • A combination of intranasal corticosteroid and intranasal antihistamine may be considered for patients whose response to monotherapy is insufficient.
  • Either an oral antihistamine or an intranasal corticosteroid may be chosen according to patient preference and circumstances.
  • A leukotriene receptor antagonist may be considered for allergic rhinitis patients with coexisting asthma.
  • Pre-seasonal treatment starting two weeks before the pollen period may be considered for seasonal disease.

6.3 Leukotriene modifiers: a regulatory warning you should know

Safety alert: boxed warning on montelukast
On March 3, 2020 the United States Food and Drug Administration required a boxed warning, its strongest warning category, about serious mental health side effects for montelukast, a drug used in asthma and allergy. Reported events include agitation, depression, sleep disturbance, and suicidal thinking and behavior. The agency additionally advised restricting use in allergic rhinitis to patients who have an inadequate response to or cannot tolerate alternative therapies. If changes in mood or behavior appear during treatment, do not make your own judgment; contact the prescribing clinician promptly. This paragraph reports a regulatory safety action and is not an instruction to stop any medication.

7. The decongestant trap: rhinitis medicamentosa

This is the section of the article most likely to prevent real harm. A topical decongestant spray, easily bought over the counter, constricts the blood vessels of the nasal lining and opens the nose within minutes. The effect is so dramatic that people begin keeping one on hand permanently. A substantial number then fall into the trap called rhinitis medicamentosa.

The mechanism is straightforward. With continued use the lining adapts, the duration of relief shortens, and in the gaps between doses the vessels dilate more than before, producing congestion worse than the original. That prompts more frequent spraying, and the loop tightens until breathing without the spray becomes difficult.

  • The US FDA over-the-counter monograph limits continuous use to three days.
  • Some European countries set the limit at seven days, and a practical ceiling of three to five days is commonly quoted.
  • The American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology advises not exceeding three consecutive days.
  • Case series describe rebound congestion appearing as early as the third day of use, and other reports describe it developing gradually over four to six weeks.
  • People who previously had rhinitis medicamentosa have been observed to redevelop rebound signs after only seven days of renewed use, even after a long drug-free interval.
  • Korea's national health information portal likewise lists avoiding long-term use of topical nasal vasoconstrictors among its prevention points for chronic rhinitis.

If you have already been using such a spray daily for weeks, it is safer to see a clinician than to keep stopping and restarting by yourself. Stopping produces a stretch of markedly worse congestion, and how to get through that period, including whether to use an alternative agent or taper one side at a time, belongs to clinical judgment. This article does not describe how to do it.

For completeness, oral decongestants do not cause rhinitis medicamentosa the way sprays do, but they have systemic effects including raised blood pressure, insomnia, and palpitations, so caution applies to people with hypertension, heart disease, thyroid disease, or prostate enlargement. Again, this is a conversation to have with a pharmacist.

8. Immunotherapy: the one treatment that changes the course, over three to five years

Every medicine covered so far suppresses symptoms. Stop the drug and symptoms generally return. Allergen immunotherapy, by contrast, delivers the causative substance in gradually increasing amounts to change the immune response itself, and it is regarded as the only treatment in allergic rhinitis that acts on the course of the disease.

  • Subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT): injections at a medical facility, typically weekly while the concentration is built up over three to five months, then monthly maintenance injections.
  • Sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT): a tablet or drops held under the tongue, taken at home after the first dose is given under observation in a clinical setting.
  • Duration: international guidelines generally recommend three to five years. Studies show that a completed three-year course sustains clinical and immunological benefit for at least two to three years after treatment ends.
  • Effectiveness: Korea's national health information portal states that immunotherapy works particularly well in children and that 70 to 80% see benefit after three to five years of treatment.
  • Indication: considered when the causative allergen has been clearly identified by testing and history and when avoidance plus medication has not achieved adequate control.
  • Limitations: it does not work equally well for everyone. The injected route can rarely cause systemic reactions, so patients are observed for a period after each dose. When causes are multiple or unclear, expected benefit falls.

In short, immunotherapy is powerful but slow and not for everyone. Whether you can commit to three years and whether the cause narrows down to one or two allergens are the decisive considerations, and these are matters for specialist allergy care.

9. Environmental control: separate the strong evidence from the mixed

Environmental control looks like the zone with no side effects and little cost, but in reality the quality of evidence differs sharply from item to item. Bedding covers are the classic example of a gap between advertising and evidence. A Cochrane review (updated 2012, nine trials, 501 participants) concluded that house dust mite impermeable bedding used as an isolated intervention did not demonstrate clinical benefit. It found that acaricides and extensive bedroom-based environmental control programs may offer some reduction in rhinitis symptoms, while also stating plainly that the trials were generally small and of poor methodological quality, making definitive recommendations difficult.

The table boils down to this. Reducing actual exposure is the right direction, but the expectation that one product solves the problem has weak support. Buying a bedding cover is not a mistake; pinning your hopes on it and postponing medical treatment is. The practical implication of the Cochrane review is closer to do several things together than to any single purchase.

Saline irrigation attracts particular confusion. The 2018 Cochrane review concluded that it is uncertain whether adding irrigation to pharmacologic treatment improves symptoms, and that whether it is better or worse than an intranasal steroid is not known. It did find that irrigation is unlikely to be associated with adverse effects. So it is reasonable to try as an adjunct because the downside is small, but any plan to replace medication with it has no evidence behind it. Use hygienically prepared solution and keep the device clean.

10. Children, pregnancy, and older adults: the advice is not the same

This is exactly where allergic rhinitis information becomes dangerous. Adult-level guidance must not be transferred wholesale to a child or a pregnant person.

  • Children: the effect of intranasal corticosteroids on growth differs by molecule. One agent showed no growth retardation in children with perennial allergic rhinitis over a year of use, while another was associated with mean growth velocity 0.27 cm per year lower than placebo over a year. Meta-analysis concludes that short-term measurements may show reduced growth velocity while the effect on longer-term growth remains unclear. The practical implication is the lowest effective dose, regular height measurement, and adjustment through pediatric or ENT care. Caregivers should not increase doses on their own or split adult products for a child.
  • First-generation antihistamines in children: drowsiness and cognitive impairment directly affect learning and safety, so checking the ingredients of combination cold remedies matters especially here.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding: some agents have comparatively large accumulated safety data and the literature is broadly reassuring, but which medicine to use must be decided in consultation with obstetric care. Nothing in this article should be read as a recommendation to use a specific agent during pregnancy. Avoid self-medicating.
  • Older adults: the anticholinergic effects of first-generation antihistamines connect to confusion, urinary retention, and fall risk. Interaction checks matter for anyone on multiple medications.
  • Hypertension, heart disease, glaucoma, prostate enlargement: combination cold remedies containing oral decongestants require caution. Tell the pharmacist about underlying conditions before purchase.
  • Drivers and machine operators: avoid driving on a day you have taken a sedating medicine. Mentioning your occupation at the appointment can change which drug is chosen.

11. Warning signs: nasal symptoms that should not be dismissed as rhinitis

Allergic rhinitis is usually not a serious disease, but there are cases where what looked like rhinitis was something else. The items below are signals that nasal symptoms should be assessed rather than assumed to be allergy. They recur consistently in general practice literature and in ENT referral criteria.

  • One side only is persistently blocked, which is different from the two sides alternating.
  • Blood-stained discharge from one side only lasting more than three weeks.
  • Unilateral blockage accompanied by facial pain, orbital symptoms, or double vision.
  • Altered facial sensation or facial nerve palsy.
  • Persistent loss of the sense of smell.
  • Clear watery fluid running from one side only and worsening with position change, particularly after trauma or surgery.
  • Purulent discharge and facial pressure persisting beyond ten days without improvement, or improving and then worsening again.
  • No response at all over two to four weeks to treatment being used correctly.
  • Using a decongestant nasal spray daily for more than a week.
  • Nasal symptoms accompanied by cough, wheeze, or breathlessness on exertion, raising the possibility of coexisting asthma.
Situations needing immediate help
Breathlessness severe enough to interrupt speech, blue lips or fingernails, a sensation of the throat closing, or widespread hives together with dizziness may indicate an emergency beyond allergic rhinitis. Seek emergency care without delay; in Korea the emergency number is 119, and in other countries use your local emergency number.

12. The atopic march: the nose and the lungs are one airway

There is a good reason not to treat allergic rhinitis as a problem confined to the nose. The tendency for atopic dermatitis in infancy to be followed by food allergy, asthma, and allergic rhinitis is called the atopic march. The literature is careful to add that this sequence is not conspicuous in every child. It is an observed tendency, not a fixed destiny.

Clinically more important is the united airway perspective, which treats the upper and lower airways as a single system. Allergic rhinitis is a recognized independent risk factor for asthma, and it frequently precedes asthma. In children with asthma, coexisting allergic rhinitis has been reported to increase the risk of exacerbations leading to hospitalization or emergency visits, along with health-care costs. That context explains why the Korean allergy society guideline suggests a leukotriene receptor antagonist specifically for rhinitis patients who also have asthma.

The practical conclusion is simple. If you thought you only had nasal symptoms but your cough worsens at night, you get breathless with exercise, or you hear wheezing, say so at your appointment. General principles for managing asthma are covered separately in our asthma management guide.

14. Frequently asked questions

Q. How do I tell a cold from allergic rhinitis?

Itching and duration are the most useful clues. Itchy nose, eyes, and palate with clear discharge and bursts of sneezing, and no fever or aching, points to allergic rhinitis. A sore throat first, low fever and muscle aches, discharge thickening after a few days and settling within seven to ten days, is the classic course of a cold. The two can overlap, so if symptoms recur or drag on, have them assessed.

Q. Why does a decongestant spray make my nose worse over time?

The lining adapts to the vasoconstriction, relief shortens, and rebound congestion fills the gaps. That is rhinitis medicamentosa. The FDA over-the-counter standard is three consecutive days and practical advice commonly caps it at three to five. If you have been using one for a long time, see a clinician rather than repeatedly stopping and restarting.

Q. Why does my intranasal steroid not work immediately?

It calms inflammation rather than constricting vessels, so it behaves differently. Some effect begins within hours, but maximum benefit generally takes two to four weeks. For seasonal disease some guidance suggests starting about two weeks before the pollen season. Decide dose and duration with your clinician.

Q. Should I have a skin test or a blood test?

It is a matter of circumstance, not superiority. Skin prick testing gives results in fifteen to twenty minutes at lower cost but requires stopping antihistamines and is affected by skin condition. Serum specific IgE needs only a blood draw and works during antihistamine use, but results take days. Either way, a positive result is not proof of cause.

Q. Does immunotherapy make allergic rhinitis go away?

Immunotherapy is regarded as acting on the underlying response rather than only suppressing symptoms, but international guidelines recommend three to five years and it does not work equally well for everyone. It is considered when the causative allergen is clearly identified, and whether and how to start is a decision for specialist allergy care.

Conclusion: the goal is control, not closure

The most useful attitude toward allergic rhinitis is setting expectations accurately. The condition can return at any time while exposure continues, which is why no treatment promises permanent closure. What good management can reach, however, is a genuinely attainable goal: sleeping undisturbed, keeping your concentration through the day, and no longer dreading the same season each year.

Four lines summarize what this article has repeated. First, cold and allergy separate on itching, fever, duration, and repeating pattern. Second, being able to describe yourself on ARIA's two axes changes the quality of your consultation. Third, intranasal corticosteroids are first-line and take two to four weeks to reach maximum effect, so do not give up after two days. Fourth, continuous use of a decongestant nasal spray stops at three to five days. That last sentence is the single line in this article most likely to prevent harm.

On environmental control, one sentence suffices: reducing exposure is the right direction, but expecting one product to solve the problem has weak support. Wash the bedding, manage humidity, apply several avoidance measures together, and do not try to replace medical treatment with any of it. That is what the evidence actually supports.

Finally, this article can only draw a map; it cannot look inside your nose. If symptoms persist beyond two weeks or keep worsening, or if anything on the warning list above applies to you, see a clinician. Bringing a short note of when it started, how often it happens, and in which situations it worsens will improve the quality of the assessment by itself.
