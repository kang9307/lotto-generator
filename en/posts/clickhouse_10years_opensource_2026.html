ClickHouse at 10: How a Columnar Analytical DB Became the Open-Source OLAP Standard

From Yandex.Metrica in 2016 to $250M ARR in 2026 — the engineering behind the speed and the honest limits

2026-06-22 Dev & Infra

Introduction: What Ten Years Built

On June 15, 2026, ClickHouse marked exactly ten years since Yandex released it under the Apache 2.0 license. The number itself is less interesting than what accumulated across those ten years.

As of June 2026, the project counts 48,000 GitHub stars, 2,632 contributors, $250M in annualized recurring revenue, and 4,000 paying customers. Its Series D round raised $400M at a $15B valuation. OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Netflix, Tesla, Shopify, Visa, and Cisco run it in production.

But the question worth answering is not how large the numbers got. It is why ClickHouse is fast, where it actually belongs, and where it falls short. This post replaces marketing recaps with an engineer's view of the internals and tradeoffs — accurate as of June 2026.

1. The Origin: A Specific Problem Demanded a Specific Engine

ClickHouse's roots trace to 2009. Alexey Milovidov and colleagues at Yandex were building Yandex.Metrica, a web analytics service, and hit a wall. The core hypothesis they needed to test: can you generate real-time analytical reports from non-aggregated, continuously-arriving raw data?

Existing tools offered two dead ends. Pre-aggregate everything — fast to query, but you lose all flexibility for ad-hoc questions. Or use a general-purpose RDBMS — flexible, but crushingly slow at billions of events. Neither fit.

Three years of development later, in 2012, ClickHouse shipped to Yandex.Metrica production. In 2016 it went open source. What is worth noting for understanding ClickHouse's design philosophy: it was never built to be a general-purpose database. It was purpose-built for a specific problem — high-throughput append workloads with fast column-level aggregations. That specificity is the source of its speed, and also the boundary of its applicability.

2. Why It Is Fast — Three Mechanisms That Multiply

ClickHouse's performance advantage comes from three properties that are individually meaningful but compounding in combination.

2.1 Columnar Storage: Only Read What the Query Needs

Conventional RDBMS store data row by row. Every row is a contiguous block of all columns. When a query asks for the average purchase amount over the last 30 days, the database reads every row, including every column it does not need, just to reach the two or three columns it does.

ClickHouse stores data column by column. Every value of a single column is stored contiguously in its own file (.bin). A query touching three columns out of fifty reads roughly 6% of the data that a row store would read. On wide-table analytics queries, this I/O reduction routinely exceeds 90%.

No matter how fast NVMe SSDs get, not reading data is faster than reading it.

2.2 Compression: Same-Type Values Compress Extremely Well

Columnar layout has a second benefit: compression efficiency. When a column holds a million country codes, the data looks like "KR, KR, KR, US, KR, JP..." — highly repetitive, same data type. LZ4 and ZSTD algorithms exploit these patterns to achieve 5–10x compression ratios that row-mixed storage cannot approach.

The practical consequence: ClickBench 2026 shows ClickHouse stores the standard 100 GB Hits dataset in 14.26 GiB — the smallest footprint of any engine in the benchmark. Smaller storage means less I/O, more data fits in memory, and cache hit rates improve. The advantage compounds before a query even begins computing.

2.3 Vectorized Execution: SIMD at Every Step

Modern CPUs contain SIMD (Single Instruction, Multiple Data) instruction sets — AVX-512, for instance, can operate on 512 bits (64 bytes) simultaneously. A single instruction can add eight 64-bit integers at once instead of one.

To exploit SIMD, data must be in contiguous memory arrays. ClickHouse processes queries in large columnar blocks that map directly onto these arrays. Row-based processing scatters column values across memory, breaking SIMD alignment. Columnar block processing gives the CPU exactly the layout it needs for SIMD acceleration at every stage of a query: filtering, projection, aggregation.

The three effects — fewer bytes read, smaller data, faster per-byte compute — are multiplicative. Aggregations that take minutes on a row store finish in milliseconds. ClickBench documents this: ClickHouse is fastest on the majority of 43 queries, with sub-millisecond responses on point-style filters and single-digit-second times on the heaviest aggregations.

3. MergeTree — The Engine That Defines the Constraints

ClickHouse's core storage engine is MergeTree. Understanding it clarifies both the capabilities and the hard limits.

3.1 How MergeTree Works

Data arrives in parts — sorted chunks written to disk according to the table's ORDER BY key. A background process continuously merges smaller parts into larger ones, maintaining sort order. This LSM-tree-inspired design is optimized for append-mostly workloads: high insert throughput, infrequent updates or deletes.

The sparse primary index only stores the first row of each granule (a fixed row interval, default 8,192 rows). This keeps the index small enough to live in memory while still allowing fast range lookups. The tradeoff: arbitrary point lookups on non-indexed columns involve scanning granules rather than jumping directly to a row.

3.2 The Design Constraints That Follow

The MergeTree design imposes non-negotiable constraints. UPDATE and DELETE operations are implemented as background mutations — asynchronous, not immediately consistent, and expensive relative to their cost in transactional databases. Frequent point modifications are a performance anti-pattern.

The choice of ORDER BY key is load-bearing. Query performance varies by orders of magnitude depending on how well the sort key matches the query's filter and aggregation columns. This means teams need to understand their access patterns at schema design time. Engineers accustomed to general-purpose RDBMS flexibility will require an adjustment period.

4. Where ClickHouse Belongs in Practice

Three workload categories define ClickHouse's practical territory as of June 2026.

4.1 Real-Time Analytics Dashboards

The canonical ClickHouse workload is high-insert, high-aggregation, low-latency: ad click analytics, e-commerce event funnels, financial transaction monitoring, fraud detection. The pattern is Kafka for ingestion, ClickHouse for storage and query, Grafana or Superset for visualization.

What makes ClickHouse unusual here is that it handles the write and read workloads simultaneously without sacrificing either. Ingesting millions of rows per second while serving millisecond analytical queries on the same dataset in the same engine used to be an architectural impossibility. ClickHouse makes it operational.

4.2 Log Management

Log data is a near-perfect ClickHouse workload: append-only writes, column-oriented queries (filter by time, service, error code; then count or aggregate), and highly compressible repetitive values. Storage savings versus Elasticsearch typically reach 5–10x for the same data at the same retention period.

ClickHouse's inroads into Elasticsearch's log market follow a simple value proposition: if your primary log queries are aggregations rather than full-text search, ClickHouse is faster and cheaper. If you rely heavily on full-text search or fuzzy matching, Elasticsearch remains superior. Both tools serve different query shapes.

4.3 Observability

ClickStack, launched in 2025, unifies logs, traces, metrics, and session replay under an OpenTelemetry-native stack built on ClickHouse. The architectural claim it makes is direct: observability in 2026 is a high-performance analytics problem, not a data collection problem. The goal is to store all raw telemetry without pre-aggregation and answer any exploratory question in real time.

OpenAI and Netflix chose ClickHouse for observability at hyperscale. The acquisition of Langfuse — an LLM observability platform — signals that AI infrastructure observability is becoming a primary growth vector for ClickHouse: AI models generate logs, traces, and evaluation metrics at enormous volume; storing and querying that data efficiently is a solved problem with ClickHouse.

6. Strengths and Limitations Without Spin

6.1 Genuine Strengths

Raw aggregation speed: No open-source analytical engine reliably beats ClickHouse on ClickBench as of June 2026. Compression efficiency: 14.26 GiB for 100 GB input means lower hardware costs and better cache utilization. Ecosystem velocity: 277 features, 319 performance improvements, 1,051 bug fixes across 12 releases in 2025 alone demonstrates a healthy, active project. Deployment flexibility: Self-hosted open source, or ClickHouse Cloud (fully managed). Proven at scale: OpenAI, Meta, Netflix, Anthropic validate production viability at hyperscale.

6.2 Honest Limitations

Streaming ingestion: Native pull-from-Kafka is immature; you need connectors or tools like Vector, Fluent Bit, or the Kafka table engine with careful batching. True real-time stream processing is more natural in Druid or Pinot. Joins: Complex multi-table normalized joins are slow. ClickHouse recommends denormalized flat tables, which requires schema design discipline teams used to RDBMS may find uncomfortable. Distributed operations: Sharding, replication, and rebalancing require manual intervention and dedicated SRE time at scale. At 500+ nodes, ClickHouse demands more operational attention than Druid or Pinot. Point mutations: Updates and deletes are asynchronous background mutations, not atomic row operations. Workloads with frequent per-row modifications should not use ClickHouse as their primary store.

6.3 Decision Framework

The honest heuristic: if your workload is insert-heavy, read-many, rarely-update, column-oriented aggregation — logs, event streams, web analytics, observability data — ClickHouse deserves first consideration in 2026. If you need transactional consistency, frequent updates, or complex multi-table joins as the primary pattern, PostgreSQL or a dedicated RDBMS is the right anchor. For local exploratory analytics under a terabyte with no infrastructure overhead, DuckDB starts in seconds.

7. The AI Trajectory: What the Next Decade Looks Like

ClickHouse's 2026 positioning goes beyond fast queries. At Open House 2026, the company announced Claude-powered natural language agents that generate and interpret ClickHouse queries from plain text. The target: eliminate the SQL expertise barrier between the people who have data and the people who need answers.

The Langfuse acquisition signals where growth comes from next. LLM inference generates logs, traces, token counts, and latency metrics at massive scale. Storing and querying that telemetry efficiently is an unsolved infrastructure problem for most AI teams. ClickHouse's combination of high insert throughput, low-latency aggregation, and extreme compression makes it a natural fit for AI observability infrastructure.

The engine Yandex built for web analytics in 2009 is becoming the analytical backbone of AI infrastructure in 2026. The specificity that defined its architecture turned out to generalize further than anyone anticipated.

Conclusion: Know the Speed, Know the Limits

ClickHouse is not magic. Columnar storage eliminates unnecessary I/O. Compression shrinks the data. SIMD-based vectorized execution accelerates computation. These three mechanisms multiply, and ten years of community engineering — 2,600 contributors, 48,000 GitHub stars, 1,600-plus improvements in 2025 alone — has refined them to the point where no open-source OLAP engine matches ClickHouse on raw query speed for its target workload class.

The same design philosophy draws a clear boundary. Append-mostly, column-oriented aggregation, denormalized schemas — that is ClickHouse territory. Outside that boundary, other tools serve better. Understanding why ClickHouse is fast also reveals exactly where to stop using it.

Ten years is a meaningful threshold for open-source infrastructure projects. The decade ahead will layer AI-native interfaces on top of the same columnar foundation. The question is not whether ClickHouse will remain relevant — the $15B valuation and hyperscaler adoption answer that — but whether the teams adopting it will invest in understanding the tradeoffs that make it fast. That understanding is what separates a successful deployment from an expensive lesson.
