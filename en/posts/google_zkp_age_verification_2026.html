Google's Zero-Knowledge Proof (ZKP) Age Verification - Proving You're an Adult Without Revealing Your Birthdate or Identity

What the open-source Longfellow library gets right about privacy-preserving age checks - and where it still falls short

Jul 4, 2026 Tech & IT

Intro: Why Age Verification Became a Privacy Problem

As I laid out yesterday in EU Mandatory Age Verification: What Is the Problem?, mandatory age checks meant to protect minors are spreading fast worldwide. The trouble is the method. Most age verification today means uploading a full ID photo or estimating age from a face scan, and in the process, unwanted data - birthdate, name, address - travels wholesale to the service provider. A process built to protect kids ends up amplifying the risk of personal data exposure. That is the paradox.

Google's answer to that paradox is zero-knowledge proof (ZKP)-based age verification. The core claim is simple: a website receives only the single fact "this person is an adult," and the birthdate or identity data used to prove it never reaches the server at all. Based on Google's official announcements and the underlying technical documentation, this post unpacks how ZKP actually works, what was really released, how far adoption has come, and - from my own vantage point - what remains unfinished.

1. What Is a Zero-Knowledge Proof? The Paradox of Proving Without Revealing

1.1 An Analogy That Makes It Click

A common analogy for ZKP: hand a colorblind friend two balls that look identical - one red, one green - and, through repeated tests, convince them the balls are different colors without ever telling them which is which. However many times you repeat it, the friend ends up certain "the two balls differ," but never learns the color itself. Map that onto age verification: the service becomes certain that the claim "this person is 18 or older" is true, but never gets its hands on the actual birthdate or a copy of the ID.

1.2 Why It Became Practical Only Recently

The concept of ZKP itself is decades old, dating to 1980s academic cryptography. The obstacle was computational cost - generating and verifying a proof in real time on a phone or in a browser used to be far too slow and heavy. Over the past few years, efficient proving protocols such as Ligero and the Sum-Check protocol have matured to the point where they can handle existing signature standards (ECDSA) and hash functions (SHA-256) at practical speed. Google's Longfellow ZK is the direct product of that maturation.

2. What Google Actually Released - The Longfellow ZK Library

2.1 What Was Open-Sourced

On July 3, 2025, Google's official blog announced it was open-sourcing a ZKP library. The post put it plainly: 「a person visiting a website can verifiably prove they are over 18」 (Google's official blog, 2025-07-03). The code lives in the google/longfellow-zk repository on GitHub. This was not a bolt from the blue - it followed Google integrating ZKP age verification into Google Wallet in May 2025 and piloting it with the dating app Bumble.

2.2 The Technical Backbone - Reusing Existing ID Infrastructure

What makes Longfellow practically clever is that it demands no entirely new credential system. An existing electronic ID such as a mobile driver's license (mDL) built on the ISO/IEC 18013-5 standard already carries an ECDSA (P-256) signature and a SHA-256 hash. Longfellow verifies those values inside a zero-knowledge circuit and extracts only the fact that "this mDL's age_over_18 attribute is true." Governments and issuers need not build new infrastructure at all - it is a privacy layer laid on top of ID credentials that already exist.

2.3 Where the Name Comes From, and a Year of Progress

Longfellow is reportedly named after the Longfellow Bridge near Google's Cambridge office. The timeline runs: May 2025, ZKP age verification launches in Google Wallet with Bumble as partner; July 2025, Longfellow ZK goes open-source; Germany's Sparkasse savings-bank network becomes the first national credential partner for EU age assurance; June 2026, passport-scan-based digital IDs and age credentials expand to Estonia, Ireland, Spain, France, and Italy. In roughly a year since open-sourcing, the project reached the early stage of commercial rollout.

3. Why Now - ZKP Versus the EU's Age Verification Mandate

3.1 Where This Connects to Yesterday's Piece

The EU's eIDAS regulation, taking effect from 2026, encourages member states to build privacy-preserving technologies like ZKP into the European Digital Identity Wallet (EUDI Wallet). The core tension I raised yesterday about mandatory age verification in the EU was that regulatory pressure demanding stronger identity checks collides head-on with users' need for privacy. Google's ZKP release is a technical answer aimed squarely at that collision.

3.2 The Structural Flaw in Today's Age-Verification Methods

Widely used age verification today splits into two approaches: uploading an ID photo for matching, and estimating age from a facial scan. Both send raw personal data - photos, birthdates, facial biometrics - to the service provider's servers during verification, and users have no control over whether that data leaks or gets reused for other purposes. The core problem is an asymmetry: to confirm one fact (age), you hand over an entire trove of far more sensitive information.

3.3 What ZKP Actually Changes

Under ZKP, the verifier receives exactly one true/false claim. The table below makes the contrast concrete.

4. Real-World Effectiveness and Adoption Hurdles - Still an Experiment

4.1 April 2026: A Switch Flipped Only in the Demo Build

Here is the part that deserves an honest asterisk. In April 2026, an experimental Longfellow-based ZKP implementation appeared in the EU's age-verification Android wallet app - but it was enabled only in a demo build variant, and disabled in both the default development build and the actual shipped release. In other words, a regular user downloading the app from an app store does not yet get the privacy benefit of ZKP in practice. There is still real distance between "released" and "used in daily life."

4.2 Points That Deserve Scrutiny

Security researchers raise concerns that are not trivial. First, proving a narrow range like "between 20 and 21" can effectively leak the exact age. Second, a "phone home" structure lets issuers track credential usage, concentrating gatekeeping power in a handful of authorities - a centralization risk. Third, roughly 850 million people worldwide lack formal identification and could be excluded from this system entirely. Fourth, parsing and soundness gaps mean a prover and verifier can interpret malformed input differently. Fifth, fragmented, vendor- and country-specific standards undermine interoperability. ZKP is mathematically elegant, but these five issues are operational and political, not mathematical - no single library resolves them.

4.3 What the IETF Standardization Push Signals

Google is not keeping this locked inside its own ecosystem - it has submitted a draft, draft-google-cfrg-libzk, to the IETF's Crypto Forum Research Group (CFRG) to pursue international standardization. That reads as more than an open-source release; it is an attempt to turn Longfellow's proving scheme into a vendor-neutral internet standard. Full standardization could take years, but the direction is clearly shifting from "Google's technology" toward "an industry-wide specification."

5. The Future of Digital Identity - My Read on the Landscape

5.1 Where This Could Meet Browser Standards

For ZKP to really deliver, it needs to plug into standard APIs at the browser and OS level. If a browser feature such as the W3C's Digital Credentials API matures, a website could send an "adult verification required" request and a wallet app could return only the ZKP proof - a much smoother flow. Today the landscape is fragmented across platforms like Google Wallet and the EUDI Wallet, but I expect it to converge, over the long run, on an OS-agnostic standard interface.

5.2 What This Signals for Identity-Verification Markets Elsewhere

Many countries, including Korea, already run dense identity-verification infrastructure through banking and telecom-linked authentication services. What strikes me as the interesting friction point is that a meaningful share of these providers' revenue comes from confirming and monetizing user data as a service. An approach like ZKP - handing verifiers no raw data whatsoever - collides directly with that business model. I expect adoption to start not with the incumbent identity providers themselves, but in narrow yes/no use cases such as blocking harmful content for minors or straightforward adult verification.

5.3 What to Watch Over the Next Few Years

As of July 2026, ZKP age verification has moved past proof-of-concept into the early stage of commercial expansion. But if the five criticisms above go unresolved, it risks staying in a state of "the technology exists, but nobody fully trusts it." Three things worth watching over the next three to five years: (1) whether IETF standardization actually completes, (2) whether the EU's EUDI Wallet turns on ZKP in its shipped release rather than just a demo, and (3) whether an inclusive alternative is built for the population without formal ID. Only once all three are in place can ZKP graduate from "a clever paper idea" to "infrastructure everyone actually uses."

Conclusion

Google's release of Longfellow ZK matters because it turns a long-theoretical promise - confirm age, ask nothing else - into code that runs on real, already-issued ID infrastructure. At the same time, the fact that even the EU's own app was, as of April 2026, still confined to a demo build is an honest signal that this technology sits somewhere between the lab and daily life. As mandatory age verification spreads worldwide, ZKP is not the only answer, but it is fair to call it one of the most credible privacy-preserving alternatives on the table. Its real-world payoff, though, hinges less on the elegance of the library and more on the much duller work of standardization and identity inclusiveness.
