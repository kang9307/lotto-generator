Hypothyroidism & Hyperthyroidism - A Complete Guide to Symptoms, Diagnosis & Care

Why a butterfly-shaped gland in your neck steers your whole metabolism

Jun 10, 2026 Health & Wellness

Introduction: the hidden conductor of fatigue and weight change

If you feel tired for no clear reason, keep gaining or unexpectedly losing weight, are unusually sensitive to cold or heat, or notice a racing heart and trembling hands, there is one organ worth suspecting. It is the butterfly-shaped thyroid, sitting at the front of your neck just below the voice box. It weighs only about 20 grams, yet the hormones it makes regulate the metabolic rate of nearly every cell in your body - from heartbeat to temperature, weight, mood, and digestion.

Thyroid disease is tricky because its symptoms are both common and vague. Signs like fatigue, weight change, and low mood are so ordinary that they are easy to dismiss as "just aging or stress." But behind them, a shortage of thyroid hormone (hypothyroidism) or an excess of it (hyperthyroidism) is not rare - and, reassuringly, most cases are confirmed with a simple blood test and controlled well with treatment.

This guide lays out, on an evidence basis, what the thyroid does, the difference and symptoms of hypo- and hyperthyroidism, causes such as Hashimoto's and Graves', how to read diagnostic numbers like TSH, T4, T3, and antibodies, the principles of treatment (hormone replacement, antithyroid drugs, iodine), and cautions around diet, pregnancy, and menopause. It does not make decisions for you - it is a map for a smarter conversation with your doctor about your own results.

⚠️ Medical disclaimer. This article is for education and information only and is not medical advice. Interpreting thyroid hormone or antibody values, diagnosis, deciding whether to start treatment, and the choice or dose of medication must be decided with a physician (such as an endocrinologist). Appropriate targets and treatment vary greatly by age, sex, pregnancy status, history, and coexisting conditions. The figures here (test references) are general reference values based on published guidelines; normal ranges can differ by laboratory, time, and individual, and this is not an instruction to self-prescribe any drug, iodine, or diet. If you have symptoms or are at a point of change such as pregnancy or menopause, do not delay - see a medical professional.

1. What the thyroid is - your body's metabolic accelerator

The thyroid is an endocrine gland that spreads like a butterfly across both sides of the windpipe, just below the larynx at the front of the neck. Its job is simple but powerful: using dietary iodine as raw material, it makes thyroid hormone, releases it into the blood, and lets that hormone set the metabolic pace of cells throughout the body.

1.1 T4 and T3 - the two thyroid hormones

The thyroid secretes two main hormones. T4 (thyroxine), which makes up most of the output, is a kind of "storage/reserve" form, while the form that acts strongly in cells is the active T3 (triiodothyronine). T4 is converted to T3 as needed in the liver, kidneys, and elsewhere. In testing, it is more accurate to look at the unbound, active forms - free T4 and free T3.

1.2 TSH - the brain's signal to the thyroid

The thyroid does not act alone. The brain's pituitary gland releases TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) to control it. The key is that this relationship works by negative feedback. When thyroid hormone is low, the brain raises TSH to say "make more"; when it is high, the brain lowers TSH to say "enough." Because of this, a single TSH value sensitively reflects whether the thyroid is under- or over-producing, which is why TSH is the most basic screening test.

Core intuition: thyroid hormone is the "metabolic accelerator," and TSH is "how hard the brain presses the pedal." When hormone runs short, the brain presses harder and TSH rises.

2. Hypo- vs hyperthyroidism - two opposite faces

The two great axes of thyroid disease are too little hormone (hypothyroidism) and too much hormone (hyperthyroidism). Depending on whether metabolism slows or speeds up, the symptoms appear almost like a mirror image.

2.1 Hypothyroidism - when metabolism slows

With too little hormone, the whole body shifts into "low gear." Typical features include chronic fatigue and sluggishness, weight gain, cold sensitivity, constipation, dry skin, hair loss, swelling (especially of the face and around the eyes), a slow pulse (bradycardia), memory or mood decline, and heavy periods. Because it progresses slowly, it often worsens gradually without the person noticing.

2.2 Hyperthyroidism - when metabolism speeds up

With too much hormone, the body goes into "overdrive." Common features are weight loss (even with a bigger appetite), heat sensitivity and sweating, palpitations and a fast heart rate, hand tremor, anxiety, irritability, insomnia, diarrhea or frequent stools, muscle weakness, and irregular periods. In Graves' disease, eye bulging or irritation and an enlarged thyroid (goiter) at the front of the neck may accompany it.

That said, these symptoms overlap broadly with other conditions (anemia, depression, menopause, heart disease, and more). So you cannot confirm or rule out thyroid disease "by symptoms alone" - it must be verified with a blood test.

3. Why it happens - autoimmunity, nodules, drugs, and more

The causes of thyroid dysfunction are varied, but the two most common are autoimmune diseases - conditions in which the immune system mistakenly attacks the thyroid rather than an outside invader.

3.1 Hashimoto's thyroiditis - the leading cause of hypothyroidism

The most common cause of hypothyroidism is Hashimoto's thyroiditis (chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis). Immune cells and antibodies chronically attack the thyroid, causing inflammation; the gland is slowly damaged and hormone production declines. TPO (thyroid peroxidase) antibodies are often positive, and because it progresses slowly, drifting into hypothyroidism over years is common.

3.2 Graves' disease - the leading cause of hyperthyroidism

The most common cause of hyperthyroidism is Graves' disease. Here, antibodies (TSH-receptor antibodies, TRAb) continuously stimulate the thyroid into making excess hormone. It is characterized by possible eye bulging or eye symptoms (thyroid eye disease) and a goiter at the front of the neck.

3.3 Other causes

  • Thyroid nodules/tumors: "functioning nodules" (toxic adenoma, toxic multinodular goiter) that overproduce hormone on their own can cause hyperthyroidism.
  • Thyroiditis: subacute (post-viral) or postpartum thyroiditis can cause a temporary phase of overactivity followed by underactivity.
  • Too much or too little iodine: either extreme can trigger dysfunction.
  • Drugs/treatments: some medications, prior thyroid surgery or radioactive iodine therapy, and head-and-neck radiation can cause hypothyroidism.
  • Congenital/pituitary issues: rarely, a pituitary problem causes abnormal TSH secretion (secondary disease).

Thyroid disease tends to be more common in women and when there is a family history. So if a relative has thyroid disease or you have related symptoms, it is wise not to put off testing.

4. How to read the numbers - TSH, T4, T3, antibodies

The starting point and core of thyroid diagnosis is the blood test. Usually TSH is checked first, and thyroid hormones and antibodies are added as needed.

4.1 Why TSH comes first

Because TSH responds most sensitively to changes in thyroid function, it is widely used as the first-line screening test. Due to the feedback described earlier, TSH is high in primary hypothyroidism and low in hyperthyroidism. When TSH falls outside the normal range, free T4 (and T3 if needed) is measured to confirm the actual hormone level and severity.

The so-called "subclinical" states are borderline zones where only TSH is slightly off while hormones are normal. Not all are treated immediately; the doctor decides treatment or monitoring by weighing the degree of the value, symptoms, age, pregnancy status, and antibody positivity.

4.3 Antibody tests - sorting out the cause

Antibodies help reveal the "cause" of dysfunction. Positive TPO antibodies (and thyroglobulin antibodies) suggest autoimmune thyroiditis such as Hashimoto's, while positive TSH-receptor antibodies (TRAb) make Graves' disease (hyperthyroidism) more likely. If needed, thyroid ultrasound and a radioactive iodine uptake test or scan further distinguish nodules, inflammation, and the cause of overactivity.

⚠️ A note on interpretation. Normal ranges differ by laboratory, assay, pregnancy status, and age, and the patterns shown here are general descriptions. Because the trend, symptoms, antibodies, and imaging matter more than a single value, always interpret your report with a doctor. Pregnant patients in particular have separate trimester-specific references.

5. Treatment 1 - hypothyroidism: hormone replacement

Treating hypothyroidism is simple at its core: replace the missing hormone with medication.

5.1 Levothyroxine (synthetic T4)

The standard treatment is taking levothyroxine, a synthetic T4, daily. In the body it plays the same role as the natural hormone and is converted to T3 as needed. The dose is set by the doctor based on weight, age, heart status, pregnancy, and TSH, and is usually fine-tuned by retesting TSH after a set period. Hypothyroidism from Hashimoto's often requires lifelong treatment.

5.2 How to take it

Levothyroxine absorption is affected by food and some drugs and supplements (such as iron and calcium). It is usually advised to take it on an empty stomach in the morning with water, separated in time from meals and other medications, and to take it consistently the same way every day. Stopping or changing the dose on your own can bring symptoms back or cause overtreatment, so any adjustment must be made with your doctor.

6. Treatment 2 - hyperthyroidism: three ways to bring it down

Because hyperthyroidism is a state of "too much" hormone, treatment aims to suppress or reduce production. Depending on cause, severity, age, and pregnancy, there are broadly three options.

6.1 Antithyroid drugs (medication)

Antithyroid drugs such as methimazole and propylthiouracil (PTU) suppress thyroid hormone synthesis. They are commonly tried first in Graves' disease, with the hope of remission after a period of use. The choice may differ by timing (for example, early pregnancy), and rare side effects involving the liver or white blood cells make regular monitoring and a doctor's direction essential. A beta-blocker is sometimes added to ease symptoms such as palpitations.

6.2 Radioactive iodine therapy

When radioactive iodine is taken, thyroid cells selectively absorb it, shrinking the overactive tissue. It is effective, but over time many people develop hypothyroidism and later need hormone replacement. It is contraindicated during pregnancy and breastfeeding, and whether it applies is judged by the doctor based on individual circumstances.

6.3 Surgery

When the thyroid is very large, a nodule or cancer is suspected, or drugs and radioactive iodine are not suitable, removing part or all of the thyroid is considered. After surgery, hormone replacement is usually needed. The pros and cons of the three approaches differ for each person, so the choice should be made after a thorough discussion with a specialist.

⚠️ To re-emphasize. The treatment descriptions above are general information and are not a recommendation of any specific drug, dose, or procedure. Thyroid medications are dangerous to start, stop, or adjust on your own, and radioactive iodine and surgery have indications and contraindications. Make treatment choices and monitoring with your care team, and if you suspect a side effect, do not stop on your own - tell your clinician first.

7. Iodine - a problem whether too little or too much

Iodine is the essential raw material for thyroid hormone, so it comes up often in thyroid health. The key is that "more is not better" - the goal is "adequate."

7.1 Both deficiency and excess are risky

Too little iodine means not enough hormone, which can cause hypothyroidism and goiter. Conversely, excess iodine can, in some people, disturb thyroid function - triggering overactivity or underactivity or worsening an existing condition. In particular, seaweeds like kelp, iodine supplements, and some contrast agents or drugs are high in iodine and warrant caution.

7.2 A regional note

In some countries, including Korea, high seaweed consumption means usual iodine intake tends to be relatively high. So rather than reflexively adding iodine supplements, it is better to adjust intake to your own thyroid state (underactive, overactive, or antibody-positive) and your testing and treatment schedule. For example, iodine restriction may be needed before some thyroid tests or treatments, so discuss the specifics of your diet with your doctor or dietitian.

8. Lifestyle - diet, stress, and co-management

The central treatment for thyroid disease is medication or procedures, but lifestyle also affects how you feel and your overall health.

8.1 A balanced diet

Rather than relying on a particular "thyroid superfood," a balanced diet is the foundation. Micronutrients such as selenium and zinc are involved in thyroid metabolism, but overdoing supplements can do harm, so it is safer to supplement only when deficiency is suspected and discussed with a doctor. Some claim certain diets help autoimmune thyroiditis, but the evidence is limited - so do not use them to replace medication; consider them only as a supplement.

8.2 Stress and sleep

Chronic stress and lack of sleep are not direct causes of thyroid disease, but they can worsen fatigue, palpitations, and mood symptoms and may affect immune balance. Regular sleep, a stress-management approach that suits you, and exercise that does not overtax you all help recovery and well-being. During periods of strong palpitations from hyperthyroidism, avoid intense exercise and discuss exercise intensity with your doctor.

8.3 Smoking and coexisting conditions

Smoking is known to worsen Graves' eye disease in particular, so quitting is recommended. Thyroid function also affects cholesterol and the heart - LDL can rise in hypothyroidism, for instance - so it helps to manage it alongside other markers (see our guide to lowering cholesterol and guide to lowering blood pressure).

9. Pregnancy and menopause - extra care at points of change

At times when the hormonal environment changes dramatically, thyroid management becomes especially important.

9.1 Pregnancy and the thyroid

Thyroid hormone is vital for the development of the fetal brain, so both under- and overactivity during pregnancy can affect outcomes. With hypothyroidism, the required levothyroxine dose often rises in pregnancy and needs adjustment, and separate TSH targets apply for pregnant women. In hyperthyroidism, the type and dose of antithyroid drugs are tuned carefully by trimester. If you are planning a pregnancy or are pregnant, manage your thyroid with obstetric and endocrine clinicians rather than adjusting medication yourself.

9.2 Postpartum thyroiditis and menopause

Postpartum thyroiditis, a temporary phase of overactivity followed by underactivity, can occur after delivery, so consider thyroid testing if post-birth fatigue or mood change is severe. Menopausal symptoms (fatigue, weight change, mood, palpitations) also overlap with thyroid symptoms and are easily confused. Before assuming it is "just menopause," getting a thyroid test alongside can help with the distinction.

10. Common myths and cautions

  • "No symptoms means the thyroid is fine": subclinical thyroid dysfunction shows almost no symptoms and appears only on testing. With a family history or related symptoms, testing is warranted.
  • "Iodine is good for the thyroid, so take a lot": excess iodine is harmful too. In hyperthyroidism and some conditions, restriction may be needed, so blind supplementation is a no.
  • "I take the pill, so diet is handled": medication is key, but managing timing, co-administered drugs, and supplements that affect absorption also influences its effect.
  • "Normal values mean lifelong reassurance": thyroid function can change over time, so periodic retesting is needed, especially with autoimmunity, pregnancy, or medication changes.
  • "A thyroid nodule equals cancer": thyroid nodules are very common and mostly benign. Some need evaluation, though, so if one is found, discuss follow-up or testing with your doctor.

11. When to see a doctor

Consider a consultation, including thyroid testing, in the following situations. This is general guidance; if symptoms are pronounced or worsen quickly, seek care sooner.

  • Persistent, unexplained fatigue or weight change
  • Unusual cold/heat sensitivity, or sweating, palpitations, or hand tremor
  • Swelling at the front of the neck or a palpable lump or nodule, voice change, or trouble swallowing
  • Palpitations and a fast heart rate, anxiety and insomnia, or conversely severe sluggishness and depression
  • When planning a pregnancy or pregnant, or with a family history of thyroid disease
  • When Graves' eye disease - eye bulging or irritation - is suspected

12. Conclusion - small gland, big impact, but well controlled

The thyroid is a small organ of barely 20 grams, yet the hormones it makes steer the metabolism of the whole body - heart, temperature, weight, and mood. So when hormone runs short (hypothyroidism) or runs over (hyperthyroidism), a wide range of symptoms appear - fatigue, weight change, palpitations - that are easily confused with other common problems. The good news, though, is that most cases are diagnosed with a simple blood test and well controlled with medication or treatment.

The single message this guide wants to leave you with is this: do not shrug off vague fatigue and weight change as "just how it is" - if you suspect something, get tested, and read your own TSH, thyroid hormones, and antibodies together with your doctor. Whether it is iodine intake, taking medication, or management during pregnancy, the right answer lies not in internet generalities but in your own test results and your clinician's judgment. It is a small gland, but understood and managed properly, it lets you protect your daily life just fine.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. What is the difference between hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism?

Too little hormone is hypothyroidism; too much is hyperthyroidism. Hypothyroidism slows metabolism - fatigue, weight gain, cold sensitivity, constipation, slow heart rate - while hyperthyroidism speeds it up - weight loss, heat sensitivity, palpitations, tremor, loose stools. Symptoms overlap with other conditions, so a blood test confirms it. See a doctor if you have symptoms.

Q2. Does a high TSH mean an overactive thyroid?

Usually the opposite. When hormone is low (hypothyroidism), the brain raises TSH to push harder; when it is high (hyperthyroidism), TSH falls. Primary hypothyroidism: high TSH, low T4. Hyperthyroidism: low TSH, high T4/T3. Exceptions like pituitary problems exist, so interpret with your doctor.

Q3. What are Hashimoto's and Graves' disease?

Both are autoimmune thyroid diseases. Hashimoto's causes chronic inflammation that lowers function - the leading cause of hypothyroidism (TPO antibodies often positive). Graves' has antibodies that overstimulate the gland to cause hyperthyroidism (TRAb often positive), sometimes with eye bulging. Antibody and hormone tests distinguish them.

Q4. If I have a thyroid disorder, should I avoid iodine (seaweed, kelp)?

There is no blanket answer. Iodine is a problem whether too little or too much, and in hyperthyroidism and some conditions, excess iodine (kelp, seaweed, supplements) can worsen things. Because intake varies by diet and region, decide the right amount for your condition and treatment with your doctor.

Q5. Are thyroid medications safe during pregnancy?

Thyroid hormone is vital for fetal development, so control in pregnancy is very important. Levothyroxine for hypothyroidism simply replaces missing hormone and is essential, with doses often rising. Antithyroid drugs for hyperthyroidism are tuned by trimester. Before and during pregnancy, manage with your care team rather than adjusting on your own.
