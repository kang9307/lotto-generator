Introduction: Why Dollar Investing Is Attracting Attention in Korea

Between 2025 and 2026, the KRW/USD exchange rate repeatedly exceeded 1,400–1,500 KRW per dollar, reaching its highest levels since the global financial crisis (as described at publication in June 2026). This sharp KRW depreciation has sparked widespread interest in dollar investing among Korean retail investors, who seek both to profit from future exchange rate rises and to hedge their predominantly KRW-denominated portfolios.

The US dollar's status as the world's reserve currency means it often strengthens during periods of global economic uncertainty — making it a potential safe-haven asset. However, when the exchange rate falls, KRW-based investors holding dollars will experience principal losses. This guide covers the four main ways to invest in dollars from Korea, the key drivers of the KRW/USD exchange rate, the costs involved, the tax treatment, and the risks. Taxes and deposit protection follow Korean rules for individual residents.

Four Main Ways to Invest in Dollars from Korea

There are four primary methods for Korean residents to invest in US dollars. Each differs in terms of safety, return potential, liquidity, and accessibility, so understanding which fits your purpose comes first.

Foreign Currency Demand Account (Waehwa Tongjang)

A foreign currency demand account is a bank account that holds US dollars (or other foreign currencies) with free deposits and withdrawals. Most major Korean banks offer these accounts, and the bank's own posted exchange rate applies when you buy or sell dollars. The interest rate is very low — typically around 0–1% per year (as of June 2026) — but it allows you to hold dollars and benefit if the exchange rate rises.

  • Pros: Freely accessible, low minimum, covered by deposit protection (up to KRW 100 million per person per bank, combined with your KRW deposits at the same bank, converted to KRW)
  • Cons: Very low interest; principal loss in KRW terms if the exchange rate falls
  • Best for: Holding dollars for overseas spending, remittances, or long-term exchange rate exposure

USD Time Deposit (Waehwa Jeonggi Yegeum)

A USD time deposit locks your dollars for a fixed term (e.g., 3 months to 1 year) in exchange for a higher interest rate. Rates are influenced by the US Federal Reserve's policy rate — in the high-rate environment of 2024–2026, some Korean banks offered around 4% per annum on USD time deposits (as described at publication in June 2026; check each bank's current disclosure for today's rate). Early withdrawal typically results in a penalty reduction in the applied interest rate.

  • Pros: Higher interest than demand accounts; bank product with relative safety
  • Cons: Limited liquidity during the term; early redemption penalty; principal loss if exchange rate falls
  • Best for: Earning interest on dollars you plan to hold for a fixed period

Dollar RP (Repurchase Agreement)

A dollar RP is a product offered by Korean securities firms where your USD is invested in dollar-denominated government bonds or high-grade corporate bonds, with a commitment to repurchase at a set yield after a short period. A 2019 column from the KDI Economic Information Center described dollar RPs as able to offer a higher yield than foreign currency deposits. However, the Korea Deposit Insurance Corporation classifies repurchase agreements (RPs) as unprotected products, so do not expect the same principal safety as a bank deposit. Returns from an RP are taxed as interest income under the Income Tax Act (gains from repurchase-conditional trading of bonds or securities).

  • Pros: Potentially higher return than USD deposits; suitable for short-term parking
  • Cons: Not covered by deposit protection (KDIC classification); issuer credit risk exists
  • Best for: Investors with brokerage accounts looking to earn short-term yield on USD

Dollar ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund)

Dollar ETFs listed in Korea are designed to track the KRW/USD exchange rate and trade on the Korea Exchange just like stocks. Futures-based ETFs with "US Dollar Futures" in the product name are the typical type. When the rate rises, the ETF gains; when it falls, it loses. Leveraged ETFs (2x) amplify daily returns and carry significantly higher risk. Inverse ETFs are designed to profit when the exchange rate falls. Their tax treatment differs from bank foreign currency deposits (see the dollar ETF item in the tax section).

  • Pros: Low minimum investment, accessible via standard brokerage account, high intraday liquidity
  • Cons: Management fees; tracking error; leveraged products are high risk; currency gains may be taxed
  • Best for: Experienced equity investors who want market-rate exposure to KRW/USD movements

What Drives the KRW/USD Exchange Rate?

The exchange rate is the most critical variable in dollar investing, and it is influenced by multiple macroeconomic forces simultaneously. Understanding the main drivers helps you interpret exchange rate news — but it does not make prediction reliable.

US–Korea Interest Rate Differential

When the US Federal Reserve's policy rate is significantly higher than the Bank of Korea's, dollar-denominated assets offer relatively higher yields, increasing demand for dollars and pushing up the KRW/USD rate (KRW depreciation). The prolonged interest rate inversion between Korea and the US from 2022–2025 was a major structural driver of KRW weakness.

Current Account and Trade Balance

When Korea runs a large trade surplus, export earnings bring dollars into the country, supporting KRW. Conversely, weak exports or a tendency for Korean companies to hold dollar earnings rather than convert them increases exchange rate pressure. Global trade tensions and slowdowns in Korean exports can weaken KRW significantly.

Global Risk Sentiment

During periods of geopolitical tension or global financial stress, investors tend to move into the dollar as a safe-haven currency. This strengthens the dollar broadly and weakens emerging-market currencies including KRW. In calmer markets, capital returns to higher-yielding assets and KRW may recover.

Foreign Capital Flows

Large-scale selling of Korean equities or bonds by foreign investors converts KRW to dollars, weakening KRW. Foreign capital inflows have the opposite effect. These flows can cause sharp short-term exchange rate moves that are difficult to anticipate.

Key takeaway: The exchange rate reflects the interaction of interest rates, trade, capital flows, and market sentiment simultaneously. No single indicator reliably predicts its direction, especially in the short term.

Conversion Fees — The Hidden Cost of Dollar Investing

Exchange conversion fees are easy to overlook but can significantly erode returns. Even a small-looking fee can exceed your investment gain.

What Is the FX Spread?

Korean banks post a base rate (the standard rate) alongside separate rates for buying cash, selling cash, sending remittances, and receiving remittances. The gap between the rate applied when you buy dollars and the base rate is the spread — what people usually call the exchange fee. The size of the spread differs by bank, currency, and transaction type (cash versus a transfer into a foreign currency account or a remittance) and changes frequently, so it is not a fixed number to memorize. The bank's own posted rate table at the time of the transaction is the most accurate reference.

How to Reduce Conversion Fees

An exchange-rate discount does not discount the exchange rate itself; it discounts part of the spread. When you see an advertised high discount rate, check which rate's spread it applies to (cash or remittance) and what conditions unlock it (applying online or in the app, primary-bank relationship, and so on) — only then do you know the real cost. The Korea Federation of Banks' Foreign Exchange Guide (exchange.kfb.or.kr) compares internet exchange fees across banks. Fee structures for cash exchange and transfers into a foreign currency account can differ, so read the terms before transacting.

Cost Formula

When you buy dollars, the applied rate is base rate + spread × (1 − discount rate). Multiply by the dollar amount and that is the KRW you pay. When you later sell dollars back into KRW, the opposite applies: base rate − spread × (1 − discount rate). In other words, even if the exchange rate does not move at all, you start out behind by the spread on both sides. The larger the amount and the more often you trade, the larger this cost becomes.

Taxes — Exchange Gains, Interest Income, and Dollar ETFs

How dollar investing is taxed depends on the product. For bank foreign currency deposits, exchange gains and interest are treated separately; dollar RPs and dollar ETFs follow their own rules. The following applies to individual residents of Korea.

Exchange-Rate Gains — Not Taxed for Ordinary FX Deposits

Korea's Income Tax Act lists taxable interest income item by item in Article 16. For deposits, what appears on that list is the "interest on deposits"; the gain from a higher KRW value when the exchange rate rises is not on the list. Article 94, which lists capital gains, does not include foreign currency itself either. As a result, exchange gains an individual earns by buying and selling dollars through a bank foreign currency account or USD time deposit are subject to neither interest income tax nor capital gains tax. If you buy dollars at 1,300 KRW and sell at 1,500 KRW, the gain is untaxed; only the interest earned during the holding period is taxed.

There is an exception. If a financial institution bundles a foreign currency deposit with a derivative such as a forward exchange contract so that the return is effectively fixed, the gain from that derivative is also taxed as interest income under Article 16(1)(13) of the Income Tax Act and Article 26(5) of its Enforcement Decree. For any foreign currency product advertising a "fixed return," ask the institution how it is taxed before signing up. Currency gains through dollar ETFs or funds are also taxed differently (see the dollar ETF item below).

Interest Income — 15.4% Withholding Tax

Interest on foreign currency deposits is deposit interest under the Income Tax Act. The 14% withholding rate (Article 129) plus local income tax gives a 15.4% withholding tax, deducted before interest is credited (National Tax Service guidance: 14% on interest income, 15.4% including local income tax). Small interest means a small tax effect, but compare products on after-tax amounts rather than gross rates. Returns from a dollar RP are also interest income, as repurchase-conditional trading gains under Article 16(1)(8).

Comprehensive Financial Income Tax

If your annual financial income (interest and dividends, excluding tax-exempt and mandatorily separately taxed income) exceeds KRW 20 million, it becomes subject to comprehensive taxation; at or below that, withholding settles the tax (National Tax Service guidance). Foreign currency deposit interest, dollar RP returns, and taxable income from dollar ETFs all count toward this total. Financial income that was not withheld in Korea, such as interest from an account at an overseas financial institution, is subject to comprehensive taxation regardless of the amount. If your financial income is large, consult a tax professional.

Dollar ETFs — Holding-Period Taxation

A dollar ETF listed in Korea is not a "domestic equity ETF" that tracks a Korean stock index one-to-one, so it follows the "other ETF" tax rule described by the Korea Exchange. Other ETFs have been subject to holding-period taxation since July 2010: 15.4% (14% dividend income tax + 1.4% local tax) is withheld on the smaller of the trading gain at sale and the increase in the taxable base price over the holding period. Distributions are also taxed at 15.4%. The key difference: exchange gains in a bank foreign currency deposit are not taxed, but currency gains earned through an ETF can be.

Risks of Dollar Investing

Dollar investing can look attractive at times, but the risks need to be understood first.

Exchange Rate Risk — Principal Loss

The most fundamental risk is that if the KRW/USD rate falls after you buy dollars, you will lose money in KRW terms. Buying 1,000 USD at 1,500 KRW/USD costs 1,500,000 KRW. If the rate drops to 1,300, your holding is worth 1,300,000 KRW — a 200,000 KRW loss, which interest earned may not cover.

Timing Risk

Buying dollars at what appears to be a high exchange rate does not guarantee further appreciation. Exchange rate normalization — driven by Fed rate cuts, Korean economic recovery, or an improvement in the current account — can cause rapid KRW strengthening and significant losses for dollar holders who bought near the peak.

Opportunity Cost

Holding dollars means forgoing the returns you could have earned on KRW-denominated assets. If the exchange rate stays flat, you receive only the (relatively modest) deposit interest with no currency gain.

Leveraged ETF Risk

Leveraged and inverse dollar ETFs are not suitable for most retail investors. Leveraged products track daily returns at 2x, which means they can lose value rapidly during volatile or sideways markets due to compounding decay.

Practical Strategies for Beginners

For beginners, approaching dollar investing as portfolio diversification is more realistic than trying to time the market.

Dollar-Cost Averaging

Rather than converting a large sum at once, consider converting a fixed amount to dollars each month or quarter. This averages your acquisition cost across different exchange rate levels, reducing the impact of buying at a single unfavorable rate. Keep in mind that splitting purchases increases the number of conversions, and as the cost formula above shows, the spread applies every time.

Define Your Purpose and Allocation

Clarify why you want to hold dollars. If you have practical dollar needs (overseas travel, foreign purchases, children's overseas education), buy dollars aligned with those requirements. For pure diversification, deciding up front what share of your total assets to hold in foreign currency is the starting point. The appropriate share depends heavily on income, debt, and the currencies you will spend in, so there is no number that fits everyone; consult a professional if needed.

Deposit Protection and Safety

Bank foreign currency demand accounts and USD time deposits are covered by deposit protection. According to the Korea Deposit Insurance Corporation, the limit rose to KRW 100 million per person per financial institution (principal plus prescribed interest) from September 1, 2025, and deposits opened before that date are also protected up to KRW 100 million. Foreign currency deposits are converted to KRW using that institution's first telegraphic-transfer buying rate on the date the insurance payout is announced, and are protected up to KRW 100 million combined with KRW deposits at the same institution. So the same dollar balance can translate into a different KRW amount depending on the exchange rate. Dollar RPs and dollar ETFs, being investment products, are not protected. For beginners, a realistic order is to start with bank foreign currency deposits within the protection limit and consider RPs and ETFs only after understanding their additional risks.

Common Misconceptions

"The rate is already high, so it must go higher"

High exchange rates do not guarantee further appreciation. US rate cuts, a Korean economic recovery, or a wider trade surplus can combine to push the rate down sharply. Market experts themselves disagree on exchange rate outlooks.

"USD deposit rates are always higher than KRW deposits"

USD deposit rates reflect US policy rates. As the Fed cuts rates, USD deposit rates fall in tandem. The relative advantage over KRW deposits narrows or disappears in a declining US rate environment.

"Tax-free gains make dollar investing risk-free"

Tax-free exchange gains are a feature of bank foreign currency deposits and do not carry over to dollar ETFs. And if the exchange rate falls and you lose principal, no tax benefit offsets the loss. A favorable tax treatment should never be conflated with safety.

Conclusion — Start with Purpose and Risk

Dollar investing is a meaningful tool for portfolio diversification and protecting purchasing power. However, exchange rates are hard to predict, and buying dollars at a high rate followed by a decline causes principal losses. The same dollars are taxed and protected completely differently depending on whether they sit in a bank deposit, an RP, or an ETF.

For beginners, a realistic approach is to set the purpose first (actual needs vs. diversification), design the dollar share of total assets, and start with relatively lower-risk products such as foreign currency accounts or USD time deposits. A speculative focus on short-term exchange rate timing carries a high risk of principal loss.

## 3. Exchange cost check

- [ ] Did you check both the base rate and the rate you will actually get (cash, or remittance / transfer into an FX deposit) on the bank's posted rate table?
- [ ] Did you calculate knowing the discount applies to the spread, not the exchange rate?
- [ ] Did you check which transaction type the discount covers and what conditions unlock it (online/app application, primary-bank relationship, etc.)?
- [ ] Did you include the spread being charged twice, when buying and when selling back?
- [ ] Did you compare internet exchange fees across banks on the Korea Federation of Banks' Foreign Exchange Guide (exchange.kfb.or.kr)?

Formula: buying rate = base rate + spread × (1 − discount) / selling rate = base rate − spread × (1 − discount)

## 4. Tax check

- [ ] For a bank FX deposit: did you compute after-tax returns assuming exchange gains are untaxed and only interest is withheld at 15.4%?
- [ ] For an FX product advertising a "fixed return": did you ask the institution whether a forward or other derivative is bundled and how it is taxed?
- [ ] For a dollar ETF: did you include 15.4% withholding on the smaller of the trading gain and the taxable-base increase?
- [ ] Could your total financial income (FX interest + RP returns + taxable ETF income) exceed KRW 20 million a year? If so, consult a professional
- [ ] Do you have financial income not withheld in Korea, such as interest from an overseas account? It is subject to comprehensive taxation regardless of amount

## 5. Safety check

- [ ] Do your KRW deposits plus FX deposits (converted to KRW) at one bank exceed KRW 100 million? The excess is unprotected
- [ ] Did you account for the KRW value of the same dollar balance changing with the exchange rate?
- [ ] Did you set allocation knowing RPs and ETFs are not protected?
