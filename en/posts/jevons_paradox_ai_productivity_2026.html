Jevons Paradox in the AI Era - Why Efficiency Doesn't Reduce Labor

From 1865 coal to 2026 AI - a Korea-market reading of the rebound effect

2026-05-20 Tech & IT

Introduction: Does Efficiency Really Set Us Free?

Since AI coding tools went mainstream, the most repeated question has been: "Will developers lose their jobs?" One layer deeper sits an older question - if machines take over our work, do we actually become freer? Since the 18th century engineers and economists have promised that efficiency gains would eventually liberate humans from labor. The 21st century, however, has unfolded almost exactly in the opposite direction of that promise.

In 1865 British economist William Stanley Jevons gave this contradiction its first rigorous treatment. He noticed that as steam engines became more efficient, Britain's coal consumption did not fall - it accelerated. He turned that anomaly into a general theory linking efficiency to total demand. This post takes his 161-year-old insight and applies it to the 2026 Korean IT market in the era of widespread AI tooling. The trigger is a recent reflection by Cal Newport on the "dark side of Jevons Paradox" and the Korean-language discussion on hada.io, but the analysis below is my own reading of the Korean tech market, not a translation of either source.

1. What Jevons Paradox Is - A Contradiction Found in Coal

William Stanley Jevons published "The Coal Question" in 1865 to analyse the energy future of industrial Britain. The reigning assumption was simple: a more efficient steam engine burns less coal to do the same work, therefore total coal use must fall. The actual statistics, Jevons noticed, said the opposite. As steam engines improved, British coal consumption grew faster, not slower.

His insight: efficiency gains lower unit cost. Lower unit cost makes uses that were previously uneconomic suddenly viable. As steam engines cheapened, they spread from mines to textile mills, railways, ships, pumps, and printing presses. The expansion of the application space outpaced the per-unit saving, and total coal demand climbed.

The shorthand is "efficiency up x application space exploding = total consumption up." This is what we call Jevons Paradox, or more generally the rebound effect.

2. Jevons Paradox in Modern Life

This is not a 19th-century mining oddity. Almost every technology we touch daily shows the same pattern.

Car Fuel Efficiency and Total Gasoline Use
A 2020s car uses about half the gasoline per kilometre that an early-1980s model did. Yet annual driving distance per Korean household has grown steadily over the same window, and so has the number of vehicles owned. Commuting, weekend trips, car camping, dawn delivery - cars now reach corners of life that did not exist before. Unit efficiency improved; total gasoline consumption did not actually fall.
Faster Internet and Exploding Traffic
From 56Kbps ADSL in the early 2000s to gigabit fiber in 2026, residential bandwidth has multiplied roughly ten-thousandfold. We did not start finishing the same pages faster - instead, 4K streaming, 8K cloud gaming, live broadcasts, video meetings and AI model downloads filled in. Aggregate traffic exploded.
Low-Power Chips and Total Electricity Use
Process shrinks keep dropping the watts per transistor every year, yet the count of devices per person and servers per datacenter rose in lockstep. Phones, laptops, watches, smart appliances, plus GPU clusters for AI training, push the IT sector's total electricity demand to new highs every year.
Cloud Compute and Total Compute Used
AWS, GCP, and Naver Cloud cut per-hour compute prices dramatically over a decade. The result was not compute savings. It was a wave of "services we never used to build" - internal experimentation environments, throwaway demos, ad-hoc analytics jobs, always-on side projects. Cloud invoices keep climbing double digits per year.

All four cases share a structure. Unit cost or unit energy fell, but the range and frequency of use expanded enough to swamp the saving. Efficiency is absorbed as expanded possibility, not as restraint.

3. Jevons Paradox in the AI Era - A New Equilibrium for Coding

Now apply the frame to the AI coding tools that became mainstream from 2024 onward. Two hypotheses are frequently heard:

  • Hypothesis A (linear): "If AI makes coding 10x faster, nine out of ten developers will be fired."
  • Hypothesis B (Jevons): "Cheaper coding will unleash software that we previously never bothered to build."

Reality moves far closer to B. As unit coding time fell, individual side projects, internal automation bots, departmental tools, one-person SaaS, solo-founder full-stack startups, student club projects, and Notion automations - all the things "we never had time for" - opened up at once.

GitHub New Repository Trends
Since Copilot's general availability in mid-2022, new GitHub repository creation has climbed steeply. Some of that is AI-training-related synthetic noise, but even after discounting that, my own observation is that personal-learning and one-person-project repos have meaningfully increased in absolute numbers. Cheaper coding led to more people starting to code.
Cost of Building a Startup MVP
Five years ago a minimum viable product typically required one full-stack engineer, one designer, and one PM for three to six months. In 2026, a single person using AI tooling can ship a comparable MVP in two to four weeks. Did this collapse developer demand? Instead, solo founders and side-product builders multiplied, and the total developer population grew rather than shrank.
Side Projects Becoming Routine Again
Previously, only a minority of full-time engineers did meaningful side projects after work. From around 2025 the mood shifted. AI tools made it realistic to finish something in three weekday-evening hours, and the after-work side project is once again a common hobby.

The point is sharp. AI did not only raise the unit productivity of "developer" as an occupation. It lowered the entry barrier to "writing software" itself. Activities whose entry barriers fall almost always see a surge in usage and frequency that exceeds the per-unit saving and ends up enlarging total labour.

4. How This Plays Out in the Korean IT Market

Korea has a few specific traits worth naming: a large SI (systems integration) sector, a deep client-prime-subcontractor structure, regular government and public-sector IT procurements, and simultaneous growth in startups and solo businesses.

4-1. Korean SI - Did AI Lead to Layoffs?

From what I have observed, partially yes. In areas AI handles well - simple SQL, boilerplate CRUD pages, formatted report screens - a former team of seven now ships with five. But the same company often uses that saved headcount cost to win additional projects, so the firm-wide developer count holds steady or even grows. The product of "fewer per project x more projects" lands differently in each shop.

4-2. Solo Founders and Full-Stack Side Hustles

"One-person SaaS" entered Korean everyday conversation only after AI tools went mainstream. Single developers now run side products with monthly revenue from a few million to tens of millions of KRW, handling marketing, design, infra, and billing with AI assistance. This did not replace a big-company job - it created work that was not previously attempted.

4-3. Polarising Hiring Market

Demand for juniors in simple coding roles is softening. Demand for seniors, tech leads, and full-stack engineers with deep business-domain context has risen. As AI handles more of the typing, the role of "deciding where and how the code should be used" gained relative value.

5. The Dark Side Cal Newport Points To

All of this carries shadow. American computer scientist and bestselling author Cal Newport reread Jevons Paradox through the lens of working time. His core claim, in short (direct quote under thirty characters):

"Efficiency does not automatically create leisure." - Cal Newport, calnewport.com

When AI lets you produce more in the same hours, employers and markets quickly upgrade the new output to be the baseline expectation. What one person delivered last year is what one person must deliver twice over this year. Working hours do not shrink, often they grow, and cognitive load per hour expands.

This is precisely what Korean IT workers report first. Coding got faster with AI, yet office hours did not shrink or got longer, meetings did not drop, and the volume of reports went up. That common complaint maps perfectly onto Jevons.

6. Personal Strategy in a Korean Context

If we accept the paradox, the personal conclusion is direct. The market will absorb most efficiency tools, so part of the saved time must be deliberately reclaimed by the individual.

  • Judgment over typing speed - AI writes code well. It still cannot decide which code is worth writing, whose problem it solves, or why it should be solved now. The Korean IT market is starting to price this judgment more aggressively.
  • Invest in non-automatable territory - Business problem framing, user interviews, domain depth, organisational politics, and ethical decisions are unlikely to be automated within five years. Deliberately allocating time to these areas pays off long-term.
  • Negotiate work-life balance explicitly - To stop 100% of AI-accelerated savings from flowing into employer expectations, redirect a share into learning, rest, exercise, and family. This does not happen by default; it requires conscious declaration and negotiation.
  • Be on the tool-building side - In the AI era there is a widening gap between "people who use AI tools well" and "people who build AI tools themselves." Building even small tools in your own domain becomes a future career differentiator.

7. Policy Implications - A Four-Day Week Will Not Just Arrive

Historically, every meaningful reduction in working time was a policy artefact, not an efficiency artefact. France's 40-hour week (1936) and 35-hour week (2000) were the product of legislation and labour negotiation. There is virtually no example in history where efficiency gains alone shortened working hours automatically.

From 2024 onward Korea has begun a serious public conversation about a four-day week. The arrival of AI will not deliver this by itself. It requires labour-market negotiation, government policy, and corporate decisions in combination. Jevons' 161-year-old observation is unambiguous on the point: how the gains from efficiency are distributed is not a market accident but a social choice.

8. Conclusion - AI Is a Tool, Freedom Is a Choice

The lesson of Jevons Paradox is not pessimism. Nor is it that efficiency is futile. AI clearly can make work faster, more precise, and more enjoyable. But the question of who captures the gains is not decided by the tool itself.

Just as 1865 Britain's better steam engines did not automatically reduce workers' hours, our 2026 AI tools will not automatically free us. Real freedom comes from two conscious choices. Individually, deliberately redirecting saved time back into a life worth living. Socially, negotiating how the gains from efficiency are shared. What Jevons saw in 1865 was not really about coal and steam - it was about every efficiency tool humans build. We are now seeing the same pattern in AI, 161 years on. This time, with awareness, we can choose better.
