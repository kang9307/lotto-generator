How to Set Up iCloud — Decide If 5GB Is Enough Before You Touch Anything

What syncs, what backs up, what disappears, and how to get it all back out

August 6, 2026 Tech & IT

The 30 seconds of decisions that come before any switch

When you sign in with an Apple Account during the first-run screens on a new iPhone, iCloud is already on. That is why most people experience the service as something they never configured that one day announces the storage is full. By the time that notification appears, backups have usually been failing quietly for weeks.

The reason iCloud confuses people is that a single name covers two features with opposite behaviour. One is sync, where several devices share one live copy of the same data. The other is backup, where a snapshot of one device is stored separately. Treat them as the same thing and you will never understand why deleting one photo on your phone makes it vanish from your iPad too, and why the backup you dutifully enabled did nothing to stop it.

This guide stays on iCloud alone and walks through the order in which you actually configure and live with it. If cloud backup as a concept is what you are weighing up, or you are still comparing providers, the complete cloud backup guide covers that ground. Here the assumption is narrower: you already use an iPhone and you want iCloud set up properly. Prices and allowances get revised, so every figure below is stated as of August 2026, and the number that actually applies to you is the one shown in your own Settings.

Working out in five minutes whether the free 5GB will hold

The free allowance is 5GB per Apple Account. Per account, not per device — an iPhone, an iPad and a Mac signed into the same account all draw from the same pool. That figure was set when Apple still shipped 64GB iPhones, and by current standards it is effectively unusable for anyone who takes photographs.

Whether it will hold for you is a measurable question, and five minutes settles it.

  • Find out how many gigabytes your photos occupy. Settings → General → iPhone Storage lists every app by size, largest first. Whatever the Photos row says is the floor for what you will need in iCloud.
  • Four gigabytes is the dividing line. Above that, a 5GB account cannot hold your photo library and a working device backup at the same time. You then either budget for a paid tier or move photos out to a different arrangement entirely.
  • Message attachments are the hidden weight. The videos and images traded in group threads dwarf the text of the conversations. Check what the Messages row costs you on the same screen.
  • Count your devices. Someone with one iPhone and someone with an iPhone, an iPad and a Mac are using the same 5GB in completely different ways. Past two devices, backups alone will fill it.
  • Look at what is already consuming the space. Tap your name at the top of Settings, then iCloud, and a bar chart breaks down current usage. If the backup segment has stalled, backups are already failing.
  • Read the date of the last backup. It is printed on the iCloud → iCloud Backup screen. If it says several weeks ago, everything created since then exists in exactly one place.

The answer determines how you read the rest of this guide. If 5GB is genuinely enough, skip the pricing section entirely. If it is not, the real decision is which categories you hand to iCloud and which ones you deliberately keep out.

What to switch on first, and what to leave until later

iCloud is not one switch but a list of them. Leaving everything on is convenient, but on a 5GB account it produces a state where nothing works properly. If you are configuring from scratch, this order is the safe one.

  1. Tap your name at the top of Settings. That is the Apple Account screen, and every iCloud toggle lives underneath it.
  2. Open iCloud and check storage before anything else. Decide what to enable only after you know what is left. Do it the other way round and you will trigger a full-storage warning within minutes.
  3. Turn on Contacts, Calendars, Reminders and Notes first. These four consume almost nothing and are the data you miss most when a device is lost. They earn their place ahead of everything else.
  4. Turn on Safari and Passwords. Saved logins and bookmarks live here, and their presence or absence is the single biggest difference between a smooth device migration and a miserable one.
  5. Turn on iCloud Backup. On the same screen, enabling it lets the phone back itself up automatically when power, lock state and network conditions line up. Then tap Back Up Now once and confirm the first run actually finishes.
  6. Leave Photos until you have seen the numbers. Photos is the largest consumer by a wide margin, so decide only after the rest is on and you can see what remains. This one switch deserves the next section before you touch it.
  7. Turn on iCloud Drive when you have a reason to. Documents and anything in the Files app go here. Genuinely useful alongside a Mac; not urgent if the iPhone is your only device.
  8. Scroll the per-app list. Individual apps appear further down the iCloud screen. Anything you no longer use can be switched off here — but switching it off marks that app cloud data for removal, so check before you do it.

That is the baseline. In this state, losing the phone still leaves your contacts, calendar, notes, passwords and home screen layout waiting for you on the replacement.

Sync and backup are different features, and confusing them costs data

Almost every case of data loss in iCloud traces back to this one distinction, so it is worth stating precisely.

Sync means several devices share a single copy. Delete a photo on the iPhone and it goes from the iPad and the Mac as well. That is not a malfunction; it is the design. Backup stores the state of a device as it was at a particular moment, so it still holds what you deleted five minutes ago.

Because of that split, anything already syncing is deliberately excluded from the backup. This is why iCloud backups measure smaller than people expect, and it is also exactly why so many people believe their photos are in the backup when they are not.

Photos: choosing between Download Originals and Optimize Storage

Settings → Photos presents two options whose names suggest either would be fine. The outcomes are meaningfully different.

The deciding question is simply whether your device is short on space. Whichever way you go, the following remains true.

  • Optimizing does not destroy your originals. They stay at full resolution in iCloud and are fetched on demand; only the local copy is reduced.
  • What it does remove is the copy in your own hands. If account access is interrupted or the plan lapses, there is no longer a route to the original from that device.
  • Irreplaceable material belongs in two places. Wedding photos, footage of your children, work masters — do not let iCloud be the only holder. Put a second copy on a computer or an external drive.
  • Shared albums do not count against your storage. They also reduce resolution, which makes them a sharing mechanism rather than an archive. Never treat one as your master copy.
  • Empty Recently Deleted on a schedule. Deleted photos sit in that album for 30 days and keep occupying storage the whole time. It is the fastest space you can reclaim in an emergency.

What an iPhone backup actually contains, and what it never will

Having backup enabled is reassuring, but not knowing its contents is how people get blindsided after a restore. A backup holds everything that is not already in iCloud by some other route.

These go into the backup:

  • Device settings — wallpaper, notification preferences, ringtones, accessibility configuration and the rest of your personalisation
  • Home screen and app layout — restored down to the folder arrangement
  • Data from apps that do not sync — game progress, local history in some messengers, the internal records of budgeting apps and similar
  • Photos and videos, but only when iCloud Photos is off — this is the one condition under which they ride inside the backup
  • Apple Watch backups — carried inside the backup of the paired iPhone
  • Purchase history — a record of what you bought rather than the content itself; a restore uses that list to re-download

These are absent, and this is where the accidents happen:

  • Photos and videos in iCloud Photos — handled by sync, therefore not in the backup
  • Messages, when Messages in iCloud is on — sync again
  • Mail — already on the server, so there is nothing to duplicate
  • Apple Pay information and settings — excluded for security; you re-enrol your cards afterwards
  • Face ID and Touch ID configuration — bound to the hardware of a specific device and not transferable
  • Anything stored in another cloud — Gmail, work Exchange data and similar remain the responsibility of those services

The conclusion is worth holding on to: a backup alone does not resurrect an iPhone completely. Backup and sync each hold about half of it, and both have to be healthy for a restore to be whole.

The migration order that does not go wrong

Moving to a new iPhone is the day iCloud earns its keep, and two failures dominate: the backup turned out to be days old, and no backup existed because storage was full. This sequence avoids both.

  1. Run a manual backup on the old device first. Settings → your name → iCloud → iCloud Backup → Back Up Now. Do not rely on the automatic schedule for this one.
  2. Confirm it completed. The timestamp on that screen should now show the last few minutes. If storage ran out, this is where it shows.
  3. If space is short, raise the plan temporarily. Stepping up one tier for the transfer and stepping back down afterwards is a common move. A month of storage costs far less than a failed migration.
  4. Power on the new device with the old one beside it. Held close together, the setup flow offers direct transfer, which can move data device to device without routing through an iCloud backup at all.
  5. Check the date on the backup you select. If several appear, take the newest — and if that date is not what you expected, you have just found evidence of a failing backup.
  6. Keep power and Wi-Fi connected after the restore. The home screen appearing does not mean the job is done; photos and app data continue downloading in the background. Do not erase the old device in this state.
  7. Verify photo counts and app logins before retiring the old phone. Check the item count at the bottom of the Photos app, open your messaging threads, and confirm banking and authenticator apps are signed in. Only then is the move complete.

Some data looking absent immediately after a restore is normal — downloads are still running, or an app needs to re-fetch its own data from its own servers. If something is still missing a day later, the first thing to establish is whether it was ever in the backup to begin with.

Clearing space, in order of return, on the day the warning appears

Ignore a storage warning and backups quietly stop. Before paying more, work through the space you can reclaim. Sorted by how much each step tends to give back:

  1. Delete backups of devices you no longer own. Settings → your name → iCloud → Manage Storage → Backups frequently reveals a backup of an iPhone you sold years ago. Several gigabytes usually appear in one go.
  2. Prune which apps get backed up. On the same screen, tapping the current device shows per-app backup sizes. Anything that can simply be re-downloaded does not need to be there.
  3. Empty Recently Deleted. That album in Photos holds its contents for 30 days and charges you for the privilege. Emptying it takes effect immediately.
  4. Attack message attachments before message text. The photos and videos in your threads are where the volume is. The storage screen in Settings lets you pick out the largest attachments and remove them.
  5. Move video out to separate storage. Roughly eight tenths of most photo libraries is video. Copy it to a computer or external drive first, then delete — this is where the largest single drop comes from.
  6. Audit large files in iCloud Drive. Sorting by size in the Files app tends to surface forgotten installers and archives.
  7. Only now consider a bigger plan. If the shortfall survives all of the above, it is structural, and more of your time is worth less than the next tier.
Before you delete anything — removing it from iCloud removes it from every synced device at once. With photos and files in particular, read the section below on exactly where data disappears before you start.

Paid storage carries the name iCloud+ and brings a few privacy features along with the capacity. The table below shows monthly prices as of August 2026. The criteria that actually decide the choice:

  • Most people are choosing between 50GB and 200GB. One iPhone with a library under 30GB fits comfortably in 50GB. Add an iPad or a Mac and 200GB becomes the realistic starting point.
  • Family Sharing is the lever that changes the maths. An iCloud+ plan can be shared with up to five family members. Six people on one 200GB or 2TB plan brings the per-person cost down dramatically.
  • Sharing splits the cost but not the capacity. Six people on 200GB are competing for the same 200GB. One relative who shoots a lot of video puts everybody short at once.
  • Family Sharing shares storage only. Nobody can see anybody else photos or files; only the capacity pool is combined.
  • Check whether a bundle applies to you. Apple sells packages that combine storage with music, TV and game subscriptions, so if you already pay for other Apple services separately, the bundle may cost less than the sum.

Using iCloud from a Windows PC and a web browser

iCloud has a reputation for being Apple-only, but a great deal of it works fine on Windows and in a browser. When the task is pulling photo originals back onto a PC, those routes are considerably faster.

  • The web needs nothing but a browser. Signing in at icloud.com gives you Photos, iCloud Drive, Mail, Contacts, Calendar, Notes and Reminders directly.
  • The Windows app grafts onto File Explorer. Install iCloud for Windows from the Microsoft Store and iCloud Drive appears as a drive, with photos downloading into a folder you nominate.
  • For bulk downloads, the app beats the browser. Pulling thousands of photos through a web session tends to break partway. Setting up folder sync in the Windows app is far more reliable.
  • The originals-versus-compatible choice applies to downloads too. You can take the untouched original or a converted, more compatible format. If HEIC files will not open on your PC, this is the setting to change.
  • Saved passwords come across as well. The browser extension shipped alongside the Windows app makes Keychain logins available in Chrome and Edge.
  • On a shared PC, sign out deliberately. A lingering web session hands the next person your photos and files. Turn off browser credential saving on machines you do not control.

What to understand before enabling Advanced Data Protection

Data in iCloud is encrypted by default, but turning on Advanced Data Protection widens end-to-end encryption considerably — to twenty-three categories, including iCloud Backup, Photos and Notes. With it on, not even Apple can read the contents.

There is a price for that, and if the following is not clear to you, leave it off.

  • Mail, Contacts and Calendar are excluded. They have to interoperate with global email and calendaring systems, so they stay outside end-to-end encryption. Enabling the feature does not lock everything.
  • You cannot enable it without a recovery route. A recovery contact or a recovery key must exist first. That requirement is a safety mechanism, not an obstacle.
  • Lose the recovery route and Apple cannot help. Forget the password with no recovery key and the data is permanently unreachable. This is the single most important consequence.
  • Write the recovery key down on paper. Storing it as a note inside iCloud is putting the key inside the safe it opens.
  • If you are locked out, follow the official process instead of improvising. Account recovery routes differ depending on device state and registered information, so working through Apple published recovery procedure is the only reliable path.

Before you turn sync off: exactly where data disappears

This section covers operations you cannot undo. Flip these switches without checking first and data really does go.

The riskiest screen is the one for turning off iCloud Photos. It offers two options whose results are opposites. Download Photos & Videos pulls the iCloud copies onto the device first; Remove from iPhone clears them without leaving a local copy. Tap the second one absent-mindedly and that device is empty.

Beyond that, if you stop using the service altogether, photos and videos held in iCloud become eligible for deletion after about 30 days. Change your mind inside that window and the deletion can be undone; after it, they are gone. The order to follow before touching the switch:

  1. Confirm a copy exists elsewhere first. A PC folder, an external drive — somewhere with files that actually open. Believing a copy exists somewhere is not confirmation.
  2. If there is no copy, download before anything else. Pull the originals through the web or the Windows app, then compare file counts and total size against what you expected.
  3. Check that the device has room. Downloading every original while optimization was on can require tens of gigabytes. Too little space and the download stops midway.
  4. Read the prompt to the end before tapping. Remove and Download sit next to each other on screen, and this is the easiest place in the whole system to tap wrong when rushing.
  5. Remember the roughly 30-day window afterwards. If you realise you made a mistake, check immediately. Elapsed time is exactly what you are trading against recoverability.

The same principle governs the other categories. Turning off Contacts, Calendars or Notes can also prompt about keeping a local copy, and declining leaves that device with an empty list. Whatever the item, the discipline is identical: secure a copy before you disable, and verify the result after.

Cancelling while getting all of your data back

When you leave for another service or trim your spending, getting the order wrong turns your own data into a hostage. Downgrade first and anything over the free allowance becomes inaccessible precisely while you are trying to retrieve it. Reverse the sequence.

  1. Retrieve before you change anything. Leave the plan exactly as it is and download every photo and every iCloud Drive file to a computer or external drive.
  2. Open what you downloaded. Do not stop at checking folder size — open a handful of photos and documents at random and confirm they are intact. People do end up with folders full of zero-byte files.
  3. Export contacts and calendars separately. These do not land as folders the way photos do. Exporting them from the web in standard formats makes the move to another service straightforward.
  4. Request a copy of your data from Apple privacy page. You can ask for a copy of everything the account holds. Preparation can take several days, so start it before cancelling.
  5. Then downgrade the plan. Use the storage plan screen in Settings to select a lower tier. Downgrades normally take effect at the end of the current billing period.
  6. Factor the grace period in — but do not rely on it. Data survives roughly 30 days past the end of the subscription. During that time anything over the limit is locked away and no new backups run, so treat it as a margin of error rather than as your retrieval window.

On the billing side there is a separate refund path. Cancel shortly after an upgrade, or within a defined period after an annual payment, and a full refund can be requested. The exact conditions and windows vary by region, so asking Apple Support directly is more accurate than any general summary.

What this guide does not claim to settle

The following are deliberately left open. They vary by region and by date, or the official documentation does not pin down a fixed value.

  • Whether the prices above apply to every reader is not claimed. Apple adjusted pricing in several markets during 2026, and the amount billed can differ by signup date and payment method. The number on your own Settings → your name → Subscriptions screen is the only authoritative one.
  • The exact number of days data survives after cancellation is not claimed, because it can vary with circumstances. Treat 30 days as a working figure and never postpone retrieval on the strength of it.
  • The precise conditions for a refund are not claimed, since they follow regional terms. If your situation might qualify, contact Apple Support directly.
  • Regional availability of Advanced Data Protection and the OS versions it requires are not claimed, as both shift over time. If the option is not visible in Settings, check your OS version and regional eligibility first.

Frequently asked questions

Q1. How much free storage is there, and can 5GB be enough?

The free allowance is 5GB per account rather than per device, so an iPhone and an iPad on the same account share it. The test is quick: open Settings → General → iPhone Storage and read what Photos occupies. Above roughly 4GB, a 5GB account cannot carry your photos and a device backup simultaneously.

Q2. What exactly separates sync from backup?

Sync means several devices share one live copy; backup means one device state is stored separately as a snapshot. Consequently anything already syncing is excluded from the backup. With iCloud Photos on, your photos are not in the backup at all, and deleting one anywhere deletes it everywhere.

Q3. Does Optimize Storage lower my photo quality?

No. Originals remain in iCloud at full resolution and only a smaller local version is kept, with the original fetched when you edit or view it large. The genuine trade-off is different: without a network you cannot reach the original immediately, and there are periods when no local copy exists.

Q4. I restored to a new iPhone and photos are missing.

Immediately after a restore that is expected. The home screen arrives long before the content does, so keep the device on power and Wi-Fi and let it finish. If it is still empty a day later, check the old device last backup date and whether iCloud Photos was on — the material may never have been in the backup.

Q5. Is iCloud alone good enough as my only backup?

Against loss and hardware failure, yes. Against your own mistakes, no. Synced items vanish everywhere when deleted on one device, and once the 30 days in Recently Deleted elapse there is no route back. Anything irreplaceable deserves one more copy outside iCloud.

Wrapping up

There are only a handful of genuine decisions in an iCloud setup: whether 5GB will hold, optimized or original photos, whether to share a plan with family, and where the second copy of anything irreplaceable is going to live. Settle those four and the rest follows on its own.

Two things are worth doing before you close this page. Check the date of your last backup right now, and adopt one rule: never flip a sync switch off without verifying a copy exists first. Very nearly every case of losing data in iCloud comes from those two places.
