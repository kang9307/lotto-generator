Why Lymphedema Is Not Like Other Swelling - The Structure Behind Its Permanence and the Value of Catching It Early

What it means for protein to stay behind, where the stage line sits, and how to tell the urgent sign from the rest

2026-09-01 Health & Wellness

Swelling is a broad word. A face puffy the morning after a salty meal is swelling, and calves that thicken by evening after a long day on your feet are swelling too. Both usually resolve overnight. But there is a kind of swelling that does not resolve. It is still there in the morning, it barely shrinks when you elevate the limb, and after a few months it becomes firm enough that pressing a finger into it leaves no mark at all.

That is where lymphedema sits. The location is the same as other kinds of swelling, an arm or a leg, but what pools there and what the tissue does afterward are different. Approach it as a problem of removing water and it does not go well, and once the tissue has hardened, the room to reverse anything narrows sharply. Which is why in this condition, when you noticed it matters more than almost any other variable.

This article explains the structure of lymphedema and its standard management in general terms. It is information, not a substitute for diagnosis or treatment. Swelling has many causes and they frequently overlap. Do not stage yourself, and do not choose and apply compression on your own. Assessment and prescription for an individual case belong to the treating clinician.

Not the same swelling - what actually differs

Fluid constantly leaks out of blood vessels into the space between tissues, and that fluid carries plasma protein with it. The channel that carries it away is the lymphatic system. The 2023 consensus document of the International Society of Lymphology reduces lymphedema to a single common denominator: lymphatic transport has fallen below the load it must handle. When the capacity of that channel falls short, it is not only water that stays behind but protein as well.

This is where it parts company with edema from heart or kidney failure. Those arise when whole-body fluid balance breaks down, and venous insufficiency is closer to a problem of rising venous pressure pushing water out. Lymphedema is a failure on the removal side. The same document describes Stage I fluid as relatively high in protein content compared with venous edema. Protein and cell products left in the tissue lead over time to proliferation of fat and fibrous tissue.

Which is why whether pressing leaves a mark changes over time. The Korean National Cancer Center patient guidance explains that early on it is pitting edema that dents easily under a fingertip and recovers on its own by morning or after rest, but that it gradually moves toward a state in which the skin tissue fibroses, becomes firm and thick, and no longer recovers on its own. The loss of pitting is not a sign of improvement but possibly a sign that the tissue has changed. This is the point where self-assessment goes most badly wrong.

  • The consensus document notes that obesity, lipedema, endocrine dysfunction, venous insufficiency and repeated infection complicate the picture, and makes a thorough medical evaluation before treatment indispensable
  • In an adult with one-sided swelling, tumors that obstruct or invade more proximal lymphatics are explicitly listed among the things that must be considered

Primary and secondary - where it starts

Lymphedema splits into two. Primary lymphedema arises from congenital dysplasia of the lymphatic system, while secondary lymphedema arises when a previously sound lymphatic channel is later blocked or severed. The causes the consensus document lists for the secondary form are operative dissection, particularly removal of axillary or retroperitoneal nodes in the course of cancer treatment, along with irradiation, trauma, repeated lymphangitis and, depending on the region, filarial infection.

The balance leans heavily toward the secondary form. The same document states that the majority of lymphedema worldwide is secondary in nature, perhaps around 85 percent. In Korea the route patients actually meet is mostly the aftermath of cancer treatment: an arm after breast surgery and radiation, a leg after treatment for gynecologic or urologic cancer.

Touching the lymph nodes does not make it inevitable. But the conditions that raise the risk are laid out fairly clearly. Here is what the consensus document classifies as supported by robust scientific evidence.

  • A higher body mass index — the document points in particular to the range above 25 to 30
  • The extent of lymph node dissection. Stopping at sentinel node biopsy is not the same as an axillary dissection
  • The extent of the surgical procedure itself
  • Receipt of adjuvant therapy such as radiotherapy or chemotherapy
  • Being insufficiently active — that this item sits on the risk list connects directly to the exercise section further down

Why nobody says cure

There are words the literature on this condition avoids: heal, eliminate, cure. The 2023 International Society of Lymphology consensus document puts it this way. Lymphedema most often becomes a chronic and generally incurable condition, and like other chronic disorders it requires lifelong care and attention along with psychosocial support.

The same passage offers two comparisons. A person with diabetes continues to need medication or dietary control; a person with chronic venous insufficiency needs lifelong compression therapy. Then it adds a line: the continued need for therapy does not in itself mean that treatment has failed. That reads closer to an instruction to reset the goal. The goal is not elimination but holding a reduced state.

A second sentence attaches here. The document states that identifying subclinical lymphedema and beginning conservative management will likely reduce the chance that the condition progresses to a chronic advanced stage, and adds that treating it at an early stage offers greater treatment success and potential cost savings. Avoiding the word cure and insisting that early action is worth something are not in conflict. The first fact produces the second.

  • What the document lists as the consequences of leaving it alone: repeated infection, progressive skin changes, and on rare occasions lymphangiosarcoma
  • There is no reason to lead with a rare event to frighten anyone. But the direction that neglect points is worth knowing
  • In the other direction, the document cites studies in which early detection and preventive measures such as compression were effective in reducing incidence
  • What cannot be reversed is tissue change that has already happened, not the course ahead

The signal that arrives before anything is visible

The most distinctive thing about this condition is that you can feel it before you can see it. The early symptoms the National Cancer Center patient guidance lists are a pulling sensation in the arm or leg, a feeling that rings or shoes have grown tight, a sense that the limb has lost strength, and pain, aching or heaviness. The visible circumference has not changed yet, but the ring sits tighter.

There are measurement thresholds too. The same guidance states that a difference of about one centimeter between the swollen side and the unaffected side raises suspicion of early symptoms. The International Society of Lymphology document looks earlier still. Subclinical lymphedema, it says, is measurable at diagnostic thresholds as low as a 3 to 5 percent change in limb volume from baseline, and that change may first appear in only one segment of the limb rather than across the whole of it.

Which is why, during cancer treatment, the recommended approach is to measure both sides before surgery. The document calls this the prospective surveillance model: establish baseline volume and functional mobility for both limbs preoperatively, then remeasure at three-month intervals through the first year and at widening intervals thereafter. Without a baseline, the thing to compare against is gone.

  • A ring, watch, sleeve or sock mark that changes on one side only is worth not letting slide
  • A pattern that used to ease by morning and worsen by evening but now lingers into the morning may mean the course is crossing a line
  • Circumference only compares if measured at the same spot, the same way, at the same time of day. Shift the position and you get noise, not numbers
  • The National Cancer Center guidance also suggests measuring circumference at home. Using those numbers to stage yourself is a different matter
  • Beyond circumference, diagnosis draws on lymphoscintigraphy and optoelectronic limb volume measurement
  • The consensus document explicitly states that where both limbs are affected, volume differences must be interpreted with caution, because there is no normal side to serve as the reference

Staging - from 0 to III

The International Society of Lymphology divides limb lymphedema into three stages with a Stage 0 in front of them. Stage 0 is the latent phase in which lymph transport is already impaired but swelling is not yet evident. The document states that this can be transitory and may exist months or years before overt edema appears. That is exactly where the value of early action comes from.

StageStateOn pressingOn elevation

IIILymphostatic elephantiasis. Acanthosis, altered skin character and thickness, further fat and fibrosis, warty overgrowthsPitting can be absentLittle effect

The cell most often misread in this table is late Stage II. Losing the ability to pit is not improvement. The fluid has not left; fat and fibrous tissue have filled the space, and that change leaves far less room for compression and physiotherapy to work with. The document adds a caveat: a single limb may exhibit more than one stage, reflecting differences between lymphatic territories.

  • Severity is scored separately from stage, by volume difference. The bands the document uses are more than 5 and less than 20 percent for minimal, 20 to 40 percent for moderate, and above 40 percent for severe
  • As the document itself notes, these stages refer only to the physical condition of the extremities. Functional disability and quality of life are assessed on separate scales
  • Proposals to fold imaging findings or genetic information into staging exist, but the same document states that none has yet been incorporated into an agreed standard

Infection is the urgent part

Signs that call for care without delay. When a swollen arm or leg suddenly develops spreading redness and warmth, when fever or chills come with it, when pain escalates quickly. The 2023 International Society of Lymphology consensus document describes such acute inflammatory episodes as marked by erythema, pain and high fever, and less commonly by septic shock, and states that antibiotics should be administered. Night or weekend, this is not something to hold until the next scheduled appointment.

A limb with lymphedema is vulnerable to infection. An environment where tissue fluid and protein remain uncleared favors bacteria, and the channels immune cells travel through are themselves damaged. The problem is what follows. Infection damages the lymphatic system again. The consensus document spells out the sequence: failure to control lymphedema leads to repeated infections, which lead in turn to progressive skin changes. It is a loop.

The risk shows up in the numbers as well. In the prospective Massachusetts General Hospital cohort published in the Journal of Clinical Oncology in 2016, multivariate analysis found the factors significantly associated with increases in arm volume to be body mass index of 25 or higher, axillary lymph node dissection, regional lymph node irradiation and cellulitis. The P value for cellulitis was below 0.001. In that same cohort, 5.9 percent of the 760 at-risk sites had at least one episode of cellulitis requiring antibiotics.

Put the other way round: while the old precautions covered later in this article failed to find supporting evidence, infection alone survived consistently. That is why skin care occupies a pillar of management rather than a decorative role.

  • The consensus document also notes that mild skin erythema without systemic signs and symptoms does not necessarily signify bacterial infection. Not a call to make alone
  • Where infection recurs despite optimal physiotherapy, the document says prophylactic penicillin may be considered under physician oversight, with continuation weighed against risk and benefit
  • Fungal infection is named as a common complication of extremity lymphedema. The spaces between the toes are a frequent site
  • Skin damage is the entry point. Cuts, burns, insect bites, cracked heels and hangnails around the nails all count
  • Compression while an acute infection is present is to be avoided. Acute infection appears on the list of contraindications for compression prescription
  • Remembering how fast the redness spread helps at the visit. Hours and days are different stories

The frame of standard treatment - four pillars

The established approach is complex decongestive therapy. It goes by several names, but the skeleton is the same, and the consensus document describes it as a two-phase program. Phase 1 consists of skin care, the very light massage known as manual lymphatic drainage, muscle pumping exercises, and compression typically applied with multilayered bandage wrapping. Phase 2 is the phase that holds the result, made up of a low-stretch compression stocking or sleeve, skin care, continued exercise, and manual lymphatic drainage as needed.

Of the four, compression carries the most weight. Manual lymphatic drainage is often treated as the emblem of this therapy, but the consensus document reports that meta-analyses and systematic reviews confined to breast cancer-related lymphedema found manual lymphatic drainage to add limited value on excess volume reduction beyond the compression and exercise components. It grants value for reducing pain and the sense of tissue tightness and hardness, and adds that areas difficult to compress, such as the breast and the head and neck, may be more appropriate for drainage alone.

  • Phase 1 is the reduction phase and Phase 2 is the holding phase. Phase 1 without Phase 2 comes back
  • The consensus document notes that pull-on and velcro-assisted compression devices, by relieving the bandaging burden, can help with adherence
  • Diuretics are not the main treatment here. The document discourages long-term administration and notes the potential for fluid and electrolyte imbalance

Why compression garments have pressure classes

A compression stocking or sleeve looks like a tight piece of clothing, but it is a medical device carrying a pressure class and a set of measurements. The consensus document places compression strength roughly in the 20 to 60 millimeters of mercury range and holds that the highest class the patient can tolerate is likely to be the most beneficial. That the range is this wide is itself a sign that choosing is not simple.

So the document names who prescribes. Elastic garments for maintaining reduction should preferably be custom made from correctly obtained, specific measurements, and a physician should prescribe them where possible in order to avoid inappropriate use. Inappropriate use here is not a matter of preference but of states that constitute contraindications. The examples given are arterial disease, painful postphlebitic syndrome, occult neoplasia, acute infections and some skin disorders.

The danger of buying your own comes from exactly here. Put strong compression on a leg with poor arterial circulation and you reduce the blood flow further. Compress an arm with an active infection and matters can worsen. Bandaging is even more direct. The consensus document states that compressive bandages applied incorrectly, meaning at the wrong pressures or gradients, can be harmful and/or useless, and that multilayer wrapping should be carried out only by professionally trained personnel.

  • Compression must have a gradient that weakens from the fingers or toes toward the trunk. Invert that gradient and it pools instead
  • The consensus document lists edema pushed proximally, with a fibrosclerotic ring forming at the root of the limb, as an outcome to be assiduously avoided
  • An off-the-shelf item bought by size does not substitute for a prescription. What the document asks for is measurement, not sizing
  • Compression garments have a service life. The National Cancer Center guidance puts usable life for a compression stocking at around six months
  • Which knit is better remains unsettled. The document states that no trials have been completed to confirm garment choices and asks that physical ability, cost and future adherence be weighed alongside

Daily care - separating evidence from custom

The list of daily rules for lymphedema is very long, and for its length few items have been verified. The International Society of Lymphology consensus document says so itself. Risk factors for secondary lymphedema have spread widely in the form of dos and donts, but these are largely anecdotal and not sufficiently or rigorously investigated.

That does not mean discarding all of it. The same passage acknowledges that some precautions rest on physiological principles: avoiding excessive heat on an at-risk limb, not administering chemotherapy into that limb unless medically necessary, and trying to avoid infections. The principle is that heat and intravenous treatment increase lymphatic load. But the sentence that follows is that scientific evidence supporting or refuting these specifics is missing.

So the list below marks which column each item sits in. Without separating the verified from the custom derived from principle, there is too much to observe and the important items get buried.

  • Infection prevention (verified risk) — do not leave cuts, burns, insect bites or cracked skin unattended. Cellulitis is one of the few items that turns up consistently across studies
  • Weight (verified risk) — a higher body mass index appears as a risk factor in both the consensus document and the cohort study. But the document separately notes that evidence that weight loss serves as treatment remains very limited. Prevention and treatment are different stories
  • Staying active (verified risk) — being insufficiently active sits on the risk factor list
  • Avoiding excessive heat (custom derived from principle) — saunas, hot water, heat packs. The principle that heat increases lymphatic load is accepted, but evidence for the item itself has not been confirmed. The National Cancer Center guidance advises avoiding these
  • Nail and foot care (custom derived from principle) — the same guidance advises trimming nails carefully, not going barefoot and using an electric razor when shaving. The principle is reducing entry points for infection
  • Avoiding tight clothing (custom derived from principle) — the same guidance recommends loose-fitting garments, on the reasoning that local flow should not be obstructed

Four old beliefs held against the evidence

Four prohibitions circulate more widely than any others in this condition: no blood pressure readings on that arm, no blood draws from it, be careful about flying, and do not exercise. A study that examined all four directly appeared in the Journal of Clinical Oncology in 2016. It was a prospective cohort of 632 patients treated for breast cancer, 760 at-risk arms and 3,041 postoperative measurements. Volume was measured with optical equipment rather than by eye, and the cumulative incidence at 24 months was 7.72 percent.

In the multivariate analysis of that study, none of these nonprecautionary behaviors was significantly associated with arm volume change. What was significant instead: body mass index of 25 or higher, axillary lymph node dissection, regional lymph node irradiation and cellulitis. In their introduction the authors wrote that the existing guidelines are based on anecdotal information and that comprehensive data demonstrating the efficacy of such precautionary behaviors do not exist, and noted that such guidelines place a large burden and high anxiety on patients and clinicians alike.

The consensus document points the same way. Applying these lists as standard may not be appropriate and may subject patients to unnecessary fear and avoidance of activity, with other unintended consequences. Korean practice sits a notch apart, though. The National Cancer Center patient guidance advises against injections into a swollen limb and also advises against acupuncture, cupping and moxibustion. It is worth simply knowing that a gap exists between Korean patient education material and the international consensus document, and settling which to follow with the treating team. That is not for this article to decide.

  • Weak evidence is not permission. It means no association was confirmed, and those are different things
  • What can be adjusted is the order of priority. Infection prevention, weight and activity sit at the top; the rest sit below
  • Delaying a procedure in an emergency because the other arm is unavailable trades one risk for another. That judgment belongs to the clinicians present

Exercise is not contraindicated

The last of the four deserves its own section. The idea that strength training worsens lymphedema circulated for a long time, and two randomized trials tested it head-on.

The trial published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2009 put 141 breast cancer survivors with stable lymphedema through twice-weekly progressive weight lifting for a year. The proportion whose limb volume rose by 5 percent or more was 11 percent in the weight lifting group and 12 percent in the control group, essentially identical. Exacerbations, however, occurred in 14 percent of the weight lifting group versus 29 percent of controls (P = 0.04). Self-reported symptom severity and muscle strength both improved more in the exercise group, and there were no serious adverse events related to the intervention.

The follow-up trial published in JAMA in 2010 changed the population. It randomized 154 women at high risk who did not yet have lymphedema, to see whether a year of strength training would increase onset itself. Onset occurred in 11 percent of the intervention group (8 of 72) and 17 percent of controls (13 of 75). The conclusion reads: a program of slowly progressive weight lifting compared with no exercise did not result in increased incidence of lymphedema.

  • Both trials increased load very slowly and under supervision. This is not evidence for starting alone and going heavy
  • The International Society of Lymphology consensus document holds that the activity guidance for the wider cancer population, at least 150 minutes per week of moderate intensity mixed aerobic and resistance activity, is likely applicable to people with lymphedema
  • The same document states that consistent trends favored exercise participation irrespective of exercise mode, degree of supervision, duration and whether the upper or lower limb was involved
  • The National Cancer Center guidance adds another angle. Muscle movement and deep breathing aid lymph movement, but strenuous exercise raises blood volume and therefore generates more lymph as well. It advises starting with few repetitions and building up, and stopping immediately if swelling worsens during exercise

Where surgery enters the conversation

Surgery rarely arrives as a first option. The consensus document sketches two places for it. One is as an adjunct to physiotherapy, typically performed after the fluid component has been removed and pitting is absent. The other is when physiotherapy has clearly failed. Even here the document sets a condition: failure of complex decongestive therapy should be confirmed only when intensive non-operative treatment at a clinic specializing in peripheral lymphedema, directed by an experienced clinical lymphologist, has been unsuccessful.

The procedures named are lymphaticovenous anastomosis, lymph node transfer, lymph vessel transplantation and lymphatic liposuction, which aspirates fat and other pathologic solids. The document states that these are effective when used in properly selected patients and performed by an experienced lymphedema surgeon, and that imaging is indispensable for identifying which functional lymphatic vessels or nodes to manipulate. Preference varies by stage: microsurgical procedures are reported to do better in early disease such as Stage I, while debulking is performed for Stage II and beyond.

  • Surgery does not remove the need for compression. The document states that advanced stages usually require long-term physiotherapy or compression after the procedure
  • The reason is not only holding the result. It also includes keeping the constructed channel patent
  • Preventive use in high-risk patients is mentioned as a focus of recent research, but this article did not verify those results
  • The document itself notes that approaches differ between centers and that patients are strictly selected. This is not a place to generalize about which procedure is better

Korean coverage - what the insurance recognizes

What follows was verified as of September 2026 and applies to the Republic of Korea. Complex lymphatic physical therapy sits on the national health insurance benefit schedule under classification number Sa-120, with recognition criteria set by Ministry of Health and Welfare notification. The criterion reads that it is recognized for primary or secondary lymphedema, up to a maximum of four weeks within the treatment period. It was first established by notification 2010-101 (effective 1 December 2010), and notification 2015-99 (effective 15 June 2015) later opened a path to two or three additional sessions per week depending on reassessment after the initial four weeks.

Compression stockings are handled as a treatment material. The guide to compression and fixation treatment materials published by the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service in August 2024 lists compression stockings as an example of a treatment material entered on the benefit schedule. That guide explains the classification system, however, and does not carry detailed recognition criteria specific to lymphedema, upper limits or patient cost-sharing rates.

  • Notifications are amended. The above is as of September 2026, and the accurate check is with the institution providing care
  • Being recognized as a covered benefit and what the patient actually pays are separate questions, varying by inpatient or outpatient status and institution type
  • The four-week figure applies to a single treatment period, not to a lifetime. That is why the reassessment clause is attached
  • The Phase 2 holding period runs mostly through daily life, so what the coverage scheme covers and what management actually requires do not overlap exactly

Summing up

One thing separates lymphedema from other swelling. The channel that clears fluid falls short, so protein stays behind in the tissue, and over time that protein brings fat and fibrous tissue with it. A spot that pitted early on stops pitting later, and that change is not improvement. This is the basis on which the international consensus document avoids the word cure and writes lifelong care instead.

The same document records the value of early action alongside it. Catch it in the latent phase and the chance of progressing to a chronic advanced stage falls. What remains as practical action is short. Measure both sides before surgery, do not let changes in rings and sleeves slide, respond to signs of infection without delay, and use compression only after measurement and prescription. Those four carry far more weight than observing every item on the old list of prohibitions.

This condition often follows cancer treatment, which makes the subject itself read heavily. Yet several of the verified facts point the other way. Exercise turned out not to be contraindicated, and much of what was long forbidden never found supporting evidence. The place to reduce fear and the place not to delay are different places, and separating the two was the point of this article.

To state it once more: this article is general information, and diagnosis and treatment follow the judgment of a clinician. Swelling has many causes and they overlap. If a swollen arm or leg develops sudden redness, warmth, fever or chills, seek care rather than waiting for the next appointment. Use compression garments only after measurement and prescription, and do not raise the strength or stage yourself.

What I could not verify

  • The common explanation that damaged lymphatic vessels never regenerate could not be confirmed as such in the consensus document. The document instead lists inadequate lymphatic growth or regrowth among the functional deficiencies. So this article used the document's own framing, transport falling below the presented load, rather than a claim about regeneration
  • The primary versus secondary split within Korea and incidence by cancer type could not be confirmed from Korean primary sources, so no figures were given. The statement that secondary predominates worldwide is the International Society of Lymphology's own estimate
  • The relative value points and actual cost of complex lymphatic physical therapy were not given, because the notification text itself could not be opened. Only the recognition criterion and the notification numbers were carried over
  • The lymphedema-specific recognition criteria, upper limit amounts and permitted quantities for compression stockings were not found in the review agency guide and so were not stated. Figures appear in several news reports but could not be checked against a primary source
  • The full text of Korean clinical guidelines from the Korean Society of Lymphedema and the Korean Academy of Rehabilitation Medicine could not be accessed. Korean material here was limited to National Cancer Center patient guidance and coverage notification sources
