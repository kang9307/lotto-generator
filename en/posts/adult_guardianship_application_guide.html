Adult Guardianship Application: A Complete Guide — Types, Requirements, Procedure & Cautions

From guardianship types to the family court process, a guardian's powers and limits, and alternatives — a general overview as of July 2026

2026-07-09 Law & Legal

Notice: This article provides general information about the concept and overall procedure of adult guardianship and is not legal advice for any specific case. The applicable laws, procedures, costs, and timeframes can differ substantially by country, region, and point in time. Anyone actually considering an application should consult a qualified attorney or the competent family court. Content reflects the state of affairs as of July 2026.

1. What Is Adult Guardianship?

As people age, or as illness or an accident takes a toll on cognitive function, a point can arrive where everyday legal acts — from withdrawing money at the bank to disposing of real estate or consenting to medical treatment — become difficult to handle safely on one's own. Adult guardianship is the legal mechanism designed to protect and support a person in that situation. Many jurisdictions once relied on stigmatizing frameworks (in Korea's case, the old "quasi-incompetent"/"incompetent" designations), but these have generally been replaced by modern guardianship systems that aim to respect, as much as possible, the remaining capacity and self-determination of the person concerned.

Who this system is for is not defined by age or a diagnosis label alone, but by a practical question: to what degree has a person's capacity to manage their own affairs been diminished? Older adults living with dementia, adults with developmental disabilities (such as intellectual or autism-spectrum disabilities), people whose judgment has been impaired by a mental illness such as schizophrenia, and people who struggle with decision-making due to brain injury or the aftereffects of an accident can all potentially fall within its scope.

Family members often end up handling banking or hospital consents informally on someone's behalf, but that informal arrangement does not, by itself, create a legal right to dispose of property or enter contracts without the person's consent. Guardianship moves this de facto representation into a formal framework supervised by a court, so the person receives the protection they need while their rights are not casually overridden.

2. Four Types of Guardianship — The General Concept and How Countries Differ

Korea's Civil Act divides guardianship into four categories: guardianship (seongnyeon-hugyeon), limited guardianship (hanjeong-hugyeon), specific guardianship (teukjeong-hugyeon), and voluntary guardianship (imui-hugyeon). This is only one example framework; other countries use different names and structures. What matters more than the number or label of the categories is the shared underlying principle: the degree of protection is calibrated to how much capacity the person retains.

2.1 Guardianship (Seongnyeon-Hugyeon)

This applies where a mental limitation has caused a persistent, substantial lack of capacity to manage one's affairs. The guardian generally holds broad authority to act on the person's behalf, and the scope within which the guardian may void the protected person's legal acts is relatively wide. That said, this does not mean every routine, everyday act is automatically restricted — the court defines the specific scope.

2.2 Limited Guardianship (Hanjeong-Hugyeon)

This applies where capacity is diminished but not entirely absent. In principle, the person can still validly perform legal acts, and the guardian's consent is required only for specific acts designated by the court. It can be understood as recognizing broader autonomy for the person than full guardianship does.

2.3 Specific Guardianship (Teukjeong-Hugyeon)

This is used where support is needed only temporarily or only for a specific matter — for example, a single real estate transaction or a single lawsuit — where both scope and duration are limited. Because it intervenes only to the extent actually necessary rather than providing comprehensive protection, it is the most limited of the three court-ordered types.

2.4 Voluntary Guardianship (Imui-Hugyeon)

Unlike the three court-ordered forms, voluntary guardianship is a contract entered into in advance, while the person still has full decision-making capacity, in anticipation of a future decline. The person chooses someone they trust to serve as guardian ahead of time, which is often regarded as reflecting self-determination most fully among the four types. Its limitation is that it becomes difficult to arrange once capacity has already been substantially lost.

2.5 Different Frameworks in Different Countries

These four categories are illustrative of Korea's Civil Act framework; other countries structure guardianship differently. Many English-speaking jurisdictions, for instance, distinguish between guardianship over a person and conservatorship over property. Japan operates a three-tier statutory system — full guardianship, curatorship (hosa), and assistance (hojo) — calibrated to the degree of incapacity, alongside its own voluntary guardianship arrangement. China has a process for determining whether a person lacks or has limited capacity for civil conduct, alongside a "yiding jianhu" (意定监护) arrangement roughly equivalent to voluntary guardianship. Names and specific requirements differ, so anyone living abroad or dealing with overseas assets should verify the relevant country's own system separately.

3. Overview of the Application Process — From Family Court Petition to Registration

Court-ordered guardianship generally begins with a petition to the competent family court. The exact petitioners, required documents, and procedure vary by country and over time, so what follows is a general overview; specific requirements should be confirmed with the competent court.

3.1 Who May File

In many systems, interested parties such as the person concerned, their spouse, close relatives, a prosecutor, or a local government official may be entitled to file. Exactly who may petition and what documents are required should be confirmed through the competent court's own guidance.

3.2 The General Flow of the Procedure

The process commonly proceeds in roughly the following order.

  • Filing the petition together with supporting documents (family relationship records, a medical certificate, etc.)
  • A fact-finding investigation, often conducted by a court investigator
  • A capacity assessment or expert opinion to establish the degree of mental limitation
  • A hearing to directly ascertain the person's own wishes
  • A ruling on whether to commence guardianship and on the appointment of a guardian
  • Registration of the guardianship once the ruling becomes final

Some guidance suggests that the weight given to formal capacity assessment can differ by type — for example, full and limited guardianship are often said to rely on a formal psychiatric assessment as a matter of principle, while specific and voluntary guardianship are more often resolved through an expert's opinion instead. This describes a general tendency rather than a fixed rule, and it can vary by case.

3.3 The Importance of Confirming the Person's Own Wishes

Throughout the process, directly confirming the person's own wishes is treated as a central step. This reflects the guiding principle that guardianship is meant to support the person, not to substitute for them. Courts generally try to hear the person's wishes as fully as the person is able to express them.

4. A Guardian's Powers, Duties, and Limits

4.1 Asset Management

A guardian holds both the authority and the duty to manage the protected person's bank deposits, real estate, income, and similar assets for that person's benefit. This does not mean the guardian may dispose of the assets as freely as their own property. Significant dispositions of property typically require the approval or consent of the court or a guardianship supervisor, and guardians are frequently required to report the state of asset management to the court on a regular basis.

4.2 Personal Welfare

Matters concerning the person's life and body — consenting to medical treatment, deciding where they live, or whether they should enter a care facility — can also fall within a guardian's role. This area, however, is handled far more carefully than asset management. Matters that touch directly on fundamental rights, such as confinement or restrictions on physical liberty, more often require court approval rather than the guardian's unilateral decision.

4.3 The Principle of Respecting the Person's Own Wishes, and Its Limits

The central principle of modern guardianship is not that "the guardian decides on the person's behalf," but that "the person is supported in deciding for themselves as much as possible." A guardian's authority is therefore confined to the scope set by the court, and a guardian should not arbitrarily intervene in routine, small-value transactions or matters the person is capable of deciding for themselves. Because the scope of a guardian's authority is defined individually in the ruling, the assumption that "becoming a guardian means being able to decide everything" is not accurate.

5. General Notes on Cost and Duration

Application costs and processing time vary considerably depending on the complexity of the case, whether a capacity assessment is required, how cooperative the parties involved are, and the circumstances of the particular court. Various items can arise, such as procedural fees (e.g., stamp duty and service costs), the cost of a psychiatric assessment, and, where needed, attorney's fees, and processing time also differs case by case. This is precisely why this article avoids stating specific figures or timeframes as if they applied universally. The actual expected cost and duration are best confirmed through the competent court's guidance or a consultation with an attorney.

6. Preventing Abuse and the Supervisory Framework

There is a real risk that a guardian could misuse assets for their own benefit rather than the protected person's, or overstep their authority over personal welfare decisions. To guard against this, the following supervisory mechanisms commonly operate alongside guardianship.

  • Appointment of a guardianship supervisor: Where the court finds it necessary, it may separately appoint a supervisor to oversee the guardian's work.
  • Periodic reporting duty: Guardians are frequently required to report the status of asset management to the court at set intervals.
  • Approval for significant acts: Major acts such as disposing of real estate or incurring substantial debt typically require prior approval from the court or the guardianship supervisor.
  • Changing or ending guardianship: If a guardian fails to fulfill their duties, or if the protected person's condition improves, a petition may be filed with the court to replace the guardian or terminate the guardianship.

These mechanisms serve as safeguards so that guardianship, offered in the name of "protection," does not end up infringing on the very rights it is meant to protect.

7. Alternatives Worth Considering

A guardianship ruling is not the only option. Depending on the circumstances, the following arrangements can be considered on their own or in combination.

  • A voluntary guardianship contract: Entering into a guardianship contract in advance with someone trusted, while decision-making capacity is still intact, can reflect a person's self-determination more fully than court-ordered guardianship.
  • A trust: Entrusting the management and disposition of assets in advance to a trustee, such as a financial institution, can supplement or, in some respects, substitute for guardianship on the asset-management side.
  • Advance directives and similar documents: Recording one's own wishes in advance regarding future medical decisions provides another way to reflect a person's intentions in the area of personal welfare.

Which combination is appropriate depends heavily on an individual's asset structure, family circumstances, and health condition, so this, too, is best decided through professional consultation.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between guardianship, limited guardianship, specific guardianship, and voluntary guardianship?

The first three (guardianship, limited guardianship, and specific guardianship) are forms of court-ordered guardianship, while voluntary guardianship is arranged in advance through a private contract while the person still has decision-making capacity. The three court-ordered types differ in the degree of protection and intervention based on how much capacity the person retains. This is one example framework, and exact names and structures vary by country.

Who can file a petition for adult guardianship?

In many systems, the person concerned, their spouse, close relatives, a prosecutor, or a local government official may be entitled to petition. However, the exact list of eligible petitioners and required documents varies by country and over time, so anyone considering a filing should confirm the specific requirements with the competent family court.

Once a guardian is appointed, can they dispose of the person's assets freely?

No. A guardian is generally obligated to manage assets for the protected person's benefit, and significant property dispositions typically require court or guardianship supervisor approval. Guardians are often required to report the status of asset management to the court periodically, meaning their authority is recognized only within the scope set by law.

How much does an adult guardianship application cost and how long does it take?

Cost and duration vary considerably depending on the complexity of the case, whether a capacity assessment is required, and how cooperative the parties involved are. This is precisely why this article avoids stating specific figures or timeframes; accurate estimates should be confirmed through the competent court or a consultation with an attorney.

Is there a way to prepare in advance, before dementia or another condition progresses?

Yes. While decision-making capacity is still intact, a person may consider entering into a voluntary guardianship contract with someone they trust, setting up a trust for asset management, or recording their wishes about future medical decisions in an advance directive. Which combination is appropriate depends heavily on individual circumstances, so professional consultation is recommended.
