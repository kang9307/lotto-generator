The Complete Clothes Dryer Buying Guide — Heat Pump vs Heater, Capacity, Electricity Cost

Heat pump vs heater (electric) compared, how to size capacity against your washer, installation type, and electricity cost math — a practical buying guide as of July 2026

2026-07-24 Life & Practical

Introduction: What Should Actually Decide Your Dryer Pick

On days thick with fine dust, throughout the rainy season, or in any home where hanging laundry outside just isn't practical, a clothes dryer has shifted from a nice-to-have to something close to an essential appliance. Once you're actually browsing a store or shopping site, though, terms like "heat pump," "heater type," "vented," "condenser," "9kg," and "16kg" pile up fast, and it's easy to lose track of what actually matters.

This guide walks through, step by step, the difference in operating principle, electricity cost, and fabric damage between drying methods, how to calculate the right capacity for your household, installation type (vented vs condenser), the key specs worth checking before you buy, fabric-specific care cautions, how to maintain the lint filter and condenser, and how to estimate electricity cost — based on general practical information as of July 2026. It doesn't recommend any specific brand or model; the goal is to give you criteria you can apply yourself. Exact wattage, pricing, and specs vary by product and over time, so always verify the manufacturer's current official specs before you buy.

1. Drying Methods: Heat Pump vs Heater (Electric)

The core of any dryer is how it evaporates the moisture left in your clothes. Residential dryers split into two main categories: heat pump type and heater (electric/condenser) type.

1.1 How a heat pump dryer works

A heat pump dryer circulates refrigerant to recover and reuse heat, working in a way that's almost the reverse of how an air conditioner operates. Because the heat generated during compression and expansion is fed back into the drying process, the unit can dry clothes at a relatively low temperature (roughly 60°C or below), which is what keeps its energy use down.

1.2 How a heater (electric) dryer works

A heater-type dryer heats air directly with an electric resistance element to dry clothes. Its structure is simpler and cheaper to manufacture, and top temperatures climbing to roughly 75-80°C mean faster drying. Because that heat isn't recovered or reused, though, it consumes relatively more power.

1.3 Electricity cost and fabric damage compared

Thanks to low-temperature drying, a heat pump dryer's electricity cost per cycle is reported to run roughly a quarter of a heater-type unit's, and the lower temperature means relatively less fiber damage and shrinkage. A heater-type unit dries faster because of its higher heat, but daily use adds up to a bigger electricity bill, and heat-sensitive fabrics face a higher risk of shrinkage and damage.

1.4 Drying time and price compared

A heater-type unit finishes a cycle quickly thanks to its higher heat, while a heat pump unit, running at lower temperature, takes somewhat longer per cycle. Pricing runs the other way: a heat pump's more complex construction means a higher upfront price, while heater (condenser) units tend to be cheaper. On durability, though, heat pump units are reported to last 10+ years, compared with roughly 7-8 years for heater/condenser units.

2. Choosing Capacity: Sizing vs Your Washer, by Household

Dryer capacity is expressed as weight per cycle (kg). Because it uses the same unit as a washing machine, a common question is whether you should buy a dryer matching your washer's capacity — but that's not actually the case.

2.1 The relationship between washer and dryer capacity

Washing machine capacity is based on the combined weight of laundry and water, but laundry that's already been spun loses much of that water weight. That's why it's generally recommended to choose a dryer sized at 60-80% of your washer's capacity. If your washer is 21kg, for example, a 14-16kg dryer is generally plenty — there's no need to insist on matching or exceeding your washer's capacity. Bumping up capacity on the same-size unit often just raises the price and the electricity cost without meaningfully increasing how much laundry it can actually process.

2.2 Recommended capacity by household size

2.3 Problems with undersizing and oversizing

If capacity is too small, you can fit less laundry per load, meaning more cycles — and more electricity and time — to get through the same amount of washing. If it's too large, you pay more upfront and it takes up more installation space, and running a small load in an oversized drum can actually hurt drying efficiency. It's most sensible to size capacity around your actual laundry volume, household size, and how often you do laundry.

3. Key Specs Checklist

Even at the same capacity and method, real-world satisfaction depends heavily on the finer specs. Check the following before you buy.

3.1 Energy efficiency rating

Dryers sold domestically carry an energy efficiency rating; the higher the grade (closer to grade 1), the lower the power draw for a given capacity. The rating label also lists annual energy cost and power consumption, making it the most objective basis for comparing products. If you plan to use it often over a long period, it can be worth paying more upfront for a higher efficiency grade.

3.2 Low-temperature drying

A low-temperature drying setting runs at a lower temperature than the standard cycle, reducing damage and shrinkage for heat-sensitive fabrics like wool and knits. Heat pump units are naturally suited to low-temperature drying, but some heater-type models also offer a low-temp or gentle-dry cycle, so check the available cycle options.

3.3 Automatic condenser cleaning

On condenser and heat pump dryers, dust buildup in the condenser (the heat exchanger that turns moisture into water) reduces drying efficiency. An automatic condenser cleaning feature cleans the condenser after every cycle or on a set schedule, cutting down on manual cleaning. Units without this feature require regular manual cleaning.

3.4 Pedestal installation

A pedestal is a stand that raises the dryer off the floor, letting you load and unload laundry without bending over, and often adding drawer storage underneath for detergent and small items. It's a different concept from stacking a dryer on top of a washer, so check the ceiling height and width of your intended installation space beforehand.

3.5 Noise level (dB)

Dryers tend to run louder than washing machines, and heater-type units in particular can add a strong blower fan and exhaust noise. If you plan to install it near a living room or bedroom, always check the rated noise level in decibels (dB).

4. Installation Type: Vented vs Condenser

Separately from the drying method (heat pump/heater), dryers also split into vented and condenser types based on how they expel moisture — an important factor for your installation environment.

4.1 Vented installation requirements

A vented dryer exhausts the humid air generated during drying directly outside through a vent hose. Its simple structure keeps the price down and drying fast, but it needs a vent opening through a window or wall, or a path for the vent hose, which limits where it can be installed. It's hard to install away from a balcony or window, and cutting a vent opening can affect insulation or limit how windows open.

4.2 The installation freedom of condenser units

Condenser units (including heat pumps) condense moisture into water internally, so they don't need an outdoor vent. That's what gives them their biggest advantage: you can install them anywhere indoors where there's no vent path. That condensate still needs handling, though — connect it to a drain for automatic disposal, or if it's a tank-based model, you'll need to empty the tank every 2-3 cycles.

4.3 Water supply/drainage, space, and stacking on top of a washer

Unlike a washing machine, a dryer needs no separate water supply — only drainage (condensate) needs handling. In a tight laundry space, stacking the dryer on top of the washer can be an alternative, but it requires a dedicated mounting bracket to prevent tipping, and while stacking is often possible on top of a front-load washer, stacking on top of a top-load washer is often structurally difficult or not recommended. Before installing, always confirm whether your washer and dryer manufacturers officially support stacked installation and whether a specific bracket is required.

5. Fabric-Specific Drying Cautions

A dryer is convenient, but it isn't suitable for every fabric. Running a load without checking the material can lead to damage you can't undo.

5.1 Materials to avoid in the dryer

Leather, fur, velvet, and silk; items containing latex or rubber; waterproof fabric; electric blankets and heating pads; clothing with sequins or bead trim; blankets with pile longer than 1cm; and thin, easily stretched materials like lace, stockings, and tights should either avoid the dryer entirely or only go in after checking for a dedicated cycle. Make it a habit to always check the care label for dryer-safe instructions first.

5.2 Why shrinkage happens, and how to prevent it

The dryer's heat loosens the bonds between fibers; as the fabric cools back down, the fibers tangle and contract, producing shrinkage — the garment coming out smaller than it went in. For shrink-prone materials like cotton, linen, and wool, use a low-temperature (gentle) drying cycle, and instead of drying to 100%, stop at 70-80% and air-dry the rest — this cuts shrinkage substantially. Grouping garments of similar thickness and material together also helps prevent thinner items from over-drying and warping.

6. Lint Filter and Condenser Maintenance — Efficiency and Fire Prevention

The two most important maintenance tasks for a dryer are cleaning the lint filter and the condenser. Neglecting either doesn't just hurt efficiency — it can lead to a fire risk.

Ideally, clean the lint filter after every cycle, and at minimum, rinse it with water every 10 cycles to fully remove lint and dust. The condenser accumulates fine dust as it converts moisture into water, so it needs brushing or rinsing at least once a month; units with automatic condenser cleaning can go longer between manual cleanings. Neglecting either raises the risk of mold, odor, or malfunction.

Filter maintenance also matters for fire prevention. Domestic fire safety statistics report that roughly half of dryer fires stem from a lint filter that wasn't cleaned. When lint builds up on the filter, heat can't dissipate properly and keeps accumulating, raising the risk of overheating and ignition — so checking and emptying the filter every time you use the dryer is the single most reliable safeguard.

7. Dryer vs Air-Drying vs a Combo Washer-Dryer

Beyond a dedicated dryer, drying options also include air-drying and an all-in-one washer-dryer combo. Choosing the right one depends on your situation.

7.1 Air-drying

Air-drying costs no electricity and carries the lowest risk of fabric damage, but drying time varies widely with weather and season, and hanging laundry indoors isn't practical during the rainy season or on days with heavy fine dust. It also requires dedicating separate space for drying.

7.2 A dedicated (standalone) dryer

A standalone dryer installed separately from the washer offers larger capacity, faster drying, and more consistent performance, but it needs additional installation space and upfront cost beyond the washer itself.

7.3 A combo washer-dryer (all-in-one)

An all-in-one unit that handles both washing and drying saves installation space, making it well suited to tight living spaces like studios or newlywed apartments. That said, its drying capacity is generally designed smaller than its washing capacity, and drying performance and speed can fall somewhat short of a standalone dryer.

As a rule of thumb: if you have room in your laundry space and do a lot of laundry, a separate washer and dryer makes more sense; if space is tight, a combo unit is worth considering. In areas with frequent fine dust or rainy-season humidity, air-drying alone has real limits, so having some form of dryer — standalone or combo — is generally the practical choice.

8. Electricity Cost Calculation and Buying Checklist

If you're curious about a dryer's electricity cost, you can estimate monthly spending from the per-cycle cost of each drying method.

A heat pump dryer is generally reported to cost roughly 100-150 KRW per cycle, while a heater (electric) dryer runs roughly 400-600 KRW per cycle — a 3-4x difference depending on the method. At 20 cycles a month, that works out to roughly 2,000-3,000 KRW in added electricity cost for a heat pump unit versus roughly 8,000-12,000 KRW for a heater unit. Keep in mind, though, that the real-world burden shifts depending on the tiered rate bracket your total household usage falls into alongside other appliances. As of July 2026, the summer (July-August) residential tiered structure applies a relaxed three-tier schedule — tier 1 up to 300 kWh, tier 2 from 301-450 kWh, tier 3 above 450 kWh — and the tier-3 energy rate is reported to run more than double the tier-1 rate, so if dryer use pushes your total usage into a higher tier, the actual cost increase can end up larger than the simple per-cycle math suggests.

Pre-Purchase Checklist

  • Does the installation space have a vent opening (vented) or a suitable drain location (condenser)?
  • Have you sized the dryer at 60-80% of your washing machine's capacity?
  • Does the capacity match your household size and how often you do laundry (mini for 1 person up to 14kg+ for a large family)?
  • Have you checked the energy efficiency rating (the cost gap between grades adds up over long-term use)?
  • Does it have a low-temperature drying cycle (essential if you often wash wool, knits, or other sensitive fabrics)?
  • Have you checked whether it has automatic condenser cleaning (factor in the manual cleaning burden)?
  • Have you checked the noise rating in dB (especially important near a living room or bedroom)?
  • Have you confirmed whether pedestal installation or stacking on top of the washer is structurally possible?

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q. Should I buy a heat pump dryer or a heater (electric) dryer?

If saving on electricity and reducing fabric damage matter most, a heat pump dryer has the edge. If lowering the upfront price and getting a faster drying cycle matter more, a heater (electric) dryer may suit you better. With frequent use, the heat pump's long-run electricity savings can offset the higher purchase price over time.

Q. Should my dryer's capacity match my washing machine's?

Not necessarily. Because spun laundry loses much of its water weight, it's generally recommended to choose a dryer sized at 60-80% of your washer's capacity. If your washer is 21kg, for example, a 14-16kg dryer is generally plenty.

Q. Which installation type is better, vented or condenser?

If you have a balcony or window where an outdoor vent can be run, a vented dryer is cheaper and dries faster. If you're installing in an indoor space where venting isn't practical, a condenser dryer (including heat pump models) offers far more installation freedom. Keep in mind that condenser units need the condensate handled — a drain connection or a tank emptied every 2-3 cycles.

Q. What fabrics should never go in the dryer?

Leather, fur, velvet, and silk; latex or rubber-containing items; waterproof fabric; electric blankets and heating pads; and thin, stretchy materials like lace and stockings should avoid the dryer. For shrink-prone materials like cotton, linen, and wool, use a low-temperature (gentle) cycle and dry to only 70-80% before finishing with air-drying.

Q. How much does it cost to run a dryer per month?

At 20 cycles a month, a heat pump dryer is reported to cost roughly 2,000-3,000 KRW while a heater (electric) dryer runs roughly 8,000-12,000 KRW. The actual bill depends on which tier of your household's overall tiered electricity rate the usage falls into, so heavier use can push the real-world cost higher.

Conclusion

Working through a dryer purchase in order — drying method (heat pump/heater), installation type (vented/condenser), then capacity — cuts your odds of buyer's remorse dramatically. Lean toward a heat pump if electricity cost and fabric care are your priority, or a heater type if upfront cost and speed matter more, and size capacity at 60-80% of your washer's, matched to your household. Add key spec checks — energy rating, low-temperature drying, automatic condenser cleaning, and noise level — plus regular lint filter and condenser maintenance, and you'll get years of satisfying, fire-safe use.
