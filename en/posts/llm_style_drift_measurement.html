Style Moved in the Corpus

What breaks when a signal visible only in aggregate is applied to one person

Aug 29, 2026 Tech & IT

What the 460,000 actually counts

A chart is circulating. One band that sat flat against the floor in early 2025 takes up two fifths of the frame by the summer of 2026, and the caption travelling with it usually reads something like 40% of pull requests are now written by AI. The chart is real. The vertical axis, however, is not a share of AI-written text.

It helps to pin down what that axis holds before arguing about it. The number 461,121 counts pull request description bodies. Not pull requests as events, not review comments, not tokens, not repositories. Each unit is one block of prose a person typed while opening a pull request, and the median length is 65 words.

How the sample was drawn shapes the picture too. Ten times a day, a five-minute window is opened and the first hundred results of the search response are kept — roughly a thousand descriptions daily. In 2026 a single day holds about 460,000 pull requests matching the same query, so this corpus sees something like one five-hundredth of a day. It samples; it does not enumerate. The window start times are drawn from a seed fixed by the date, so there is no bias in time of day, but a sample it remains.

  • Span — 6 January 2025 to 17 August 2026, eighty-five whole weeks. Partial weeks at either end are discarded outright.
  • Size — 461,121 descriptions, 51,079,244 word appearances, and 19,798 words written by at least fifty distinct accounts.
  • Removed from the denominator — empty bodies (45% of all pull requests), four apps excluded by name, 3,784 accounts whose login looks like a bot (13.2% of collected rows), identical wording within a week, and anything past three descriptions per author per week.
  • Model — k-means over word distributions with KL divergence as the distance, ten clusters, every document assigned to exactly one. There is no time variable anywhere in the fit. The weekly curves are drawn by counting assignments afterwards.

That is the whole of what the measurement said. Descriptions with similar word distributions were sorted into ten groups, and one group that barely existed in early 2025 accounts for about 39% of the last four weeks. It never said who wrote that 39%, or with what. The author did not claim it either. The page reads that a cluster which appeared in 2026 represented 39% of human-attributed pull requests last month, and the next line says its most representative words should look familiar to anyone who uses coding agents. The title names Claude, but the analysis runs on word distributions with no knowledge of which tool, if any, was involved. The gap between the confidence of the title and the care of the method is exactly the territory this article is about.

Word frequency says nothing about a single document

In this measurement, a single description does exactly one thing: it lands on whichever of ten centres its own word distribution sits closest to. The assignment is nearest-neighbour rather than probabilistic, so what survives is which group and never how confidently.

At a median of 65 words, picking one of ten centres is barely distinguishable from chance for any individual document. It is close to guessing which of ten coins you were handed after sixty flips. Repeat that judgement 461,121 times and count by week, though, and a direction emerges sharply. The signal exists only in the aggregate, while every individual judgement that produced it is unreliable. Both sentences are true at once, and that combination is ordinary statistics rather than a contradiction.

The most misread figure is the lift on the leading word. Inside the cluster it appears 929 times and outside it 82, which is where the 39× comes from. That ratio is large. But the word was written by 848 accounts, while the cluster holds tens of thousands of descriptions. The arithmetic leaves only one conclusion: the word that most characterizes a cluster is absent from the great majority of its documents. Characteristic means unusually concentrated here, not present here.

  • Holds in aggregate — the share of writing dense in this kind of vocabulary has grown.
  • Breaks on an individual — this word is present, therefore this text was machine-written.
  • A lift is not a prevalence. 39× is a ratio of two frequencies, not the probability that a document contains the word.
  • Hard assignment carries no confidence. A document on the boundary and a document at the centre each cast exactly one vote.

Four stories that produce the same chart

This is the section the rest of the article hangs on. The curve went up; that part is settled. But at least four stories can produce that curve, and all four are plausible.

  • 1. Generated and pasted. An agent produced the description and it went up as written, or nearly so. This is the first explanation anyone reaches for, and it surely accounts for a real share.
  • 2. People absorbed the style. Read that register all day and your own prose drifts toward it. If reviewers read it comfortably, there is an incentive to imitate it deliberately. Hand-written text then lands in the same cluster.
  • 3. Tooling proposed the wording. Pull request templates, commit summarizers, drafts pre-filled by an editor or a CLI. A person may read, edit and post it, and the skeleton vocabulary is still the tool's. Assigning authorship to one side is the wrong shape of question here.
  • 4. The population of writers changed. The sample is not GitHub; it is accounts that open pull requests with a body. If developers who use agents open more pull requests, the corpus shifts even if no individual's style moves at all.

4. The population changedNew-account share and PRs per person move togetherNo — account composition is not controlled for

Separating the four would take three things: panel data linking the same account before and after, an outside label for tool usage, and a decomposition of new against existing accounts. This measurement has none of them. That is not a flaw. It simply is not a measurement built to answer that question. The cap of three descriptions per author per week softens story four a little, but a cap is not a control variable, and bot removal leans on the shape of a login, so automation running under a human name stays in the corpus untouched.

Story two is the one that lasts. Languages copy each other. To the degree that people absorb model prose, the next model trains on those people. A few more years of that and tracing who moved first, using vocabulary alone, becomes impossible in principle. This curve is best read as a record of a period when that boundary was still faintly visible.

Why certain phrasings pile up — the narrowing of a distribution

If stories one and three carry weight, why do particular expressions rise together? That part has an explanation at the level of mechanism. Each stage of producing text nudges the word distribution toward the middle.

StageWhat it does to the distributionWhat shows up in the text
PretrainingThickens genre-specific conventionsThe clichés of technical prose become the default
Next-token predictionPulls toward what is plausible on averageUncommon choices appear less often
Preference alignmentReinforces forms people rated highlyStructured, hedged, explanatory registers grow
Decoding and samplingTruncates the probability tailLexical variety narrows further
Instructions and tool contractsFixes paragraph shape and orderThe same kind of sentence recurs in the same slot

The conclusion to draw from that table is not a list of words to avoid. It is two things. First, every stage tilts probabilities slightly rather than stamping a specific string into the output. So the trace is, by construction, weak in any single document and present only in the distribution. Second, what moves is therefore not one word but a cloud of expressions. One rising word is a fad; twenty rising together is a distribution that shifted, and the latter is what this measurement caught.

The contrast makes the character clearer. A mark designed so that whoever holds the key can run a statistical test has a false-positive rate you can calculate in advance. A natural drift in word frequency has nothing of the sort. The difference between those two structures is covered separately in how AI text watermarking works and where it breaks.

The baseline problem — increased compared with what?

Language moves on its own. So increased is always relative to something. Here the comparison is the other nine clusters in the same corpus. Even the lift is computed as this group against the rest, not against the whole. That is a defensible choice, but it has to be said clearly: the baseline is not human writing.

The same dataset contains the evidence for why that matters. One of the ten clusters is simply pull request descriptions written in Spanish. It split on the language of the writer, with nothing to do with AI. Other clusters carry tokens at the top of their lists that belong to specific tools or repository families. Clustering was never told to find an axis called AI-ness, and it did not find only one axis.

A single punctuation mark shows how hard baselines are. In the same corpus the em dash went from 0.2 appearances per ten thousand words in early 2024 to 123 by mid-2026, a factor of six hundred. But what if editors and autocorrect began inserting em dashes over the same period? What if a markdown tool changed a default? Without a comparison series there is no way to settle it. A very large multiple may be a signal that there is more room for some other cause to be sitting inside it.

  • Other text from the same platform over the same span — did issues, wikis and commit messages move the same way?
  • Repositories that ban or declaratively exclude these tools — the closest thing available to a control group.
  • Per-account before and after — the only axis that separates people changing from the mix of people changing.
  • The arbitrary constants the author disclosed — ten clusters was chosen by looking at the outcome (below eight the group is a mixture, above fourteen it splinters), and the seed is listed as moving the headline figure.

That last item raises rather than lowers confidence in the work. The direction — a new group arrived — reproduced in 31 of 32 unconditioned fits. Where exactly it ends up as a percentage is one fit's answer. What replicated is the direction, not the decimal.

Why pull request descriptions are a peculiar place to measure

The venue shapes the result as much as the method does. A pull request description is not free writing; it is a slot that was formalized to begin with. Templates ask what changed and why, repositories carry conventions, and reviewers push back on format. People on the same team writing similar sentences is the default state of this genre.

  • 45% of all pull requests have no body at all. Only the remainder is sampled, so the corpus already tilts toward people in the habit of explaining themselves.
  • Bot removal keys on the shape of the login. Automation wearing a human name, in-house scripts, and pull requests opened by CI on someone's behalf all survive the filter.
  • The proof of that is visible in the other clusters, whose top vocabulary includes tooling and pipeline tokens outright. Templates and generated boilerplate are sitting inside the corpus.
  • So the drift found in this venue should not be carried over to blogs, papers or email. The more formalized the slot, the faster stylistic convergence shows up in it.

How the practice of code review itself is changing is treated in code review through the lens of maintainability. The point here is narrower: this is a venue that is unusually easy to measure and unusually hard to generalize from.

What breaks when an aggregate is aimed at one person

Someone will inevitably try to extract a rule from this chart — if you see this phrasing, it was written by AI — and take it to a classroom, a hiring pipeline or an internal audit. At that moment the tool is being used for a purpose it was never built for.

The deeper reason is that the cost of error is asymmetric. A miss, where machine-assisted text is read as human, produces essentially nothing. A false accusation costs a grade, a job, a disciplinary record, a reputation. It is hard to undo, and worse, it puts the accused in the position of proving they did not use a tool — a demand to prove a negative.

  • The first people hit are predictable: contributors whose first language is not English, people writing a formal register they learned rather than absorbed, and juniors who follow the team template carefully. Their prose sits close to the average, and prose close to the average is precisely what reads as generated.
  • A rule dies the moment it is published. Anyone who knows the criteria can route around it; those who never heard of it stay caught. What the sieve actually selects for is not tool use but not knowing the criteria.
  • An aggregate does not hand you an individual probability. About 39% over four weeks does not mean any given pull request has a 39% chance of being machine-written. That calculation contains no prior and no per-document interval.
  • This is why the cluster's word list is not reproduced here. Reprinting it would make this article a component of that sieve. And as computed above, even the top word with its 39× lift is missing from most documents in the cluster.

Moving the axis from verdict to verification, and the question of who absorbs the cost of a wrong call first, is covered at length in reader trust and the price of misjudging style.

Why the measurement is valuable anyway

Useless for judging individuals does not mean useless. Turn it around and material this good is rare. Read as an instrument panel rather than a verdict machine, this curve answers several real questions.

  • A proxy for adoption — surveys are self-reported and skew in both directions. This counts text that people actually left behind.
  • The speed of homogenization — the trend over the last twelve weeks runs at roughly 1.2 percentage points per week, which puts a number on how much time an organization has to settle its norms.
  • An observation point for policy — a team that introduces a disclosure norm now has a series to check it against, before and after.
  • A contamination warning — public text destined for future training is already mixed with model output, and the curve shows it. For anyone assembling the next generation of corpora, that is the most consequential implication here.

Part of the value is that the method is open. Collection code and corpus are committed daily, and the constants that were chosen arbitrarily appear in a table alongside how they were chosen. The author's account of switching collection paths is especially instructive: an earlier version built on the public event archive found the leading word in only 17 documents, off by a factor of 158. A single choice of collection path can change the order of magnitude of the conclusion. That is where a reproducible measurement and a free-floating conclusion part company.

Review quality is not measured in prose style

A smooth sentence and a correct change are different properties. A well-written pull request description speeds up review, and that is a genuine gain. The problem starts when smoothness substitutes for scrutiny. Comfortable prose creates the impression that something has already been examined, and that impression displaces the examination.

  • Does the scope of the change match the description? Why a file appeared that the description never mentions is the first question.
  • Is there any consideration of alternatives? A description listing one option is a description where no choice was made.
  • Are the failure paths written down — how to roll back, what is affected, what state remains after a partial rollout?
  • Do the tests actually hold the change? The criterion is not that they passed, but that reverting the change makes them fail.
  • Is there a line separating what was checked from what was not? The gap between descriptions that carry that line and ones that do not dwarfs any difference in style.

So the question a team's rules should ask is not was this written with AI but what was verified. The first has an unverifiable answer and rewards concealment; the second produces an answer that is itself the evidence.

What the writing side can actually do

The conclusion must not become a guide to scrubbing the machine smell out of prose. That is only a technique for passing the sieve described above, and it helps exactly the people who already know the criteria. The direction is the opposite one: stop hiding style and start making responsibility legible.

  • Put verifiable facts in. What was run, in which environment, and what was observed. Those sentences carry value regardless of style, and if they are invented the next person notices immediately.
  • Leave one line for the path you did not exercise. Something as short as this branch was only run locally is enough. A reviewer's attention goes exactly there.
  • If a tool was used, say so. Concealment is cheaper today and far more expensive the day something goes wrong.
  • Spend the editing time on facts rather than sentences. What is missing from a generated description is almost never the prose; it is the evidence.
  • Three team rules cover it. Give the template separate fields for what was checked and what was not, keep style out of the review checklist, and run automation under its own account rather than a human one.

That last rule reaches past your own team. A bot operating under a human login contaminates every measurement of this kind, and it does so where nobody is looking. Labelling automation as automation is not etiquette; it is maintenance of a shared resource.

What could not be established here

  • Per-account time series, tool-usage labels and a new-versus-existing account split are absent from this data. So how much each of the four stories contributed cannot be answered here.
  • How far the cluster's vocabulary actually overlaps a specific model's output lies outside this measurement, and no material for that comparison is in hand.
  • The corpus covers public GitHub repositories only. Internal repositories, other hosts and the conventions of private organizations are not in it.
  • Whether the same drift occurs in non-English pull request descriptions is a separate question. Vocabulary clusters form per language, so the finding does not transfer as written.
  • The figures quoted here come from the build dated 28 August 2026. The analysis reruns daily, so the share and the slope keep moving.

In closing — this article sits inside the same limits

Three sentences survive. The shift in word frequency is real — it reproduced in thirty-one of thirty-two fits and it has a slope. Its cause cannot be separated with this data — generation, absorption, tooling and population change all draw the same picture. And either way it cannot ground an accusation against an individual, because the signal exists only in aggregate.

Honesty requires one more note. What happened here was reading one public measurement and its methodology document and interpreting them. The arbitrary constants were quoted as the author listed them; nobody re-ran the fit with different values to see how far the answer moves. So this interpretation rests on the same grade of provisionality it demanded of the original. There is no reason the standard applied above should exempt the article applying it.

The next time a chart like this comes past, four questions get you most of the way. What exactly is the vertical axis a share of? What was removed from the denominator? Increased relative to what? And what other story would draw the same picture? Whatever survives those four is what the measurement actually said, and it is usually much narrower than the sentence travelling with the image. A narrower sentence is not a weaker one. Being narrow is how it gets to be true.
