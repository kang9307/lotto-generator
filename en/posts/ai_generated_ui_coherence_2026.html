Why AI-Generated UI Feels Off: The Coherence Problem in AI Design

Visually plausible but somehow wrong — why coherence, not consistency, is the real gap in AI-generated interfaces

2026-06-18 Tech

The "not bad, but off" problem

The first reaction to AI-generated UI is almost always "not bad." Buttons are rounded. The color palette is inoffensive. The typography is legible. But spend another ten seconds and something feels wrong. It's hard to put into words. "Amateurish" maybe. "I wouldn't ship this without a lot of cleanup." That feeling is real, and it has a name: lack of coherence.

As of June 2026, tools like Figma AI, v0 by Vercel, and Google Stitch (formerly Galileo AI) have entered real production workflows. Single-screen generation speed is genuinely impressive. But a consistent pattern emerges in practitioner reports: "each screen looks like it was designed independently," "spacing is all over the place," "there's no through-line." This analysis unpacks exactly what is missing — not as a general "AI can't design" critique, but as a precise identification of which capability is absent and how humans should compensate.

1. Consistency and coherence are not the same problem

The crucial distinction is between consistency and coherence. Most practitioners describe AI design problems as "consistency issues," but the more accurate diagnosis is a coherence failure.

1.1 Consistency is rule adherence

Consistency means using the same components, color palette, and typography scale repeatedly across a product. It is rule-following, and AI is quite capable of following rules. When you provide design tokens and a component library, AI tools can generate output that respects those rules reasonably well. By 2026, Figma AI has reached the point of reading component libraries, ingesting spacing tokens, and generating screens that honor naming conventions.

1.2 Coherence is unity of intent

Coherence is different. It is the quality that makes every element exist where it does, in the form it has, because of a deliberate intention that runs through the entire design. Why is this button large and bold? Because it represents the single most important action at this step of the user journey. Why is that text small and muted? Because it is contextual metadata the user does not need to read immediately. Coherence is not "what is used" but "why this is here in this form."

AI can enforce consistency through rules. But deciding what is coherent requires understanding the product's purpose, the user's mental model, the brand's personality, and the emotional context of that specific screen. This is where current AI design tools most consistently fall short.

2. Three concrete reasons AI-generated UI feels off

2.1 Screens are generated independently

Practitioner reports on Figma AI share a common observation: when asked to generate five screens for a mobile app, each screen can look independently designed. Spacing, component choices, and visual weight vary between screens in ways that require significant cleanup. The feeling is "these screens are not from the same app."

This is a coherence failure at the flow level. AI can generate each screen as a statistically plausible UI, but it cannot design the continuity of context a user feels when moving from one screen to the next. A human designer considers where the user is, what they just did, and what they need to do next — and adjusts each screen's visual weight and focus accordingly. AI optimizes the screen in front of it, not the sequence it belongs to.

2.2 There is no spacing rhythm

In design, spacing is not just a number — it is rhythm. Deciding where to use 8px versus 16px is not a rule; it is a meaning decision. These elements are a tightly grouped unit; those elements are a separate section. One researcher put it plainly: "Using the same spacing everywhere becomes boring without rhythm. This is the essence of that 'looks okay but boring' feeling in AI-generated UI."

AI can apply token values from a design system. But which value belongs where, in a way that creates meaning, requires understanding the screen's intent. The result is AI-generated UI that is either mechanically uniform (every gap the same) or arbitrarily varied (values change based on how the prompt was interpreted). Neither produces rhythm.

2.3 Emotional and cultural context is unread

Whether a screen "feels too cold," "too overwhelming," or "appropriately reassuring in a stressful moment" — these are the judgments AI design tools handle worst. Research consistently points out that AI can identify what is happening in an interface, but understanding why it feels a certain way requires human interpretation of emotional state, motivation, and cultural context.

A medical app's test result screen, a banking app's error state, and a children's app's onboarding flow each require completely different emotional design. AI can process the word "medical app" but cannot translate the tension a user feels in that moment into specific design decisions across hundreds of details.

3. State of AI design tools — June 2026

The tools are evolving fast. Here is an honest account of where the major players actually stand.

3.1 Figma AI

Figma AI improved its First Draft and AI Grid capabilities in early 2026. It can now read component libraries, reflect spacing tokens, and follow naming conventions. Single-screen generation accuracy is reported at roughly 70%. However, multi-screen coherence remains problematic — screens generated together still show inconsistent visual weight and spacing that requires cleanup. Figma's real strengths remain in design system management, large-product component consistency, team collaboration, and handoff. AI does not automatically resolve that complexity.

3.2 v0 (Vercel)

v0 is strongest in production-ready React/Next.js component generation. Its code-level output makes it easy for developers to modify directly. However, without design intent and product context in the prompt, the output is statistically plausible but coherence-free. It is genuinely useful for rapid prototyping, but the leap from "this component works" to "this feels like our product" still requires human intervention.

3.3 Google Stitch (formerly Galileo AI)

Galileo AI was acquired by Google in 2025 and relaunched as Google Stitch. It generates reusable components with proper variants (default, hover, active, disabled) and follows auto-layout rules. First-pass accuracy is reported around 55%. Its strengths are rapid UI exploration and multi-framework code export. But beyond a single screen, coherence breaks down in the same ways as the other tools.

3.4 The shared pattern: no structure means a dice roll

All three tools share a critical pattern. Without design tokens, a component system, and navigation logic, generation is statistical rather than structural. Identical prompts produce substantially different results across runs. This is not AI "designing" — it is AI sampling plausible patterns from training data. The reproducibility problem is direct evidence that no structural understanding is happening.

4. What coherence actually requires — the human designer's real work

Understanding what AI cannot do requires understanding what human designers actually do.

4.1 Hierarchy decisions are intent decisions

What is large, what is small, what comes forward, what recedes — these are all expressions of intent. This button is the most prominent element on the screen because it represents the single most important action at this moment in the user journey. That text is small and muted because it is contextual support that does not need immediate attention. Hierarchy comes from purpose, not rules. AI knows "buttons should be visible," but not "at this step in this journey, this action is the one thing that matters."

4.2 White space is intentional breath

Generous white space is emphasis. Dense, information-rich areas are signal compression. The deliberate contrast between these creates visual rhythm. When AI-generated designs feel "boring" or "suffocating," it is almost always because this rhythm is absent. Everything is equally dense or equally spacious. Without the contrast of emphasis and rest, the eye has nowhere to go, and the viewer senses something is subtly wrong without being able to name it.

4.3 Product personality cannot be learned from prompts

Two to-do apps with identical features can require completely different design if one is "minimal and focus-enhancing" and the other is "bright, friendly, and motivating." This personality emerges from target users, brand philosophy, and competitive positioning. AI can receive "minimal" or "friendly" as prompt words, but consistently translating that personality into hundreds of micro-decisions across every screen — that is beyond current AI design tools.

5. The "AI slop" moment — when incoherence becomes the whole product

Some practitioners have begun using the term "AI slop" to describe the specific failure mode: each element is individually defensible, but the whole feels empty. The mechanism is this: AI extracts statistically common patterns from training data. Therefore it is good at producing "not wrong" design. But "not wrong" and "intentional" are not the same thing. Averagely acceptable UI is averagely awkward.

When everything is safe and predictable, nothing is memorable. There is no product personality. There is no emotional connection with users. The paradox of AI slop is that no individual element is wrong, yet the whole feels wrong. That is coherence failure. Coherence is not the sum of individual elements. It is the quality that emerges when elements are unified by a single intent. And that intent lives in the mind of the person building the product.

6. Practical strategy: how to use AI design tools correctly

6.1 Define structure first, then constrain AI to it

The most important principle: define design tokens (color, typography scale, spacing system) and a component library first, then force AI to generate within that structure. The instruction should not be "make it look like X" but "generate within these components, these tokens, and these layout principles." The quality gap between constrained AI generation and unconstrained AI generation is dramatic. Within a system, AI sees patterns; without one, it sees noise.

6.2 AI for single-screen exploration, humans for flow design

AI is excellent at rapid idea exploration and single-screen drafts. Comparing ten button styles in 30 seconds, or getting a few landing page layout options quickly — AI is genuinely strong here. But when designing a five-screen flow where each screen transition must maintain contextual continuity, when the emotional texture must shift as users progress through a journey — humans must lead.

6.3 Treat AI output as raw material

Practitioners consistently report a "verification tax" when using AI output directly: correcting hallucinated padding, broken navigation logic, and drifting typography can take longer than designing from scratch. The correct workflow is to treat AI output as raw material — fast, explorable, imperfect — and have designers layer hierarchy, spacing rhythm, and coherence on top.

6.4 Prompt with intent, not appearance

AI output coherence improves when prompts specify purpose rather than appearance. Instead of "make a dashboard," try: "Design a dashboard where users can immediately identify their three most important metrics today, and notice any anomalies without searching." When intent and user behavior are explicit in the prompt, AI makes decisions closer to what a designer would make.

7. Looking ahead: how much coherence can AI learn?

The honest answer is: slowly, and partially. AI design tools are improving fast. Design system recognition has reached genuine usefulness. Multi-screen consistency is incrementally better. But the core of coherence — synthesizing product purpose, user context, emotional nuance, and brand philosophy into hundreds of micro-decisions — is not a problem that more training data alone will solve.

The direction current tools are moving toward is from isolated tool use toward workflow-integrated context-retaining agents — systems that remember past decisions, reflect design system changes, and learn from feedback over time. Whether this fully resolves the coherence problem is uncertain, but more context for AI means better-coherence output in a predictable direction.

One thing is clear: coherence comes from understanding a product's purpose and its users. That understanding belongs to humans — now and for the foreseeable future. AI can accelerate the execution of that understanding, but it cannot replace the understanding itself. When a person who knows why this button is this size and this color uses AI tools, coherent UI follows. Without that person, AI produces statistically plausible incoherence.

Conclusion: naming the problem is the first step to fixing it

AI-generated UI feels off not because individual elements are wrong, but because the intention that should tie those elements together is missing. Consistency is achievable through rules. Coherence requires purpose and contextual understanding. As of June 2026, AI design tools deliver real value for rapid exploration and single-screen drafts. But binding multiple screens into a unified intent, translating product personality into specific design decisions, and reading the emotional context of each screen — these remain firmly human work.

The solution is not to avoid AI tools. It is to use them in the right position: define structure first, prompt with intent, treat output as raw material, and let humans layer the coherence that turns a collection of screens into a product.
