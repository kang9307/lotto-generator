Where Google Sheets Blocks You and Where It Merely Slows Down - Limits, Recalculation Cost, and When to Leave

Separating hard limits from soft ones, what makes recalculation expensive, and where to move next

2026-09-08 Tech & IT

The sentence the file will not take any more and the sentence the file feels sluggish do not point at the same cause. The first is a question of limits, the second a question of cost. Limits have published numbers, and crossing one gets the action refused. Cost has no number attached to it. What it has instead is a set of rules about what is expensive, and those rules are published.

Keeping the two apart matters, because there are problems that deleting cells solves and problems that deleting cells leaves untouched. Most complaints that begin with the sheet got slow belong to the second kind.

Every figure below is the value published in Google official help and developer documentation as of September 2026. Google has raised some of these ceilings in the past, so a number copied without its date eventually becomes a wrong number. Each source is linked in the final section. Choosing an account and setting up sharing before the first sheet exists is covered in Getting Started with Google Sheets, and putting formulas or Apps Script to work is covered in The Complete Google Sheets Automation Guide.

Being blocked and being slow are different problems

The blocking side is easy to judge. A number exists, and once it is crossed the interface refuses. No more rows go in, an import fails, a script stops halfway through. The work is not detective work but arithmetic against a published figure.

The slow side has no such number. Two files of identical size behave completely differently, and it is common for half the cells to disappear with no change in feel at all. There is one reason for this. Cost is produced by how many things have to be recalculated, not by how many cells exist.

So this article keeps two axes apart. The early sections deal with numbers that must not be crossed, the middle sections with structures you may keep but will pay for, and the last sections with the point at which the tool itself should change.

Hard limits - the numbers that get an action refused

How much a single spreadsheet can hold is decided by the number of cells. There is no separate ceiling on rows, and there is a separate ceiling on columns.

Rows come out of a division. With 26 columns in use the cell ceiling arrives at roughly 380,000 rows, with 100 columns at 100,000 rows, and with 1,000 columns at 10,000 rows. In other words, leaving unused columns in place is the same thing as shaving off the rows you could have held. The empty rows and columns a new sheet hands you by default count toward the total as well.

The 50,000 character rule on Excel conversion behaves differently in one respect: it works quietly. Conversion does not stop with an error, it finishes with the contents of that cell gone. If a file has long notes or a pasted document sitting inside a single cell, that is a case worth comparing before and after.

Soft limits - the numbers you may cross at a price

The public help pages give no threshold in the form of things get slow past this many cells. What two performance pages give instead is a list of what is expensive. Rules rather than a threshold, which is in fact the more accurate choice, since the real threshold shifts with the machine and the browser.

One number from a different angle does exist. Google documentation for its no-code app tool tells you not to exceed 100,000 rows or 1,000 columns in a spreadsheet used as an app data source, and puts the compressed size ceiling for all the data in one app at 5MB or 10MB depending on the device. The same page adds that performance degrades well before the limit is reached.

That figure is not a ceiling on the spreadsheet itself, but on a spreadsheet with an app sitting on top of it. Even so, the distance between 10 million cells are allowed and do not exceed 100,000 rows gives a sense of how large the soft limit really is. At one percent of the hard limit, another Google document is already writing a warning.

Recalculation follows dependencies, not cell counts

The opening sentence of the performance page is the anchor. Each time a cell is edited, the formula in that cell and the formulas in all dependent cells are evaluated. The same page notes that changing one value can trigger many changes, and that all of them have to finish before the screen comes back.

Two consequences follow immediately.

  • Empty cells are close to free. A thousand interlocking formulas cost more than a million blanks. This is where the case of deleting cells and feeling no difference comes from.
  • The cost of editing one cell is set by how many things hang off it. The same formula therefore carries a different price depending on where it sits.

Hold on to that one sentence and every item below reads as a variation on the same theme. They are all ways of reducing what has to be recalculated.

What makes recalculation expensive

Volatile functions - re-evaluated on every single edit

The functions Google names as volatile are TODAY, RAND, RANDBETWEEN and NOW. Their values change on their own, so they are re-evaluated no matter what you edit, and everything hanging off them turns over as well. The performance page advises using them sparingly and, where appropriate, moving them into their own cell.

Per row  =TODAY()-A2        <- one volatile cell per row

In H1    =TODAY()
Per row  =$H$1-A2           <- exactly one volatile cell

The recalculation interval itself is a setting. It lives under File > Settings > Calculation as the Recalculation option, and the API documentation enumerates three values: on every change, on every change and every minute, and on every change and hourly. Choose minute or hour and volatile functions keep turning even while nobody touches the file. The Iterative calculation control on the same screen sets how many rounds a formula with a circular reference is allowed to run.

Open range references - what A:A actually reads

The documented explanation is short. A reference that does not pin the start and end of the range to specific rows or columns is an open range reference; one that pins them is a closed range reference. SUM(A:A) reads the whole column even though most of it is empty, while SUM(A1:A10) reads ten cells.

=SUM(A:A)
=COUNTIF(B:B, "Done")

=SUM(A2:A5000)
=COUNTIF(B2:B5000, "Done")

Open ranges are convenient because new rows need no edit to the formula. The price of that convenience is a full column read on every edit. For a table that keeps gaining rows, a closed range with generous headroom is usually the better trade.

Reference chains - one cell changes and the rest queue up

A2 reading A1 and A3 reading A2 turns the calculation into a serial line. Each cell waits for the one before it, so the longer the chain the more waiting piles up. The documentation says to avoid this shape and to point at the original source directly with absolute references.

A2 =A1*1.1
A3 =A2*1.1          <- waits for the cell before it
A4 =A3*1.1

A2 =$A$1*1.1
A3 =$A$1*1.1^2      <- reads the source directly
A4 =$A$1*1.1^3

Nesting functions inside a lookup range argument

The performance page states that VLOOKUP and MATCH are optimized to run over simple range arguments. Put a function where the range belongs and that optimization no longer applies, which is why the documented advice is to use a helper column computed in advance.

=VLOOKUP(A2, ARRAYFORMULA(TRIM(D2:E999)), 2, FALSE)

In column F  =ARRAYFORMULA(TRIM(D2:D999))
=VLOOKUP(A2, F2:G999, 2, FALSE)

The syntax of the lookup functions themselves and the errors they throw are covered in The Complete VLOOKUP and XLOOKUP Guide.

Conditional formatting - not a formula, yet it calculates

The documented sentence is the whole answer. Conditional formatting can take a long time to calculate, and all the rules are applied to the entire data range. As the data grows, so does the calculation. Files commonly accumulate rules that overlap or overwrite one another, and those rules are still calculated in full.

This is the item that hides best. It is a cost that no amount of staring at the formula list will reveal, so it is easy to spend an afternoon rewriting formulas and never touch the actual cause.

External references - import functions run on their own clock

Referencing data inside the same spreadsheet is faster than using import functions, and the documentation says so plainly. The refresh interval of each function is published as a table.

  • IMPORTRANGE - 30 minutes
  • IMPORTHTML, IMPORTFEED, IMPORTDATA, IMPORTXML - one hour, checking for updates every hour while the document is open
  • GOOGLEFINANCE - delayed by up to 20 minutes

There is one restriction on top of that. Import functions cannot reference a cell containing NOW, RAND, RANDARRAY or RANDBETWEEN. The stated purpose is to keep the spreadsheet from being overloaded, and TODAY is the single exception, because it is volatile but does not update more than once per day. The same page notes that deleting and re-adding the cells, or overwriting them with the same formula, triggers a refresh.

Usage of import functions is described as subject to strict limits, with no figures given. Cross them and a message appears saying the data may take a while because of the large number of requests.

How to narrow down where the slowness lives

Fixing on a hunch leaves nothing behind to reason from. Make a copy first, then change exactly one thing at a time. It ends up being the faster route.

  1. Work in a copy. The original stays untouched. Every step below is destructive testing that nothing needs to be undone from.
  2. Delete sheets one at a time and watch the response. Hiding a sheet does not reduce calculation. Which deletion changes the feel is the first coordinate.
  3. Overwrite a formula column with values and compare. Copy the column, then use Edit > Paste special > Paste values only. The difference against the calculated state is that column's share of the bill.
  4. Count the conditional format rules. The Conditional formatting panel under the Format menu lists them. Start with any rule whose range is a whole column.
  5. Count the import functions. IMPORTRANGE does its work every 30 minutes, the rest every hour, each on its own schedule.
  6. Find where volatile functions are scattered. How many cells contain TODAY, NOW, RAND or RANDBETWEEN is the cost, stated directly.
  7. Check the calculation setting. If Recalculation under File > Settings > Calculation is set to every minute or every hour, calculation runs even while nobody is in the file.

The machine is a variable too. If the same file behaves differently on different computers, the cause lives outside the file. When two states are compared, the measurement only holds if the browser, the machine and the extension situation are the same. A difference in feel measured without those conditions is hard to use as evidence.

The automation side has a different kind of limit

Once scripts or outside programs start touching the sheet rather than formulas alone, an entirely different set of limits applies. These vary by account tier.

Simultaneous executions30 per user30 per user
Spreadsheets created250 per day3,200 per day
Properties read and write50,000 per day500,000 per day

The 30 second custom function limit is hit especially often. Google documentation notes that errors are common when a custom function is called repeatedly within a single spreadsheet, and recommends structuring the code so it runs only once per data range. A custom function called once per row is the same shape as the per-row formula from the previous section.

The Sheets API is a separate axis again. Reads and writes each allow 300 per minute per project and 60 per minute per user, and crossing that returns a 429. Code that writes one row at a time reaches that wall far sooner than code that batches its writes.

Ways to stay without leaving

Changing tools is the last option. Several cheaper moves come before it.

  • Split off an archive. Move previous years into another file. It is the only move that reduces cell count and recalculation targets at the same time.
  • Separate the input sheet from the calculation sheet. Once the place people type into is distinct from the place formulas run, which side carries the cost is visible at a glance next time.
  • Replace per-row formulas with one array formula. ARRAYFORMULA displays values returned from an array formula across multiple rows and columns, and allows non-array functions to be used with arrays. Ten thousand formula cells become one.
  • Freeze settled data into values. Past records that have no reason to recalculate can be overwritten with paste values only. Once the formula is gone, that region leaves the evaluation set.
  • Delete formatting and rules nobody uses. Conditional format rules, leftover data validation rules, and the empty rows and columns that run on far below the table are all fair game.
Per row   =IF(A2="", "", A2*B2)                        <- 10,000 formula cells

One line  =ARRAYFORMULA(IF(A2:A="", "", A2:A*B2:B))    <- one formula cell

Array formulas are not a cure-all. One cell carries the whole range, so an error empties the entire output, and slipping an exception into the middle is awkward. As a way of reducing formula count, though, nothing is more direct.

When it is time to leave the spreadsheet

Not because a limit was reached. If any one of the three below applies, the tool is already the wrong fit no matter how few cells are filled.

  • Several people write the same row at once. There is no row-level locking in a spreadsheet. Two people editing the same cell means the later write survives. If a team keeps things working by talking to each other about who is editing what, that conversation is already standing in for something the tool cannot do.
  • Who changed what and when is part of the record. Version history exists, but it does not stand in for an audit trail, and Google documents the gaps itself. Added or deleted rows and columns, cell format changes, and changes made by formulas may not show up in the edit history. That last item is the decisive one. Named versions are capped at 15 per spreadsheet.
  • Referential integrity is required. When a row disappears from the customer table, what should happen to the rows in the order table that pointed at that customer? A spreadsheet has no mechanism that answers the question. Data validation rules block input but cannot protect relationships that are already in place.

Why moving to Excel is usually not the answer

If recalculation is what makes a file slow, Excel recalculates too. None of the expensive habits listed above disappear because the file format changed. A whole-column reference reads the whole column anywhere, and interlocking formulas wait for each other anywhere.

The shape of the hard limits genuinely differs. An Excel worksheet is 1,048,576 rows by 16,384 columns, and a cell holds up to 32,767 characters. For data that is tall and narrow, Excel has more headroom; for data with a very large number of columns, the spreadsheet ceiling is the higher one. But that is a statement about where you get blocked, not about where you get slow.

What actually changes on the move lies elsewhere. Simultaneous editing turns into file locking, sharing that used to be one link becomes handing a file around, and calculation now spends that particular machine's resources. Each of those cuts both ways depending on the situation. The Excel side tooling is covered separately in The Practical Excel PivotTable Guide.

Connected Sheets deserves a closer look as the compromise option. The surface stays a sheet while the data lives in BigQuery, and its published limits differ from the sheet's own: 200,000 rows for pivot tables, and 500,000 rows or 5 million cells for extracts. Those are separate numbers from the 10 million cells of the sheet, so it is possible to move and then meet a different wall.

Whichever destination is chosen, one cost is shared. The way people put data in and look at it changes. Spreadsheets have survived as long as they have because of that accessibility rather than their performance, and deciding to move is deciding to give some of it up. Which is why, until one of the three conditions in the previous section genuinely applies, staying is usually the cheaper call.

Frequently asked questions

How many rows fit in a single Google Sheets file?

There is no published figure for rows on their own. What the help pages publish is a ceiling of 10 million cells and 18,278 columns, and the row count falls out of that division. With 26 columns in use the ceiling arrives at roughly 380,000 rows, with 100 columns at 100,000 rows, and with 1,000 columns at 10,000 rows. These are the values as of September 2026, and Google has raised this ceiling before, so older articles may carry a different number.

The file is nowhere near the cell ceiling, so why is it slow?

Because cell count and calculation cost are separate axes. The performance page states that each time a cell is edited, that cell and all dependent cells are evaluated. Cost is set by how many dependencies exist, not by how many cells exist. A million blanks are close to free, while a thousand formulas that each read a whole column are expensive.

Should TODAY and NOW be avoided entirely?

Use them, but keep the count down. The documented advice is to move a volatile function into its own cell and have everything else point at that cell absolutely. One TODAY per row creates as many volatile cells as there are rows, while one in a single cell leaves exactly one. The rule that import functions cannot reference NOW or RAND family cells comes from the same design concern.

Why does an IMPORTRANGE result not update immediately?

Because the refresh interval is fixed. The help table lists IMPORTRANGE at 30 minutes, IMPORTHTML and IMPORTFEED and IMPORTDATA and IMPORTXML at one hour, and GOOGLEFINANCE as delayed by up to 20 minutes. When a refresh is needed sooner, deleting and re-adding the cells or overwriting them with the same formula triggers one.

When is it time to move to a database?

Not when a limit is reached, but when the tool stops matching the shape of the work. Several people writing the same row at once, a change history that is itself the record, or relationships that need referential integrity all mean the fit is already wrong. If none of the three apply, archive splitting and array formulas alone will carry a file a long way further.

Closing

There are two axes, in summary. The hard limits are 10 million cells and 18,278 columns, and on the automation side six minutes, thirty seconds and sixty requests per minute. Those numbers refuse an action once crossed and have no influence at all before that.

Soft limits have no numbers, only rules: reduce what turns over on every edit, stop reading ranges that need not be read, avoid serializing calculation into chains, and strip out the invisible formatting calculation. Those four are, between them, the whole of what the performance documentation lists.

And if one of three things applies, it is time to move regardless of any number. Concurrent writes, change history, referential integrity. None of the three were ever on the list of things a spreadsheet does well.

What could not be pinned down

The items below cannot be settled from public official documentation alone. Where a widely circulated number exists but its basis is not published, it has not been copied here.

  • Maximum number of sheets or tabs - no figure appears in the public help. The cell ceiling acts as the practical limit, so no number is stated here.
  • Maximum number of conditional format rules - no ceiling is documented. The performance page only says to reduce them.
  • Character ceiling for one cell on ordinary input - what the documentation contains is the statement that cells over 50,000 characters are removed during Excel conversion. That has not been carried over as a general input limit.
  • On-screen wording of the recalculation options - the help page names the setting and stops there. The three values come from the enumeration in the API reference, and whether the interface wording matches word for word cannot be settled.
  • Ceiling on iterative calculation rounds - the API reference has the field but states no maximum value.
  • Relative cost of aggregation functions such as QUERY or FILTER - no performance statement exists in official documentation. Several function speed rankings circulate, but their measurement conditions are not published, so they are not repeated here.
  • The threshold in cells or formulas at which the experience degrades - absent from official documentation and dependent on machine and browser. That is why not a single speed multiple appears in this article.
  • Concrete figures for import function usage limits - the documentation says only that strict limits exist and gives no values.
