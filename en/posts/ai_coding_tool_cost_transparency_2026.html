The Dollars Vanished From Your AI Coding Tool - How to Price Your Own Tokens

The total survived, the breakdown did not - and here is what that costs you

Aug 3, 2026 Dev/Infra

Intro: You open the usage page and the money is gone

You go to check how much you spent on your AI coding tool this month. The numbers are there. But the unit is tokens, not dollars. You download the CSV and the cost column reads 0.0, and last month's rows are empty too.

That is what happened to Cursor's self-serve plans on July 31, 2026. On Individual and Teams, the spend metric and the cost column were removed from the interface, and the dollar values disappeared from the usage CSV along with them. Enterprise plans kept their dollar display.

Read as a UI refresh, it is minor. But what disappeared is not a screen element. It is an input to decisions you still have to make. If you do not know which work is expensive, you cannot choose what to optimize. If you do not know who spent what, you have no basis for splitting the bill. If you do not know the usage-based equivalent, you cannot calculate whether your current plan is the right one.

This article answers three questions. What specifically becomes impossible when the money is hidden. What structural reasons might lead a vendor to show less cost data. And where the paths back from tokens to dollars actually are.

Controlling token spend at the organization level is a separate subject, and I covered it in bringing FinOps discipline to AI token spend. This piece stays one step earlier: observability when the vendor will not hand you cost data at all. Control comes after measurement, and when measurement is blocked, the control conversation never starts.

Compress the table into one line and you get this. The totals survived and the breakdown did not. That distinction decides everything else in this article. With totals alone you can answer "how much did I spend," but not "why that much" or "where should I cut."

1.1 The stated reason is a real problem

On the public forum, the company's explanation came down to this: showing included usage converted into dollars makes the figure look larger than the plan price and confuses people. That is not an invented excuse. It is a situation the pricing model genuinely produces.

Cursor's public pricing documentation attaches a dollar-denominated allowance of third-party model usage to each plan. Pro includes 20 dollars' worth, Pro Plus 70, and Ultra 400, and past that point usage continues at the same API rates on a pay-as-you-go basis. In other words, a user paying 20 dollars a month seeing "47 dollars of usage this month" on screen is a normal outcome of the plan design, not a bug. The support tickets write themselves.

That said, the explanation does not by itself justify removing the dollars. The same confusion is solved by separating included usage from overage in the display. And judging by the guidance that included rows now carry an "Included" label in the CSV, that distinction already exists in the data. If confusion was the driver, several remedies were available, and the one that was chosen was removing the amounts. That choice is the observable part of the story.

2. What breaks when you have totals but no breakdown

"It is inconvenient not to see costs" is a vague complaint. Split it into the specific decisions that get blocked and the size of the loss becomes visible.

What you were trying to doData requiredPossible with totals?Consequence of being blocked
Budget allocationCost by work typeNoYou cannot pick what to cut
Team and project attributionCost by user or tagPartial (admin totals only)No way to settle a shared account
Plan break-evenUsage-based equivalentNoYou miss the moment to move tiers

Three of those deserve more than a table row.

2.1 Losing regression testing hurts most

Say you changed one agent setting. You narrowed the file range that goes into context, or capped the number of autonomous steps, or dropped the default model one tier. Verifying that the change actually saved money requires comparing per-request cost before and after.

With only totals, the comparison does not hold. The workload itself may have differed across the two periods, and someone else may have been doing something else entirely. A total-to-total comparison with an unstable denominator does not even tell you the direction. Optimization then proceeds on instinct, and instinct-driven optimization usually gets reverted.

2.2 Small teams feel it most

Individuals are mostly fine. If everything fits inside the monthly flat fee, a total is all you need. The pain lands on the three or four people sharing one org account. There, a total means "somebody spent this," and if you cannot identify the somebody, you have nothing to say at next month's budget meeting.

A large enterprise is on an Enterprise contract anyway and sits outside the blast radius. So the segment this change actually lands on is small teams on self-serve plans, which is also the segment where cost data is scarcest to begin with.

2.3 Plan comparison is blocked at the source

In a pricing structure that mixes flat and usage-based billing, the user has exactly one question to ask: "for my usage pattern, which plan is cheaper?" That calculation requires the dollar figure the usage would carry under pay-as-you-go. If included usage is never expressed in dollars, a user has no way to know whether they are extracting the value of their subscription or leaving most of it on the table.

The same gap blocks comparison against other tools. If you do not know how much value you consumed in tool A last month, you have no baseline against which to test the claim that tool B is cheaper.

3. Structural reasons vendors show less cost data

This section is inference. The only publicly confirmed motive is the confusion-reduction rationale above. The items below are explanations that could hold for the broader trend of shrinking cost visibility in subscription AI tooling, not claims about any single company's intent. Each one carries a note on what should be observable if that explanation is correct. Inference is only useful when it comes with a test.

  • 1. Mixing flat and usage-based billing creates two different numbers. "Usage converted at list price" and "amount actually charged" diverge, and users look at the first while worrying about the second. → Test: vendors that separate included from overage should not have the same problem
  • 2. Frequent pricing changes make old displays inconsistent with the present. When rates or allowances change, the amounts on historical screens are not recomputed against current terms. Deleting the data becomes the cheapest way to preserve consistency. → Test: display reductions should cluster right after pricing overhauls
  • 3. In a resale structure, the margin becomes computable. An intermediary tool buys from the model provider and resells to you. If per-model, per-token-type amounts are all visible, users can compare against published rates and back out the markup. → Test: vendors that publish their markup explicitly have an incentive to keep detail
  • 4. Routing strategy leaks. When the vendor decides which model handles a request, visible per-model cost lets users reverse-engineer which requests went to the expensive model. That is closer to product design information than to billing data. → Test: the incentive should be weaker in products where the user picks the model
  • 5. Steering toward flat plans works better when usage-based instincts are dulled. If a dollar amount pops up on every request, people ration. Rationing reduces product usage. Hide the amount and usage rises while churn falls. → Test: display reductions should cluster in periods where usage growth is the goal
  • 6. Support genuinely costs money. If one displayed number generates tickets, answering them consumes staff hours. At a small company that is not a trivial reason. → Test: the most ticket-generating items should be the first to disappear

These six are not mutually exclusive, and there is no basis for declaring any one of them the real cause. From a user's side, what matters is not the motive but the preparation. The outcome is the same whichever motive applies. Cost data is a line item a vendor can shrink at any time, and the only way to reduce that exposure is to keep one observation point outside the vendor's screen.

4. Turning tokens back into dollars - procedure and error

If only token counts remain, you can multiply by published rates to recover a dollar figure. The catch is that doing it properly means about eight multiplications, not one. The order goes like this.

Minimum procedure for token → dollar conversion

  Step 1  Split rows by model
          No model column means you stop here. Conversion is impossible.

  Step 2  Split columns by token type
          · uncached input
          · cache read      (cheapest by far)
          · cache write      (can exceed the input rate)
          · output           (most expensive)

  Step 3  Multiply each cell by its own rate and sum
          cost = Σ (model m, type t) tokens[m][t] × rate[m][t]

  Step 4  Add the intermediary fee
          A reseller layers its fee on top of provider list price
          e.g. a flat per-token fee on team plans

  Step 5  Separate included usage from overage
          converted total ≠ amount billed
          billed = max(0, converted total − plan allowance)

  Multiplying one total token count by one rate is guaranteed to be wrong.

It helps to have a feel for how much error step 2 absorbs. Coding-agent work has an asymmetric shape, large input and small output, while output rates run several times the input rate. On top of that, repeated work pushes the share of cache reads up sharply. For the same total token count, the real dollar figure can differ by multiples depending on composition alone.

4.1 Six ways the conversion goes wrong

  • Ignoring the output share. Applying the input rate to a total token count lands far below the truth
  • Ignoring cache composition. Treating cache reads as ordinary input overstates the cost; treating cache writes as ordinary input understates it. The errors run in opposite directions, which looks like cancellation but produces two wrong numbers, not one right one
  • Collapsing models into one. The gap between a frontier model and an economy model can be an order of magnitude. A conversion without model breakdown is effectively meaningless
  • Forgetting the intermediary fee. In a resale structure, the list-price conversion is always below the actual charge
  • Ignoring batch and discount tiers. If asynchronous batch processing or a committed-use discount applies, list-price conversion overstates
  • Mistaking included usage for the amount billed. This is exactly the misreading the company cited, and it is genuinely common

Which leads to the rule. A converted figure cannot replace an invoice. Use it only for relative comparison.

  • ✅ "Configuration A costs three times what B costs" → conversion is enough. The same error applies to both sides, so the ratio survives
  • ✅ "This work type accounts for 60 percent of the total" → conversion is enough
  • ❌ "This month's bill will be 47 dollars" → do not use conversion. Allowances, fees and discounts are all missing
  • ❌ "Up 20 percent year over year" → if rates changed in between, the comparison does not hold

Published per-model rates are collected in the AI model comparison hub, so I am not reproducing the table here. For the arithmetic itself, use the LLM API price calculator and the token counter. How tokens balloon inside a single agent run is covered separately in analyzing token consumption inside the agent black box.

5. Four observation points that survive the vendor's decision

The core problem is that one vendor screen is the only source of truth for cost. In practice there are four independent observation points, and two of them work regardless of what the vendor decides to display.

PathWhat you getRequirementsAffected by plan changes?
1. Vendor dashboardOn-demand total, limitsNoneYes
2. Vendor admin APITeam spend, per-request charges, per-user rollupsAdmin key, higher tierYes
3. Model provider APIFull tokens and cost, split by key, workspace, modelProvider account, admin keyNo
4. Local instrumentationRaw per-request recordsProxy or wrapper setupNo

5.1 The vendor admin API - gone from the screen is not gone from the API

This is the part most easily missed. A value that vanished from the web interface has not necessarily vanished from the documented admin API. They are different interfaces.

As of August 3, 2026, Cursor's public team admin API documentation still lists these endpoints along with their cost fields.

  • /teams/spend — spend for the billing cycle, overall spend, monthly limit and effective per-user limit
  • /teams/filtered-usage-events — per-event charged amount, the intermediary token fee, and token usage with an attached total in cents
  • /teams/daily-usage-data — daily request counts split into plan-included, usage-based and API-key requests (no dollar amounts)
  • /teams/audit-logs — configuration and security events (not cost data)

What was reported to start returning zero, by contrast, is the usage-events response the web dashboard calls internally. The documented admin API and the dashboard's internal call are separate paths, so if you hold a team admin key, the admin API is worth checking first. I have no measured result from that endpoint as of this writing; what is confirmed is that the fields are still in the documentation.

5.2 The provider's own API - the only official path independent of plan tiers

Even if you work through an intermediary tool, keeping one workflow on a direct API key gives you complete usage and cost data from the provider side. Anthropic's organization-level reporting API, for example, is structured like this.

  • Usage report /v1/organizations/usage_report/messages — aggregation in one-minute, one-hour or one-day buckets, with uncached input, cache read, cache creation and output tokens reported separately
  • Breakdown dimensions — filter and group by API key, workspace, model, service tier and context window size. "Who used which model and how much" comes out here
  • Cost report /v1/organizations/cost_report — dollar-denominated cost at daily granularity, groupable by workspace and line-item description
  • Requirements — a separate admin API key, and it is not available on individual accounts. An organization setup is the precondition
  • Limits — priority-tier cost is not included in the cost endpoint, so it has to be tracked through the usage endpoint instead. Data typically lands within a few minutes

Here is a judgment call. When you are on a self-serve plan and the moment arrives where you genuinely need per-request cost, do not reflexively buy the higher tier. Open a provider account and move at least some of the work onto a direct API key. The reasoning is simple: the visibility you buy with a tier upgrade can be taken back by the vendor, and provider-side data cannot. The point is to place your observation point in a structurally different layer.

5.3 Local instrumentation - the last resort

When no vendor will give you the data, what remains is recording it yourself. Put a local proxy or wrapper in the path, log request and response token counts, and that record answers to nobody's policy. The approach itself is covered in analyzing AI coding tool spend locally, so I will leave the tooling there.

The limits are equally clear. Local instrumentation does not know what the vendor actually charged you. Internal retries, system prompts the vendor injects on its own, and extra calls generated during summarization steps never appear in your log. So a locally measured figure should always be read as a lower bound.

6. A cost observability checklist for choosing tools

When comparing AI coding tools, most people look at model quality and integration coverage. Cost observability is a property that only reveals itself after the contract, which is exactly why it drops out at selection time. These thirteen items are the ones that, checked before adoption, save you from reversing a decision later.

#CheckWhat you lose without itPriority
1Is per-request cost visible?Before-and-after config comparisonHigh
2Does it break down by model?Model routing decisionsHigh
3Does it break down by token type (input, output, cache)?Cache efficiency, accurate conversionMedium
4Does spend attribute per user?Team settlement, isolating anomaliesHigh
5Does spend attribute by project or tag?Per-client cost accountingMedium
6Can you export by CSV or API?External analysis, long-term retentionHigh
7Is the retention period stated?Year-over-year trend analysisMedium
8Are included and overage usage distinguished?Plan break-even calculationHigh
9Are intermediary fees disclosed?Real cost versus list priceMedium
10Does the data differ by plan tier?Predicting visibility loss on downgradeMedium
11Are historical records ever rewritten?Reconciliation, reproducible analysisHigh
12How long is ingestion lag?Real-time spike detectionLow
13Are there limits and alerts?Blocking accidental overspendHigh

Few tools satisfy all thirteen. So you need a priority order. Cutting it by organization size makes it usable.

  • Working alone — check item 13 (limits and alerts) and nothing else. The rest has no payoff while everything fits inside the flat fee
  • Three or four people — add items 4 (per-user attribution) and 6 (export). This is the size at which settlement begins
  • More than ten people — items 1, 2 and 8 become mandatory. At this scale the absolute savings from optimization exceed the cost of observing
  • Rebilling clients — items 5 and 11 become contract terms. The basis for an amount you pass through must not change later

7. Why the retroactive part is the bigger problem

The most unusual aspect of this episode is not that the dollars left, but that the change reached back into historical records. A three-month-old row that showed dollars yesterday shows zero today.

That is not a display policy question. It is a data model question, because billing data has one property that sets it apart from ordinary data.

  • Accounting assumes point-in-time fixity. If the numbers used in last month's close change this month, the close itself is void
  • The evidence behind decisions already made disappears. When the data supporting "this work is expensive, let us cut it" is gone, that decision can never be revisited
  • Analysis stops being reproducible. A dataset that answers the same query differently on two runs cannot serve as an analytical foundation
  • Dispute evidence evaporates. If the record you would cite in a billing dispute lives only on the vendor's side, then the moment that record changes, disputing becomes impossible
  • Backups change character. In a system where retroactive edits are possible, downloading the CSV stops being a convenience feature and becomes an evidence-preservation step

The general principle is this. Billing data should be append-only. If the pricing scheme changed, write new records in the new shape and leave the old records in the old shape. Changing a display format and erasing past values are entirely different acts.

There are two practical responses. First, download the usage CSV at the end of every month and keep it on your side. You can do this today and it costs nothing. Second, add a clause requiring advance notice of retroactive changes to historical usage data in procurement or contract documents. Self-serve plans leave no room to negotiate, but at team level and above this is a fair ask.

8. Would a standard help - FOCUS

The request "give me cost data in this shape" already has a published answer. FOCUS is an open specification published by the FinOps Foundation as a Linux Foundation project, aimed at normalizing billing data from AI, cloud, SaaS and data center vendors into a common set of column definitions. As of August 2026 the current version is 1.4.

What a standard actually changes is roughly this.

  • You get a shared vocabulary. Once column names are pinned down, you can tell "they do not provide that value" apart from "they just call it something else"
  • Switching vendors gets cheaper. Change tools and you can still stitch your cost history onto the same axis
  • Requirements become writable. Putting "provides FOCUS-compatible export" in a procurement document has enforcement weight that "show detailed costs" does not
  • But it carries no force. Adoption is entirely the vendor's choice, and uptake in AI tooling trails uptake in cloud
  • Even with a spec, tier differentiation persists. "We provide it" and "we provide it on every plan" are different promises. Fail to distinguish them in the contract and situations like this one repeat

The realistic leverage lies less in the specification itself than in naming it as a purchase condition. That is irrelevant for individual users, but if you sit anywhere near a tool adoption decision at a company, adding this line to the requirements list costs you nothing.

9. Decisions by condition - what to actually do

Cost observation has its own cost. In the range where observing costs more than it saves, not measuring is the right answer. Split by condition, it looks like this.

  • If monthly spend fits inside the flat plan → do not measure. Turn on the limit alert and stop. Detailed analysis at this scale is a hobby, not optimization
  • If overage occurred two months running → start keeping token records. One month can be chance; two is a pattern
  • If overage exceeded the price gap to the next tier → upgrading is almost always cheaper. But before you upgrade, confirm what data that tier actually exposes
  • If four or more people share an account and settlement matters → either move to a tier with per-user attribution, or restructure so each person holds their own provider API key. The latter adds administration but eliminates the attribution problem at the root
  • If you are running model comparison experiments → do not use the vendor dashboard as your baseline. Intermediary fees and routing are mixed in, so you cannot isolate the model's own cost difference. The provider-side usage API has to be the baseline
  • If you rebill clients at cost → do not depend on a single vendor screen; make your own records the system of record. The basis for a pass-through charge must not live only in someone else's system
  • If you are turning on autonomous agent execution → set the limit before you turn it on. Autonomous runs consume tokens while nobody is watching, so quota exhaustion arrives abruptly. Blocking comes before observing
  • If you are evaluating a company-wide rollout → paste the section 6 checklist into the selection document. Ask for it after adoption and your leverage is gone

One sentence version. If hidden costs are bothering you right now, first calculate whether the size of that annoyance justifies building an observation system. For most individual users the answer is no, and from small-team scale upward it flips to yes. Control design at organizational scale is covered in putting a per-employee cap on AI spend and in the FinOps piece mentioned earlier.

10. What I could not confirm

These are the items I left without values. The path to check each one is included.

  • The current live response of the documented team admin API. What is confirmed is that the cost fields remain in the public documentation. What that endpoint returns today for a self-serve team account is not confirmed. To check: call it with an admin key
  • The span of the retroactive application. Reports of historical records turning to zero exist, but how far back the change reaches is not confirmed
  • Whether a formal changelog entry exists. The basis here is the public forum thread; a separate release note or announcement page is not confirmed
  • The dashboard's internal response on Enterprise plans. Beyond the dollar display remaining on screen, the API layer is not confirmed
  • Cost display status across other AI coding tools. Whether the same direction of change occurred elsewhere is outside this article's scope. The section 6 checklist is written as a general standard, not an evaluation of specific products
  • A full comparison of usage and cost APIs across major model providers. The example given is the one whose public documentation was available; other providers' endpoint structures are not covered here
  • FOCUS adoption rates in AI tooling. The specification's existence and scope are confirmed; adoption counts are not

11. Conclusion - do not keep your observation point in one place

The lesson here is not a verdict on one company. If cost data lives only on a vendor's screen, that data is in a state where it can disappear at any time. Whether the reason is confusion reduction or a pricing overhaul, the outcome is identical.

Five lines, then.

  • Totals and breakdowns are different objects. With only totals you answer "how much" and never "where to cut"
  • Token-to-dollar conversion is for relative comparison only. A conversion that is not split by model and token type is guaranteed wrong, and even when split it does not replace an invoice
  • Keep observation points in at least two different layers. Vendor screen plus provider API, or vendor screen plus local logs. Two points in the same layer is the same as one
  • Monthly CSV backup is the one free precaution available today. Since retroactive changes are real, the download itself is the evidence
  • Ask about cost observability as a line item when selecting a tool. Ask afterward and you have no leverage

One last thing. This is not an argument against using a particular product. The confirmed change is an adjustment to what self-serve plans display, and total spending remains visible. But the fact that it applied retroactively, and the fact that the same data is served differently depending on plan tier, both belong on the question list for the next time you choose a tool.
