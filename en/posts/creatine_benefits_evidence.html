Creatine Benefits and the Evidence - What Is Settled and What Is Oversold

How phosphocreatine regenerates ATP, which outcomes have been reproduced often enough to count as established, where cognition and aging remain open, when loading actually matters, and what the kidney and hair-loss research really shows - a working line between what to trust and what to filter

2026-08-06 Health & Wellness
Before anything else: this article is general health information about creatine. It is not a substitute for medical diagnosis, prescription or treatment, and it is not written to push you toward or away from any product. Every dose mentioned below is an amount used in research or set by a regulator, not a personal recommendation. If you have kidney or liver disease, take prescription medication, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or are an adolescent, talk to a doctor or pharmacist before starting creatine or any other supplement.

Why creatine is the exception in the supplement aisle

Supplement conversations tend to land in the same place. The evidence is thin, the studies are in rodents, the human trials are small and short. For most ingredients that is an accurate summary. Applying the same test to the best-selling ingredient in the longevity market produced exactly that verdict in our breakdown of NMN benefits and the evidence.

Creatine sits outside that pattern. It has been studied in humans for more than three decades, and the International Society of Sports Nutrition describes creatine monohydrate in its position stand as the most effective ergogenic nutritional supplement currently available for increasing high-intensity exercise capacity and lean body mass during training. European regulators have accepted a cause-and-effect relationship under specific conditions. In South Korea, creatine is listed in the official health functional food codex as a notified functional ingredient. By the standards of this market, that is an unusual amount of institutional backing.

And that backing is exactly where the trouble starts. Credibility earned in the settled zone leaks into the unsettled one. Findings about strength get recycled as the evidence base for nootropic marketing, and results that only hold when you train get rewritten as if they hold when you do not. On the other side of the same product, decades-old rumours about kidney damage and hair loss keep circulating.

So this article does not argue that creatine is good or bad. The goal is to draw a line: where does the reproduced, established evidence end, and where does the open question begin? Once you can see that line, most marketing copy sorts itself.

1. What actually happens in the body - phosphocreatine puts ATP back together

1.1 Creatine is already in you

Creatine is not purely an external substance. Your liver, kidneys and pancreas synthesise roughly 1 g a day from arginine, glycine and methionine. The rest comes from food. Red meat and fish are the meaningful dietary sources, so an adult eating a typical omnivorous diet takes in another 1 to 2 g daily.

About 95% of the creatine in the body sits in skeletal muscle, and roughly two-thirds of that pool is stored in a phosphorylated form as phosphocreatine (PCr). Meanwhile 1 to 2% of the total creatine pool converts non-enzymatically to creatinine every day and leaves in the urine. Hold on to that last sentence; it is the seed of the kidney-test discussion later on.

1.2 The creatine kinase reaction - a ten-second battery

The fuel a working muscle actually spends is ATP, and the ATP stored inside muscle does not last more than a few seconds at maximum intensity. This is where phosphocreatine steps in: the creatine kinase enzyme hands its phosphate group to ADP and regenerates ATP immediately. The reaction needs no oxygen and involves almost no metabolic detour, which is why it is fast.

That determines the character of the whole ingredient. The system holds the floor for the first few seconds up to roughly ten seconds of maximal effort. And the rate at which phosphocreatine refills during rest between sets governs the output of the next set. This is why creatine transfers well to some sports and poorly to others.

1.3 Supplementation raises the store, and the store has a ceiling

Taking creatine typically raises total muscle creatine content by something in the range of 20 to 40%. But the store has an upper limit. If your tank is already close to full, there is little room left to fill. That single fact explains most of the individual variation discussed further down.

The brain has its own creatine kinase system too, and supplementation is reported to raise brain creatine content by roughly 5 to 15%. That is a smaller rise than the muscle figures. Keeping that number in mind makes cognition claims much easier to read.

2. What is well established - the results that keep reproducing

Everything in this section has been reproduced by different research groups in different populations and has been formalised either in a scientific society position stand or in a regulatory assessment. This is the sturdiest part of the article.

  • Increased muscle creatine and phosphocreatine stores: reproduced almost without exception. It is the starting point of the entire literature.
  • Improved performance in short-duration, high-intensity, repeated exercise: the European Food Safety Authority accepted a cause-and-effect relationship for this outcome in its 2011 assessment, and the wording creatine increases physical performance in successive bursts of short-term, high intensity exercise was subsequently entered on the EU register of authorised claims. The condition attached is 3 g per day.
  • Increases in lean mass and strength when combined with resistance training: this is the territory behind the society's description of monohydrate as the most effective nutritional ergogenic aid available.
  • Improved muscle strength in adults over 55: in a separate 2016 assessment, European regulators accepted a cause-and-effect relationship between creatine consumed together with resistance training and improvement in muscle strength in adults older than 55. The stated conditions were at least 3 g per day taken every day, plus resistance training three times a week at moderate intensity for several weeks.
  • The same dose taken only on training days: no effect was demonstrated in that assessment. That is precisely why both daily intake and the training itself sit inside the conditions of use.
  • Greater muscle uptake when co-ingested with carbohydrate: adding carbohydrate, or carbohydrate plus protein, increases creatine uptake into muscle. Whether that uptake difference converts into a performance difference is a separate question, and the society's reading is that it does not clearly exceed monohydrate alone.
  • The depth of the tolerability and safety record: the position stand describes short- and long-term supplementation as safe and well tolerated, citing data extending to 30 g per day for up to five years in healthy people. In clinical populations, high doses equivalent to 0.3 to 0.8 g per kilogram of body weight per day have been used for years.
  • Its legal footing in Korea: creatine is listed in the Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety health functional food codex as a notified functional ingredient. The recognised function is help with exercise performance during strength training, and the daily intake is set at 3 g.

That last point deserves emphasis, because it is rarer than it sounds. Most of the ingredients selling well in any given year have never been recognised as functional ingredients anywhere. NMN, mentioned above, has not been. Creatine starts from a different line: it is an ingredient permitted to carry a functional statement. But you also have to read which statement. The recognised wording is about exercise performance during strength training. It is not about the brain, and it is not about aging.

Read the authorised wording literally

Wording that a regulator has accepted carries its conditions inside the sentence. The European claim contains the phrase in successive bursts of short-term, high intensity exercise, and the Korean notified wording opens with during strength training. In other words, exercise is part of the condition. Advertising that implies muscle appears from the powder alone is telling a different story from the one the regulator signed off on.

3. What is not established, or is being oversold

This is the other side of the line. The items below have conflicting results, have not been adequately tested in humans, or sit outside any authorised claim in the first place.

  • Endurance capacity and endurance performance: in the same 2011 assessment, European regulators found the evidence insufficient for the endurance-related claim applications. Only the short-duration, high-intensity, repeated-effort claim survived.
  • Fat loss as such: no authorised claim covers creatine burning fat. Body composition changing because you trained harder for longer is not the same sentence as an ingredient reducing fat.
  • Cognitive improvement: there is a direction, but no conclusion. It gets its own section below.
  • Slowing aging or extending lifespan: no human trial has tested creatine with mortality or lifespan as an endpoint.
  • Treating or preventing disease: no country has approved creatine as a therapeutic agent for any indication. Research has run for years in neuromuscular and neurodegenerative conditions, but that is a different layer from drug approval.
  • Superiority of forms other than monohydrate: covered separately below, and unsupported by current evidence.
  • Safety during pregnancy and breastfeeding: data designed around these populations are lacking.
  • Safety in people whose kidney function is already impaired: findings from healthy adults cannot simply be transferred, and dedicated data are sparse.
  • Drug interactions in general: this has not been systematically catalogued. Improvising is a poor idea if you take renally cleared drugs or agents with known nephrotoxicity.
  • The idea that creatine builds muscle by itself: the presence of exercise inside the authorised conditions of use is itself the refutation.
Do not carry credibility sideways into the next column

The most common move in creatine marketing is to lead with the strength evidence and land the conclusion somewhere else. Phrases like 30 years of research or over 500 published studies almost always refer to accumulation in the exercise-performance literature. If a nootropic or anti-aging claim follows, the earlier sentence is not holding up the later one. Pause at the exact point where the subject of the sentence moves from muscle to brain, and most of the copy sorts itself out.

4. Do you have to load? Two protocols, one ceiling

This is the single most common question at the start. The short answer: loading is a way of arriving sooner, not a way of arriving somewhere else.

The two approaches described in the International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand look like this. Every number below is an amount used in research.

Approach Protocol used in research When stores fill Character
Load, then maintain About 0.3 g per kg body weight per day in four divided doses for 5 to 7 days, then 3 to 5 g per day About one week The fastest route. Because each dose is large, gastrointestinal discomfort is reported comparatively more often
Gradual intake Roughly 3 g per day from day one, sustained for three to four weeks About three to four weeks Reaches a comparable level; only the timing differs. The condition on the authorised European claim is also 3 g per day
Larger athletes Some sources note 5 to 10 g per day during maintenance - Reflects the point that maintenance needs scale with body mass and muscle mass
Strength in adults over 55 At least 3 g per day, every day, plus resistance training three times weekly at moderate intensity Several weeks The conditions under which European regulators accepted causality. Dosing only on training days showed no demonstrated effect

The column worth reading is the last one. Loading matters when timing matters. An athlete two weeks out from competition and a general trainee with no deadline are not in the same situation. If a product page puts you must load first in bold above the fold, that sentence may have more to do with first-month volume than with physiology.

As for timing around workouts, small studies have compared intake before and after training, but from the standpoint of filling the store what matters is topping it up every day. The finding that training-day-only dosing failed to demonstrate an effect in the over-55 strength assessment makes the point neatly.

5. Safety - kidneys, hair and water weight, one at a time

5.1 Kidneys - the confusion starts with a similarly named molecule

In adults with normal kidney function, there is no established evidence that creatine worsens renal function. Studies that tracked creatinine clearance and glomerular filtration rate found no meaningful deterioration, and systematic reviews and meta-analyses point in the same direction.

So why does the rumour refuse to die? Because of the mechanism noted earlier. Part of the creatine in your body converts to creatinine and is excreted in urine, so supplementing can raise the serum creatinine value itself. And the equations used at a routine check-up to estimate kidney function take that value as their main input. When the input goes up, the estimated glomerular filtration rate comes back down.

Put plainly: the kidney has not deteriorated, the arithmetic has been fed a different number. The same thing happens in people carrying a lot of muscle, right after hard training, or after a large serving of cooked red meat. The practical consequence is simple. If you have kidney function testing coming up, tell the clinician you are taking creatine. It can change how the result is interpreted.

Existing kidney disease is a different conversation

Everything above concerns adults with normal renal function. If you have chronic kidney disease, reduced renal function, or you take medication that stresses the kidney, findings from healthy volunteers do not transfer, and data designed around your situation are sparse. Do not start on your own initiative; speak to your physician first. The same applies if your kidney function is being monitored because of diabetes or hypertension.

5.2 Hair loss - one trial, and what happened afterwards

The creatine hair-loss story comes from a single study published in 2009. Twenty college rugby players took 25 g a day for seven days and then 5 g a day for fourteen days, and dihydrotestosterone (DHT) rose about 56% during loading and remained about 40% above baseline during maintenance.

Two things need saying. First, that trial never measured hair. Hair density, follicle miniaturisation, shedding - none of it appeared among the outcomes. It was a hormone study, and hair loss was an interpretation attached afterwards. Second, no subsequent study reproduced the hormonal finding.

In 2025 a randomised controlled trial took the question on directly. Resistance-trained men aged 18 to 40 received 5 g of creatine monohydrate or an identical placebo daily for 12 weeks, while hair density, follicular unit count and cumulative hair thickness were measured directly using a trichogram and a hair-imaging system. There were no significant differences between groups in dihydrotestosterone concentration, the DHT-to-testosterone ratio, or any hair-related outcome.

One trial does not cover every situation. The participants were a few dozen young men and the duration was 12 weeks, so whether it extends to someone with actively progressing pattern hair loss or a strong family history is a separate question. But the weight of evidence has clearly shifted. A 2009 study that never looked at hair and a 12-week randomised trial that measured hair directly do not belong on the same side of the scale.

5.3 Weight gain and so-called water bloat

Gaining weight in the first weeks is common. Early studies reported an increase in body water on the order of 0.5 to 1.0 L. Creatine is osmotically active and draws water into muscle cells, so what increases is mainly intracellular water. That is a different location from fluid pooling under the skin and making you look puffy.

Where it genuinely matters is weight-class sport. If you have to make weight, the shift itself becomes a variable to manage. Where body weight is not a constraint, early weight gain is closer to a signal that the store is filling.

5.4 Cramps and dehydration - a case where the arrow points the other way

The claim that creatine causes cramping and dehydration circulated for years but has been refuted in controlled clinical studies. The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand notes the opposite observation: users experienced a lower incidence of cramping and heat illness or dehydration. The same osmotic property is why hyper-hydration in the heat was investigated as a possible application at all.

The complaint that does show up in practice is gastrointestinal. It is most likely when a large amount goes down at once with little fluid. The four-way split in the loading protocol exists partly for that reason.

6. Cognition and aging - the least settled ground

This is where creatine marketing has shifted its centre of gravity over the past few years, and it is the section to read most carefully.

The premise is not invented. The brain does run a creatine kinase system, and supplementation is reported to raise brain creatine content by 5 to 15%. The society position stand cites work on reduced mental fatigue, working memory, processing speed and performance after sleep deprivation.

The problem is that the human results do not converge.

  • A 2023 meta-analysis of memory outcomes: pooling ten randomised controlled trials in healthy people, the overall effect was modest. But it broke apart by age. A large effect appeared in adults aged 66 to 76, and a non-significant effect in participants aged 11 to 31.
  • A 2023 randomised controlled trial in young adults: 30 healthy young adults received 10 or 20 g per day, and neither dose improved processing speed, episodic memory or executive function.
  • A 2024 meta-analysis: pooling randomised trials published between 1993 and 2024, it suggested possible benefit for memory, attention-related measures and information processing speed.
  • Sleep deprivation: an area of real interest, but the trials are small and the designs differ enough that generalising is difficult.
  • The regulatory picture: no cognitive wording appears among the authorised creatine claims in the European Union, and the recognised function of the Korean notified ingredient is likewise limited to exercise performance during strength training.

Compress all of that into one sentence and it reads: the possibility is open and the direction is visible, but this is not a settled effect. The evidence is thinnest exactly where the marketing is loudest - in young, healthy adults. That also fits the mechanism, since a store that is already close to full has little room left.

Aging claims need one more layer of care. Regulators did accept causality for muscle strength in adults over 55, but that is a strength finding with resistance training attached as a condition. It sits on a different level from claiming that creatine slows aging or extends life.

7. Do the other forms earn their price?

The shelf carries forms priced several times above monohydrate, usually labelled with better absorption, better solubility, or easier on the stomach. Here is where the evidence actually stands.

Form Marketing claim Evidence status
Monohydrate The basic form The most extensively studied and clinically effective form. Oral absorption is reported above 95%. The European authorised claim and the Korean codex standard both sit on this form
Hydrochloride (HCl) Far more soluble, therefore better absorbed The solubility is real, but there is no evidence that more of it reaches muscle. Dissolving in a glass and being retained in muscle are different measurements
Ethyl ester Crosses cell membranes more readily Does not promote greater retention than monohydrate, per the society position stand
Buffered forms Degrades less in stomach acid The claim of reduced in vivo degradation is described as unfounded
Nitrate and citrate Better blood flow or better uptake No evidence of greater retention than monohydrate
Micronised monohydrate Milled finer so it mixes better Chemically identical to monohydrate. It settles less in the glass, but it is not a different ingredient

One sentence runs through the whole table. There is currently no evidence that any other form raises muscle creatine more than monohydrate. The position stand states directly that citrate, serum, ethyl ester, buffered forms and nitrate do not promote greater creatine retention than monohydrate.

This matters practically because of price. Precisely because the evidence is thick, the basic form is cheap. The premium attached to alternative forms generally buys solubility and marketing, not evidence. That is unlike a category such as protein supplements, where the source material genuinely changes what is in the tub. With creatine, the molecule going in is the same one.

8. Why the same dose produces different results in different people

Thick evidence does not mean uniform response. The factors that determine the size of the effect are reasonably predictable.

8.1 Baseline stores set the size of the response

  • The variable that best explains response size is muscle creatine concentration before supplementation. Empty space in the tank is the room available for an effect.
  • People who eat a lot of red meat and fish already carry a substantial load and have proportionally less room to add.
  • Vegetarians and vegans, by contrast, get almost no dietary creatine and start lower - reported at roughly 20 to 30% below omnivore baselines - and tend to show larger increases in stores and larger gains in high-intensity performance when they supplement.
  • Being a so-called non-responder is not a sign that something is wrong with you. It is closer to already sitting near the ceiling with little left to fill.
  • Non-response rates around 20 to 30% have been reported for some formulations, and one trial reported that a particular combination eliminated non-responders - but that is a single study and hard to generalise from.
  • Muscle fibre composition, training history and total muscle mass all shape the outcome as well.

8.2 What you take it with

  • Carbohydrate: co-ingestion increases muscle creatine uptake. Whether the performance benefit exceeds monohydrate alone is doubtful in the society's reading.
  • Caffeine: no dangerous interaction has been reported. Some early studies raised the possibility that caffeine blunts the ergogenic benefit of creatine, but subsequent results have not been consistent enough to treat that as settled.
  • Protein: mentioned as part of the carbohydrate-plus-protein combination that increases uptake. Still, creatine and protein act at different points, and neither substitutes for the other.
  • Water: because creatine draws water into muscle cells, this is not the moment to get careless about fluid intake.

8.3 The sport changes how much you notice

  • Sports built on repeated maximal efforts - resistance training, repeated sprints, combat sports - are where the effect is most noticeable.
  • Steady-state endurance sport is outside the authorised claim altogether, and in some events the added body weight can work against you.
  • If you are not training at all, the condition inside the authorised wording is simply not met.

9. Where creatine stands under different regulators

Creatine is not an ingredient whose legal status swings wildly between countries. What does differ by jurisdiction is the wording you are allowed to put on the label. That is where copying a foreign product page usually goes wrong.

Country or body Regulatory position What may be stated
South Korea Notified functional ingredient listed in the health functional food codex Recognised function: help with exercise performance during strength training. Daily intake set at 3 g. Products meeting the codex standards and specifications may use it without a separate individual approval
European Union Holds an authorised health claim Creatine increases physical performance in successive bursts of short-term, high intensity exercise, under a condition of 3 g per day. In 2016 causality was also accepted for improvement in muscle strength in adults over 55 when combined with resistance training
United States Marketed as a dietary supplement There is no pre-market efficacy review in this framework. Structure-function statements are permitted; disease treatment or prevention claims are not. The phrase FDA approved does not apply to a supplement
Anti-doping rules Not on the World Anti-Doping Agency prohibited list A performance-affecting substance that is nonetheless not banned. Because of contamination risk, tested athletes are safer choosing batch-tested products

Two things stand out. First, creatine is one of the few supplement ingredients with a regulatory basis for a claim in major jurisdictions. Second, every one of those permitted claims is tied to exercise performance and muscle strength. Cognition, anti-aging and fat loss are not authorised claim territory anywhere.

10. Six filters for products and advertising

Compressed into something usable while standing in front of a shelf, it comes to this.

  1. Where is the subject of the sentence? Claims about strength and repeated high-intensity effort sit on thick evidence. The moment the subject moves to the brain, to aging, or to body fat, the thickness of the evidence changes underneath it.
  2. Is exercise part of the condition? Both the Korean notified wording and the European authorised claim include training. Copy written as if the effect happens without it has stepped outside the approved scope.
  3. Is the price justified by the form? Nothing supports the premium on hydrochloride, ethyl ester or buffered versions. Monohydrate is the form with the evidence.
  4. Is loading being sold as mandatory? Loading is an option that changes arrival time, not a requirement. Bold type on this point is worth a second look.
  5. Does the label say approved or certified? Because creatine is a notified ingredient in Korea, a product meeting the codex standards may carry the functional statement - but certification is not the regulatory term, and the permitted wording is the recognised functional wording, verbatim.
  6. Can you see purity and testing? Creatine is an ingredient where manufacturing impurities can matter. Look for stated purity and available certificates of analysis, and if you compete under anti-doping rules, look for batch testing specifically.

Frequently asked questions

Q1. Does creatine do nothing if you skip the loading phase?

Loading moves the date on which your stores fill up. It does not move the ceiling they fill up to. The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand describes two protocols: roughly 0.3 g per kilogram of body weight per day split into four doses for 5 to 7 days followed by 3 to 5 g per day for maintenance, or simply 3 g per day from the start for three to four weeks. Both bring muscle creatine to a comparable level; the difference is how quickly you get there. The condition attached to the authorised European claim is 3 g per day, with no loading assumed. These figures are amounts used in research, not a personal recommendation - decide with a doctor or pharmacist who knows your situation.

Q2. Is it true that creatine damages your kidneys?

In adults with normal kidney function, there is no established evidence that creatine worsens renal function. Studies tracking creatinine clearance and glomerular filtration rate found no meaningful deterioration, and systematic reviews and meta-analyses point the same way. The confusion comes from a similarly named molecule: part of the creatine in your body converts to creatinine and is excreted in urine, so supplementing can raise the serum creatinine value itself. Because the standard equations estimate kidney function from that value, an estimated glomerular filtration rate can come back lower. The kidney has not deteriorated; the input to the arithmetic has changed. If you already have kidney disease or reduced renal function, this reasoning does not transfer, and you should talk to your physician before starting.

Q3. Does creatine cause hair loss?

The claim traces back to a single 2009 trial in 20 college rugby players, which reported dihydrotestosterone rising about 56% during loading and staying elevated above baseline during maintenance. That trial never measured hair at all - not density, not follicle miniaturisation, not shedding - and no study since has reproduced the hormonal finding. In 2025 a randomised controlled trial took the question head-on: resistance-trained men received 5 g of creatine monohydrate or placebo daily for 12 weeks while hair density, follicular unit counts and cumulative hair thickness were measured directly with a trichogram and a hair-imaging system. There were no significant differences between groups in hormone levels or any hair outcome. If you already have progressing pattern hair loss or a strong family history, it is still worth raising with a clinician who knows your case.

Q4. Does creatine help memory or brain function?

This is the least settled part of the topic. The brain has its own creatine kinase system and supplementation is reported to raise brain creatine content by roughly 5 to 15%, but whether that translates into cognitive performance is where results split. A 2023 meta-analysis of memory trials found a modest overall effect that broke apart by age: a large effect in adults aged 66 to 76 and a non-significant effect in people aged 11 to 31. A 2023 randomised controlled trial in healthy young adults found no improvement in processing speed, episodic memory or executive function at either 10 or 20 g per day. A 2024 meta-analysis suggested possible benefit for memory, attention measures and information processing speed. There is a direction here, but not a settled conclusion, and no cognitive wording appears on the European register of authorised claims.

Q5. Are the expensive forms better than plain monohydrate?

There is currently no evidence that any other form raises muscle creatine more than monohydrate does. The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand states that creatine citrate, serum, ethyl ester, buffered forms and nitrate do not promote greater creatine retention than monohydrate. The hydrochloride form does dissolve far better in water, but solubility in a glass and arrival in muscle are two different measurements. Monohydrate is reported to have oral absorption above 95% and is the form with by far the deepest research base. Micronised monohydrate differs only in particle size; it is the same molecule. A price difference is not an evidence difference.

Conclusion

Here is what can be said firmly about creatine today. Taking it raises muscle creatine and phosphocreatine stores. Improved performance in short-duration, high-intensity repeated efforts is an outcome European regulators have accepted as causal, and increases in lean mass and strength alongside resistance training are the territory formalised in the society position stand. In adults over 55, strength improvement was accepted under the conditions of daily intake plus resistance training. The safety record is deeper than almost anything else on the shelf, and in Korea creatine is a notified functional ingredient permitted to carry a functional statement.

Outside that boundary the picture changes. Cognition shows a direction but splits by age group, and the evidence for improvement in young, healthy adults is thin. No human trial has used aging or lifespan as an endpoint. Endurance performance and fat loss are outside the authorised claim scope. Superiority of non-monohydrate forms remains unsupported.

Two long-standing fears also resolve. The kidney story turns out to be about serum creatinine and an estimating equation, and the hair-loss story began with a trial that never looked at hair and now has a 12-week randomised controlled trial measuring hair directly. Cramping and dehydration have been observed in the opposite direction from the rumour.

Reading creatine, then, is simple enough. Sentences whose subject is muscle are generally trustworthy; sentences whose subject has moved to the brain or to aging are still open. And even the settled part holds only on the condition that you train. Somewhere along that line, after a conversation with a healthcare professional who knows your history and your medications, is the right place to decide.
