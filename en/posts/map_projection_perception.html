A Map Is Not a Copy of the World - What Gets Discarded on the Way to a Flat Screen

What a projection keeps and what it throws away, and why navigation gives up on scale

August 30, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: a screen that folds the road ahead

You have probably watched a navigation display where the next junction was clinging to the very top edge, or had already slid off the screen entirely. Zoom in and your current position swells until context disappears. Zoom out and the road ahead becomes a thread. The screen is flat and finite while the road runs long, so one of the two has to be sacrificed.

A demonstration that attacks this problem head-on circulated in developer forums recently. It folds the road and terrain in front of the vehicle upward, keeping the nearby stretch in something close to a driver's-eye view while the distant road bends over into a top-down map, all inside one frame. The people behind it summarise it in a single line, Navigation Reimagined, and they published a 3D scene you can spin in a browser. The page credits the PlayCanvas engine for rendering, carries a patent-pending notice with an application number (PCT/EP2026/058725), and expects you to move around with the keyboard and mouse drags. In other words this is a demo you poke at in a browser, not a product. It is not shipping in a car, and the people who built it do not claim it is.

This article is not a review of that demo. It is about the very old question the demo pushed back onto the table: a map is not a copy of the world, it is the result of a choice. Somebody already decided what to preserve and what to discard, and without knowing that decision you cannot explain why the screen looks the way it does. How geographic data is stored, transformed and queried is a separate subject and is left out here. This piece stays on how to show what you already have.

1. Flattening always breaks something

Peel an orange and try to press the skin flat on a desk without tearing it. You cannot. Something rips and something stretches. Putting the Earth onto paper or a display is exactly that operation, and the obstacle is a property of curved surfaces rather than a shortfall of technique. Faster computers do not change it.

Choosing a projection is therefore choosing what to keep and, in the same motion, what to pay with. The US Geological Survey's projection material states the situation in one sentence: a map can show one or more, but never all, of true directions, true distances, true areas and true shapes.

In practice projections are grouped by which of those they defend.

ClassWhat it keepsWhat it loses insteadWhere it is used
ConformalLocal angles and shapes over small areasArea, inflating badly the further you go from the equatorMarine and air navigation, web map tiles
Equal-areaThe ratio of areasShape, especially crushed or smeared near the polesPopulation, output, any comparison of quantity
EquidistantDistance measured from one chosen point or lineConsistent distance relationships across the whole mapReach or range drawn from a single origin
AzimuthalDirection from the centre toward any other pointDistance and area as you move away from the centrePolar routes, radio coverage
CompromiseNothing completelyBut nothing collapses catastrophically eitherTextbooks and wall maps of the world

The important content of that table is the relationship between the first two rows. Conformal and equal-area cannot both hold. If a map keeps shapes honest then its areas are necessarily wrong, and if it keeps areas honest then its shapes are bent. If you think you have seen a world map that did both, it was almost certainly a compromise projection getting both slightly wrong.

2. So there is no such thing as an accurate map

Once you accept that, the question changes. Not which map is accurate, but what did this map decide to preserve. The phrase accurate map is as empty as the phrase accurate graph, and for the same reason: strip out what it was drawn to answer and there is nothing left to judge it against.

The famous case is the Mercator projection. First presented in 1569, this cylindrical projection is conformal, and because of that property a course held at a constant compass bearing is drawn as a straight line. Lay a ruler between two ports, measure the angle of that line, hold that bearing, and you arrive. That is why it became the standard projection for navigation from the eighteenth century. It is not the shortest path. It was, however, an executable path in an era with nothing but a compass.

Use the same map to compare the sizes of countries and it answers wrongly at once. The map is not bad; the use is mismatched. Purpose selects the projection.

  • You must hold a compass bearing - a conformal projection that keeps angles. The old choice of sailors and pilots.
  • You must compare areas - an equal-area projection. For distribution and statistical maps there is effectively no alternative.
  • Distance from one point matters - an equidistant projection anchored on that point, such as an epicentre or a transmitter.
  • Direction across the poles matters - an azimuthal projection. Cylindrical projections diverge at the poles and cannot draw them at all.
  • You are working over a small region - a coordinate system fitted to that region. Most of the distortion that appears when the whole world goes on one sheet never arises over a small extent.

3. Why web maps settled on one particular projection

A map in a browser is not one large image. It is a grid of square tiles stitched together. Each zoom step splits one tile into four, and only the tiles inside the viewport are fetched. That structure imposes several requirements on the projection.

  • The whole world must fit neatly into a single square, so that halving it repeatedly leaves no remainder.
  • The shape of the grid must not change as you zoom, or the tile seams become visible.
  • Converting coordinates to pixels must be cheap. That calculation runs for every tile and every scroll.
  • Buildings and roads must stay recognisable at every zoom level, which is where conformality earns its place.

The Mercator family satisfies nearly all of that. As tiling conventions consolidated through the 2000s, spherical Mercator became the de facto standard, and today it travels under the identifier EPSG:3857. One further compromise is baked in to make the arithmetic lighter: the Earth is treated as a perfect sphere rather than an ellipsoid. Because of that approximation, Web Mercator is not strictly conformal but only near-conformal.

Fitting the world into a square also means cutting the top and bottom off. The cut sits at roughly 85.05 degrees north and south, which is why the polar regions are simply absent from web maps. It is not that the poles are unimportant. In a cylindrical projection the pole is infinitely far away.

The summary is that the web map projection was not chosen to aim at geographic truth. It was chosen to satisfy the engineering demands of tiling and real-time rendering, and that choice comes with a bill.

4. The impression of size and the misreading it creates

In the Mercator projection the scale factor is the secant of the latitude. The same multiplier applies both north-south and east-west, so area grows as its square. Put into numbers:

LatitudeLength multiplierArea multiplierWhat happens on screen
0 degrees (equator)1x1xThe reference. Scale is correct only here
30 degreesabout 1.15xabout 1.33xStill hard to notice by eye
45 degreesabout 1.41x2xThe same country begins to look twice as wide
60 degrees2x4xNorthern Europe and southern Canada visibly swell
80 degreesabout 5.76xabout 33xComparison becomes meaningless

The most quoted consequence is Greenland. On a world map it looks about the size of Africa, while in reality Africa is roughly fourteen times larger. Using figures that can be checked: Greenland's total area is 2,166,086 square kilometres and the African continent covers about 30,365,000 square kilometres. The division gives 14.0.

The point is not that the map lies. This output follows the definition of the projection exactly, and it was priced in the moment conformality was chosen. The problem is that people read size on a screen as importance. What is drawn large is remembered as large, and after enough years of the same picture the impression occupies the place where knowledge should be. The map is not wrong; the rule for reading it was never handed over.

Screen size silently becoming meaning is not unique to maps. The way an observation method quietly fixes the range of questions you can even ask came up with different material in what it means to see network traffic. The mirror-image case, where erasing something turns into a signal of its own, is in the paradox of map blurring.

5. A navigation display is not a map

Everything above concerns maps. Navigation is a different object entirely. While driving, what you need is not the shape of a region but a single next action. Right in 300 metres, then left at the second signal. Scale contributes almost nothing to delivering that.

So navigation displays cheerfully do things a map would never do. They enlarge the area around a junction beyond its true size and compress the long straight stretches where nothing happens. In lane guidance the road width and curvature vanish and only arrows remain. Discard scale, preserve sequence is the operating principle of this whole family of displays.

  • Transit diagrams - the idea taken to its limit. Distances and bearings between stations are wrong, yet transfer order and connectivity are exact, and that order is precisely what the rider came for.
  • Strip maps - an old format that unrolls a single route into a ribbon laid out in order. Anything off the route is simply not drawn.
  • Turn lists - drop the map entirely and write the actions in sequence. This works best exactly where no screen is available or usable.
  • Lane guidance - keeps only the question of which lane you should be in and throws away everything else.
  • Junction close-ups - blow one complex intersection up to fill the frame. At that moment the display has two scales at once.

If a map answers what the world is shaped like, navigation answers what to do next. Applying the same rules to both because they came from the same data makes both worse.

6. The idea of folding or bending the view

Folding the road ahead belongs to this lineage. Under the constraint that a screen is flat and finite, it keeps the near view large and compresses the far view into the same frame. Information visualisation has worked this ground for a long time under the heading of showing focus together with context, and fisheye-style displays that swell the centre and squeeze the periphery use the same principle.

What is gained and what is lost separates fairly cleanly.

AxisWhat folding or bending gainsWhat the same choice costs
Field of viewThe next two or three actions fit in one frameDensity rises, so scanning the frame takes longer
SwitchingNo need to alternate between driver view and map viewThe boundary blurs, so which view you are reading gets ambiguous
Distance senseDistant features never fall off the screenThe relation between screen distance and real distance collapses
ShapeRoad continuity is shown without a cutA straight road is drawn curved and cannot be told apart from real curvature
ImplementationOne 3D scene replaces two separate viewsIt only works where 3D terrain and building data exist

Rows three and four carry the most weight. On a curved surface, two pairs of points separated by the same distance on screen are separated by different distances in reality, and that difference varies continuously with position. It is the problem from section three repeating itself, except this time it happens on the road in front of you rather than on a world map. Being unable to tell whether curvature came from the display or from the road has the same root.

7. The cost of a display that moves

From here the issue stops being about the screen and becomes about people. When a view rotates, tilts and slides, it can help orientation and it can equally damage it.

The widely accepted account of dizziness and nausea is sensory conflict. Discomfort arises when the motion reported by the eyes disagrees with the motion reported by the vestibular system in the inner ear, and visuo-vestibular mismatch is commonly reported across modes of transport and in virtual reality environments. A car interior already sits close to that condition, because the body is moving while the gaze is tied to a fixed display. If the display itself rotates and tilts, one more layer of mismatch is stacked on.

The magnitude of the response varies enormously between individuals. Some people look at the same display and feel nothing whatsoever; others cannot read the text after a few minutes. A passenger in the back seat watching a screen is in worse conditions than someone looking out of the window.

This is not a detail to wave away. Motion sickness is a bodily response, not a matter of taste or toughness. One bad experience and that person never turns the display on again, and in some cases avoids the journey itself. If you build motion into a display, the option to reduce it or switch it off completely has to exist before any effort goes into polishing how it looks. Honouring the operating system's reduce-motion setting belongs to the same obligation.

Even for people who feel nothing, there is a charge. A moving display has to be re-read. A static frame stays in your head once you have looked at it, while a frame that keeps deforming must be interpreted from scratch every time. That means more time spent looking, and for the reason in the next section, that time is not merely time.

8. The condition called driving

However good a display is, while driving the time spent looking at it is itself the risk. At this point the criteria for evaluating an interface change completely.

The visual-manual driver distraction guidelines issued by the US National Highway Traffic Safety Administration in 2013 pin the criterion to numbers. A single glance away from the forward roadway should average two seconds or less, and the sum of all glances away during one task should be twelve seconds or less. Under the occlusion test method, a task must be completable in a series of 1.5-second glances with a cumulative total not exceeding twelve seconds. The guidelines state plainly why glance behaviour was chosen as the yardstick: research establishes the link between eyes off the road and crash risk.

Turned around toward the display side, that reads as follows.

  • A display is judged by whether it can be read within two seconds in one glance. Not by its beauty, and not by how much it contains.
  • An addition that buys one more glance may be a net cost even when the information itself is genuinely useful.
  • A frame that must be reinterpreted lengthens reading time. The price of motion from section seven converts into real risk here.
  • Anything requiring manipulation while moving consumes both eyes and hands. Tasks such as entering a destination belong before departure, as a rule.

Good in-vehicle interfaces therefore push in the direction of making you look at the screen less. Voice guidance is decisive precisely because it consumes no gaze at all. How far ahead the turn is and whether you need to change lanes are perfectly deliverable through hearing, and in practice most of a journey runs on audio alone. Haptic alerts exploit the same property. Moving notifications onto channels that do not use the eyes leaves that much more of your gaze on the road.

Seen this way the display's role is a confirmation channel, not the primary one. It is where you glance to check that the spoken instruction matched what you expected, and that glance has to finish inside two seconds. Curved or flat, the same condition applies.

9. North-up and track-up

Orientation is an old argument. Should the map be locked with north always at the top, or rotated so that the direction of travel is always up? Looking at the published research, the case for a universal winner is weak.

  • Cuevas and colleagues, 2001 - an experiment in the Proceedings of the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society Annual Meeting. No difference in accuracy or speed appeared between the two. The track-up group did report the task as more difficult, rated their workload higher, and judged the display less helpful. Spatial ability scores correlated significantly with performance accuracy.
  • Park, Dixit and Pariafsai, 2024 - Applied Sciences volume 14, article 4012, testing forty participants in a virtual maze. The result split by cognitive style: field-dependent participants, who lean on surrounding context, did better with a forward-up map, while field-independent participants did better with north-up.

Overlaying the two studies gives a reasonably clear picture. It splits by person and it splits by purpose. To support the decision in front of you right now, track-up helps, because the top of the screen is the way ahead and the mental rotation step disappears. To learn the area itself, north-up helps, because the region is drawn the same way every time and the images accumulate into one.

A workable rule: unfamiliar road where you only need to survive the next junction, track-up. A neighbourhood you want to internalise, or a comparison between several routes, north-up. And letting people choose generally beats doing one of them very well. Forcing a single mode onto a question that demonstrably splits guarantees that half your users are uncomfortable.

10. The distance between a demo and a product

A demonstration gets to pick its conditions, and that is part of its job. You sit at a still desk, rotate the viewpoint slowly by hand, and look at one well-built 3D scene. The demo mentioned above is set up that way, and the page carries credits for the rendering engine and for the author of the scene.

Everyday conditions sit on the opposite side of that list.

  • Vibration - reading small text on a curved surface with road vibration underneath is not the same as reading it at a desk.
  • Light - under backlight and reflections, most depth cues expressed through contrast simply disappear.
  • Hardware - redrawing a 3D scene dozens of times a second returns as heat and battery drain. When frame rate drops on an older device, the discomfort of motion gets worse rather than better.
  • Data - this kind of display only works where 3D terrain and building data exist. What the screen should draw in areas without that data remains an open question.
  • Edge cases - tight curves, junctions stacked one after another, intersections under an overpass. The places where information is needed most are usually the places hardest to render.
  • Duration - being impressive for thirty seconds and being bearable for two hours are different properties, and most display problems only surface in the second.

Public discussion split along similar lines. Alongside praise for novelty came immediate concerns about motion sickness, about information thinning out on tight turns, and a suggestion that the amount of curvature ought to be adjustable. The patent-pending notice likewise marks a stage of securing an idea, not a stage of shipping a product.

None of this is a put-down. The job of a demo is to show that something is possible, and on that count it succeeded. It demonstrated that the problem of not seeing far enough ahead is real, and that deforming a 3D space is one way to address it. But between looking good in a demonstration and being usable every day lies exactly the list above, and that gap usually closes not by refining the display further but by taking things out of it.

What could not be confirmed

  • Whether the demo has been tested during actual driving, and with what result. Nothing in the published material covers it, and the same goes for performance figures or any productisation timeline.
  • The scope of the claims in the patent application. Only the application number notice was seen; the specification was not opened.
  • The number of participants in the 2001 Cuevas experiment. It is not in the publicly available abstract.
  • Any study measuring the effect of curved displays on motion sickness directly. The general principle of sensory conflict is established, but no material addressing this specific presentation style was found.
  • Statistics on default orientation settings and how often users change them in consumer navigation products.
  • The multipliers in the table are computed for spherical Mercator. Real services sometimes apply additional correction at display scale, so measurements taken off a screen may not match exactly.

Conclusion: a map is not the answer, it is a chosen answer

Flattening a sphere is never free. The moment you pick which of area, angle, distance and direction to defend, the rest is paid out. So the question to ask of a map is not whether it is accurate but what it decided to preserve.

Navigation pushes that question one step further, because it gives up most of the properties of a map in order to keep one next action. The scale being wrong is design rather than defect, and whether the design is right is settled not by geographic fidelity but by how many seconds it takes a driver to recognise what to do next.

Attempts to fold and bend the view have to be recomputed on that scoreboard. Did fitting more into one frame lengthen the time needed to read it? Did the motion help orientation, or did it make people unwell? And was the discomfort made switchable? Where looking is itself the risk, a display that makes you look less beats a display that shows you more.

Saying that a map is not a copy of the world is not an instruction to distrust maps. It means that once you know what a map chose to preserve, you know which questions it can answer and which it cannot. That distinction is needed just as much on a world map as it is on the road beyond the windscreen.
