What Changes When the Terminal Is Always There - The Few Seconds It Takes to Open One

Where an always-on shell fits, where it does not, and the risk of leaving one open

2026-09-02 Dev & Infra

Count the actions it takes to open a terminal for a single command and you land on five or six. Call up the app switcher, pick the right window out of several that share an icon, check which directory that window happens to be sitting in, move if it is the wrong one, and only then type. No single step takes a full second. All of them together take a few.

But repeat those few seconds several dozen times a day and at some point a person stops paying them. That is how a task that would take thirty seconds in a shell ends up taking two minutes across a file browser and a settings pane. Friction does not make work slower. It makes work happen differently. That is why the always-available terminal keeps getting rebuilt after more than twenty years, and it is also what decides which questions are worth asking before you adopt one.

This article does not recommend or rank a product, and it is not an installation or configuration guide. The tools mentioned appear only as examples of a shape, and their features and limits are described only as far as their official documentation states them. Current as of September 2026.

The trigger - one terminal pinned beside the Dock

In early September 2026 a small open-source macOS app that parks a terminal permanently next to the Dock made the rounds in a Korean developer community. The repository describes itself in one line: a terminal that is just always there, neither summoned nor dismissed. It is written in Swift under an MIT license, requires macOS 13 or later, and asks for Accessibility permission so it can follow the Dock as it moves and resizes. What runs inside is a real login shell rather than an imitation, so state persists between commands, and there is one shortcut for switching height and another for picking a theme.

The thing worth noticing is not the feature list but a single design choice. Almost everything in this family is the summoned kind, and this one went with the never dismissed kind. Most of what follows in this article forks at exactly that point. Hotkey conflicts only exist on one side; permanent screen occupancy and permanent exposure only exist on the other.

There is nothing to overstate about its maturity. At the time the repository was checked it had fewer than a hundred commits and roughly three hundred stars, and its own documentation lists a known issue where pasted text renders in the wrong colour until the next keystroke. Community discussion added that users who keep the Dock on the left or right edge are not covered yet. This is an early-stage project by one person. The article does not recommend the tool. It looks at the shape the tool revived.

Why the same shape gets rebuilt for twenty years

An input line that slides down from the top of the screen became widely known through the console in the 1996 game Quake. Press the tilde key and a console drops from the top, and from there you change settings or enter commands. When that interaction moved onto desktop terminals it kept the name, and people have called it Quake-style ever since.

According to a survey that traced the lineage, the early Linux desktop implementation was Kuake around 2003, and the name Yakuake itself came from being yet another Kuake. Tilda followed by 2006, and Guake was built in 2007 after its author saw Yakuake. Yakuake is a drop-down terminal built on KDE Konsole technology that rolls down from the top of the screen, and Guake is the GNOME-side equivalent, summoned and dismissed with F12 by default. On macOS, iTerm2 has long offered a hotkey window tied to a profile, and on Windows, Windows Terminal shipped quake mode as a first-class feature.

There is a reason the same idea gets written from scratch this many times. This shape is not really a terminal feature; it is a window-management feature. Keeping a window above everything else, showing it on every workspace, and summoning it with a global key all hang off operating-system APIs. So it does not port. Every time the desktop environment changes, someone rewrites it in that environment's language.

  • The problem is not inside the terminal emulator. Switching to a better terminal does not fix it
  • The fix is small. Pinning one window to a position and claiming one key is something one person can build over a weekend
  • It is welded to platform APIs, so it does not travel. Rewriting for macOS is faster than porting a Linux implementation
  • Taste in it is narrow. Height, transparency and animation duration differ per person, and somebody else's settings never quite fit your hand

What the friction actually is

Saying it takes a few seconds to open a terminal lumps several different things together. Split it into stages and it becomes clear which of them a resident window actually touches.

StageWhy it takes timeDoes a resident or drop-down window remove itWhat is left over
Launching a windowStarting a process, reading the profile and the shell startup filesYes. It is already openThe cost did not vanish, it moved to login time
Finding the windowSeveral windows of the same app, buried or on another workspaceMostly yesKeep several resident windows and the same problem comes straight back
Changing directoryA new shell starts at home and the target path gets typed by handNoA resident window sits wherever it was last. Nothing guarantees that is the right place
Switching contextIt covers what you were looking at, and eyes and hands move to a different rule setDepends on the formAn always-visible form reduces switching but permanently splits your attention instead
Waiting for a resultThe window is tied up until the command finishesNoLong-running work occupies the resident window exactly as it would any other

There is one thing to read out of that table. Friction genuinely disappears in only the first two stages. The rest stays. And yet the perceived effect is larger than that, because the point where a person decides to just do it in the GUI is almost always one of those two. Nobody who is already sitting at a prompt gives up because of a directory change. Giving up happens before you start.

The other direction deserves the same attention. When starting gets cheap, things get started that otherwise would not have been, and some of those are delete commands typed without a second look. Any design that removes friction produces both results at once. What a mouse-first interface leaves out on the keyboard side is covered separately in making a GUI fully keyboard operable; this article only looks at the opposite threshold, the one in front of the command line.

One-line jobs are not long-running jobs

The work an always-on terminal suits and the work it does not split along a sharp line. Trying to do both in one window is the most common way this ends badly for both.

Kind of workDoes the resident window suit itWhy
One-line checks (free space, a process, the time, an IP)Suits it wellThe result appears immediately and the window is not tied up. This is the entire reason the form exists
Short conversions and calculations (encoding, dates, hashes)Suits it wellFaster than hunting for a web tool, and input and output fit in one view
Builds and test runs that take minutesDoes not suit itIt occupies the window. The moment a one-line job arrives you end up opening another one anyway
Several tasks running side by sideDoes not suit itThere is usually only one resident window. Add tabs and you have rebuilt an ordinary terminal
Connecting to remote serversDoes not suit itCovered in the next section. The lifetime of a window and the lifetime of a session are different problems
Tailing logs liveDepends on the formUsable in an always-visible window, pointless in one you have to summon, because it is invisible the rest of the time

One rule falls out of that. Most of the value of a resident window comes from it always being empty. Start a build in it and it stops being a ready prompt, and three minutes later you are hunting for a window again. The friction was not removed, it was relocated. Work that is mostly about moving files around belongs closer to the gap a terminal file manager fills than to a single input line.

  • The default state of a resident window should be an empty prompt. Calling that the only operating rule would not be an exaggeration
  • If something looks likely to run past about thirty seconds, start it in another window from the beginning. Moving it halfway through costs more
  • Send anything whose output you will want to reread elsewhere too. Scrollback in a short, narrow window rolls off fast
  • How often you end up with an occupied resident window is a direct measure of how far the discipline has slipped. Several times a day means the form does not fit you

State accumulates - convenient until it is an accident

A resident shell never exits. So directories, environment variables, shell functions and history keep piling up in it. That is both this form's biggest convenience and its quietest hazard.

The convenient side is obvious. It is still in yesterday's working directory, and a value you exported earlier is still set, so you do not retype it. The problem is the same property running the other way. Everyone knows a freshly opened window is at home. Nobody knows where the resident window is. And the two look identical. Combine that with the habit of typing without reading the prompt and you get an irreversible command executed in the wrong directory.

Environment variables behave the same way. A proxy setting or a connection profile dropped in temporarily disappears on its own in a new window, but in a resident one it is still attached to a command hours later. A good share of the it-works-on-my-machine cases start right there. It is also why the advice to reproduce the problem in a clean shell fails: there is no clean shell.

History is subtler. The bash documentation states that when an interactive shell exits it writes history to the file named by HISTFILE, and that if that variable is unset or null the shell does not save the command history when it exits. A shell that has not exited in weeks is therefore holding that period's commands in memory and not in a file. Kill it and the whole period is gone, and commands typed in other windows were never in this window's history to begin with. That is exactly where the up arrow you rely on starts behaving unlike you expect.

  • A prompt configured without the current path, combined with a resident window, is the pairing that raises accident probability the most
  • A temporary environment variable's lifetime becomes the lifetime of the window rather than the lifetime of the task. Forget that difference and you will not find the cause
  • Making the resident window visually distinguishable from ordinary ones removes much of both problems. Colour, prompt shape, whatever, the means does not matter
  • One rule that says irreversible commands do not get typed in the resident window is worth more than ten settings

It solves a different problem than session management

Discussions about whether to use a resident window or a terminal multiplexer come up constantly. But the two were never on the same layer, and the documentation makes that plain.

The tmux manual says a session may be detached from a screen and continue running in the background, then later be reattached, and that each session is persistent and will survive accidental disconnection such as an ssh connection timeout, or intentional detaching. The architecture matches: a session is displayed on screen by a client while all sessions are managed by a single server process. The GNU screen manual says the same thing from its own angle, that programs continue to run when their window is not currently visible and even when the whole screen session is detached from the user's terminal.

What those protect is the lifetime of a process. What a resident window protects is the availability of a prompt. They do not overlap. Adopting a resident window does not make a remote session survive anything; close the laptop and the connection inside that window goes with it. In the other direction, a multiplexer does nothing about summoning a window. However healthily a session lives on the server, the local window that displays it still has to be found and raised.

Problem being solvedResident or drop-down windowTerminal multiplexerCan one replace the other
The few seconds it takes to summon a promptSolves itDoes not solve itNo
Whether a process keeps running after a disconnectionDoes not solve itSolves itNo
Seeing several tasks side by sideLimited. The window is narrow and shortSolves it. Splits and window lists are its day jobOnly partly
Picking work back up from another machineDoes not solve itSolves it. Reattach and it is where you left itNo
Screen occupancyIncreases itReduces it. Many things fit inside one windowThey point in opposite directions

Which lands here. If most of your work is local, a resident window pays well. If most of it is on remote servers, the return on investing in session management is overwhelmingly larger. People who do both end up using both, and that is not waste.

A toggle key is a resource there is only one of

Choose the summoned form and you need a global hotkey. Global hotkeys are a trickier resource than they look, and the Windows Terminal documentation says so without hedging.

  • Keys bound to the global summon action will not work in other applications while the terminal is running. They always focus the terminal window
  • If another running application already registered those keys through the RegisterHotKey API, the terminal cannot listen for those keystrokes at all
  • Elevated and unelevated instances cannot both register the same keys. Preview and stable builds are the same, and whichever launched first wins
  • The keystrokes only work when an instance of the terminal is already running. Which makes launching at login effectively a precondition

The same page defines quake mode: a special mode a window enters when it is named _quake, and a shorthand for a global summon action with the name, drop-down duration, visibility toggle, monitor handling and virtual-desktop handling already decided. The interesting part is that the global summon action is not bound to any key in the default settings. Given the conflicts above, that is a reasonable choice: it hands the decision to whoever knows what else is running on that machine.

There is also the matter of where a hand remembers. The tilde position and F12 became conventional because they are reachable without moving your hand far. But that neighbourhood is small and already crowded. Debugger keys in editors, screen capture, virtual desktop switching and input-method switching all live around there. So the criterion is not is this comfortable to press but what is this key already doing.

A badly chosen toggle key is worse for not announcing itself. Days later you notice some shortcut no longer works in another app, and it takes a while longer to connect that to a terminal hotkey you claimed months ago. Pick the always-visible form and this problem does not exist at all. You pay the next section's price instead.

The price of giving up screen space

The always-visible form spends no hotkey and spends screen space permanently instead. That trade flips its answer depending on how big the screen is.

FormWhat it removesWhat it costsOn a small screen
Always-visible panelThe act of summoning. You never forget it existsPermanent screen occupancy. Anything moving in it keeps pulling your eyeWorst case. Vertical space runs out first
Summoned drop-downFinding and launching a windowOne global hotkey. It is forgotten while invisibleFavourable. It only takes space while in use
Editor integrated panelApp switching. The project path is already correctPoor fit for anything outside the editor, and heavy if the editor is heavyMiddling. You end up folding and unfolding it
Ordinary terminal windowNothingFind it and raise it every timeDepends entirely on your window-management habits

Vertical space is the expensive kind. On a single laptop display, a panel pinned along the bottom takes the equivalent of a few lines of code up to about ten, depending on font size. Something else used to be in that band: the editor's problem list, the browser's developer tools, or simply the text. And if you already use an editor's built-in terminal, the same role now exists in two places, and one of the two eventually goes unused.

Where a space-saving design pays for itself is documented in quake mode. When that window is minimized it hides from the taskbar and from the window switcher. The screen gets clean, but the window also disappears from view. Which is why the same page carries a warning: if you have not bound the summon action to a key and you minimize the quake window, you have to open Task Manager to exit it. A textbook case of paying for invisibility somewhere invisible. That text on screen and text with a readable structure are not the same thing is covered separately in why terminal UIs have no structure.

An always-open shell is an unlocked execution window

This is the least discussed part of adopting this form. It is also the hardest part to walk back.

An ordinary terminal window has to be found and raised before anyone can type into it. A resident window skips that step. For as long as the screen is on, anyone sitting in front of that machine types straight into a ready prompt, and anyone includes whoever walks past while you step away. This is not a new category of risk. It is an existing risk with its default flipped.

Three situations make the difference visible. The first is stepping away. The only line of defence is the screen lock, and while a resident terminal does not bypass the lock, it does turn the minutes before it engages into an exposure window. On an unlocked screen most applications still require someone to open something; the shell is already open. Which is why shortening the automatic lock delay belongs in the real cost of adopting this form.

The second is screen sharing, presenting and recording. What is always visible is always captured. Putting hostname, username, path and version-control branch in the prompt is a common convention, and the output of the previous command is sitting in the scrollback. A summoned window does not appear unless you summon it, but a resident one is hidden only if you remember to hide it. Defences that rely on human memory fail most reliably in the minute before a presentation.

The third is command history. Bash has a HISTCONTROL variable, and when its value includes ignorespace, lines beginning with a space are not saved in the history list. A value of ignoredups drops lines matching the previous entry, and erasedups removes all earlier matching lines. But that is a mechanism for keeping something out of a file, not off a screen. A command typed with a leading space is still fully visible in the scrollback, and a resident window's scrollback lives as long as the shell does.

The history-writing behaviour from the previous section inverts here. A shell that never exits leaves less in the file and more on the screen. Whether that is good or bad for auditing depends on the situation, but either way the assumption that reading the history file tells you what was done stops holding. If an incident-response process leaned on that assumption, it needs another look.

Permissions belong in this section too. Following the Dock's position and size requires macOS Accessibility permission. Apple's own documentation says an app granted this can access and control your Mac, reaching contacts, calendars and other information, and tells you to act carefully and grant access only to apps you trust. This is not a reason to suspect one particular app; it is a statement that the shape itself structurally demands something. On other operating systems, keeping a window above everything and claiming a global key costs a comparable permission. Line up the convenience of one pinned window against that permission and count them together.

  • On a machine that handles company or customer data, the permanently exposed form deserves a second look. The summoned form delivers most of the same benefit
  • If a procedure is needed to hide it before presenting or sharing, that procedure is better entrusted to something other than memory
  • The rule about never passing passwords or tokens as command-line arguments matters more here, because the scrollback lives a long time
  • In shared or open-plan spaces, decide screen orientation and lock delay first and pick the form second. The other order arrives too late
  • When recommending this to a team, do not sell the convenience alone. Say lock delay and presentation procedure in the same breath, or the recommendation is risk distribution

Each OS has a different natural shape

The same idea has a different natural implementation on each operating system. Rather than asking which tool is best, it is faster to ask what the standard way of keeping a window on top is on this OS.

macOS already spends its screen edges on the menu bar and the Dock, and its virtual desktops divide window membership. So tools in this family have to handle showing on all workspaces and showing above full-screen apps as separate concerns. The iTerm2 documentation lists a floating-window option that lets a hotkey window appear above other apps' full-screen windows, notes that the default behaviour is to hide when another window takes focus, and describes a pin option that keeps it open when focus moves away. It also states that multiple hotkeys can be assigned to one profile, or a single hotkey to multiple profiles.

On Windows, shipping quake mode as a first-class feature in Windows Terminal fixed the behaviour in documentation. That window snaps to the top half of the monitor, cannot be resized horizontally or from the top and only from the bottom, automatically enters focus mode, and only one window at a time can be the quake window. Those constraints look like limitations but read more like the result of hitting this shape's problems first.

Linux has the oldest and the widest range. Yakuake is built on KDE Konsole technology and offers tabs, adjustable size and animation speed, skins and a D-Bus interface. Guake is summoned and dismissed with F12 by default on the GNOME side, and its feature list includes autostart, multi-monitor support, automatic tab renaming, D-Bus commands and per-directory configuration files. Because the user picks the window manager, there is also the option of enforcing rules such as always-on-top at the window-manager level.

  • If you move between operating systems, unifying the key is more realistic than unifying the form. The OS decides the form; the hand remembers the key
  • If permissions or autostart are locked down in a corporate image, this form is not an option to begin with. Check the policy first
  • If most of your work is on remote servers, investing in session management comes before the local window form. Reverse the order and you feel nothing
  • People who rearrange their screen layout often pay a re-layout cost every time with the always-visible form. That cost is invisible in the first few days

When you are better off without it

This form is not a gain for everyone. In some cases the loss is clear, and in a few of them the conclusion holds regardless of how heavily you use a terminal.

  • People who open a terminal a few times a day. Friction needs repetition to become a problem. At two or three times a day a few seconds is not a cost, while an always-visible window eats screen space all day regardless
  • Shared computers. A shell that never exits hands its directory, environment variables and scrollback straight to the next person. Here, persistence is a defect rather than a convenience
  • Desks people walk behind. A ready prompt permanently in view is exposure in itself, and a screen lock does not cover it
  • Small screens short on vertical space. This is where the always-visible form is most expensive, and switching to the summoned form solves most of it
  • Anyone already served by an editor integrated terminal. Put the same role in two places and one of them dies, and the one that dies is usually the newer
  • People whose work is mostly long-running. A resident window pays only while empty, and in this case it is permanently occupied

Count it yourself before you adopt it

Judged by feel, the gain gets overestimated and the cost underestimated. A few days of actually counting settles most of it. The questions below are not about picking a tool; they decide whether the form is needed at all.

  • How many times a day do you open a terminal. Under twenty and there is not much to gain by changing form
  • How many of those end in a single line. That ratio is the resident window's real benefit
  • How many times this week did you go the GUI route for something a shell would have done faster. That is the loss friction actually produced
  • How many terminal windows are open right now. If it is already several, adding one may be adding to the problem
  • How many times a month do you share your screen or present. Often, and the permanently exposed form gets expensive fast
  • How many minutes after you step away does the screen lock. Without that number, section nine's discussion cannot even start

And make the trial reversible. Use it for two weeks, turn it off, and if you miss it the gain was real; if you did not notice it was gone until days later, it was a reaction to novelty. Taking a tool away yields more information than adding one.

Summary

The few seconds it takes to open a terminal do not make work slower. They make work happen a different way. That is why a shape that removes those seconds has been rebuilt for over twenty years, rewritten each time the desktop environment changed, because the problem was never inside the terminal but in window management.

What the shape actually removes, though, is only two stages: launching a window and finding one. Changing directory and waiting for results stay exactly where they were, state piles up in a resident shell, a global hotkey is a resource there is only one of per machine, and an always-visible window spends screen space permanently. And an always-open shell is an execution window reachable without a lock, so when you step away and when you share your screen, the default is tilted toward exposure.

So what you are choosing is a shape, not a tool. If you have more than twenty one-line checks a day, a roomy screen, infrequent screen sharing and a short lock delay, this form pays. If even one of those runs the other way, the summoned form or session management is the better spend. And either way, one rule holds: a resident window is most useful while it is empty.

What I could not verify

  • I did not install the macOS app that prompted this and confirm its behaviour firsthand. Requirements, permissions, shortcuts and known issues are described only as far as the repository documentation states them, and the star and commit counts are values read in early September 2026
  • The point about the Dock on the left or right edge not being covered came out of community discussion, and I could not confirm the same statement directly in the repository documentation
  • The lineage of drop-down terminals and their first release years rest on one person's survey of the topic. That author explicitly left open the possibility of earlier examples between 1996 and 2000, and this article does not claim more than that either
  • I could not verify how global hotkey registration and window positioning differ between compositors under Wayland. Circumstances vary by environment, so I did not generalize
  • I found no controlled study on how much working time an always-on terminal saves. The few-seconds argument here is at the level of counting actions, not a measurement
  • I also found no public statistics linking screen-lock delay to actual incident rates. Section nine argues structurally that the exposure window grows; it makes no claim about frequency
