South Korea Tries Again to Add Its Government Root Certificate to Firefox

Putting a government root into Mozilla's trust store - why it is wanted, why it was rejected before, and what to weigh

2026-06-16 Dev & Infra

Introduction: A government certificate knocks again

News that the South Korean government is again attempting to add its government root certificate to Mozilla Firefox's trust store drew attention in developer circles. Reduced to one line: it is an effort to make government domains open cleanly in Firefox without users installing a certificate by hand. But behind that single line sit layers of the browser trust model, a history of past rejections, and a long-running security debate over what it means for a browser to trust a government-operated CA.

Let me set the caveats up front. The facts here are based on Mozilla's public Bugzilla records, CA policy documents, and related reporting. Certificate trust is a sensitive subject entangled with politics, security, and privacy, so this article does not pronounce a verdict for either side. It lays out what has actually been disclosed and what it means for users and developers, from a neutral, technical angle. Where inference is needed, I will mark it as inference.

1. What is GPKI - a government public key infrastructure

For context, start with GPKI. GPKI (Government Public Key Infrastructure) is South Korea's government public key infrastructure - the system that issues and manages digital certificates used for official authentication and e-government and administrative services. Based on public records, KLID (Korea Local Information Research and Development Institute) operates GPKI's root CA and subordinate CAs.

1.1 A different starting point from commercial TLS

There is one key distinction. GPKI originated not as a commercial TLS authority for general public websites, but as a system for identity authentication of government systems and citizen-facing administrative services. In other words, it differs in purpose and governance origin from the commercial CAs (think DigiCert or Let's Encrypt) that put the familiar "HTTPS padlock" on websites. This difference matters for understanding both the rejections and the renewed attempt discussed below.

1.2 Why only Firefox is affected

A common question arises here: "It works in Chrome and Edge, so why does only Firefox throw a warning?" The answer lies in the source of the trust store. Chrome, Edge, and many other browsers defer to the certificate store the operating system holds. In Korea's environment, government and institutional certificates are often installed into the Windows store, so the same government domain opens without much fuss on Windows. Firefox, by contrast, uses its own trust store managed by Mozilla, independent of the OS. If a GPKI-family root is not in Mozilla's store, only Firefox users meet a trust warning. That is the surface reason the government targets Mozilla inclusion.

2. The browser trust model - the power a root holds

To grasp the weight of this issue, you first have to understand how a browser decides whom to trust. This is the origin point of every part of the debate.

2.1 The chain of trust

In an HTTPS connection, the browser verifies whether the certificate the server presents is trustworthy. That verification walks up a chain of trust: root CA to intermediate CA to server certificate. If the root at the top of the chain is in the browser's trust store, every certificate that root has (directly or indirectly) signed is trusted. The trust store is, in effect, a pre-committed list of "issuers this browser trusts."

2.2 The enormous power of a root CA

Here comes the decisive fact. A trusted root CA can in effect issue a valid certificate for any domain at all. Technically, if one root mints a certificate for example.com or bank.co.kr, that certificate appears valid in every browser that trusts the root. So the decision to accept an organization as a root is not a mere compatibility matter - it is a decision to delegate the trust of users worldwide to that organization. That is exactly why Mozilla scrutinizes root inclusion so heavily.

3. Mozilla's inclusion process - why it is strict

Mozilla runs its own root store rather than leaning on the OS, and it conducts inclusion review publicly and rigorously. Based on public policy documents, here are the core requirements.

3.1 Public review and CCADB

Mozilla's root inclusion is not a private deal. An applicant CA applies through the CCADB (Common CA Database) and faces community review and questioning in a public discussion forum. The application, audit reports, and operating policies (CP/CPS) are published, and anyone may raise concerns. That transparency is itself part of the trust model.

3.2 Audits and standards alignment

Per public policy, a CA seeking the websites (TLS) trust bit must undergo a WebTrust or ETSI audit with no gaps in audit periods. It must also satisfy the CA Browser Forum Baseline Requirements and the Mozilla Root Store Policy. Recent policy (around v3.0) has kept raising the bar, even requiring automation support for issuance, validation, and renewal. In short, the threshold is not something the fact that "a government runs it" can clear on its own.

4. The past rejections - what went wrong

The fact that this is not the first attempt is central. Based on public Bugzilla records, here is how earlier requests were closed.

4.1 Requests around 2015

Per public records, GPKI root inclusion requests existed from around 2015, and notably Bug 1226100 and Bug 1377389 were closed as WONTFIX. The cited reasons split into two broad strands. One was a structural problem: undisclosed private subordinate CAs existing under the root, and Mozilla advised that subordinate CAs needed separate review. The other was concern over certificate misissuance.

4.2 Single-subCA concentration and operational reliability

Another concern often raised in community discussion was a structure where issuance was overly concentrated in a particular subordinate CA, plus operational reliability issues such as the stability of the OCSP (certificate revocation status) responder. If revocation infrastructure frequently drops offline, it becomes hard to verify a certificate's validity in real time, which becomes a weak link in the trust model. These observations are based on reporting and public discussion and may reflect a point-in-time state.

4.3 Mozilla's guidance

Per public records, in closing earlier requests Mozilla is described as advising the applicant to submit again with a new root that was compliant from creation with policy and the CA Browser Forum Baseline Requirements. That is, rather than "accept our existing administrative root as is," the signal was that a root newly designed to meet commercial TLS trust standards was needed.

5. The renewed attempt - what has changed

So how does this attempt differ from the past? Based on public Bugzilla records (for example Bug 1845081), here are the differences.

5.1 A new, TLS-dedicated hierarchy

Per public records, the crux this time is not lifting the old administrative GPKI hierarchy as is, but presenting a newly built hierarchy dedicated to TLS. Described in reporting and records as the MOIS SSL Root CA, this hierarchy was established in 2023 and is characterized as a simple structure with a single issuing CA (MOIS SSL Server CA) beneath it. This reads as a design meant to structurally avoid the earlier criticism of "a complex hierarchy mixing in undisclosed private subordinate CAs."

5.2 Emphasis on audits and standards alignment

The public records emphasize that this new hierarchy has undergone a WebTrust audit and operates in line with the CA Browser Forum TLS Baseline Requirements validation practices. The operator is described as KLID handling GSSL CA services under the Ministry of the Interior and Safety (MOIS), with no externally delegated registration authorities (RAs). This reads as an intent to make issuance authority and validation responsibility explicit.

5.3 Variables that remain

That said, improved structure and audits do not guarantee inclusion. Mozilla root inclusion must pass the CCADB process and public review, and the community examines past history, operational transparency, and actual issuance practices. As of June 2026, final approval cannot be stated with certainty, and the precise status is best confirmed in public Bugzilla.

6. The core debate - can we trust a government-operated CA

The real crux is not technology but trust. Here is what concerns arise when a browser broadly trusts a government-operated CA, and how to view it with balance.

6.1 The heart of the concern

As seen above, a trusted root can mint valid certificates for arbitrary domains. If a government-operated root is widely trusted, there is a theoretical concern that it could be misused to issue certificates for arbitrary domains and intercept HTTPS traffic in the middle. That is the heart of the security debate around government CAs. The very fact that a state actor holds issuance power - even if issuance is limited to government domains - invites vigilance about the possibility of surveillance or censorship.

6.2 The Kazakhstan precedent

The case that showed this concern is not abstract is Kazakhstan. According to reporting, the Kazakhstan government pushed users to install a state-issued root certificate, through which it intercepted HTTPS traffic in the middle. In response, Mozilla and Google blocked that government root in their browsers. This precedent concretely imprinted "what can happen if you broadly trust a government root," and it raised community vigilance toward later review of government-affiliated roots.

6.3 A balanced view

Yet projecting the Kazakhstan case onto every government CA is also inaccurate. Two things must be distinguished. Kazakhstan was a case of forcing users to install a root and intercepting traffic. A normal Mozilla inclusion, by contrast, must pass public review, audits, and standards alignment, with issuance scope and operations under continuous scrutiny. The actual magnitude of risk is decided less by the binary "is it government-run" and more by issuance scope, audit transparency, revocation and Certificate Transparency (CT) logging, and the response to misissuance. In my judgment, rather than judging inclusion politically, it is more productive to ask whether these technical controls actually work. The fact that community tools rejecting GPKI trust (such as the NoGPKI project) have emerged shows this trust question is contentious even at the user level.

7. The impact on users - what changes

Down to the practical question: if a GPKI-family root is added to Firefox, and if it is not, what changes for users in each case?

7.1 If it is included

The most direct effect is convenience. The trust warning that appeared when opening a government domain in Firefox goes away, and users can access government services smoothly without installing a certificate by hand. For the government, it unifies a browser-fragmented access experience and moves away from asking users to install certificates manually. This can be positive for security too, because the practice of having users install a root themselves is itself risky. Inclusion through the official process reduces that risky practice.

7.2 If it is not included

Conversely, if inclusion fails or is delayed, Firefox users keep meeting warnings on government domains or must add exceptions manually. The more worrying scenario is the continuation of the practice of instructing users to install the government root themselves. That can lead users to grant broad trust without understanding the root's power and risk, which from a security standpoint may be a worse outcome. So it is hard to view "the inclusion attempt itself is bad" as the whole story.

7.3 The developer and operator angle

If you build government-integrated services, you need certificate trust testing that assumes client diversity (including Firefox). This case also re-reminds us that behavior diverges depending on whether the trust store is the OS or the browser. If your organization has a practice of manually deploying roots into internal systems, this is a good occasion to review that root's issuance scope and revocation procedures.

8. Limits and caveats

Finally, let me be explicit about what this article does not pronounce on.

First, the status is fluid. Mozilla inclusion is a public process and its state changes frequently. The facts here rest on public records as of writing (June 2026), and the latest state should be checked in the relevant Bugzilla and CCADB. Second, separating tech from politics. This article addresses the technical structure of certificate trust and publicly stated concerns only; it does not pronounce on the intent of any government policy. Third, avoiding binaries. Both extremes - "a government CA is inherently dangerous" and "an official inclusion is inherently safe" - are inaccurate. Actual safety depends on whether verifiable controls of issuance scope, audits, and transparency are working.

9. Conclusion - what to watch when judging

South Korea's renewed Firefox root inclusion attempt is not a simple compatibility task; it sits at the point where the browser trust model meets government authority. Earlier requests were closed over structural flaws and operational-reliability concerns, and this attempt appears to answer those points with a TLS-dedicated new hierarchy, audits, and standards alignment. But the final judgment rests with Mozilla's public review and community scrutiny.

I would watch three things. First, whether issuance scope is limited - does the new root confine issuance to government domains? Second, the continuity of audits and transparency - do the WebTrust audit, CT logging, and revocation infrastructure run without gaps? Third, the misissuance response system - is there a procedure to revoke, disclose, and remediate quickly when something goes wrong? If these three are solid, the fact that a government runs it is not by itself disqualifying. If they wobble, then regardless of inclusion, the trust has a weak link. In the end, the crux is not the identity of the operator but whether verifiable controls actually work.
