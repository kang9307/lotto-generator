Why Databricks Built Its Own Coding AI Benchmark

Past SWE-bench contamination, overfitting, and gaming - a case study in designing a coding AI eval that fits real work

Jul 17, 2026 Dev & Infra

Intro: Why It's Time to Question the Benchmarks Themselves

On July 8, 2026, Databricks published a blog post titled "Benchmarking Coding Agents on Databricks' Multi-Million Line Codebase." The gist is simple: rather than take standard coding benchmarks like SWE-bench or TerminalBench at face value, Databricks built its own benchmark from tasks its own engineers had actually handled, and re-scored a range of coding AI models and tools against it. The results themselves are interesting (more on that below), but what I want to focus on here is not the results but the method. SWE-bench already sits as an industry standard - so why go to the trouble of building a new benchmark from scratch?

This question is not trivial. As of 2026, the coding-AI market is a firehose of new models and benchmark scores every week. And alongside that firehose, skepticism is growing about how much those scores actually represent real-world performance. Using the Databricks case as a thread to pull, this piece lays out the structural limits of public coding benchmarks - contamination, overfitting, leaderboard gaming, and the gap from real work - what a good evaluation needs, and what practical lessons this case offers organizations adopting coding agents.

1. Why Companies Abandon Standard Benchmarks for Their Own

1.1 Data Contamination - Benchmarks Rot Over Time

The most fundamental problem is contamination. SWE-bench is built from real GitHub issues and pull requests, and because those issues and PRs are public on the internet, they naturally get absorbed into the pretraining data of models released afterward. Researchers have actually measured this. One analysis found roughly 10.6% data overlap in SWE-bench Verified and about 8.7% in SWE-bench overall with a given model's training data. More concretely, roughly a third of Verified issues have solution code that appears almost verbatim in the issue description or comments, letting a model pass by essentially copying rather than synthesizing a fix. At that point the benchmark is measuring not "coding ability" but "how well the model memorized this particular benchmark." OpenAI's reported deprecation of SWE-bench Verified from its own evaluation pipeline is said to trace back to exactly this memorization-and-contamination problem.

This is precisely Databricks' first motivation for building its own benchmark. Its codebase is private, and the tasks its engineers actually handled have never been published anywhere on the internet. There is no data to contaminate in the first place - the structure rules it out.

1.2 Overfitting and Leaderboard Gaming - Passing Without Being Right

Separate from contamination, there is also overfitting. A so-called "overfitting patch" passes every given test yet is actually wrong. Research has repeatedly found that when a test suite (the oracle) is imperfect, a patch narrowly tailored to pass just those tests, without meeting the real requirement, is a common failure mode. Leaderboard gaming compounds this. In a structure where leaderboard position directly translates into funding, press coverage, and partnerships, there is a constant incentive to raise a benchmark score through tuning aimed at the test rather than genuine capability gains. One reported case: a model (Kimi K2) self-reported 50% on the HLE benchmark, but independent reproduction found only 29.4%. That gap between a self-reported number and an independently verified one is itself a reason to be wary of taking a vendor's published benchmark score at face value.

1.3 The Gap From Real Codebases - SWE-bench Is Not Your Company

The third issue is representativeness. SWE-bench is composed mostly of issues from well-known Python open-source projects. Databricks' codebase, by contrast, is a multi-million-line internal system mixing more than ten languages - Scala, Go, Rust, Java, Python, TypeScript, Protobuf, and more. No matter how sophisticated a public benchmark is, it has no way to reflect this language mix, this scale, or a company's internal frameworks and conventions. A model that tops a public benchmark comes with no guarantee it will do well fixing your company's legacy Scala service. This is Databricks' explicitly stated second motivation - that for making its own operational decisions, a benchmark built from its own code is simply more trustworthy.

2. What a Good Coding AI Evaluation Needs

Flip the three limits above around and the requirements for a good evaluation come into view. I'd summarize them as five things.

  • Grounded in real tasks: pull tasks from issues and PRs that engineers actually dealt with, not competitive-programming puzzles or LeetCode-style problems.
  • Reproducibility: running the same task under the same conditions must produce the same result, with clear, unambiguous scoring criteria.
  • Contamination resistance: tasks and answers must be kept private, or if public, refreshed on a rolling time basis so they cannot be absorbed into pretraining data.
  • Diversity: languages, frameworks, difficulty, and task types should mirror the actual distribution of your workload.
  • Qualitative plus quantitative: quantitative signals like test pass rate need to be paired with qualitative judgment on code quality and maintainability - though handing qualitative scoring to an LLM judge risks introducing the judge model's own bias as a new source of contamination.

3. The Core Principles Behind Databricks' Approach

Looking at how Databricks actually built its benchmark shows it implemented the requirements above fairly faithfully.

3.1 Data Source - Real Issues and PRs, Excluding Automated Commits

Databricks mined task candidates from real coding-interaction logs accumulated through its internal AI coding gateway, Unity AI Gateway. It excluded commits generated by automated tooling and selected only human-authored pull requests, filtering on whether a high-quality test suite came attached, whether the change was self-contained, and whether it represented the full stack. After identifying a PR's actual intent, the team stripped the solution description and rewrote it as a prompt, isolated the related tests, and manually reviewed the entire process (AI was used only as an assistant).

3.2 Environment Isolation - Sealing the Git History Entirely

This is the most interesting part. In early experiments, Databricks found agents recovering the answer directly from the git commit history - exactly the same class of problem reported elsewhere, where some public benchmark containers expose future git history (i.e., the solution commit) through commands like git log -p or git show. To block this, Databricks creates an isolated working copy for every task run, sealed off from the repository's future history. Rather than measuring contamination after the fact, it designed an environment where contamination is impossible from the start.

3.3 Scoring - Test Suites Only, No LLM Judge

There is a notable decision on scoring too. Databricks explicitly states it does not use an LLM judge. Instead, correctness is determined purely by running a prepared test suite and checking whether it passes. An LLM judge is convenient, but the judge model's own preferences and biases risk leaking into the scoring. Databricks chose to shut that risk out at the source.

4. What the Results Show - Three Capability Tiers and a Cost Trap

Databricks' published results carry as much signal as its methodology. Models split into roughly three capability tiers. The top tier, Opus 4.8, cost about $1.94 per task at 87% completion. Notably, the open-weight GLM 5.2 matched that same 87% completion at about $1.28 per task - statistically tied with Opus 4.8 in quality, at roughly 66% of the price. Sonnet 5, meanwhile, spent about $2.09 per task yet only reached 81% completion - the least efficient combination of the three.

Among the conclusions Databricks drew from this, the one I find most worth underlining is that "price per token is a poor proxy for real cost." Because reasoning efficiency differs from model to model, a lower nominal token price can still end up costing more in practice if the model burns through more tokens or turns to finish the same task. On top of that, Databricks found that even with the same model, cost varied by more than 2x depending on which agent harness (tool-integration layer) was used. In other words, "which model is best" is already the wrong question; "which combination of model and harness is most efficient for my workload" is the right one. And no public benchmark can answer that - only measuring it against your own workload can.

5. SWE-bench and Standard Benchmarks - An Overview and Their Limits

None of this means standard benchmarks are useless. SWE-bench remains one of the few common languages for comparing different models on the same yardstick. Follow-on efforts like LiveCodeBench tackle contamination head-on: it continuously collects problems from LeetCode, AtCoder, and Codeforces along with their publication dates, and scores a given model only on problems published after that model's training cutoff. This approach has actually revealed a sharp performance drop for several models on the post-cutoff slice - which is itself evidence that earlier-window scores were contaminated. In short, refreshing problems on a rolling time basis is one genuinely effective way to fight contamination.

LiveCodeBench is not a cure-all either, though. It still leans heavily on competitive-programming-style problems and cannot capture the complexity of a real codebase - legacy code, internal conventions, dependencies spanning many files. In the end, public benchmarks are valid as a "first-pass filter for roughly gauging relative standing between models," but they cannot answer "how much will this model actually help at my company." Databricks' case is precisely an instance of filling that gap with its own benchmark.

6. The Lesson Here - Goodhart's Law

One principle runs through all of this. Economist Charles Goodhart's dictum: once a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure - Goodhart's Law. A benchmark is a measurement tool when it is first created, but the moment its score becomes tied directly to funding, hiring, or marketing, raising the score itself becomes the goal. MMLU is a clean example. It was a hard test when introduced in 2020, but by 2023 GPT-4 scored 86.4% on it, near human-expert level - not because the test got easier, but because the entire industry optimized toward it and it lost its discriminative power. Coding benchmarks are no different. Raising a SWE-bench score and actually fixing a real company's code well started out pointing the same direction, but they have gradually become two different, diverging goals.

The real lesson of the Databricks case is not "SWE-bench is bad" but "a benchmark must be customized to its purpose." Using a generic leaderboard rank for marketing and making an actual adoption decision are entirely different purposes, and different purposes require different yardsticks. Miss that principle, and no matter how sophisticated a benchmark gets, it eventually goes powerless in the face of Goodhart's Law.

7. Practical Takeaways for Companies Adopting Coding Agents

So what should an organization evaluating coding-agent adoption actually do? I'd recommend three things.

7.1 Build Your Own Golden Set

Pull 50-200 real issues or PRs your team has recently resolved and restructure them as tasks. Following Databricks' pattern - strip the solution description, rewrite as a prompt, isolate the test suite, and seal off the git history - already gives you a contamination-free internal benchmark. It does not need to be perfect from day one; even 20-30 tasks give a far more useful signal than a public leaderboard.

7.2 Measure the Harness and Routing, Not Just the Model

As the Databricks case shows, cost can swing widely for the same model depending on the harness. Don't measure model choice and harness choice separately - compare them as the actual combination you'd deploy (model + harness + prompting strategy). Routing that automatically switches models by task difficulty (an approach Databricks has previewed via Unity AI Gateway and Omnigent) may also prove a better long-run answer than picking a single model.

7.3 Treat Public Benchmarks as a First-Pass Filter Only

You don't need to ignore SWE-bench or LiveCodeBench scores entirely. Just use them as a "first-pass filter for narrowing candidates," not a "final conclusion." They're useful for shortlisting a few top-ranked models, but which of those actually fits your company needs to be re-verified with your own golden set. The same goes for a vendor's self-published benchmark numbers. Approach them skeptically, and always make the final call with your own data.

Conclusion

As of July 2026, Databricks building its own coding benchmark is not just an internal tool - it is one answer to a question the entire AI-evaluation ecosystem is facing. Public benchmarks get contaminated, overfit, gamed, and drift away from real work. Making the benchmark more sophisticated does not make these problems go away, because the moment a measure becomes a target, Goodhart's Law kicks in. The fix is not finding one universal benchmark, but building the capability to design a benchmark fit for your own purpose. For any organization bringing coding agents into real production work, this Databricks case is a blueprint worth studying.
