CVERader — Your Unified Radar for CVEs and Security News in 2026

Why aggregating CVE feeds is no longer optional — and how NVD, CISA KEV, and EPSS fit together in a practical triage stack

2026-06-23 Dev & Infra

Introduction: 132 CVEs a Day — How Do You Keep Up?

In 2025, the public CVE program published 48,185 new vulnerabilities — an average of 132 per day and a 20.6% year-over-year increase. CVE submissions have grown by 263% since 2020, and Q1 2026 is tracking roughly one-third higher than Q1 2025. The idea of a security analyst manually scanning NVD every morning and processing every entry is no longer realistic.

This is the backdrop against which CVERader emerged. Described by its author as "a small program to monitor the latest published vulnerabilities and match them with desired brands and products, then notify via email, Discord, etc.," it represents a category of tools built for one purpose: cut through the noise so that when a CVE relevant to your stack lands, you hear about it immediately rather than days later during a weekly review.

This article goes beyond a tool introduction. It examines why CVE aggregation and prioritization have become the defining challenge of security operations in 2026, how the three key signals — NVD, CISA KEV, and EPSS — fit together, and what a practical triage stack looks like. Statistics are drawn from the 2026 Vulnerability Statistics report and official NIST and CISA announcements.

1. What CVERader Does — and Why It Matters

CVERader (github.com/Mehdi0x90/CVE-Radar) is a Python-based open-source tool that monitors newly published CVEs, filters them against user-defined brand or product keywords, and delivers matched results to configured alert channels. The core design principle: instead of a human visiting multiple feeds daily, the tool visits them automatically and surfaces only what is relevant.

Comparable tools in the same space include OpenCVE (aggregates MITRE, NVD, Red Hat, CISA, and more into a community or cloud platform), Security Feed Aggregator (a C++17 tool unifying CVE, KEV, GitHub Advisories, and Hacker News via a JSON config), and SaaS services like CVEFeed, SecAlerts, and CVEPulse. Each addresses the same underlying problem: the cost of attention has become the binding constraint in vulnerability management.

1.1 The Actual Problem Being Solved

Consider the daily workflow of a security team without automated aggregation. Every morning: open NVD, scan the latest CVE additions, cross-reference each against the internal asset inventory, check whether the vendor has an advisory, check whether CISA flagged it as exploited, and filter out everything that does not apply. Repeat for every other feed — GitHub Security Advisories, Cisco PSIRT, Microsoft Security Response Center, and so on.

CVERader and its peers compress this workflow. Pre-configure the product keywords you care about ("nginx", "apache httpd", "citrix adc", "openssl"), and the tool returns only CVEs that match. The reduction in cognitive load is real. But — and this matters — keyword filtering is only the first step. Once a relevant CVE surfaces, the triage question remains: how urgently must we act?

2. The 2026 CVE Landscape in Numbers

Before going further, it is worth grounding the analysis in data. The volume problem is not abstract.

Of the 48,185 CVEs published in 2025, only 1,484 appear in the CISA KEV catalog — roughly 0.5% of the total. The remaining 99.5% range from theoretical vulnerabilities to those requiring highly specific conditions that may never arise in practice. The challenge is not processing all 48,185; it is correctly identifying which of those 1,484 (and the smaller number of imminent future entries) demand immediate action.

The time pressure is severe. Mandiant's M-Trends 2026 report measured mean time-to-exploit at negative seven days, meaning adversaries typically weaponize vulnerabilities before vendors release patches. CrowdStrike found that 42% of exploited vulnerabilities were attacked before public disclosure, with the fastest observed breakout time of just 27 seconds. Waiting for a weekly vulnerability review meeting is not a viable strategy for high-severity exposures.

3. NVD — Still the Reference Point, but Now a Partial One

The National Vulnerability Database has been the backbone of vulnerability management for decades. It adds CVSS scores, CPE software identifiers, and reference links to each CVE — the structured metadata that makes automated asset matching and severity-based filtering possible.

On April 15, 2026, NIST formally announced a transition of NVD to a triage model. The cause was the same CVE volume explosion described above. Approximately 29,000 backlog CVEs were reclassified as "Not Scheduled" and will not receive full enrichment. Going forward, only an estimated 15 to 20% of incoming CVEs will receive complete CVSS and CPE enrichment. The prioritization criteria: KEV catalog membership, federal government software usage, and EO 14028 critical-software status.

The practical implication for security teams is significant. Any policy that relies on "patch all NVD CVSS High or Critical" is now broken for the 80–85% of CVEs that NVD will not fully enrich. Teams need supplementary sources. This is precisely where aggregation tools must pull from multiple feeds — not NVD alone.

4. CISA KEV — The Strongest Signal in Vulnerability Management

The CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog is the authoritative list of CVEs confirmed to have been exploited in real-world attacks. At end-2025, it contained 1,484 entries with 245 additions in the year (+20%). By vendor, Microsoft accounts for approximately 24% (350 CVEs), followed by Apple (86), Cisco (82), Adobe (76), and Google (67).

KEV's power comes from its confirmation signal. EPSS predicts the probability of future exploitation; KEV confirms that exploitation has already occurred. For triage purposes, this is the hardest trigger available. Federal agencies are required to remediate KEV-listed vulnerabilities within 14 days. Private-sector organizations should treat KEV additions as their highest-urgency queue, period.

The 2025 composition of KEV additions tells a story about attack patterns: 19 of the 59 ransomware-tagged additions in 2025 targeted network security appliances from Citrix, Ivanti, and Fortinet. This aligns with the data showing edge-device and VPN exploitation surging approximately 8x in a single year (from 3% to 22% of initial-access cases). If you manage network perimeter equipment, KEV is not optional reading — it is mission-critical intelligence.

5. EPSS — Predicting What Will Be Exploited Next

The Exploit Prediction Scoring System is a machine-learning model maintained by FIRST (Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams). It assigns each CVE a score from 0 to 1 representing the estimated probability that the vulnerability will be actively exploited in the wild within the next 30 days.

EPSS v4, released in March 2025, achieved an ROC AUC of 0.838 — distinguishing exploited from non-exploited CVEs with approximately 84% accuracy. This is substantially more predictive than using CVSS severity alone.

The core insight EPSS provides: high CVSS score does not mean high EPSS score. Among CVEs rated CVSS 9.0 or above, only a fraction ever see active exploitation. Conversely, a CVSS 7.0 vulnerability with an EPSS score of 0.5 (50% probability of exploitation in 30 days) should jump the queue over a CVSS 9.5 vulnerability with an EPSS score of 0.01.

5.1 How to Apply EPSS in Practice

A common threshold in security operations is EPSS 0.1 (10%). Only about 2–3% of all CVEs exceed this level. Segmenting by EPSS: scores above 0.5 are candidates for immediate response; scores of 0.1 to 0.5 are short-cycle patch targets; scores below 0.1 default to the standard patch cadence unless other signals elevate them. Always combine EPSS with asset exposure context — a CVE with EPSS 0.4 on an internet-facing server is a different risk than the same CVE on an air-gapped internal system.

6. The Four-Tier Triage Stack

Combining NVD, KEV, and EPSS into a workable daily process requires a clear decision hierarchy. Here is the framework this analysis recommends, as of June 2026:

Tier 1 (Immediate)CISA KEV listedWithin 14 daysConfirmed real-world exploitation — strongest available signal
Tier 2 (Urgent)EPSS ≥ 0.5Within 7 days50%+ probability of exploitation within 30 days

This is where CVERader-class tools earn their value. They handle the top of the funnel — filtering thousands of daily CVEs down to those relevant to your product keywords. The triage logic above then classifies each matched CVE into its tier. An effective aggregator surfaces KEV status and EPSS score alongside the CVE identifier, enabling a security analyst to tier-assign in seconds rather than minutes per CVE.

7. Alternative Feeds After the NVD Overhaul

With NVD enriching only 15–20% of CVEs, the missing 80–85% need alternative sources. The main options as of June 2026:

  • CVE.org CNA data: CVE Numbering Authorities (major vendors like Microsoft, Google, and Apple) submit structured data directly. For their products, CNA submissions often arrive faster than NVD enrichment did historically.
  • VulnCheck: A commercial feed that backfills CPE identifiers and CVSS scores for CVEs NVD does not enrich. Directly addresses the enrichment gap.
  • GitHub Advisory Database: Best source for open-source package vulnerabilities in npm, pip, Maven, and RubyGems ecosystems. Typically faster to update than NVD for library-level CVEs.
  • Vendor advisories: Direct subscription to Cisco PSIRT, Microsoft MSRC, Red Hat security alerts, and others for your specific stack components.

An aggregation tool configured to pull from these sources — not just NVD — provides materially better coverage in the post-triage-overhaul environment. This is an area where open-source tools like CVERader have an advantage over simple RSS readers: they can be extended to pull from multiple structured APIs and correlate the results.

8. Open-Source vs. SaaS — Choosing the Right Tool

The aggregator landscape splits into self-hosted open-source tools and managed SaaS platforms. The right choice depends on team context.

8.1 Open Source Advantages

Full control over filter logic, alert routing, and data residency. CVE data stays within your infrastructure — relevant for regulated industries or organizations with data sovereignty requirements. Direct integration with internal asset databases and ITSM systems is possible. CVERader's Python codebase makes it straightforward to extend. OpenCVE adds a richer web UI and subscription management layer for teams that need more than raw alerts.

8.2 SaaS Advantages

Zero infrastructure overhead, immediate onboarding. CVEPulse delivers a real-time dashboard tracking CVE attention across 11 intelligence sources with a hype score. SecAlerts provides continuously updated product subscriptions with curated alerts. For teams without dedicated tooling engineers, SaaS gets useful signal within hours of signup.

8.3 The Pragmatic Path

Start with SaaS for rapid visibility. Once your team is making daily triage decisions and needs to integrate CVE data with your asset inventory or automate remediation tickets, migrate the workflow to an open-source tool. The most important thing is not which tool you use — it is building the habit of consulting KEV and EPSS every day. Tools support habits; they do not create them.

9. Three Actions Security Teams Can Take This Week

9.1 Subscribe to CISA KEV Alerts Today

CISA provides KEV updates via RSS and email at no cost. Setup takes under five minutes. Create a process where a KEV addition triggers an immediate check against your asset inventory. This single workflow addresses your highest-urgency exposures at zero tooling cost.

9.2 Add EPSS to Your Open Vulnerability Backlog Review

FIRST provides a free EPSS API. Take your list of open CVE-IDs and batch-query the API weekly. Any CVE whose EPSS score has risen significantly since last week becomes an immediate review item. Catching "sleeper" vulnerabilities that CVSS deprioritized but EPSS now flags is where this signal adds the most value.

9.3 Build or Adopt a Keyword Filter for Your Stack

Compile the software and vendor names that make up your infrastructure — web servers, databases, container runtimes, network equipment, cloud provider SDKs. Configure these as keywords in CVERader or an equivalent tool, and route matched CVE alerts to a dedicated Slack channel or ticketing queue. This is the minimum viable CVE radar for any organization running mixed technology stacks.

Conclusion: Aggregation Is the Foundation, Judgment Is the Goal

CVERader solves a real problem efficiently: it reduces the daily cost of staying current with CVE disclosures relevant to your environment. As the CVE firehose intensifies — 48,000 in 2025, tracking higher in 2026 — automated aggregation and filtering is no longer optional for any team taking vulnerability management seriously.

But the tool is infrastructure, not the answer. The answer is a triage system that combines KEV's confirmed-exploitation signal, EPSS's probabilistic prediction, and asset-context to produce a daily ranked list of what to act on. Build that system first, then choose the tooling that feeds it. The combination of a well-configured aggregator and a disciplined four-tier triage process is what turns 132 daily CVEs from a flood into a manageable queue.
