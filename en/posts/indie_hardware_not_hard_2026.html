What Selling 2,500 MIDI Recorders Taught Me: Hardware Is Not So Hard

How a solo developer turned an automatic piano MIDI recorder called Jamcorder into a sustainable hardware business - and the pushback that followed

Jul 21, 2026 Trends & Culture

Introduction: "Hardware Is Hard" Is Back Under Debate

"Hardware is hard" has been treated as conventional wisdom in startup circles for years. You need to hold inventory, absorb defect rates, clear logistics and customs, and above all, you may not have capital left to recover if things go wrong. In July 2026, though, a software-engineer-turned-hardware-founder named Chip Weinberger published an account of selling more than 2,500 units of his automatic MIDI recorder, Jamcorder, that pushes back directly on that assumption. His conclusion is simple: it wasn't the hardware that was hard. It was the software.

This piece uses that story as a starting point to examine, as of July 2026, exactly where the hardware is hard adage holds up and where it doesn't, and what this moment means for anyone considering an indie hardware business. Rather than just repeating the original claims, I want to weigh the evidence and the pushback together and add my own analysis on top.

1. The Jamcorder Case - What Was Actually Sold, and How Much

1.1 An Automatic MIDI Recorder You Stick to a Piano

Jamcorder is a small device that attaches to the side of a piano and automatically captures a recording every time you play. It stores MIDI data, pitch, timing, and velocity, rather than audio, which makes the file sizes far smaller; a single 16GB microSD card is reported to hold tens of thousands of hours of playing. It's priced around $99. It was first shown publicly at the NAMM musical instrument trade show in early 2025. Weinberger is described as a computer science graduate of Northwestern who spent over a decade as a software engineer, and the product reportedly grew out of his own frustration, as a pianist, at losing spontaneous improvisations he never got to record.

1.2 What 2,500 Units in 1.5 Years Actually Means

About a year and a half after launch, he had sold more than 2,500 units and describes the business as self-sustaining. That number is nowhere near unicorn territory or a viral breakout. But that's precisely what makes it worth studying. It isn't a story about a dramatic exit - it's evidence that a single person can actually run a hardware business at a scale one individual can manage as a side project or a solo venture.

2. The Adage vs. the Rebuttal - Inventory, Manufacturing, Logistics, Defects, Capital

2.1 Why Hardware Is Widely Considered Hard

There are real reasons behind the belief that hardware startups are difficult. Software can be patched and redeployed instantly; hardware, once it hits a production line, is much harder to undo. You have to prepay for components and hold them as inventory, which ties up capital, and defect rates translate directly into refunds, returns, and reputational damage. Add tariffs, logistics, and country-by-country certification, and the barrier to entry looks steep. Analyses citing venture-investor portfolio data generally don't dispute that hardware is more capital-intensive than software - they just disagree on the multiple.

2.2 The Author's Rebuttal - "What Was Hard Was the Software"

Weinberger's counterargument doesn't reject the adage wholesale - it relocates where the difficulty actually lived. He says manufacturing itself, sourcing parts, assembling units, managing defects, went more smoothly than he expected. What was genuinely hard, he says, was writing more than 200,000 lines of code across firmware, the companion app, and internal manufacturing tooling, a project that took over three years. In other words, the bottleneck in his business wasn't the factory - it was software development itself. The honest reading isn't hardware is easy; it's that the hardware side of this particular project happened to be the easier half.

3. Why Indie Hardware Entrepreneurship Is More Accessible Now

3.1 China's Manufacturing Ecosystem and Low-Cost Prototyping

The path Weinberger took reflects this shift. He sourced a Chinese assembly partner through Alibaba and prioritized off-the-shelf components over custom parts. That approach is possible because small-batch PCB fabrication and assembly services with little or no minimum order quantity have proliferated. Factories that once only responded to orders in the thousands now accept prototype-scale runs. It's worth noting, though, that Alibaba itself mixes unverified trading companies with actual factories, so sourcing without filtering for verified manufacturers and doing real due diligence is genuinely risky.

3.2 Cheap Microcontrollers and Open-Source Hardware

Inexpensive, capable microcontrollers and freely shared open-source hardware reference designs have also lowered the barrier. Expertise and time that circuit design once demanded can now largely be replaced with off-the-shelf modules and proven references. This ecosystem is also why Weinberger could ship a product built around a board with just 25 unique components.

3.3 Crowdfunding and Direct-to-Consumer - Two Distinct Paths

Indie hardware founders now have two clearly distinct routes. One is crowdfunding platforms like Kickstarter, which validate demand in advance and raise capital for an initial production run. The other is selling direct-to-consumer through your own storefront from day one. Crowdfunding buys validation and marketing simultaneously, but platform fees typically run 5 to 12 percent, and a meaningful share of campaigns report shipping delays. Jamcorder, notably, launched without crowdfunding at all, selling direct through Shopify - a sign, I'd argue, that the founder was confident enough to carry inventory and cash flow risk on his own from the start.

4. What Was Genuinely Hard, and What Was Easy - In the Founder's Own Terms

4.1 What He Calls Easy - Assembly and Sourcing

Weinberger says he deliberately drove down assembly complexity: a structure that closes with a single screw, 25 unique components on the PCB, and the aggressive removal of extras like USB-C, a power button, and an ambient light sensor. The payoff, he says, was hand-assembling the first 500 units in four days with zero design changes. This should be read not as hardware happened to be easy for him, but as he engineered it to be easy by design from the outset.

4.2 What He Calls Hard - Firmware, App, and Manufacturing Tooling

Everything he flags as hard, by contrast, is software. Firmware running on the device, the companion app, and internal tooling used for inspection and calibration on the line together required over 200,000 lines of code, he says. That this was the real, three-plus-year bottleneck for someone who is himself a software engineer by training also undercuts a second adage: that a hardware startup only requires electrical and mechanical expertise.

4.3 What the Article Doesn't Cover - The Hidden Costs of Certification and Support

That said, there's territory the original piece doesn't dwell on: regulatory compliance such as radio and safety certification, and post-sale customer support costs. Both are commonly underestimated line items in hardware entrepreneurship, and I return to them below alongside a Korea-specific angle.

5. How This Differs From a Software Startup

5.1 Margin and Inventory as Capital Risk

The most repeated piece of advice Weinberger offers is to design for a gross margin of at least 70 percent. Software's marginal cost is close to zero; hardware carries parts, assembly, and logistics cost on every unit sold. Unsold inventory, on top of that, is effectively cash sitting idle. Where a software startup can run at a loss while it grows a user base, a hardware business that fails to protect margin from day one gets more dangerous, not safer, as it scales.

5.2 Returns, Logistics, and Support as Tail Costs

A software bug gets fixed with a deploy. A hardware defect means taking back or replacing a physical unit already sitting in a customer's home. Returns, exchanges, and shipping are classic tail costs that erode margin. Weinberger's advice to require pre-production samples and to keep in-house final QA plus local finished-goods inventory reads as a direct hedge against exactly this risk.

5.3 Replication Difficulty - Forking Is Easy, Reproducing Is Not

Software can be forked and copied into a near-identical product almost instantly. Hardware requires reproducing an entire supply-chain relationship, assembly know-how, and QA process to replicate. Ironically, this is both the basis for hardware's reputation for difficulty and, for the founder, a moat: the better-engineered and simpler the product, the harder it actually is for a competitor to copy it outright.

6. What 2,500 Units in 1.5 Years Really Represents

6.1 The Overlap With the Indie Hacker Model

The message here echoes the software indie hacker movement surprisingly closely: not explosive, venture-funded growth, but a business one person can run profitably and sustainably at a scale they can manage. The difference is that indie hackers typically build this model around SaaS subscriptions, while Weinberger applied the same playbook to a physical product.

6.2 A Niche, Passion-Product Strategy

Jamcorder doesn't target every musician - it targets a narrow niche of pianists who hate losing a spontaneous improvisation. A small market is also, by definition, a market large companies won't bother chasing. This case shows that a passion product, built to solve a problem the founder personally cared about, can more easily reach break-even at a scale that doesn't require large outside investment.

7. Practical Advice for Anyone Who Wants to Build Hardware

7.1 Simplification Is the Strategy

The principle running through Weinberger's advice is: cut part count and features. A simple bill of materials that doesn't depend on a single supplier, a design built for easy assembly and calibration, and detailed photo-based assembly documentation all come up together. What this case makes clear is that the decision to cut features, not add them, is what actually requires the deeper judgment.

7.2 Design the Margin First

He advises targeting at least 70 percent gross margin, running lean during scaling, building in anti-counterfeiting measures, and maximizing packaging density to cut shipping costs. What all of these share is a direct link back to protecting margin. Separate from making an appealing product, calculating a structure where you actually keep money on every sale is the real starting point for a hardware business.

7.3 A Checklist to Run Through Before You Start

Pulling this case together, here's the checklist I'd suggest running through first.

  • Can the product be radically simplified - fewer parts and fewer features mean less risk.
  • Is there a process to require and inspect pre-production samples before every run.
  • Have you actually calculated a price and cost structure that clears at least 70 percent gross margin.
  • Do you have in-house QA and a local finished-goods inventory plan for handling returns and defects.
  • Have you avoided underestimating the software timeline for firmware, the app, and manufacturing tooling.
  • Have you confirmed the certification requirements - radio, safety - for your target market in advance.

8. Limits and Pushback - Not Every Hardware Project Is a Jamcorder

8.1 Community Pushback - Complex Projects Are a Different Story

The pushback this story drew after it circulated on GeekNews (hada.io) is worth noting too. Some commenters argue this is an unusually simple product, and that a typical hardware project involves dozens of custom parts, injection-molded components, and several complex PCB assemblies, meaning the headline undersells the difficulty most founders will actually face. Others point to cash-flow problems, upfront tooling costs, long inventory cycles, component obsolescence, that genuinely sink many projects before they reach break-even. The counterpoint that physical constraints like thermal design, power delivery, and PCB routing mistakes can't be patched the way software can, and that a single bad trace can ruin an entire production run, is a fair one. This case shows hardware is as hard as you make it - not that all hardware is easy.

8.2 A Korea-Specific Angle - Certification as a Separate Wall

Anyone attempting something similar in Korea should weigh one more variable. Commenters on the discussion thread pointed out that domestic KC certification and related regulatory burden make it genuinely impractical for an individual to manufacture and distribute hardware in Korea without government support, a very different situation from distributing imported goods through channels like AliExpress. Anyone planning formal domestic distribution needs to fold certification procedures and costs into the business plan from the earliest stage - a variable the original story never has to deal with.

Conclusion: Hardware Is as Hard as You Make It

What Weinberger's case supports isn't the optimistic claim that hardware is easy, nor the conventional wisdom that hardware is inherently hard. Difficulty swings heavily on how simply you design the product, how aggressively you protect margin, and how precisely you identify where the real bottleneck sits, software included. 2,500 units over 1.5 years isn't a flashy number, and that's exactly why it reads as a more reproducible model than most hardware success stories. For anyone weighing a hardware startup, the lesson here isn't "so it's easy" - it's the question itself: what can you simplify, and where are you willing to protect margin.
