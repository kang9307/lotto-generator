Currency Exchange - Where the Money Actually Leaks

Not a question of catching a good rate, but of which fee items attach to which route

Aug 25, 2026 Finance & Economy

What this article is. A general explanation of the fee structure behind currency exchange and cross-border payment. It is not investment or tax advice. No bank, app or card is recommended. No forecast of currency movement is offered, and nothing here says whether now is a good time to exchange. Preferential rates, fee levels and limits differ by institution and by date, so confirm directly with the provider before you transact.

Rules described here are Korean rules, checked against official sources on 25 August 2026. Items that could not be verified are collected in section 12. Regulations are amended; other jurisdictions have their own thresholds and procedures.

A travel budget breaks on line items, not on the rate

You open a rate app while planning a trip. You watch the number for a few days, then go to the bank on a day it dips. Yet when you divide the won that left your account by the dollars you received, the result is not that number. It is slightly worse. Come home, sell the leftover dollars, and it drifts again — this time you get slightly less.

Most people conclude they mistimed the market. In fact a gap that has nothing to do with timing was there from the start. And that gap does not attach once per exchange. Depending on how you moved the money, it attaches repeatedly under different names.

Why exchange rates move — concepts, drivers, monetary regimes — is set out in Understanding Exchange Rates. What follows is the next problem: when you actually convert money, where does it leak?

The short answer is that the leak sits in line items, not in the rate, and the items differ by route. So the way to keep more of your money is not to pick a good day but to pick the route that attaches the fewest items to your amount and purpose.

Why one currency carries several prices

Open a bank rate board and a single currency shows several lines: a base rate, a cash selling rate, a cash buying rate, a telegraphic transfer selling rate and a telegraphic transfer buying rate. That layout is where everything in this article begins.

The Bank of Korea puts the mechanism most economically in its public education material. Banks take the base exchange rate and add or subtract an exchange commission to produce quoted cash buying and selling rates and telegraphic transfer buying and selling rates. When the bank buys currency from you it applies a lower rate; when it sells to you it applies a higher one. The tilt always runs the same way.

Which means the base rate is not a price you can trade at. The big figure at the top of the board is a reference point, and the amount leaving your account comes from a smaller line underneath. That is the first reason the app and the receipt disagree.

  • Base exchange rate. The reference for the quote. You cannot exchange at it.
  • Cash selling rate. Applied when you walk out with banknotes. Higher than the base rate.
  • Cash buying rate. Applied when you hand banknotes back. Lower than the base rate.
  • Telegraphic transfer rates. Used where no physical cash moves — transfers and foreign currency account activity.
  • A round trip pays twice. Buy and later sell, and you cross the upper gap once and the lower gap once. Leftover holiday cash feels expensive because it is, arithmetically.

Cash carries costs that transfers do not

For the same currency, the cash lines sit further from the base rate than the transfer lines. The reason is blunt: banknotes are a physical good.

The Korea Federation of Banks consumer portal, in its guidance on exchange commissions, lists what to weigh for cash transactions: import and export costs, air freight, insurance and armoured transport. Paper is flown in, insured, moved by secure vehicle and then held in a vault as an asset that earns nothing while it sits. All of that is baked into the rate.

One practical conclusion follows. Spreads differ sharply by currency, and the difference tracks how widely the currency circulates and how hard it is to ship. The Bank of Korea material posted in February 2021 used the dollar, the Chinese yuan and the Vietnamese dong as an ascending example. The specific percentages in that piece belong to that date and will not match a board today. The ordering, however, is stable: the less a currency circulates, the more it costs to obtain as cash.

A common misread. Buy dollars at home, convert to the local currency on arrival, because the local currency is expensive to buy in Korea. Sometimes the direction is right, but it is not free. You cross a gap from won to dollars, another from dollars to local currency, and a third if you convert anything back. Double conversion adds crossings, so it only pays when the domestic spread difference is wide enough to cover them.

What a preferential rate actually discounts

Read a Korean bank advertising a ninety percent preferential rate and it sounds like paying almost nothing. It is not that.

The Korea Federation of Banks foreign exchange guide shows the arithmetic. The difference between the base rate and the rate actually applied is the exchange commission, and the preferential rate applies to that difference. So it discounts the gap, not the final amount.

The distinction bites. Ninety percent leaves a tenth of the gap still payable, and only a hundred percent closes it. Even then, closing the gap is not the same as paying nothing, because items outside the spread survive untouched.

  • The preference attaches only to the spread. Per-transaction remittance charges and costs tied to how cash is collected are untouched by it.
  • It differs by currency. Reading the dollar terms and assuming the rest match will mislead you.
  • It differs by channel. Branch counters, mobile applications and airport desks normally run on separate terms.
  • Amount and frequency conditions are common. Above a threshold, or a capped number of transactions per month.
  • The base rate itself is never discounted. No preference makes you better off than the reference.

Which is why lining up preferential percentages side by side is not a comparison. What to compare is the final applied rate plus the sum of items charged separately. It is also why this article prints no institution's current spread or preference: those values move with conditions and dates, and writing them down converts them into misinformation.

Each route leaks in a different place

The same hundred dollars costs differently depending on the path it travelled. The table below names the items only. Rates vary by institution and date, so none are quoted.

Questions for the card issuer differ from questions for the bank, and the cut taken by a local ATM operator cannot be answered from home at all.

Card payments abroad - the fee is not one layer

Paying by card abroad feels like skipping the exchange. In fact the currency changes twice.

The local currency you paid is first converted into the settlement currency of the card network, and that is converted again into your home currency for billing. Each step has its own claimant.

  • International network fee. Charged by the card network on cross-border transactions. The Korea Consumer Agency refers to it as the overseas usage fee.
  • Issuer foreign transaction fee. Set separately by your own card company. A distinct item from the network fee.
  • The rate is not fixed on the day you pay. The same Korean consumer guidance notes that a credit card transaction is converted at a rate roughly three to four days after the purchase, not at the moment of purchase. That is why computing the bill from the app screen you saw at the till never quite works.
  • Some cards are blocked abroad. The guidance also notes that a card marked for domestic use only under the network logo cannot be used overseas.

Card mechanics themselves — the fee layers, the loss procedure, the post-trip reconciliation — are covered separately in Travel Cards Issued in Korea. The point here is only how the items differ from cash exchange. Cards carry no logistics cost. What takes its place is the cost of crossing the network and the uncertainty about when conversion happens.

Would you like to pay in your own currency?

Terminals abroad and checkout pages on foreign websites often offer to bill you in your home currency. Seeing a familiar currency looks helpful. The choice is usually the worse one — not as a matter of feel, but because of the items.

The Korea Consumer Agency guidance on cross-border transactions separates the two cases. Pay in the local currency and an overseas usage fee applies. Pay in your own currency and an exchange commission plus a conversion service fee are added on top. The same purchase carries more items, so the direction is settled.

Why the structure works that way becomes clear once you ask who sets the rate. Pay in local currency and the conversion is handled by the network and your issuer. Accept home-currency billing and the job passes to a provider on the merchant side. The rate that provider offers already contains its margin, and that margin is never printed on the receipt under the word fee. It moved somewhere invisible; it did not disappear.

  • The familiar figure on the screen is a product, not information. That comfort is priced.
  • Comparison is designed to be hard. The final bill under local-currency billing is not settled for days, so you cannot lay the two options side by side at the counter.
  • A blocking service exists. Card issuers in Korea let you disable home-currency billing in advance, which removes the decision from the moment of payment.
  • Online checkouts ask the same question. Foreign sites sometimes default the currency selector to your home currency, so check the currency label immediately before confirming.
  • Blocking can cause declines. If a merchant only attempts home-currency billing, the payment may fail. Korean consumer guidance also warns that repeated declines on an overseas site can get the account locked.

A transfer is built differently from an exchange

An exchange finishes inside one institution. A transfer passes through several, and each takes its own cut. That is the recurring source of the complaint that the amount sent and the amount received do not match.

The Korea Federation of Banks consumer portal, publishing comparative disclosure of foreign exchange fees, splits the items as follows. These names are the places money leaves.

ItemWho deducts itFixed before sending?
Remittance feeSending bankYes. Frequently tiered by amount
Wire feeSending bankYes. Largely a flat per-transaction charge, so it weighs heavily on small transfers
Correspondent bank feeOverseas bank in the pathNot set by your bank. The number of hops depends on the route
Receiving bank feeBank at the far endGoverned by that bank's own policy
Telegraphic transfer spreadSending bankVisible on the published rate board. A different line from the cash spread

The third and fourth rows are the heart of this section. However carefully you calculate at the sending end, those two are the policies of banks in other countries. A recipient receiving less than the figure they were quoted is not an error; it is the structure. Two things to confirm: which party bears these items under the option you select, and how many intermediary hops your currency and destination imply.

Beyond banks, Korea also licenses small-amount overseas remittance operators under the Foreign Exchange Transactions Act, with per-transaction and annual handling limits set by statute and ministerial notice. The current figures are not printed here. Confirm them with the operator and the relevant notice before using such a service.

Amount and timing change what the cost is made of

The same item weighs differently depending on the amount. Without that instinct, people economise in the wrong place.

  • Fixed items dominate small sums. A wire fee or a per-withdrawal ATM charge does not scale, so as a share of a small transfer it grows.
  • Proportional items dominate large sums. A spread scales with the amount, so the absolute cost rises linearly. This is the band where negotiating preferential terms genuinely matters.
  • Splitting a transfer multiplies the fixed part. Sending a little at a time feels safer but pays the wire fee once per attempt.
  • ATM withdrawals behave the same way. Per-transaction elements make many small withdrawals structurally expensive — though that trades against theft risk, so amount alone should not decide it.
  • Timing is its own item. Card conversion happens days later, and a transfer may fall under a different published rate depending on when it is processed. That is not a saving problem but a predictability problem.

The decision rule sorts out like this. When the amount is small, cutting the number of transactions comes first. When it is large, narrowing the gap comes first. How much you move at once determines which axis to look at.

Declarations and limits - start with knowing they exist

Separately from cost, foreign currency dealings above certain amounts carry declaration procedures. People do get caught by rules they never knew existed, so it is worth knowing the thresholds exist at all. What follows is Korean law; other countries set their own.

Carrying cash across a border

The Korea Customs Service page on entry and departure currency declaration, opened on 25 August 2026, states that carrying means of payment worth up to ten thousand US dollars requires no permission or declaration, and that anything above must be declared to customs. The cited basis is the Foreign Exchange Transactions Regulations, Article 5-11 paragraph 1, Article 6-2 paragraph 2 and Article 6-3 paragraph 1.

  • The aggregation is broad. External means of payment, domestic currency and won-denominated cashier cheques all count. Foreign banknotes alone is the wrong count.
  • Entry and departure follow different steps. On arrival you tick the currency field on the traveller declaration form, enter the total, submit it, and receive a declaration certificate after inspection.
  • Penalties are stated. Where the amount involved is thirty thousand US dollars or less, an administrative fine applies; above that, the guidance states imprisonment of up to one year or a fine of up to one hundred million won.
  • Duty-free allowance is a separate regime. The same customs guidance puts the traveller allowance at a dutiable value of up to eight hundred US dollars. Easy to conflate with the currency threshold, but they are different rules.

Sending money by transfer

Korea's plain-language legal information service states that for a resident's outward payment, amounts within an annual cumulative total of one hundred thousand US dollars may be paid without submitting supporting documents, citing the Foreign Exchange Transactions Regulations, Article 4-3 paragraph 1, subparagraphs 1 and 2. A separate threshold for small individual transactions is described alongside it.

The Bank of Korea's foreign exchange review pages set out the treatment of payment to or from a third party. Amounts of five thousand US dollars or less require no declaration; above that up to ten thousand is declared to a foreign exchange bank; above ten thousand goes to the Bank of Korea. Sending your own money to yourself and paying someone who is not the counterparty sit in different boxes.

How to read this section. These figures reflect the guidance as published at the time of checking, and foreign exchange thresholds are amended — the documentation threshold has been adjusted in recent years. If your amount is anywhere near a line, do not memorise the number. Confirm with customs, the central bank and your own bank immediately before you travel or send. Whether a specific transaction is reportable turns on its content, so the final determination belongs to your bank and the competent authority.

When not economising is the better call

Once the cost structure is visible, the urge is to hunt for the optimal route. But the hunt itself costs something. Read this section against everything above it.

  • Small amounts make small differences. On a modest exchange, the time spent comparing spreads can exceed what the comparison saves.
  • Travel is a real cost. Fold in the fare and the hour spent reaching a better-priced branch and the conclusion sometimes reverses.
  • Airport desks being expensive is not the same sentence as losing money there. Certainty with a flight to catch is part of what that gap buys. Converting only the minimum you need on the spot is often the sensible compromise.
  • Emergency cash is insurance, not expense. A small reserve for when cards fail should not be judged on its spread.
  • Waiting for a rate to move is not optimisation. Where a currency is heading is outside what can be said here. But waiting has costs that are structural rather than predictive: plans go unmade, favourable advance-order terms lapse, and you end up pushed onto whichever route is fastest.

If you want one rule: when the expected saving is smaller than the value of the time it takes, the most convenient route is the cheapest route. The larger the amount, the further that line moves the other way.

Frequently asked questions

Why can I never exchange at the base rate?

Because it is a reference, not a customer price. As the Bank of Korea material puts it, banks add or subtract an exchange commission to the base rate to produce quoted cash and telegraphic transfer rates. Buying widens upward, selling widens downward. That is why the big number on screen and the receipt disagree.

Does a 90 percent preferential rate mean almost no fee?

No. The preference discounts the difference between the base rate and the applied rate — the exchange commission — not the final amount. Items outside the spread are not covered at all. Compare the final applied rate plus separately charged items, never the headline percentage.

Should I accept home-currency billing abroad?

Local currency is structurally better. Korean consumer guidance shows that local-currency payment attracts the overseas usage fee, while home-currency billing adds an exchange commission and a conversion service fee on top. More items on the same purchase. Enabling your issuer's blocking service in advance saves the argument at the counter.

Why does the received amount never match the sent amount?

Because more than one party deducts. The Korea Federation of Banks splits the items into remittance fee, wire fee, correspondent bank fee and receiving bank fee. The first two are set by the sending bank; the last two are the policies of overseas banks and cannot always be fixed in advance. Confirm who bears them and how many hops the route implies.

How much currency can a traveller carry out of Korea?

Korean customs guidance allows up to ten thousand US dollars without declaration and requires a customs declaration above that. The part that trips people up is that the threshold aggregates won cash and won-denominated cashier cheques, not just foreign banknotes. Undeclared amounts up to thirty thousand dollars draw an administrative fine; above that, criminal penalties are stated. Thresholds are amended, so re-check before departure.
