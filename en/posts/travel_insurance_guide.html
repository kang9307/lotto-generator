Introduction: Why Travel Insurance Matters

A single emergency room visit in the United States can run into thousands of dollars. An ambulance ride in parts of Europe, a hospital stay in Japan, or an evacuation flight from a remote destination can cost tens of thousands of dollars — all billed directly to you, without the protection of your home country's public health system. Add the possibility of lost luggage, a cancelled flight, or accidentally damaging someone else's property, and the financial exposure during a trip becomes significant.

Travel insurance is designed to address these risks. Yet many travelers find the product category confusing: What exactly is covered? How does it interact with existing health insurance? What happens when a claim is denied? This guide cuts through the complexity and gives you a clear, honest picture of what travel insurance does and does not cover — so you can make an informed choice before your next trip.

This guide draws on publicly available guidance from South Korea's Financial Supervisory Service (FSS), the General Insurance Association of Korea (KNIA), and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. It covers general principles applicable to most markets as of 2026, though specific product terms vary by insurer.

1. Types of Travel Insurance

Before comparing coverage, it helps to know which category of product fits your situation.

1.1 Short-Trip International Insurance

The most common form — designed for overseas leisure travel, business trips, or short-term study programs typically lasting from one day to a few months. You set the departure and return dates, choose your coverage components (medical, baggage, delay, liability, etc.), and the premium reflects your destination, trip length, and selected benefits.

1.2 Long-Stay Overseas Insurance

Standard short-trip policies often have maximum duration limits that make them unsuitable for stays of three months or longer. Working holiday visa holders, degree students, overseas employees, and long-term researchers typically need a dedicated long-stay overseas insurance or overseas study insurance product. Coverage structure, maximum periods, and benefit limits differ substantially from short-trip products — verify with individual insurers.

1.3 Domestic Travel Insurance

Covers accidental injury and illness during domestic travel. Because national health systems apply within one's home country, the priority use cases tend to be adventure activities, group tours, or supplemental accident coverage where domestic health insurance has gaps.

2. Core Coverage Components Explained

2.1 Overseas Medical Expenses (The Most Critical Benefit)

This covers treatment costs at foreign hospitals and clinics — consultations, hospitalization, surgery, prescriptions — when you fall ill or are injured abroad. Because medical costs vary enormously by country (the United States and many Western European countries are especially expensive), the coverage limit you choose matters greatly. Experts commonly recommend a minimum of USD 100,000 for destinations with high medical costs, though the right limit depends on destination and trip length.

South Korea's FSS has specifically noted that travel insurance domestic medical coverage and existing real-loss (Silson) health insurance cannot both pay in full — the total payout is capped at actual expenses incurred. However, overseas medical coverage fills a gap that domestic health insurance typically does not cover at all.

2.2 Accidental Death and Permanent Disability

Pays a lump sum if the insured dies or suffers a permanent disability as a direct result of an accident during the trip. The disability payout is usually proportional to the severity of the impairment according to a graded scale in the policy. This benefit is separate from medical expense reimbursement.

2.3 Baggage and Personal Effects

Reimburses you for loss, theft, or damage to personal belongings during the trip — typically covering items such as smartphones, cameras, laptops, and passports. Key details to check:

  • Whether loss (including mysterious disappearance) is covered, or only theft with evidence
  • Per-item limits and overall aggregate limits
  • Depreciation rules (older items may be valued at less than replacement cost)
  • Cash and credit cards are almost universally excluded
  • A police report is generally required for theft claims

2.4 Flight Delay and Cancellation

Reimburses reasonable out-of-pocket costs — meals, hotel accommodation — when your flight is delayed beyond a policy-specified threshold (commonly 3 to 6 hours). South Korea's FSS has clarified that general shopping or purchases unrelated to waiting for the flight are not reimbursable. You must obtain an official delay or cancellation notice from the airline. Note that compensation already provided by the airline (meals, vouchers) is typically deducted.

2.5 Personal Liability

Covers your legal liability if you accidentally injure a third party or damage their property during the trip — for example, a collision on a rented bicycle, breaking hotel property, or a skiing accident injuring another person. Coverage limits vary widely; checking the limit and any relevant exclusions (deliberate acts, business activities) is important. This benefit is often underrated but can be extremely valuable.

2.6 Trip Cancellation and Interruption

Trip cancellation reimburses non-refundable, prepaid travel costs (flights, hotels, tours) if you cancel before departure for a covered reason. Common covered reasons include sudden hospitalization of the insured or an immediate family member, natural disasters, and travel advisories issued for the destination. Change of mind, visa denial, and schedule changes due to work are generally not covered.

Trip interruption applies if you must cut a trip short after it has begun for similar covered reasons, and typically covers unused prepaid costs plus the additional cost of an emergency return flight home.

2.7 Emergency Medical Evacuation and Repatriation

Pays for medically necessary transportation to an appropriate treatment facility or back to your home country when local care is inadequate. This can be one of the most expensive claims in travel insurance and is particularly important when traveling to remote areas or countries with limited medical infrastructure. South Korea's Ministry of Foreign Affairs specifically recommends confirming this coverage before departure.

2.8 Optional Add-Ons to Consider

  • Adventure sports rider: Standard policies often exclude scuba diving, skiing, bungee jumping, and similar activities. A separate rider is required if your itinerary includes these.
  • Rental vehicle excess: Covers the deductible (excess) you would owe on a rental car if it is damaged.
  • Kidnap and ransom: Available for travel to higher-risk destinations.
  • Electronic device coverage: Extended coverage for high-value electronics beyond standard baggage limits.

3. Duplicate Coverage Caution: Domestic Health Insurance and Travel Insurance

A common misconception is that holding both domestic health insurance and travel insurance guarantees double the payout. South Korea's FSS issued a consumer alert (April 2025) specifically addressing this:

If a traveler already holds domestic real-loss (Silson) health insurance and also has a travel insurance policy with a domestic medical coverage rider, the two policies cannot both pay in full. Total reimbursement is capped at actual medical costs incurred.

This principle — known as indemnity or non-duplication — applies broadly: insurance is designed to indemnify actual loss, not create a profit. If you already have comprehensive domestic health insurance, you may be able to remove or reduce the domestic medical coverage rider from your travel policy and save on premium. That said, overseas medical coverage remains a separate and generally non-overlapping benefit that most domestic health plans do not provide — so that component should not be removed.

4. Key Checkpoints Before You Buy

4.1 Coverage Adequacy

  • Is the overseas medical limit sufficient for your destination? (US, Canada, Western Europe require higher limits)
  • Does the policy cover both illness and injury, or only one?
  • Is the trip cancellation coverage limit enough to cover all prepaid non-refundable costs?
  • Are planned activities (skiing, diving, etc.) included or excluded?

4.2 Exclusions to Check

  • Pre-existing medical conditions: Are any of your conditions likely to be excluded?
  • Alcohol and substance use: Most policies exclude accidents occurring while intoxicated
  • High-risk destinations: Does the policy have restrictions for travel advisories (e.g., Level 3 or 4 warnings)?
  • Pregnancy: Typically excluded from standard travel insurance; verify if relevant
  • Intentional acts: Never covered

4.3 Documentation You Must Collect During the Trip

  • Medical bills, diagnosis letters, and prescription receipts from overseas providers (originals or certified copies)
  • Police report (Police Report) for theft or loss of belongings
  • Official flight delay or cancellation confirmation from the airline
  • Any incident reports, hotel damage reports, or other relevant documents
  • 24-hour emergency assistance number — save it before you leave

4.4 Where to Compare Policies

For travelers based in South Korea: the FSS-operated comparison platform Boheom Damoa (e-insmarket.or.kr) and the KNIA disclosure portal (knia.or.kr) allow side-by-side premium and benefit comparisons. Online direct channels are typically 20% or more cheaper than airport counters or travel agency packages. Final pricing should always be confirmed directly with the insurer before purchase.

6. Short Trip vs. Long Stay: Choosing the Right Product

6.1 Short Trips (Up to a Few Months)

Standard international travel insurance covers the vast majority of leisure, business, and short study trips. Select coverage amounts appropriate for the destination (higher medical limits for high-cost healthcare countries), confirm activity exclusions, and purchase before departure — policies cannot be issued after you have already left your home country.

6.2 Long-Term Stays (3 Months and Over)

Working holiday programs, degree-level study abroad, overseas employment, and extended research stays require dedicated long-stay products. Key differences often include: longer maximum coverage periods, different benefit structures suited to sustained residency, and sometimes requirements for local supplementary health enrollment. Confirm eligibility and terms with individual insurers well in advance of departure.

6.3 Travel Advisories and High-Risk Destinations

Some insurers restrict or exclude coverage for destinations under elevated travel warnings. In South Korea, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs issues four-level travel advisories, with Level 3 (Departure Recommended) and Level 4 (No-Travel) destinations potentially triggering policy exclusions. Always check the advisory status of your destination at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs travel safety portal (0404.go.kr) before departing.

7. Filing a Claim: Step-by-Step

  1. Contact your insurer's 24-hour emergency assistance line as soon as an incident occurs — many policies require prompt notification
  2. Seek appropriate medical care; keep all receipts, bills, and medical documents
  3. For theft or loss: report to local police immediately and obtain a written report
  4. For flight delay: request an official written delay confirmation from the airline
  5. After returning home, submit all documentation through the insurer's app, website, or customer service center within the claim filing deadline (typically within three years of the incident, but check your policy)

8. Summary: Five Rules for Smart Travel Insurance

  1. Set the medical limit for the destination, not a generic figure — a policy that works in Thailand may be dangerously underinsured for the United States
  2. Check for duplicate coverage with your domestic health insurance before paying for overlapping benefits
  3. Read the exclusions list — pre-existing conditions, intoxication, and adventure sports are the most common sources of claim denial
  4. Collect all documents on the spot — no paperwork, no claim
  5. Buy online in advance — comparison platforms typically offer better rates than airport counters, and you need the policy before you board
