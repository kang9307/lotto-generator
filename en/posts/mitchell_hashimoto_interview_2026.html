Mitchell Hashimoto Interview - Why He Returned to Low-Level Open Source With Ghostty and Zig

The HashiCorp co-founder left infrastructure automation for a GPU-accelerated terminal and a language that isn't 1.0 yet

Jul 11, 2026 Dev & Infra

Intro: Why Mitchell Hashimoto, Why Now

As of July 2026, Mitchell Hashimoto's name is circulating in developer circles again - but not for Terraform or Vault this time. The subject is Ghostty, the GPU-accelerated terminal emulator he is building, and Zig, the young low-level language underneath it. In a recent interview (published on alexalejandre.com and surfaced on GeekNews), Hashimoto talked about why he left a thriving infrastructure company to dig into something as old and narrow as a terminal emulator, and why he's betting his time on a language that hasn't even hit 1.0.

Rather than reproduce that interview, this piece works through why he made these choices and what they say about the wider open source ecosystem, in my own words. Where I quote something he actually said, I mark it clearly as coming from the interview.

1. Who Is Mitchell Hashimoto - From HashiCorp to Ghostty

1.1 The Infrastructure Lineage: From Vagrant to Vault

Hashimoto met Armon Dadgar at the University of Washington in 2008, working together on a project to make public cloud technology usable for scientific research. After graduating, both moved to San Francisco, where Hashimoto first launched the open source project Vagrant. As Vagrant gained traction, he and Armon co-founded HashiCorp in 2012 - the company name itself a portmanteau of his surname and "Corporation." Over the following decade he was part of the founding engineering behind Vagrant, Packer, Consul, Terraform, Vault, Nomad, and Waypoint, tools that became the de facto standards of cloud infrastructure automation. Terraform normalized "infrastructure as code" in production; Vault became the standard for secrets management.

1.2 Leaving in 2023, and a Change of Direction

Hashimoto reportedly spent about four years as CEO and about five as CTO at HashiCorp, followed by roughly two years as an individual contributor. In December 2023, he left the company entirely. Since then he has lived in Los Angeles, spending most of his development time on Ghostty. It's a striking turn: someone who built a career at the top layer of infrastructure automation - declarative configuration, cloud orchestration - pivoted to one of the oldest, lowest-level pieces of software in computing: the terminal emulator.

2. Ghostty - Why Rebuild the Terminal

2.1 The Paradox of 15 Years of CLIs Without Understanding the Terminal

In the interview, Hashimoto was candid about what motivated Ghostty. After more than 15 years building command-line tools, he realized he didn't actually understand how the terminal emulator rendering those tools worked. He said that filling that gap would let him dig into GPU programming, desktop application development, and Zig all at once. Nothing on the market satisfied what he wanted at the same time - fast, feature-rich, and natively cross-platform - so he decided to build it himself.

2.2 The Performance GPU Acceleration Delivers

The result shows up in numbers. Ghostty offloads rendering to the GPU via Metal on macOS and OpenGL on Linux. Published benchmarks report 120fps scrolling, sub-2ms input latency, and around 45MB of memory usage. Throughput for dumping large amounts of text is reported at roughly 4x iTerm2 and Kitty, and about 2x macOS's built-in Terminal.app. On raw speed it's comparable to Alacritty, the minimalist Rust-based terminal, but Ghostty differs by offering tabs, splits, and hundreds of themes as native OS widgets. Reaching 1.0 after roughly two years in beta suggests it took time to validate speed and feature-richness together.

2.3 Terminals Shouldn't Become Browsers

At the same time, Hashimoto draws a clear line around what a terminal should be. In the interview, he said terminals shouldn't turn into "do everything" platforms the way browsers did. Instead, he believes they should focus on composability, automation, and clear security models for text-based applications. He also acknowledges a fundamental limit to that vision: the PTY (pseudo-terminal) connecting terminal and shell is nothing more than an unstructured byte stream, and no amount of polish on the terminal itself can fully escape that constraint. Behind the flashy GPU acceleration sits a clear-eyed awareness of this decades-old technical debt.

3. Why Zig, and What Makes the Language Distinct

3.1 Zig as a Low-Level Replacement for C

Ghostty was written in Zig from day one. Zig is a young systems programming language led by Andrew Kelley, positioned as filling the role C occupies today, done better. Like C, it gives you direct control over memory, but its explicit allocator model makes memory management safer and more visible. You can import C headers and call C functions without macros or a preprocessor, and Zig code can be linked into C projects in the other direction. Built-in cross-compilation with no extra setup is another feature that matters a great deal in practice.

3.2 Comptime, Cross-Compilation, and Not Being 1.0 Yet

Zig's most distinctive feature is comptime, a mechanism that lets ordinary Zig code run at compile time, replacing macros and template specialization rules with one unified system for generics and conditional compilation. However, Zig has not reached 1.0. In the interview, Hashimoto noted that Zig's creator, Andrew Kelley, prioritizes API quality over API stability, meaning breaking changes keep happening - a real burden for downstream developers like himself. Even so, he said he respects that stance, arguing that continuing to iterate until the design is right beats locking in a flawed API too early. He said he expects Zig is still years away from 1.0.

3.3 Zig in the Age of AI

An interesting wrinkle is that Hashimoto treats AI as a buffer against this language instability. In the interview he said that even as Zig's syntax changes, showing a model a few examples and letting it fill in the rest has substantially reduced the burden of large-scale migrations. He has also said on social media that Ghostty is heavily AI written, pushing back on the common claim that LLMs handle niche languages like Zig poorly. Related experiments in the Zig ecosystem are covered in my analysis of revo, a Zig-based dynamic language.

4. Why a Successful Founder Went Back to Low-Level Work

At first glance it's puzzling: a founder who built a multi-billion-dollar infrastructure company chose to obsess over polish on a GPU-accelerated terminal and a language that isn't even 1.0, instead of launching a new SaaS. Layer in a few facts and it looks like a fairly coherent choice. First, his career was already about the "invisible plumbing" of infrastructure automation, and the terminal is another piece of plumbing that every developer touches daily yet nobody rebuilds from scratch. Second, having already achieved financial independence through HashiCorp, he can choose projects purely by what he wants to learn - GPU programming, desktop development, Zig - without worrying about market fit or a business model. That is a fundamentally different starting point from the vast majority of open source developers who maintain code as unpaid side work while holding down a job. Before romanticizing his "return to low-level work," it's honest to name the material foundation that made the choice possible in the first place. Third, the question he raises is still worth asking. The fact that a successful founder chooses to get his hands dirty rebuilding fundamentals is a signal that moving into management or strategy roles isn't the only growth path as a career matures.

5. The Reality of Open Source Maintenance - Burnout and Sustainability

5.1 How to Read "I Owe Users Nothing"

The most debated part of the interview concerns how far a maintainer's responsibility extends. Hashimoto said open source maintainers have zero obligation to users. At the same time, he said he personally feels responsible for building quality software. That's not a contradiction so much as a distinction: he owes no one anything, but he holds himself to his own standard. In practice, he said he prefers understanding the underlying problem behind several requests and solving it with one elegant design, rather than granting individual feature requests one at a time.

5.2 The Burnout Crisis Facing Open Source in 2026

That statement carries different weight against the backdrop of where the open source ecosystem stands in 2026. Recent surveys put roughly 60% of maintainers working unpaid, with 60% having quit or considered quitting, and 44% of those citing burnout. In 2026, that crisis translated into real infrastructure incidents: the widely used Kubernetes Ingress NGINX stopped receiving security patches after March due to maintainer burnout, and External Secrets Operator, used across many enterprise environments, saw updates effectively freeze after four maintainers burned out. It's worth naming plainly that "I owe nothing" lands very differently coming from someone with financial security than from an unpaid maintainer who burned out propping up a project their livelihood depended on.

5.3 A New Burden in the Age of AI

On top of that, AI is adding a new layer of pressure. AI-generated pull requests reportedly carry roughly 1.7x more defects than human-written ones, and they arrive in far greater volume. The result is maintainers facing a surge in code to review with no corresponding increase in reviewer capacity. Homebrew's near-collapse and subsequent rebuild around a core team, rotating leadership, and clear boundaries, or Vue.js creator Evan You leaving his job for full-time open source once sponsorship reached a sustainable level, both show that sustainability doesn't happen on its own - it has to be structurally designed.

6. The Developer Philosophy Behind the Interview

One attitude recurs throughout this interview: separating technical merit from community culture. Hashimoto said he respects how the Zig community takes "distinctive, sometimes polarizing" positions both technically and in how it manages itself. At the same time, he distinguished Rust's technical achievements from his personal preference for a different community culture - refusing to conflate a language's merit with how much he likes its community. That same posture runs through how he treats Ghostty and Zig broadly: accepting uncomfortable breaking changes in pursuit of quality, prioritizing system-wide coherence over individual requests, and deliberately keeping the tool's scope narrow.

Conclusion: What to Take From This Interview Today

If this interview boils down to one line, it might be: success buys you the freedom to choose what's next, but that choice isn't always the comfortable one. Instead of the already-validated infrastructure automation market, Hashimoto chose a low-level space with no benchmarks or standards yet - GPU-accelerated rendering, a language that isn't 1.0, a decades-old PTY architecture. What's worth sitting with isn't Ghostty or Zig themselves, but the two attitudes underneath them: an engineering habit of digging until you understand the root of a problem, and a pragmatism about drawing clear limits as a maintainer. But it's important not to forget that his freedom rests on the specific condition of HashiCorp's success. For most open source maintainers, declaring "I owe nothing" isn't a luxury - it's closer to a survival strategy. In the middle of 2026's open source burnout crisis, this interview reads as more than an interesting update from a successful founder; it's a clue toward building open source that can actually be sustained.
