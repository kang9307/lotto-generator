How to Set Up Spotify — Decide If the Free Tier Will Hold Before You Pay

What free actually restricts, what Premium buys, and how to get your playlists in and out

August 9, 2026 Tech & IT

The one decision to make before anything else

Signing up for Spotify takes a minute. What happens next is where people get stuck. Free users hit why is it playing something I did not choose within the first hour, and paying users hit how do I bring years of playlists over from my old service on day one. This guide works through both, in the order you actually meet them when setting Spotify up for the first time.

The first decision is a single question: will the free tier hold for you, or are you going to pay? The answer decides which sections below matter. If music is mostly background for you, the free tier's limits are more livable than their reputation suggests. If your habit is queuing up specific songs and replaying them, you will fight the free tier every single day.

One thing stated up front: prices and plan lineups change often. Every figure in this article is as of August 2026, and the number that binds you is the one on the official premium page at the moment you subscribe. Anything this article cannot pin down is collected near the end, under the section on what is deliberately left unanswered.

Why free will not simply play the song you picked

The free tier's reputation is you get ads, but the wall people actually feel is not the ads — it is playback control. The current rules work like this.

  • On mobile, on-demand playback is capped per day. You can search for a track and play it directly, but the time available for that is limited daily; once it runs out, playback falls back to shuffle. This is the softened form of the old shuffle-only rule, changed in a September 2025 revamp.
  • After the allowance, skips are limited per hour. In shuffle mode, even skipping songs you dislike is rationed. This is usually the exact moment free users start considering Premium.
  • Ads interrupt between tracks. Audio ads run every few songs and display ads appear in the app. If interruptions ruin listening for you, this is the first source of friction.
  • No offline downloads. The free tier cannot store music at all, so you always need data or Wi-Fi.
  • Quality caps at 160kbps. The 320kbps tier and lossless are Premium-only. Through cheap earbuds the gap is modest; the better your gear, the more audible it becomes.
  • Desktop is less restricted than mobile. The PC app allows freer on-demand playback, so a workable compromise is free listening at a desk and shuffle on the move.

The rules also have regional history worth knowing: some markets ran for years without any free tier at all. South Korea, for example, launched in February 2021 as paid-only and did not get the free tier until October 2024. Old articles claiming Spotify has no free option in a given country may simply predate the change.

A few things about this lineup are easy to miss.

  • The lineup is not global. Some markets have no Family plan at all — South Korea caps out at Duo — and some carry a cheaper Basic tier that the US page does not show. Never assume a plan you read about exists in your country.
  • Basic, where it exists, removes ads but not much else. It typically drops offline downloads, which is exactly the feature heavy mobile listeners need most.
  • Duo has an address condition. Both people must live at the same address. The accounts stay fully separate, so recommendation algorithms never mix.
  • Student requires verification and expires. Enrollment is checked through a verification provider, and the discount runs at most 4 years.
  • Lossless is included in Premium. Spotify folded lossless audio into the existing Premium plans rather than creating a pricier tier, so there is no lossless surcharge to look for.

Basic or Premium — the fork in the road is offline downloads

If your market offers a cheaper ad-free Basic tier, the decision between it and full Premium comes down to one question: will you ever need music stored on the device? Basic removes ads and unlocks on-demand playback but cannot download. Here is how that plays out in real life.

  • If your commute is a subway or bus, full Premium wins. Downloads absorb both the dead zones and the data cost. Two streamed hours a day adds up to multiple gigabytes a month.
  • If you only listen on Wi-Fi, Basic can be enough. When listening happens at home and at a desk, offline storage buys you little.
  • If you fly regularly, Premium is not optional. Downloads are the only way to listen in the air.
  • If lossless matters to you, check the fine print. Lossless is listed under the main Premium plans; where a Basic tier exists, its page entry may not mention lossless at all, so verify before paying.

Audio quality and data use — do the math before enabling lossless

A few settings worth deciding at the same time.

  • Normal quality is the sensible default on cellular. Through earbuds the difference between 96 and 160kbps is small, and the data saving is close to half.
  • Do not enable lossless over cellular casually. It can burn hundreds of megabytes an hour; unless your plan is truly unlimited, keep it Wi-Fi only.
  • Bluetooth earphones blunt the benefit of lossless. Bluetooth transmission itself applies lossy compression. Hearing lossless properly requires wired output or a supported device.
  • Download quality is a separate setting from streaming quality. Higher download quality eats device storage, so phones with small storage can reasonably download one tier lower.
  • Automatic quality adjustment is on by default. It silently lowers quality on weak networks; if you want consistent quality, turn it off in settings.

The offline rules — 10,000 tracks, 5 devices, 30 days

Premium's offline downloads come with explicit ceilings. In numbers:

  • Up to 10,000 tracks per device can be stored — effectively unlimited for normal use.
  • Up to 5 devices can hold downloads. Downloading on a sixth wipes the downloads from your least-used device.
  • You must go online at least once every 30 days. Otherwise stored tracks are released. The rule exists so play data can be collected to pay artists.
  • Downloads work at playlist and album level. You do not store a lone track directly; you add it to a playlist and download the playlist.
  • Deleting the app deletes the downloads. After a reinstall or a device change you download again. On Android devices that allow it, moving the storage location to an SD card is worth doing early.

Liked Songs and playlists — how the library actually works

Coming from another service, Spotify's library model can feel unfamiliar at first. It rests on three pillars.

  • Liked Songs is the default vault. Tapping the plus button on a track sends it there. It doubles as the recommendation engine's primary input, which makes it more consequential than a simple favorites list.
  • Playlists are unlimited. Splitting music by mood, activity or genre is the native way to use Spotify. You can also follow other people's public playlists into your own library.
  • Following artists drives new-release alerts. New tracks from artists you follow gather in Release Radar every Friday.
  • Albums can be saved to the library too. If you listen album-by-album, saving albums keeps things tidier than liking every track.
  • A long press on the plus button files a track straight into a chosen playlist. Liking and filing become one gesture, which speeds up library upkeep considerably.
  • Collaborative playlists let several people edit one list. Useful for couples, parties and shared workout lists.

Training the recommendation algorithm — the first two weeks decide it

Half the reason to use Spotify is the recommendations. They are built from signals in your account, which means what you play and like during a new account's first two weeks shapes everything after. The fast way to train it:

  1. Like 30 to 50 songs you genuinely love. This is the first signal the algorithm receives. Like carelessly and you will be recommended carelessly.
  2. Follow at least 10 artists. They anchor Release Radar and widen the recommendation pool in the right direction.
  3. Do not just skip songs you dislike — mark them. The track and artist menus include an option to exclude from your recommendations. It teaches the system far faster than skipping.
  4. Check Discover Weekly every Monday. It gathers tracks that people with similar taste are playing. Liking songs from it is the single most efficient feedback loop.
  5. Collect new releases from Release Radar on Fridays. Since it is built from followed artists, managing follows is managing your new-release feed.
  6. Turn on a private session when playing music for others. Kids' songs and shop background music pollute the algorithm otherwise — the most common cause of recommendations suddenly going strange.

From phone to PC speakers mid-song — Spotify Connect and the one-stream rule

Switching devices on Spotify is not unplug-and-restart. Spotify Connect binds every device signed into the same account into one remote-control system.

  • The device icon on the playback screen picks which device makes sound. Moving a song from phone to PC or speaker without interruption is that one button.
  • Your phone becomes the remote. Sound comes from the speaker while the phone only handles skipping and volume — so an incoming call does not stop the music.
  • Devices appear in the list only on the same Wi-Fi. If a speaker is missing, the first thing to check is whether phone and speaker share a network.
  • One stream per account. Start playback on a second device and the first one stops. Households sharing one account cut each other off constantly at exactly this point — which is why Duo and Family exist.
  • TVs, consoles and smart speakers join the same system. Sign in on each device's Spotify app and it appears in the Connect list.

Moving playlists in from another service — official versus third party

The most painful thing to leave behind at an old service is years of playlists. There are two routes in, and which one applies depends on where you are coming from.

  1. Check the official import feature first. The Import your music entry at the bottom of the Your Library screen in the mobile app is an official route built with the transfer service TuneMyMusic, and it works on free accounts.
  2. Major global services transfer through this official path. Pick the source service, sign into it, select playlists, and they are copied over. Nothing is deleted at the source.
  3. Regional services are often not on the list. Korea's Melon is a typical example. For those, exporting the track list as text or CSV and feeding it to a third-party tool's text import is the realistic route.
  4. Audit the matches afterwards. Tracks with non-Latin titles frequently match to the wrong version or drop out entirely. Skim the transferred list right away.
  5. Revoke tool permissions when done. The apps section of your Spotify account page lists connected third-party tools and lets you remove their access. That step is part of the transfer, not an optional extra.
Third-party transfer tools are convenient, but they are things you hand account access to. Check the following before using one.
  • Avoid any tool that asks for your password directly. A legitimate tool goes through each service's official login window (OAuth) and never collects credentials on its own screens.
  • Read the permission scope it requests. Reading and creating playlists is all a transfer needs. Anything broader is a reason for suspicion.
  • Disconnect the tool as soon as the job is done. A migration is a one-off task; there is no reason to leave standing access behind.

Cancelling and refunds — move before the billing date and you lose nothing

Cancellation happens on the web account page, not in the app. Plenty of people give up after hunting for a cancel button inside the app; by design it is not there. The order:

  1. Sign into the Spotify account page in a browser. The subscription section shows your current plan and the next billing date.
  2. Check the next billing date first. Cancellation takes effect from the next cycle, so acting any time before that date means no further charge and full Premium until the date arrives.
  3. Choose change plan or cancel. Your account, playlists and likes all survive; the account simply drops to the free tier.
  4. If you subscribed through an app store, cancel there. Subscriptions billed via the App Store or Play Store must be cancelled in that store's subscription manager — the Spotify site cannot touch them.
  5. For refunds, go through customer support. Whether an already-charged amount is refundable depends on payment method, timing and regional policy, so the official support channel is the only accurate answer.

What this article deliberately leaves unanswered

The following points either have no officially published value or change too often to state as fact, so no answer is asserted here.

  • The exact size of the free tier's daily on-demand allowance is not officially published and may vary by situation, so no number is given here.
  • Whether Basic tiers include lossless is not stated on the plan pages that carry them. If lossless is the point, confirm the per-plan wording on the official premium page before paying.
  • Concrete refund conditions and windows vary by payment method and regional terms. If it applies to you, ask official customer support directly.
  • Which schools pass Student verification is determined by the verification provider. The only real check is whether your institution appears during signup.
  • How long the listed prices will hold is unknowable. The table reflects August 2026; the official premium page at your moment of payment is the only binding number.

Frequently asked questions

Q1. Can I keep using Spotify for free indefinitely?

Yes — the ad-supported tier has no time limit. But on mobile the time for picking specific tracks is capped daily, after which you get shuffle plus limited skips. No offline downloads, and quality caps at 160kbps. Fine for background listening; a daily fight if you play songs on demand.

Q2. What is the biggest difference between Free and Premium?

Not the ads — playback control and offline downloads. Premium plays anything on demand, skips freely, stores up to 10,000 tracks per device for data-free listening, and unlocks 320kbps plus lossless. Free restricts all four.

Q3. Is the plan lineup the same everywhere?

No. As of August 2026 the US lineup is Individual, Student, Duo and Family, but some markets drop Family or add a cheaper Basic tier (ad-free, no offline downloads) — South Korea does both. Check your own region's official page.

Q4. Can I move playlists over from my old service?

The official Import your music feature in the app covers major global services like YouTube Music and Apple Music. Regional services outside the list can usually be moved by exporting track lists to text or CSV through a third-party tool. If you used a third-party tool, revoke its access on the account page afterwards.

Q5. Do I lose my music the moment I cancel?

No. Premium continues to the end of the paid period, then the account drops to free. Playlists and likes remain. But downloaded music stops playing the moment Premium ends, and ads plus playback limits return.

Wrapping up

Setting up Spotify really comes down to four decisions: whether free will hold, if paying then Basic or full Premium (the fork is offline downloads), where to set quality versus data, and which route your existing playlists take in. Settle those four and the remaining settings work fine at their defaults.

Two habits are worth building on day one. First, spend the first two weeks liking and excluding tracks diligently to train the algorithm. Second, if you started with a free trial, put the next billing date in your calendar. The money people regret spending on Spotify almost always leaves through a forgotten billing date.
