What Does 1,000 Concurrent Players Actually Measure - Where a Game Server Breaks First

Connections, active players, and players sharing one space cost orders of magnitude apart

Aug 31, 2026 Dev & Infra

Intro: The number is large, and nobody says what it counts

Suppose a spec lands on your desk with the line must support 1,000 concurrent players. That sentence cannot tell you whether to buy one machine or ten. The same thousand finishes on a laptop in one game and forces you to split servers in another. What is missing is not hardware; it is conditions.

Concurrency is a convenient word, which is why it hides at least three different things. Someone who merely holds a session, someone actively doing something, and someone standing in a space where everyone sees everyone else are three entirely different guests from the server's point of view. Count them ever so precisely and, without that split, the number still cannot be compared to anything.

This piece has one axis. A concurrency number means nothing without its conditions. There are no settings to copy here and no values to paste. Only the structure: what multiplies what, and which resource therefore empties first.

1. The trigger — one small server, a thousand bots

In late August 2026 an independent developer published a write-up about pushing a browser-based multiplayer game he built onto a single virtual server in the roughly six dollar a month class, one vCPU and one gigabyte of memory, to see whether it would hold a thousand concurrent players. The short version, in his own account, is that the thousand-player goal failed and what stayed stable was somewhere around three hundred.

What matters is not the failure but what exactly was measured. Here are the conditions as stated. Everything from here to the end of this list is the author's description of his own setup, not my measurement.

  • The clients were bots, not people. A thousand automated clients, run from a separate machine, as he wrote it
  • The bots were not idling. They ran a loop of fighting monsters, leveling up or dying, and picking up items. So this was not a test of connections held open
  • The update interval was 100 milliseconds, meaning state went out ten times per second
  • The world was cut into ten zones with a cap of one hundred per zone, and the bots were placed one hundred to a zone
  • Transport was WebSocket, and by his measurement more than 99.9 percent of the traffic ran server to client

The breaking point is equally specific. He reported that around three hundred clients the kernel raised a TCP out-of-memory condition and connections collapsed, that slowing the rate of new connections produced the same symptom at the same point, and that even after kernel-side adjustments the outbound send queue grew to roughly 75 MiB until memory ran out. Application memory sat in the tens of megabytes, which means what broke was not game logic but the place where data waiting to leave had piled up.

This is where the article turns. Reading that record as a six-dollar box handles three hundred players is the wrong lesson. It is one person's single result, from one game, under one set of conditions. What is worth taking is not the number but why the number stopped where it did.

2. What N concurrent measures — connections, activity, or shared space

Lay the three apart and it becomes obvious why identical numbers refuse to be compared.

What the number countsWhat the server actually doesRough character at 1,000
Open connections
including sessions merely left logged in		Keeps sockets alive and checks periodically that they still are. Almost no state changesA buffer and a kernel structure per connection. Computation is close to nothing
Active players
sending input, changing state		Receives input, resolves it, delivers the result to whoever is concernedComputation and outbound traffic rise with headcount. Broadly linear
People in one shared space
who see and affect each other		Tells everyone else in that space about every one person's changeUpdate volume grows closer to the square of headcount. This is where orders of magnitude appear

The third row is the point of this article. A thousand players alone in a thousand rooms leaves the server essentially idle. The same thousand gathered on one screen creates something on the order of a million pairs to update. Layout sets the cost, not specification.

So when a service announces its concurrency, the question worth asking is not how many but whether those people can see each other. That is why a chat service and a competitive shooter quoting the same figure are not saying the same thing.

3. Cost comes from interactions, not headcount

What a server pays for is not people but pairs — who needs to be told about whose change. If N people share a space and all see each other, the update targets scale roughly with N squared. Going from 100 to 200 doubles the people and roughly quadruples the burden. Doubling the server does not double the headcount.

That is exactly why the experiment behind this article nailed the per-zone cap at one hundred and used ten zones. That is not convenience; it is a device for cutting the square down. Half the reason walls, doors and channels exist inside games is fiction, and the other half is this arithmetic.

  • Visibility filtering — changes to anything off screen are not sent. The gain is largest in wide open areas
  • Zoning — the world is cut so updates circulate only within a zone. Boundary handling becomes the new homework
  • Grids and interest lists — the map is divided into cells and a player subscribes only to their own and neighboring cells, swapping subscriptions as they move
  • Priority tiers — nearby things update often, distant things rarely. The pressure is relieved by information priority rather than headcount
  • Separating the static from the changing — values that never change, such as appearance or name, are sent once on entry, and afterward only position and state flow

The last item is a direction the original record actually took. Stripping static data out of each snapshot, the author calculated, could bring things down to roughly a 1.4 KB base snapshot per zone plus about 36 bytes per additional player. In the other direction, he wrote that he tried sending only what changed and abandoned it, for an interesting reason: computing the difference cost more than it saved. Trading fewer bytes for more computation is a common turn in realtime servers.

4. Tick rate multiplies everything

Every cost named so far is multiplied by how many times per second you do it. That coefficient is the tick rate. Move from ten updates per second to twenty and both computation and outbound roughly double. The reverse holds too, which makes it more effective and more predictable than almost any other adjustment.

The experiment ran at ten per second, and the author's next step was to consider dropping to five. Halving it roughly halves what goes out. One place to touch, a linear result, and arithmetic you can actually do in advance: there are not many dials like that.

But this dial is not a performance setting; it is part of the game's design. Widening the interval means the client has to fill the gap, and the only way to fill it is prediction. When prediction is right the motion is smooth, and when it is wrong the character snaps backward. Players do not remember that snap as latency. They remember it as unfairness.

  • Genres decided in an instant cannot thin updates easily. A single frame of a grazing shot settles the round
  • Genres built on movement and conversation barely change in feel when thinned, which is exactly how much cheaper their servers get to be
  • It varies within one game too. Dense during combat, sparse in town is a natural gradient
  • Pushing accuracy to the client has a price. The more the client decides, the more room there is to manipulate it

5. What breaks first — the order is set by the genre

Saying the server cannot cope is not a diagnosis. The response depends entirely on which resource filled first, and that order is set by the genre.

Resource that fills firstWhere it arrives firstVisible symptom
CPUGames heavy on physics, collision and pathfinding, with much to finish inside one tickTicks slip. Everyone slows evenly, and it degrades sharply as headcount rises
Outbound bandwidthGames with many people in one space and frequent state broadcastsOnly some players drop. It starts with the slowest connections
MemoryGames that must hold world state and inventories continuouslyHeadroom shrinks as players arrive, then at some point the process disappears
Kernel socket handling and buffersServices with high raw connection counts, where unsent data poolsUtilization graphs look idle while new connections are refused. The most misread case

The bottom row is the order that actually appeared in the experiment. Application memory was comfortable while data queued for sending piled up on the kernel side and took everything down. What makes this type nasty is that it does not appear on the screens people usually watch. Process utilization and application memory both look fine.

This is not confined to games. The pattern where scale breaks not at the place doing the work but at the place preparing it repeats elsewhere. I covered a case where session validation broke at startup rather than at lookup, and the shape is the same.

6. A thousand standing still versus a hundred fighting

Which of the two is expensive? Usually the hundred who are fighting. Pull the reasons apart.

A thousand standing still change no state. No change means nothing to send. The server's job reduces to confirming that connections are alive. The hundred who are fighting, by contrast, each generate several changes every tick. Position moves, health drops, an effect applies, and that result feeds back into the surrounding calculation.

  • The frequency of change differs. A standing player produces zero events per second; a fighting one produces several
  • The audience per change differs. Fights happen where people have gathered, so each change has many recipients
  • The nature of the computation differs. Idling only occupies a slot; combat demands resolution, and resolution is sensitive to order and timing, which makes it hard to defer
  • The cost of failure differs. Miss a tick while standing and nobody notices. Miss a tick mid-fight and the complaint arrives immediately

So capacity should always be stated with both a normal case and a worst moment. The worst moment is usually predictable: a boss appearing, maintenance ending and everyone logging in at once, a crowd converging on an event site. Size for the normal case and you break precisely when people are enjoying themselves most.

7. The limits of one server, and what each way of splitting gives up

Once one machine is not enough, you split. There are three broad ways, and all three hand something back. Nothing scales for free.

ApproachWhat you gainWhat you give up
Zoning
cut the world across servers		Fewer interaction pairs per server, and the world stays singleCrossing boundaries is hard. Both zones' state must be known at once, and a sloppy handoff stutters on every move
Instancing
clone the same space many times		The headcount ceiling effectively disappears, and it is the simplest to buildThe sense of one shared world is lost, and friends land in different copies
Sharding
assign players to groups		Almost no server-to-server traffic, so scaling stays cleanPeople on different shards never meet, and a split population can leave one side empty

One misconception shows up here often: the expectation that more servers means less latency. Usually the opposite. The more you split, the more the servers have to talk to each other, and each of those round trips is newly added delay. Splitting is a trade that buys throughput by paying complexity and a little latency, not a way to go faster.

Which suggests inverting the order of the decision. Decide first which experience must be preserved. If the value of your game is that everyone meets in one world, instancing is not the answer. If small-group play is the center, instancing is both the cheapest and the safest. It looks like a technical choice and is actually a design one.

8. Latency is not a bandwidth problem

Doubling the pipe does not shorten the round trip. Bandwidth is how much you can push at once; latency is how long one trip takes. Confuse the two and the money goes to the wrong place.

Round-trip time is set mostly by distance and the number of devices in the path. It barely moves with the tier of the line. So the practical lever for latency is putting servers near players, which is a placement question rather than a connectivity one.

And in a realtime game, what people feel more strongly than average latency is jitter — how much the delay varies. A steady 80 milliseconds beats bouncing between 40 and 200. Steady is something the body adapts to; unsteady offers nothing to adapt to. The principle that the tail governs the felt experience holds outside games too, and I expanded on it in a piece on how people actually perceive delay.

  • Compensation exists but is not magic. Client-side prediction buys smoothness and pays for it with a correction when the guess is wrong
  • Rewinding to resolve a past moment makes the shooter's experience accurate at the cost of the target being hit after apparently dodging. It is a choice about whose screen counts as the truth
  • No compensation invents information. It plausibly fills what is missing; it does not reduce the delay itself
  • How the connection was established counts too. A relay lengthens the path, and the conditions that separate direct connections from relayed ones are their own subject

9. Where load tests deceive you

Numbers produced by bots are useful for locating a ceiling. Used as an operating forecast, though, they tend to err optimistic. Bots differ from people in several ways, and every one of those differences runs against the server.

  • Bots usually run from one place. The experiment here drove a thousand from a single separate machine. Real players sit on scattered regions and connections, and the slow ones hold the server's send queue hostage
  • Bots do not disconnect. In a live service connections drop and reattach constantly, and each reconnect ships the world state in full, far heavier than steady traffic
  • Bots do not crowd. Evenly distributed bots and a stampede toward a newly opened area are completely different loads
  • Bots do not spectate. People watch what others are doing, and watching is itself receiving updates
  • Bot loops are predictable. Caches and branch prediction hit better than they will in the field, so the same computation finishes cheaper than it really is
  • Bots do not render. Whatever limit exists on the client side is entirely invisible to this kind of test

So the right way to read a load test is not how many it held but which part broke first. The former collapses when conditions change; the latter mostly survives them. The valuable part of this experiment is not the figure three hundred but the observation that the kernel's outbound queue gave way before anything else.

One more thing: a load test must be read together with how long it ran. Passing the peak and then falling over hours later is common, and the cause is usually something leaking slowly. That class of problem is unfindable without a view that can trace what changed and when.

What I could not verify

Every experimental figure this article leans on is what the author wrote in a public record, and I did not reproduce the conditions. The following could not be established from that record, so this piece does not assert them.

  • The load generation tooling and measurement method are not identified, so the precision of the numbers cannot be assessed
  • How closely the bot behavior resembles real player distribution is unknown. Travel distance, crowding, and login time distribution are not published
  • The path and round-trip time from the bot machine to the server are not given, so the gap from real player conditions cannot be estimated
  • Client-side limits fall outside this test. Whether a browser copes with hundreds of visible characters is a separate question
  • Whether other hardware or other games break in the same order cannot be known from a single case. The order in this article's table is reasoning from principle, not a measurement

To be explicit, this article recommends no provider and no product. Hardware is described only by core count and memory class, and terms such as monthly transfer allowances vary by contract, so that is a question for your own agreement.

Conclusion: what this experiment can and cannot teach

Start with what it cannot teach. The sentence a small box handles three hundred cannot be drawn from this record. That three hundred came from a particular genre, a particular update interval, a particular zone structure and a particular kind of load. Change one and it moves. Raise updates to twenty per second and it will fall below half; drop the zone cap to twenty and it will climb.

The record also carries a constraint heavier than the number itself. Separately from peak throughput, the author calculated that staying inside a monthly transfer allowance pins sustained concurrency to the low hundreds. Accepting three hundred for a moment and holding three hundred for a month are entirely different problems. The real limit of a realtime service often comes from the accumulated total rather than the peak.

Now what it can teach, in three lines.

  • Attach conditions to the number. N concurrent is not comparable to anything until you say whether N is connections, activity, or a shared space
  • Cost comes from interaction, not headcount. Visibility, zones and update rate set the mood of the game and the invoice at the same time
  • Decide in advance where you will break. Most teams pick hardware without knowing whether their game jams on CPU or on bandwidth. Knowing the order turns it into one small test

This is why a published failure is more useful than a success story. A success story leaves a result; a failure leaves a boundary, and boundaries transfer across conditions. The next time a line reading must support 1,000 concurrent arrives, ask whether those thousand can see each other before you pick a machine.
