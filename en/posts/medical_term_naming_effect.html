Disease Names Change What Patients Choose - What Renaming Does and What It Cannot Do

The trial where one word moved surgery preference from 47 to 31 percent, the committees that actually renamed diseases and when, why a 6.5 percent progression risk stays 6.5 percent regardless - and the shape of the questions worth asking once you have been handed a diagnosis

Aug 17, 2026 Health & Wellness
Before anything else: this article is for general information and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Confirm your diagnosis and what it means with your own clinician. The conditions and figures below are used to explain terminology policy and research findings; none of them describe any individual person's prognosis. What to test, when to follow up, and what to decide are matters for the clinician who knows your history and your results.

Introduction: how kidney disappointment ended up in the literature

Search an academic index for kidney disappointment and papers come back. The intended phrase was kidney failure. Somewhere upstream, failure was swapped for a thesaurus neighbour, and the usual suspects are plagiarism-detection evasion and automated translation or paraphrasing tools. Nobody proposed this. It is an accident.

What makes the accident interesting is how badly it reads. Inside a thesaurus, failure and disappointment sit close together. In front of a patient they are not close at all. One sounds like the body has collapsed. The other sounds like a mildly bad exam result. Same referent, wildly different landing point.

Which raises the real question. Does the name of a condition actually change how patients understand it and what they do next? It does, and the effect has been measured in randomised trials. The same body of evidence also carries a warning that points the other way. The moment a softer name is read as a softer disease, the change backfires. This article holds both directions at equal weight.

1. How far the evidence actually goes

1.1 Breast: one word moved surgery preference by 16 points

The most cited study is Omer and colleagues, published in JAMA Internal Medicine in 2013. Researchers gave 394 women with no history of breast cancer an identical clinical situation, ductal carcinoma in situ, described three different ways, and asked what they would choose.

  • Noninvasive breast cancer - surgery chosen first by 47%
  • Breast lesion - surgery chosen first by 34%
  • Abnormal cells - surgery chosen first by 31%
  • Non-surgical options were preferred by 53%, 66% and 69% respectively

Same pathology. Same risk. Same participants. The only variable was vocabulary, and 16 percentage points moved.

1.2 Thyroid: softer does not mean calmer

A randomised crossover trial by Nickel and colleagues in JAMA Otolaryngology–Head & Neck Surgery in 2018 adds a wrinkle that most summaries drop. It tested 550 Australian adults with no thyroid cancer history (mean age 49.9, 50.7% female) across three descriptions.

  • Anxiety on an 11-point scale at first exposure - papillary thyroid cancer 7.8, papillary lesion 7.0, abnormal cells 7.3
  • Surgery as first choice - papillary thyroid cancer 32.3% (60/186), papillary lesion 24.1% (46/191), abnormal cells 27.2% (47/173)

The calmest option was not the gentlest-sounding one. Lesion beat abnormal cells on both measures. That ordering is hard to dismiss as noise. The word abnormal strips away identity while leaving the threat intact. Softening a label does not reduce fear in a straight line; make a thing unnameable and the fear climbs back up.

1.3 The direction is solid, the evidence type is not

The 2017 BMJ Open systematic review Words do matter screened 1,399 papers and pooled 20. Its conclusion runs one way: more medicalised terminology shifts preference toward more invasive management and raises both anxiety and perceived severity.

But nearly all of it rests on hypothetical vignettes. These are not recordings of real patients with real biopsies making real decisions about their own bodies. Trust the direction; do not transplant the magnitudes. That caveat becomes load-bearing later in this article.

2. The renamings that actually happened, and who made them

Terminology does not change because someone finds a word distasteful. In almost every case there is a party that requested it, a body that decided it, and a separate step that made it stick institutionally.

The line worth pausing on is the Japanese dementia panel's third reason. Not that the word was offensive, and not that it was imprecise, but that it was obstructing early detection and early diagnosis. That reframes stigma from a matter of feelings into a matter of access to care, which is a claim health systems can act on. The Korean renamings rest on the same logic.

WHO has had a naming policy since 2015: new disease names should minimise unnecessary negative impact on trade, travel, tourism and animal welfare, and avoid offence to cultural, social, national, regional, professional or ethnic groups. The premise that a name moves things well outside the clinic is already written into policy.

3. The case that ran the other way: nephrology chose failure

Renaming does not always travel toward the gentler word. The KDIGO consensus conference of June 2019, reported the following year in Kidney International, recommended the opposite.

  • Use kidney rather than renal or nephro-
  • Use kidney failure in place of end-stage kidney disease and ESRD
  • Kidney failure is defined as GFR below 15 mL/min per 1.73 m² or treatment by dialysis
  • Chronic applies beyond three months; the acute form (AKI stage 3) is reserved for three months or less

Here is the point. End-stage is not a soft phrase. It reads as a verdict. In reality transplantation and dialysis lie downstream, and there is time in front of them, yet the label has already announced the final chapter. Failure sounds blunter, but it describes a state without predicting an ending.

That yields a rule for the whole debate. The test for a new name is not gentleness but accuracy. Once gentleness becomes the objective, the next thing through the door is underestimation.

If you take one thing from this table: the left column is entirely human perception, and the right column is entirely fact about tissue and process. Renaming operates only on the left. The right side stays exactly where it was, under any name.

NIFTP makes the split unusually visible. The 2016 change removed carcinoma from the name, and the evidence behind it was strong: 109 patients with the noninvasive form treated by lobectomy alone, without radioactive iodine, were all alive and disease-free at 10 to 26 years of follow-up, while the invasive form produced adverse events in 12 of 101 cases (12%). And yet the NIFTP diagnosis itself requires histological examination of the entire tumour capsule to exclude invasion. It is a name applied after removal and inspection. The word got gentler; the operation did not disappear.

5. The risk of dropping the label: what the DCIS debate shows

The 2023 evidence review by Ma and colleagues in Breast Cancer Research and Treatment (199(3):415-433) works through whether low-risk DCIS should lose the cancer label from both sides. This is where the article's counterweight sits.

5.1 The case for dropping it

  • Invasive cancer risk in low-risk DCIS is 6.5% at 7.5 years (95% CI 3.5 to 12.0)
  • Ten-year breast-cancer-specific mortality of 1.2 to 2.2%
  • Low-grade DCIS shows ten-year survival of 98.6 to 98.8% with no significant difference by whether surgery was performed
  • Autopsy studies put the mean prevalence of undetected in situ carcinoma at 8.9%, indicating lesions that never surface clinically within a lifetime
  • At population level, treating DCIS aggressively has not translated into falling rates of invasive or metastatic disease

5.2 The case against dropping it

  • Inter-pathologist agreement runs at kappa 0.23 to 0.78, mostly below 0.60
  • Accuracy of low-grade DCIS diagnosis against a consensus standard is 46% (95% CI 42 to 51) - the definition of low risk is itself unstable
  • Observation without treatment has produced progression: one series reported 3 of 17 (18%) low-grade DCIS cases diagnosed with invasive cancer at a median of 51 months, another 1 of 4 (25%)
  • A non-cancer label carries a real risk that patients drift out of scheduled surveillance
  • There is still no unified definition of low risk; the active trials are each using different criteria
  • Both clinicians and patients remain reluctant about active surveillance, which has slowed trial recruitment

The review accepts that there is evidence supporting reclassification, but attaches three preconditions: consensus on the definition of low risk, improved diagnostic reproducibility, and results from the trials in progress. Its own summary is blunt. Changing terminology may be part of the solution to overtreatment, but it cannot solve the problem on its own.

Where underestimation creeps in: soften the name all you like and 6.5% is still 6.5%. The instant a gentler label is read as licence to postpone imaging or skip surveillance, the change delivers the reverse of its purpose. Renaming exists to pull delayed care forward, not to lower the stakes. If care ends up later rather than earlier, that renaming has failed.

In November 2025 the European Heart Journal ran a CardioPulse piece by Habibi, Gatzoulis and Brida (2026;47(3)) arguing that heart failure deserves reconsideration as a term. It draws on focus groups and cognitive interviews with adult congenital heart disease patients, quoting statements such as hearing heart failure feeling like a death sentence, and the word failure making patients feel their own body had betrayed them.

The irony is geographic. The destination English-language cardiology is contemplating is where Korean and Japanese have been sitting for a century. Heart failure in those languages is heart insufficiency, not heart defeat. Which means the problem those languages have with the term runs in the opposite direction: not that it hits too hard, but that the characters are opaque enough that nothing lands at all - neither the meaning nor the seriousness.

7. Suffixes, false friends, and the ceiling on plain language

Medical suffixes carry different amounts of information, and knowing which is which lets you extract a little more from a diagnosis than the bare name gives you.

  • -itis: tells you the mechanism is inflammation (gastritis, arthritis). The most informative suffix in the set
  • -osis: a state or condition, with no claim about cause or course (osteoporosis, fibrosis)
  • -pathy: something is wrong with the organ, unspecified (neuropathy, cardiomyopathy)
  • -emia: describes what is in the blood and how much (anaemia, hyperkalaemia)
  • failure / insufficiency / dysfunction: degree of shortfall, with the degree itself left out of the name
English has its own trap, and it has nothing to do with stigma: a positive test result sounds like good news to most people, and negative sounds like bad news. The clinical meaning is usually the reverse. This is a pure transmission failure rather than a stigma problem, and it is fixed on the spot by asking whether positive here means the thing was found or not found.

Plain-language substitution has a ceiling, and terminology committees have documented it. Korea's medical dictionary, published in successive editions since 1977, settled on a compromise rather than wholesale replacement: leave familiar borrowed terms alone, and only replace the ones that are both hard to understand and genuinely clearer in everyday words.

The failures are recorded too. Terms proposed as plainer alternatives have simply not been adopted by the people who would have to say them. A replacement term does not survive because it is more accurate; it survives when both clinicians and patients use it.

That is also why the renamings that stuck, stuck. A society decided, the national medical association endorsed it, statutory language was amended, and hospital documentation and media followed. Break any link in that chain and the old name lives on in the corridor. Renaming is an institutional act, not a linguistic one.

8. What actually works in the room: structure, not vocabulary

The terminology argument misses something. What a patient needs walking out of a consultation is not the emotional register of the name but what happens next. A name is fixed once and reaches everyone identically; an explanation is tuned per person.

There are long-established tools for this half of the problem.

  • Ask Me 3 - developed by the US National Patient Safety Foundation and distributed by IHI. Three questions to ask as an appointment ends: what is my main problem, what do I need to do, and why is it important for me to do this
  • Teach-back - Tool 5 of the AHRQ Health Literacy Universal Precautions Toolkit. After explaining, the clinician asks the patient to say it back in their own words to check what actually transferred
  • The core of teach-back is that it never asks whether you understood. Almost everyone answers yes to that question

Explanations tend to succeed or fail at three specific points.

  • What the number means - not the value itself but which band it falls in
  • When the next step is - not the phrase we will monitor it, but which test in how many months
  • Whether there is a decision on the table right now, or none

The third is the one most often skipped. Believing a decision exists when it does not simply generates anxiety; believing none exists when one does means missing the window. Underestimation caused by a gentle-sounding name almost always enters here.

9. The shape of the questions worth asking

The right question depends on the situation, but the shapes are few. These are written out so they can be used as they stand.

9.1 Immediately after hearing the diagnosis

  • How exactly is this diagnosis written, including the abbreviation
  • Does the same condition go by any other name (searching under an old term returns a different picture)
  • Has this name changed recently

9.2 Severity and timing

  • Is there something I need to do right now, or is there not
  • When is the next check and what will you be looking at
  • What signs would tell me this is getting worse
  • If things stay as they are, what course does this usually take

9.3 Numbers and options

  • Where does this number sit relative to the range
  • At what value would the plan change
  • How many options are there right now
  • If watching is an option, what does watching actually consist of (it is not the same as doing nothing)
  • Is there a window during which this decision can wait
You can say what the name did to you: if a term made you feel reassured or frightened out of proportion, that reaction is clinically useful. This name makes it sound minor to me - is it? is a legitimate question, and so is the reverse.

10. What could not be verified

  • The European Heart Journal piece sits behind a subscription, so the exact replacement terms the authors propose could not be confirmed
  • No national follow-up statistics isolating the effect of the renaming itself on early presentation or diagnostic disclosure could be found for the Korean schizophrenia and epilepsy changes
  • Japan has related data, but from a single institution. A survey of psychiatric outpatient documentation at one university-affiliated site found the new schizophrenia term recorded in 70.5% of official documents before 2000 rising to 94.7% after 2003, and in 5.9% of informal documents rising to 40%. The authors themselves note that wider adoption of international classification systems, greater patient participation in treatment, and health welfare policy were also likely at work. It cannot be read as the effect of the renaming alone
  • No official decision to rename stigmatised disease terms in Chinese-language jurisdictions could be confirmed. Sources showing an academic term and a colloquial term coexisting are not the same as a renaming decision
  • Whether the individual papers containing the phrase in the opening section were subsequently retracted could not be confirmed

Conclusion: a name is a door, not the room

If the door is heavy, people do not come in. That was true of the old words for schizophrenia and epilepsy, and it was precisely the third reason Japan's ministry gave for replacing its term for dementia: an obstacle to early detection and early diagnosis. Doors are worth fixing.

But changing the door leaves the room untouched. The 6.5 percent seven-and-a-half-year invasive risk in low-risk DCIS is 6.5 percent under any label. NIFTP dropped carcinoma from its name while remaining a diagnosis that requires the whole capsule to be examined, which happens only after excision. And while inter-pathologist agreement wobbles below kappa 0.60, even the definition of low risk is still being argued.

Make gentleness the goal and you lose the plot. KDIGO went the other way, discarding end-stage in favour of failure - not because it was kinder but because it was truer.

For a reader, one practical thing survives all of this. Do not infer severity from how a diagnosis sounds. How it sounds is an artefact of translation, of the era it was coined in, and of the script it was coined in. It is not a property of the disease. Severity has to be asked for, the shapes of the questions are listed above, and the person who can answer them is not the committee that chose the name but the clinician holding your results.

To repeat: this article is for general information and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Confirm your diagnosis and what it means with your own clinician. The study figures above are group-level research results and do not predict any individual outcome. The fact that a term has been softened does not mean the condition has become less serious. Decisions about testing, surveillance and treatment belong to the clinician who knows your history and your results, and this article does not substitute for that judgement.
