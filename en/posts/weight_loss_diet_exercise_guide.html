Weight Loss: Less About What You Eat, More About Why You Cannot Keep It Up

The evidence that differences between diets are small, what exercise actually does, and why regain is not a failure of willpower

Jul 29, 2026 Health & Wellness

Introduction: why so much information produces such similar results

Few subjects generate as much information as weight loss. Yet most of that information answers the same question. What should I eat. Should I cut carbohydrates, should I cut fat, at what hour should I stop eating. The question is not a wrong one. The problem is that the answer is already in, and its practical effect is smaller than people assume.

Large randomized controlled trials that put diets directly against each other produce remarkably consistent results. In DIETFITS, which compared low carbohydrate against low fat over 12 months, the difference in weight loss between the two arms averaged 0.7kg and was not statistically significant. A network meta-analysis pooling 48 randomized trials and 7,286 participants found 12-month weight loss of 7.25kg for low carbohydrate and 7.27kg for low fat, effectively identical. In a trial that followed 811 people for two years, what predicted the amount of weight lost at two years was not macronutrient composition but the number of counseling sessions attended.

The direction these results point is clear. Diet choice is already largely a settled variable. The real remaining variable is why people cannot keep it up, and that is a question of environment and physiology rather than willpower.

This article reorganizes the subject around that view. The order is the limits of the popular arithmetic, the recommended rate of loss, what the head-to-head diet comparisons concluded, sustainability as the remaining variable, the trap in the scale reading, repositioning the role of exercise, the variables that protect muscle, the physiology of regain, maintenance strategy, where medication sits, and warning signs. Figures were taken wherever possible from institutional guidance and primary papers, and numbers that could not be verified were left out.

1. Is the 7,700 kcal per kilogram rule actually supported?

It is the first number to appear in almost any discussion of weight loss. Since 1kg of fat corresponds to about 7,700 kcal, eating 500 kcal less per day should remove 1kg in roughly two weeks. Start by checking where the number comes from.

The estimate is a metric conversion of the 3,500 kcal per pound rule proposed in 1958. The physical basis itself does exist, in that adipose tissue holds roughly that much energy. The trouble begins when it is carried over into a formula for predicting weight loss.

The review by Hall and Chow in the International Journal of Obesity identifies the central error as a failure to reflect the dynamic changes in energy balance. As weight falls the body gets smaller, a smaller body costs less to maintain, and the same movement burns less. The static rule ignores all of this and draws a straight line that keeps descending with no plateau. Real life does not produce that graph.

A commentary by Thomas and colleagues in the same journal checked the rule against measurements. Comparing the rule's predictions with the actual results of seven weight loss studies in which intake was controlled or objectively measured, the rule substantially overpredicted the loss.

Here is the magnitude in numbers. A modeling study by Hall and colleagues in the Lancet takes the example of cutting 2MJ (about 478 kcal) per day. Apply the static rule and it predicts 22kg lost in a straight line over the first year, which is about 100% larger than the dynamic model predicts. Close to half of it is an artifact of the arithmetic.

1.1 A usable estimate to put in its place

The same Lancet paper offers an alternative. A permanent change of 100 kJ (about 24 kcal) per day eventually produces a body weight change of roughly 1kg. In pound units it is expressed as 1 pound per 10 kcal per day. What matters is the time axis. Half of that change appears over about one year and 95% over about three years.

The US National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) makes the same point in its own explainer. The 3,500 kcal rule has been in use for a long time, but the body is not that simple, and because it responds in ways that offset the deficit when intake drops, actual weight loss falls well short of what the rule promises. The body weight planning tool the institute published was built to show not only the loss itself but what has to change permanently to hold that weight.

2. So how fast should the weight come off?

Official guidance converges on roughly one point. The UK National Health Service (NHS) states in its weight loss guidance that the target should be 1 to 2 pounds, that is 0.5 to 1kg, per week. On the intake side it presents cutting about 600 kcal per day as a safe and sustainable approach, and gives reference intakes for general adults of 2,500 kcal for men and 2,000 kcal for women.

Korean standards are worth reading alongside. The national health information portal run by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency defines obesity as a body mass index of 25 kg/m² or above and abdominal obesity as a waist circumference of 90cm or more in men and 85cm or more in women. The Korean Society for the Study of Obesity 2024 guideline sets the threshold for considering drug treatment at a body mass index of 25 or above, or 23 or above where a comorbidity is present, while stating that what comes before it remains diet, physical activity and behavioral intervention.

2.1 The realistic target of 5 to 10%

Setting the goal as a percentage rather than an absolute weight fits the evidence better. An analysis of Look AHEAD trial data reported that participants who lost 5% or more but less than 10% of their initial body weight were markedly more likely to achieve a 0.5% reduction in HbA1c, a 5mmHg reduction in systolic and diastolic blood pressure, a 5mg/dL increase in HDL cholesterol and a 40mg/dL reduction in triglycerides. The concluding sentence is that modest loss of 5% or more but less than 10% significantly improved cardiovascular risk factors, and larger losses were linked to larger benefits.

This number matters because it makes the size of the goal realistic. For someone weighing 80kg, 5% is 4kg. Believing that nothing under 20kg counts makes the burden heavy before the plan even starts, but knowing that 4kg is already a clinically meaningful point changes the character of the plan.

2.2 What happens when the loss is rushed

The risk of raising the pace is not an abstract caution. A cohort study that followed participants in a commercial weight loss program for one year compared a 500 kcal per day very low calorie diet with a 1,200 to 1,500 kcal per day low calorie diet. One-year loss was 11.1kg on the very low calorie diet and 8.1kg on the low calorie diet, 2.8kg more on the very low calorie arm. But the hazard ratio for symptomatic gallstones requiring hospital admission or gallbladder removal was 3.4, and for gallbladder removal itself 3.2.

So the price of 3kg extra was a threefold risk of a complication requiring surgery. On top of that come a higher proportion of lean mass lost, micronutrient deficiency, and deeper metabolic adaptation, which is covered later. A very low calorie diet is a therapeutic tool used in specific situations under medical supervision, not an approach an individual should choose at will.

3. What the head-to-head diet trials concluded

Now to the core of it. The answer to which diet is superior has to come from trials that put them directly against each other: randomly assigned, over the same period, under the same support conditions, rather than from observational studies or individual success stories.

3.1 DIETFITS: genotype did not split it either

This trial, published in JAMA in 2018, randomized 609 adults aged 18 to 50 without diabetes and with a body mass index of 28 to 40 to a low-fat or a low-carbohydrate arm and followed them for 12 months. The result was -5.3kg on low fat and -6.0kg on low carbohydrate, a between-group difference of 0.7kg with no statistical significance.

What makes this trial notable is that it tested the personalization hypothesis. It checked whether combinations of gene variants related to fat and carbohydrate metabolism, and insulin secretion measured 30 minutes after a glucose load, could identify which diet suits which person. Neither the diet by genotype interaction nor the diet by insulin secretion interaction appeared. The common belief that there is a diet matched to your constitution was tested head on, and in that trial it was not confirmed.

3.2 POUNDS LOST: what predicted the result was attendance, not composition

This trial, published in the NEJM in 2009, assigned 811 overweight adults to four diets differing in the ratio of fat, protein and carbohydrate and followed them for two years. At six months every arm had lost about 6kg on average, and at two years they converged around 3 to 4kg with no significant difference between arms.

The most frequently quoted sentence from this trial is not the conclusion but a secondary finding. Attending one more counseling session was associated with greater weight loss. In other words, how often participants were held to the plan separated the outcomes more than what they were assigned to eat.

3.3 The network meta-analysis: the gap between named diets

The network meta-analysis published in JAMA in 2014 pooled 48 randomized trials with 7,286 participants. At six months, weight loss was 8.73kg for low carbohydrate and 7.99kg for low fat, and by 12 months it was 7.25kg for low carbohydrate and 7.27kg for low fat, effectively identical. Differences between individual branded diets were smaller still: at six months the gap between two well-known programs was 1.71kg.

The authors' concluding sentence reads almost like a practice instruction. It supports the practice of recommending any diet the patient is able to adhere to.

3.4 Is the Mediterranean diet an exception?

The Mediterranean diet has strong evidence for cardiovascular outcomes, but the story changes when the outcome is the amount of weight lost alone. A systematic review of five randomized trials with 998 participants followed for 12 months or longer concluded that the Mediterranean diet produced more weight loss than a low-fat diet but showed no difference against a low-carbohydrate diet or the American Diabetes Association diet.

4. That leaves sustainability as the variable that matters

Invert the conclusion above and it reads like this. Most of the benefit available from diet choice has already been spent. What remains sits entirely in adherence. The claim that an ordinary diet held for a year beats a perfect diet held for three weeks is not a proverb but the direction the data from the trials above points.

But treating adherence as a matter of personal resolve fails for most people. The World Health Organization makes this explicit in its obesity fact sheet. Patterns of diet and physical activity are the result of environmental and social conditions that heavily constrain individual choice, so obesity is a societal responsibility more than a personal one, and the answer has to be found in creating supportive environments.

Translated to the level of the individual, this becomes the work of building structures that require less willpower. What follows takes the elements that actually produced results in the trials above and puts them in a form an individual can reproduce.

  • Design the contact frequency: what predicted loss in POUNDS LOST was attendance. Build recurring appointments such as a weekly weight entry or a monthly review
  • Reduce the number of decisions: deciding what to eat at every meal spends willpower three times a day. Fixing weekday breakfast and lunch removes those decisions
  • Change the defaults: whatever sits in plain sight gets eaten first. Rearranging the front shelf of the fridge and the top of the desk outlasts a resolution
  • Set the buffer rules in advance: days when the plan breaks, such as a work dinner or a trip, will certainly come. Write those days in as part of the rules rather than as exceptions
  • Do not discard the whole plan over a single failure: going over on one day and abandoning the plan are entirely different events
  • Keep the record format light: detailed logs are accurate but do not last. One photo or a single check mark is still there three months later
  • Choose the social context: who you eat with and where drives how much you eat. Start with the settings you can change
  • Watch sleep and schedule load together: adherence drops in weeks with short sleep and heavy workload. In those weeks lower the goal to maintenance

It is worth noticing that not one item on this list tells you which food to eat. That is the argument of this article.

5. The trap in the scale reading: water and glycogen

Many people lose 2 to 3kg within the first few days of a weight loss attempt. And they believe it is fat. Mostly it is not.

A classic discussion in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition explains this through the way glycogen is stored. Glycogen held in liver and muscle is stored together with water, and the literature describes three to four parts water accompanying each part of glycogen. Cut carbohydrate intake sharply and glycogen is consumed first, taking the attached water with it. Eat carbohydrate again and the same water comes back. That single mechanism explains both the sudden early drop on a low-carbohydrate diet and the jump on the morning after one large meal.

Other sources of variation stack on top. Every item below moves the number on the scale independently of body fat.

  • A change in sodium intake: the number rises the day after salty food through fluid retention
  • A change in carbohydrate intake: glycogen and its bound water move together
  • Bowel timing: the weight of gut contents changes in real time
  • The menstrual cycle: cyclical fluid retention is common
  • Immediately after resistance training: a temporary rise in muscle water and the inflammatory response overlap
  • Measurement conditions: time of day, clothing and whether it is before or after a meal can produce more than 1kg of difference on the same day

So a day-to-day change in weight carries almost no information. A reasonable read is a seven-day moving average measured under the same conditions, and what matters more is keeping indicators other than weight alongside it.

6. Repositioning exercise: a maintenance engine, not a weight loss engine

This is where misunderstanding runs deepest. Exercise is good for health and exercise makes you lose weight are different propositions, and the evidence behind them differs in strength.

A Cochrane systematic review examined 43 studies with 3,476 participants to summarize the weight loss effect of exercise. Added to a dietary intervention, exercise produced an average of 1.1kg of additional loss over diet alone, and raising the exercise intensity removed 1.5kg more. The direction is clear but the magnitude is not large.

The American College of Sports Medicine position stand gives more specific numbers. 150 to 250 minutes per week of moderate intensity activity is effective for preventing weight gain but produces little loss; clinically clear weight loss is observed above 250 minutes per week, and weight maintenance after loss also improves above 250 minutes per week. On resistance training it concludes that it does not increase the amount of weight lost but increases lean mass and assists fat loss.

Yet another finding reported in the same Cochrane review may matter more. Exercise improved cardiovascular risk factors even when weight did not fall. Improvements in diastolic blood pressure, triglycerides and fasting glucose were confirmed. In other words, a scale that does not move does not mean exercise had no effect. Judging exercise by weight loss alone throws away its most reliable benefit.

Setting exercise volume by a weight target means looking well above that, and the fact that most people find that volume hard to sustain over a long period is exactly why exercise is hard to use as a weight loss engine. 7. Why the idea that exercise earns extra food does not add up

Compensatory eating after exercise is common. The problem is that the calculation goes wrong at two points at once.

First, the extra energy spent does not all translate into extra total expenditure. An analysis of 1,754 adults published in Current Biology in 2021 reported that about 28% of the energy additionally spent on activity is offset by a fall in basal metabolism, so in practice only 72% shows up as an increase in that day's total expenditure. What comes next matters more. This compensation grows with body fat: the compensation rate rose from 29.7% at the 10th percentile of body mass index to 45.8% at the 90th percentile. In other words, the energy benefit of exercise is trimmed most for the people who need weight loss most.

Second, the deficit created by exercise is easily erased by a single meal. The energy burned in a 30-minute jog varies with body weight and pace, but in every case it lands somewhere near the size of one or two snacks. Multiply that by the compensation rate above and the effective deficit shrinks further. It is not hard for the amount eaten out of a sense of having earned it to exceed that deficit.

So the practical conclusions are these.

  • Do not use exercise as the basis for an eating budget: manage exercise volume and food volume as separate accounts
  • Judge exercise by fitness, blood pressure, blood glucose, mood and sleep: the effect appears far more reliably in those measures
  • During a loss phase, holding activity steady is itself an achievement: when intake drops, unconscious activity tends to drop with it
  • Treat the calories burned figure on a device as a rough reference only: individual variation and measurement error are large, and it does not account for compensation
  • The real dividend from exercise arrives in the maintenance phase: it contributes to slowing regain after loss

8. Two variables that protect muscle: protein and resistance training

Weight loss does not remove only body fat. Lean mass falls too, and the proportion varies with the rate of loss, the composition of the diet and whether you exercise. Protecting lean mass matters not because of appearance but because of function and metabolism.

Start with the baseline. The 2020 Dietary Reference Intakes for Koreans set the recommended protein intake at 0.91g per kg of body weight per day. But that value was derived to maintain nitrogen balance, that is to prevent deficiency, not to preserve lean mass during a loss phase. Miss that distinction and the number gets used incorrectly.

Research on the loss phase exists separately. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 20 randomized controlled trials in adults aged 50 and over reported that groups with higher protein intake preserved more lean mass and lost more body fat during caloric restriction. Because the intake levels set in individual trials differ, however, this article does not prescribe a single number that applies to everyone. The appropriate range varies with activity level, age and kidney function.

There are qualifications that must be attached here.

  • Protein intake may need to be restricted where kidney disease is present: in chronic kidney disease the prescription may run in the opposite direction, toward controlling intake, so this must be confirmed at a medical appointment
  • Liver disease, gout and other metabolic conditions also require individual judgment: general recommendations are not applied as written
  • A supplement is not a prerequisite: if total intake reaches the target, the form is secondary
  • Raising protein raises total calories as well: without cutting something else the deficit disappears

The second variable is resistance training. The ACSM position stand cited earlier concludes that resistance training does not increase the amount of weight lost but increases lean mass and assists fat loss. It is more accurate to view resistance training during a loss phase as a tool that changes the composition of the weight being lost rather than a tool that moves the scale faster. The details of protein intake are covered in The Complete Guide to Protein Supplements, and the evidence for individual supplement ingredients in the diet supplements comparison.

9. Weight regain is a physiological response

Weight returning after a loss is often handled as a moral failure. The evidence tells a different story.

9.1 Appetite hormones had not returned even a year later

A study published in the NEJM in 2011 tracked appetite-related hormones in participants who lost an average of 13.5kg on a low calorie diet. Immediately after the loss, leptin, peptide YY, cholecystokinin and insulin fell and ghrelin rose. That much is within expectation. The problem is what came next. One year after the loss, these measures and hunger scores had still not returned to pre-loss levels.

The authors' conclusion is explicit. After diet-induced loss, the appetite mediators that push toward weight regain do not return to their original levels even a year later. Someone who has lost weight is not resisting the same temptations in the same body as before; they are resisting them in a hungrier body.

9.2 Metabolic adaptation was still present six years later

A six-year follow-up study published in Obesity in 2016 looked at participants in an extreme weight loss program. They lost an average of 58.3kg over 30 weeks, and at that point their resting metabolic rate was 610 kcal per day lower. Six years later they had regained an average of 41.0kg of the loss, and even at that point, with much of the weight back, resting metabolic rate was still 704 kcal per day lower. The metabolic adaptation component, after subtracting the share explained by body size, was 499 kcal per day.

This study involved extreme conditions and generalizing from it calls for care, but the direction matches the hormone study above. The body adjusts appetite and expenditure at the same time in the direction of restoring the lost weight. Calling a failure to maintain under those two forces a lack of willpower is not an explanation but a label.

9.3 That does not mean maintenance is impossible

Evidence in the opposite direction is equally clear. A long-term lifestyle intervention trial in 5,145 overweight and obese adults with type 2 diabetes reported results at eight years. The intensive lifestyle intervention arm was holding 4.7% of initial body weight and the control arm 2.1%, and the proportion maintaining a loss of 5% or more was 50.3%, with 26.9% at 10% or more.

So half of them were still holding a clinically meaningful loss eight years on. What made the difference was the continuation of the intervention. Accepting regain as a physiological phenomenon is not grounds for giving up but grounds for a design principle, that resources have to be allocated to maintenance.

10. Design the maintenance strategy from the start

Most weight loss plans end at the point the target weight is reached. Accept the evidence above and that point is not the end but the start of the hardest stretch. So a maintenance strategy should not be built after the loss is finished; it should be built at the beginning, alongside the plan.

  1. Set a transition date, not an end date: write down in advance the date you move from the loss phase to the maintenance phase and what changes that day
  2. Calculate the maintenance-phase intake in advance: a smaller body needs fewer calories to maintain. Return to the pre-loss diet and regain is the scheduled outcome
  3. Set a regain threshold as a number: deciding in advance how many kilograms above your lowest weight triggers renewed intervention makes the response faster
  4. Make activity the key indicator of the maintenance phase: the evidence says exercise contributes more to maintenance than to loss
  5. Do not stop recording weight: a lapse in recording often arrives together with the early signs of regain
  6. Keep the lean mass protection elements: resistance training and protein intake should not be the first things dropped in the maintenance phase
  7. Treat short sleep as seriously as a failed diet: it affects both appetite regulation and activity level
  8. Leave the support structure in place through the maintenance phase: in the long-term follow-up trial what made the difference was the continuation of the intervention
  9. Do not aim for a complete return to where you were: holding 5% is a clinically meaningful result
  10. Do not read regain as a personal defect: change the interpretation and the time it takes to restart gets shorter

The key point is that half of this list has nothing to do with food. Problems in the maintenance phase usually arise from structure rather than knowledge.

11. Medication and procedures: the domain of a prescription

Over the last few years it has become difficult to discuss weight without this topic. But this article does not cover administration or dosing. It is not a domain for individual judgment. What is set out here is only what is approved in Korea and for whom and where it sits in the sequence.

The Korean Society for the Study of Obesity 2024 guideline lists the drugs used for chronic weight management in Korea as orlistat, naltrexone and bupropion extended release, phentermine and topiramate extended release, liraglutide 3.0mg, semaglutide 2.4mg and tirzepatide. The threshold for considering drug treatment is given as a body mass index of 25 or above, or 23 or above where an obesity-related comorbidity is present. The same guideline states that lifestyle intervention including diet, physical activity and behavioral therapy is the foundation of obesity management and drug treatment is an adjunct to it.

Approved indications for individual products can be checked in the product information registered with the Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety. For semaglutide 2.4mg products the adult weight management indication is defined as obese patients with an initial body mass index of 30 or above, or overweight patients with an initial body mass index of 27 or above and below 30 who have at least one weight-related comorbidity. There is a separate indication for reducing the risk of major cardiovascular events in overweight or obese patients with established cardiovascular disease and an initial body mass index of 27 or above, and separate conditions are set for adolescents aged 12 and over. Tirzepatide products carry the same structure of criteria for chronic weight management, 30 or above or 27 or above with a comorbidity, with an additional indication related to moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea.

The parts that must be read alongside this paragraph are the following.

  • All of these are prescription-only medicines: obtaining them without a prescription or using another person's medication creates problems on both the safety and the legal side
  • Contraindications and adverse effects exist: the product information specifies who must not receive the drug and which situations call for caution, and that judgment belongs to a clinician
  • Their position as an adjunct has not changed: the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency has also stated recently that obesity medication alone is not sufficient and that lifestyle management including diet control and exercise is needed
  • What happens after stopping is a separate problem: weight change after discontinuation is not unrelated to the physiological mechanisms described above

Mechanisms of action, trial results and the details of adverse effects are set out separately in the complete guide to GLP-1 weight loss drugs, so they are not repeated here.

12. Signs to stop here and see a clinician

Weight loss is a goal most adults can attempt safely, but that is not true of everyone. And in some cases the attempt itself becomes the entrance to a different problem.

12.1 When weight loss is not recommended or requires medical supervision

  • During pregnancy: the UK National Health Service states explicitly that you should not try to lose weight during pregnancy, advising that it does not lower the chance of complications and may not be safe. It recommends a balanced diet, everyday physical activity and regular antenatal care instead
  • While breastfeeding: nutritional requirements differ, so individual counseling is needed
  • Children and adolescents who are still growing: adult weight loss methods are not applied as written. Specialist assessment that accounts for growth and development is required
  • Taking medication for diabetes, high blood pressure or similar conditions: when intake and weight change, drug effect can change too, and a dose adjustment may be needed
  • Kidney or liver disease: recommendations on protein and electrolytes differ from the general case
  • A history of an eating disorder: calorie counting and restriction can themselves trigger a relapse
  • Older adults: the risk of muscle loss and reduced bone density has to be assessed at the same time
  • Unintentional weight loss: if weight fell without any effort, identifying the cause comes before any weight loss plan

12.2 Warning signs of an eating disorder

Materials from the Korean National Center for Mental Health describe anorexia nervosa as the psychiatric condition with the highest mortality and list physical complications including loss of menstruation, arrhythmia, reduced bone density and multi-organ damage. The national mental health information portal gives the diagnostic requirement for bulimia nervosa as a pattern in which binge eating and inappropriate compensatory behavior occur at least once a week over three months, and advises that without treatment the risk of the condition becoming chronic is high, so early diagnosis and treatment matter.

A screening tool in wide clinical use is the five-item SCOFF questionnaire. Introduced in the BMJ in 1999, it is structured so that two or more positive items prompt further assessment. What matters is that it is a screening tool for connecting people to care, not a self-diagnosis instrument. If the signs below recur, it is safer to seek an assessment before continuing a weight loss plan.

  • Eating large amounts quickly with a loss of control, repeatedly, even when not hungry
  • Inducing vomiting after eating, or using laxatives or diuretics
  • Fasting or exercising excessively to prevent weight gain
  • Feeling fat despite being underweight
  • Hiding eating, and feeling severe guilt or self-loathing afterward
  • Thoughts about weight and body shape taking up so much time that they interfere with daily functioning
  • Menstruation stopping, or physical symptoms such as dizziness, fainting or arrhythmia

14. Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

Q. Does eating 7,700 kcal less really remove 1kg?

The physical basis exists in the energy density of adipose tissue, but using it as a formula to predict weight loss goes wrong. The rule fails to reflect metabolic adaptation and so overpredicts actual loss. The alternative estimate is that a permanent change of 100 kJ (about 24 kcal) per day ends in about 1kg, with half of it appearing over about one year and 95% over about three years.

Q. Which takes off more weight, low carbohydrate or low fat?

In head-to-head trials the difference is small. The network meta-analysis result at 12 months was 7.25kg for low carbohydrate and 7.27kg for low fat. Changing the question to which approach will I still be following six months from now helps the outcome more.

Q. Does eating late at night make you gain more?

The axis tested by the head-to-head trials covered here is macronutrient composition, not meal timing. That said, it is worth noting in practice that late eating often comes together with higher total intake or poorer sleep quality. Rather than making the clock itself the rule, look first at total amount and sleep.

Q. I have hit a plateau. Should I eat even less?

A plateau is a normal event predicted by the dynamic model. Cutting intake sharply at that point raises gallstone risk, lean mass loss and metabolic adaptation together. The first things to look at are the accuracy of your records, activity level and sleep, and switching the goal itself to maintenance is also a valid choice.

Q. My weight went up after I started strength training. Why?

Post-exercise water shifts and an increase in lean mass work together. Resistance training is a tool that changes the composition of the weight being lost, not a tool that increases the amount. Watching waist circumference and performance instead of weight makes the judgment easier.

Q. If I take medication, can I skip the lifestyle changes?

That is not where the guidelines place it. The Korean Society for the Study of Obesity 2024 guideline states that lifestyle intervention is the foundation and medication is an adjunct. Korean public health materials likewise indicate that medication alone is not sufficient. The indications and contraindications of any drug must be confirmed at a medical appointment.

Conclusion: change the environment, not the plan

Here are the points repeated through this article, in five lines.

  1. The widely used calorie arithmetic promises faster loss than actually happens. Slowing down is not failure but a scheduled event.
  2. Differences between diets were small in head-to-head trials and grew smaller with time. So the variable that remains is sustainability.
  3. Short-term movement on the scale is mostly unrelated to fat. Watch waist circumference, fitness and blood measures alongside it.
  4. Exercise is a maintenance engine more than a weight loss engine. Even so, health measures improve when weight does not move.
  5. Regain is a physiological response produced jointly by metabolic adaptation and appetite hormones. That is why the maintenance strategy has to be designed at the start.

One last point of perspective. The World Health Organization frames obesity as a societal responsibility more than a personal one and looks for the answer in creating supportive environments, not because individual resolve is meaningless but because the environment was not designed for resolve to carry alone. Translated to the individual level, that sentence becomes the work of building a structure that keeps running on less willpower.

And this article can only draw a map; it cannot examine your body. If you have an existing condition or take medication, or if any of the warning signs above apply to you, checking at a medical appointment before building a plan is the fastest route.

Medical disclaimer
This article is provided for general health information only and does not replace medical diagnosis, prescription or treatment. The figures and guidance cited are current as of July 2026, may be revised, and apply differently depending on individual circumstances. Starting, changing or stopping a weight loss plan, a nutritional restriction or a medication must be discussed with a physician or pharmacist. Do not stop a prescribed treatment on your own based on information found online. Recommendations differ for pregnant and breastfeeding people, growing children and adolescents, older adults, people with kidney or liver disease, people taking medication for diabetes or high blood pressure, and people with a history of an eating disorder, so seek specialist care.
