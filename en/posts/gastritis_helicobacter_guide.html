Complete Guide to Gastritis and H. pylori — Symptoms, Causes, Tests, Treatment, Prevention

Everything you need to know about gastritis and Helicobacter pylori — Updated June 2026

⚠️ Health information notice — This article is for general information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. Consult a clinician if you have symptoms or need a decision.

Introduction: When Stomach Troubles May Have a Bacterial Cause

Many people dismiss persistent heartburn or bloating as stress or dietary habits without ever investigating further. Yet a significant proportion of stomach discomfort traces back to Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori), a bacterium that colonizes the stomach lining. The World Health Organization's International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classifies H. pylori as a Group 1 carcinogen and identifies it as the leading environmental risk factor for gastric cancer.

Globally, roughly half the world's population carries H. pylori, with particularly high prevalence in East Asia and developing regions. This guide — updated as of June 2026 — covers the types and causes of gastritis, how H. pylori infects and harms the stomach, how it is diagnosed, current eradication treatment options, dietary management, and the evidence linking eradication to gastric cancer prevention.

This article is intended for general health information only and does not constitute medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment. If you have symptoms or are considering treatment, please consult a board-certified gastroenterologist.

1. What Is Gastritis — Acute vs. Chronic

Gastritis refers to inflammation of the stomach lining (mucosa). It is broadly divided into acute gastritis and chronic gastritis based on onset and duration.

1.1 Acute Gastritis

Acute gastritis develops suddenly, often triggered by:

  • NSAIDs and aspirin: These drugs inhibit prostaglandin synthesis, impairing the stomach's protective mucus barrier.
  • Excessive alcohol: Alcohol directly irritates the mucosa and increases acid secretion.
  • Severe physiological stress: Major surgery, trauma, burns, or critical illness can cause stress-related mucosal injury.
  • H. pylori infection: Initial H. pylori colonization can trigger an acute inflammatory response.

Symptoms typically include sudden upper abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and loss of appetite. Acute gastritis often resolves within days to weeks once the cause is removed.

1.2 Chronic Gastritis

Chronic gastritis involves persistent, low-grade inflammation of the stomach lining over months or years. H. pylori infection is by far the most common cause, accounting for 70–80% of chronic gastritis cases. Other causes include autoimmune gastritis (affecting the stomach body), bile reflux, and prolonged NSAID use.

Left untreated, chronic gastritis can progress along a well-described pathological sequence known as the Correa cascade: chronic gastritis → atrophic gastritis → intestinal metaplasia → dysplasia → gastric cancer. H. pylori drives the early stages of this cascade.

2. H. pylori — How It Infects and Spreads

H. pylori is a spiral-shaped, gram-negative bacterium uniquely adapted to survive in the highly acidic stomach environment. It produces the enzyme urease, which converts urea into ammonia and carbon dioxide, locally neutralizing stomach acid around the bacterium.

2.1 Transmission Routes

H. pylori spreads primarily through oral-oral or fecal-oral routes. Infection usually occurs in childhood, often within the family unit, and persists indefinitely without treatment. Key transmission scenarios include:

  • Sharing utensils or eating from communal dishes
  • Drinking contaminated water or consuming inadequately cooked food
  • Environments with poor sanitation and hand hygiene

2.2 H. pylori and Stomach Disease

Most infected individuals remain asymptomatic throughout life. However, in a subset, infection can lead to:

  • Peptic ulcers (gastric and duodenal): Develop in approximately 10–15% of infected individuals
  • Chronic gastritis → atrophic gastritis → intestinal metaplasia: Pre-cancerous changes in the stomach lining
  • Gastric adenocarcinoma: H. pylori is the most important environmental driver
  • Gastric MALT lymphoma: A rare but H. pylori-associated stomach lymphoma

3. Symptoms of Gastritis and H. pylori Infection

H. pylori infection itself is often silent. Symptoms, when present, are usually associated with gastritis or ulcer disease.

3.1 Common Symptoms

  • Heartburn / burning epigastric pain: A gnawing or burning sensation in the upper abdomen, especially when the stomach is empty or after meals.
  • Bloating and indigestion: Post-meal discomfort, early satiety, and a sense of fullness.
  • Nausea: Often worse on an empty stomach.
  • Loss of appetite: Anticipatory avoidance of eating due to pain.
  • Belching and flatulence: Excessive gas may accompany dyspepsia.

3.2 Warning Signs — Seek Prompt Medical Attention

The following symptoms warrant immediate medical evaluation:

  • Black, tarry stools (melena) or blood in stool — signs of gastrointestinal bleeding
  • Vomiting blood or coffee-ground material
  • Unintentional significant weight loss
  • Difficulty swallowing (dysphagia)
  • Severe, persistent abdominal pain

4. Diagnostic Tests — How H. pylori Is Detected

H. pylori can be diagnosed through non-invasive and invasive methods, each with distinct advantages.

4.1 Urea Breath Test (UBT)

The UBT is the gold-standard non-invasive test, with sensitivity and specificity both exceeding 95%. The procedure:

  1. Swallow a capsule containing ¹³C-labeled urea.
  2. If H. pylori is present, its urease breaks down the urea, releasing ¹³CO₂ into the bloodstream and then exhaled breath.
  3. Exhaled air is collected 30 minutes later and analyzed for ¹³CO₂ concentration.
  4. A positive result indicates active H. pylori infection; a negative result indicates no infection (or successful eradication).

For accurate results, antibiotics must be stopped at least 4 weeks before, and PPIs at least 2 weeks before the test.

4.2 Upper Endoscopy with Biopsy

Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (EGD) allows direct visualization of the stomach lining and tissue sampling. Biopsy-based tests include:

  • Rapid urease test (CLO test): Quick detection of H. pylori urease in biopsy tissue
  • Histological examination: Microscopic evaluation of bacterial presence and mucosal status (atrophy, intestinal metaplasia)
  • Culture and susceptibility testing: Useful for identifying antibiotic resistance, though time-consuming

Endoscopy simultaneously evaluates for ulcers, cancer, and other structural abnormalities, making it especially valuable in symptomatic or high-risk patients.

4.3 Serology (Blood Antibody Test)

Blood tests detecting H. pylori IgG antibodies are simple and inexpensive but cannot distinguish current from past infection, and are not suitable for confirming eradication success.

5. H. pylori Eradication Treatment

H. pylori treatment requires combination antibiotic regimens because single antibiotics fail against the bacterium in the acidic gastric environment, and combination therapy reduces resistance development.

5.1 Standard Triple Therapy (First-Line)

The most widely used first-line regimen consists of:

  • Proton pump inhibitor (PPI): Suppresses gastric acid, making antibiotics more effective (e.g., omeprazole, esomeprazole, pantoprazole)
  • Amoxicillin — penicillin-class antibiotic
  • Clarithromycin — macrolide antibiotic

Taken together for 7–14 days, with 14-day courses generally achieving higher eradication rates (approximately 70–80% with standard triple therapy).

5.2 Alternative Regimens for Resistant Cases

Rising clarithromycin resistance has prompted wider use of alternative regimens:

  • Bismuth quadruple therapy: PPI + bismuth salts + metronidazole + tetracycline for 10–14 days. Achieves eradication rates of 85–90%+ and is recommended by the 2024 ACG guidelines as an alternative first-line option.
  • Vonoprazan-based therapy: Vonoprazan (a potassium-competitive acid blocker) combined with antibiotics; highlighted in the 2024 ACG guidelines for superior acid suppression compared to traditional PPIs.
  • Rifabutin-based triple therapy: Effective salvage regimen for multiply resistant infections; included in the 2024 ACG update.

5.3 Confirming Eradication

A test of cure — ideally UBT — should be performed at least 4 weeks after completing treatment to confirm eradication. If first-line therapy fails, a second-line salvage regimen is selected based on antibiotic susceptibility patterns.

6. Eradication and Gastric Cancer Prevention

The connection between H. pylori eradication and gastric cancer prevention is one of the most important findings in gastrointestinal oncology.

  • A 2025 meta-analysis of 8 randomized controlled trials (58,628 participants) found the relative risk of gastric cancer in the eradication group was 0.64 — a 36% reduction compared to no treatment.
  • In patients with intestinal metaplasia or dysplasia, eradication reduced gastric cancer relative risk by approximately 45% according to a separate 2025 meta-analysis.
  • A large-scale population study of over 900,000 individuals demonstrated significantly lower gastric cancer incidence and mortality in H. pylori-treated individuals.
  • In 2025, the IARC Working Group reaffirmed H. pylori eradication as a globally actionable and cost-effective primary prevention strategy for gastric cancer.

Importantly, eradication does not eliminate cancer risk entirely, especially in patients who already have advanced pre-cancerous changes. Regular endoscopic surveillance remains essential for high-risk individuals.

7. Diet and Lifestyle Management

7.1 Dietary Principles for Gastric Health

  • Eat regular meals: Irregular meal timing disrupts acid secretion patterns and increases mucosal irritation when the stomach is empty.
  • Avoid overeating: Large meals distend the stomach and increase acid production.
  • Limit spicy and salty foods: Excessive capsaicin and salt can directly irritate the gastric mucosa.
  • Moderate alcohol consumption: Alcohol weakens the stomach's protective mucosal barrier. Avoid drinking on an empty stomach.
  • Manage caffeine: Coffee and strong teas can stimulate acid secretion; consider reducing intake during symptomatic periods.
  • Avoid carbonated beverages: These can worsen bloating and reflux symptoms.

7.2 Quit Smoking

Smoking impairs gastric mucosal defense, delays ulcer healing, and is associated with lower H. pylori eradication success rates. Smoking cessation is strongly advisable for anyone with gastritis or H. pylori infection.

7.3 Caution with NSAIDs

If long-term NSAID use is necessary (e.g., for arthritis or cardiovascular conditions), ask your doctor about co-prescribing a gastroprotective agent such as a PPI to reduce stomach injury risk.

7.4 Stress Management

Psychological stress increases acid secretion and impairs gastric motility. Regular physical activity, adequate sleep, and relaxation techniques (mindfulness, deep breathing) can help manage stress-related digestive symptoms over time.

8. Prevention of Reinfection and Screening

8.1 Preventing Reinfection

Annual reinfection rates in adults after successful eradication are generally 1–3%. To minimize reinfection:

  • Use individual utensils and serving plates when sharing food
  • Wash hands thoroughly before meals and after using the restroom
  • Drink clean, treated water
  • Have household contacts tested if one family member is infected

8.2 Gastric Cancer Screening Recommendations

In Korea, the national cancer screening program recommends gastroscopy or upper GI series every 2 years from age 40. Individual risk factors may warrant earlier or more frequent screening:

  • Atrophic gastritis or intestinal metaplasia: Annual or biennial endoscopy surveillance
  • After gastric ulcer treatment: Follow-up endoscopy 4–6 weeks later to confirm healing
  • Family history of gastric cancer: Consult your doctor for personalized earlier screening
  • After H. pylori eradication: UBT 4–8 weeks post-treatment to confirm cure

9. Frequently Asked Questions

Q. If I test positive for H. pylori but have no symptoms, do I still need treatment?

Current guidelines recommend eradication for asymptomatic H. pylori-positive individuals who have risk factors such as a family history of gastric cancer or pre-cancerous mucosal changes. For those without any risk factors, the decision is individualized. Discuss your specific situation with a gastroenterologist.

Q. What happens if I stop antibiotics early during H. pylori treatment?

Stopping antibiotics prematurely significantly increases the risk of treatment failure and antibiotic resistance. If you experience side effects, contact your doctor rather than discontinuing on your own. Your doctor can adjust the regimen or offer supportive measures to help you complete the course.

Q. How long does it take for gastritis symptoms to improve after treatment?

Symptomatic improvement varies. Some patients notice relief within days of starting acid suppression therapy, while full mucosal healing may take 4–8 weeks or longer. Successful H. pylori eradication typically leads to gradual improvement in chronic gastritis over months. Lifestyle modifications often accelerate recovery.

Conclusion: Early Detection and Consistent Management

Gastritis and H. pylori infection are among the most prevalent gastrointestinal conditions worldwide, yet they are highly manageable with proper diagnosis and treatment. Key takeaways:

  • Persistent heartburn, indigestion, or upper abdominal pain should not be ignored — consult a gastroenterologist.
  • H. pylori can be detected non-invasively with the urea breath test (sensitivity and specificity over 95%).
  • Eradication therapy with combination antibiotics is effective and has strong evidence for reducing gastric cancer risk.
  • Lifestyle changes — regular meals, limiting alcohol, quitting smoking, and stress management — support long-term gastric health.
  • Adhere to recommended gastric cancer screening schedules, especially if you are at increased risk.
