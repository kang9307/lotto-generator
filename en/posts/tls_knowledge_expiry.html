Why What You Learned About SSL Is Now Wrong - A Record of the Name, the Protocol and Certificate Lifetimes Changing

The name changed. The protocol changed. The lifetime changed.

Aug 23, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: A Field Where Yesterday's Right Answer Becomes Today's Wrong One

Anyone who has ever put a certificate on a server carries a hardened procedure somewhere in memory. Generate a private key, generate a signing request, concatenate the issued file with the intermediate, write a long list of cipher suites into the configuration, and put a renewal reminder on the calendar one year out. That procedure was once exactly right. Today at least three of those five steps are stale.

Stale does not mean less good. It means you can no longer do it that way. The cipher suite list in the configuration uses a syntax the current protocol does not share, the one-year cadence on the calendar is a value the rules no longer permit, and the revocation feature you were told to enable was shut down outright by the largest issuer.

What is interesting is how quietly this happened. New protocols get announced loudly, but nobody tells you the date your knowledge stopped being true. This article walks back through those dates. What was deprecated when, what becomes mandatory when, and what judgment is left over, with document numbers and effective dates attached.

Why Knowledge in This Field Goes Stale So Fast

Three forces overlap. First, security knowledge is asymmetric. New capabilities spread slowly, but a single flaw invalidates the whole picture at once. So the documents in this field are updated through prohibitions rather than additions. Second, there are two authorities. The protocol is set by the IETF as RFCs; certificate issuance rules are set by the CA/Browser Forum, where browsers and certificate authorities meet, as Baseline Requirements. They move on separate clocks, so you have to watch both. Third, browsers are the real enforcers. Whatever a document says, if Chrome and Safari drop the connection, that becomes the operative standard.

What this article does not cover. No configuration snippets. They differ per environment and, more to the point, they are the fastest-rotting part of a piece like this. If you want to walk through issuance by hand, Let's Encrypt Free SSL Certificate Complete Setup Guide is the right piece, and the politics of who gets into a trust store are covered in Korea's GPKI Root Certificate and the Firefox Root Store. What is in scope here is the way this knowledge expires.

The Name Went First - SSL Is Deprecated and What We Run Is TLS

The first thing to expire was not a protocol but a name. Not one protocol carrying the name SSL is still running. This is not a matter of taste or convention; it is written down.

SSL 2.0 was prohibited by RFC 6176 in 2011. SSL 3.0 was deprecated by RFC 7568 in 2015. Their successors did not last much longer: TLS 1.0 was defined by RFC 2246 in 1999 and TLS 1.1 by RFC 4346 in 2006, and RFC 8996, published in March 2021, nailed both down with the sentences «TLS 1.0 MUST NOT be used» and «TLS 1.1 MUST NOT be used», moving the defining documents RFC 2246 and RFC 4346 to Historic status. That document is part of BCP 195, and it also did the work of rewriting the minimum-version requirements of more than seventy other RFCs that referenced the old versions, replacing them with TLS 1.2.

So what is alive is TLS 1.2 and TLS 1.3, and nothing else. And one of those two, TLS 1.2, was defined by RFC 5246, which RFC 8446 marked obsolete. Alive in deployment, but with its defining document already handed to the next generation.

The industry keeps saying «SSL certificate». It is in product names and it dominates search. There is no reason to fight the shorthand itself. The problem is that the knowledge attached to the name comes along with it. Configuration habits learned from SSL-era documents, the renewal cadence of that period, the revocation checking that was valid then — all of it rides in on the name. The name can survive as an idiom, but every procedure trailing behind it has to be inspected one at a time.

What TLS 1.3 Changed - Fewer Round Trips, Fewer Choices

RFC 8446, published in August 2018, is not an incremental improvement on TLS 1.2. The document itself devotes a section to major differences and enumerates what was taken out, which tells you the scale. Split it three ways and the structure comes clear.

Round Trips Went Down

In TLS 1.2 a full handshake required two round trips before application data could flow. TLS 1.3 cut that to one, and defined a zero round trip mode that lets a client send data in its first flight to a peer it has talked to before. One fewer round trip means one round trip less latency, and the difference is felt most where round trips are expensive, such as mobile networks.

On top of that, every handshake message after ServerHello travels encrypted. A substantial part of the negotiation that used to be visible in the clear is simply no longer observable.

The Menu Got Shorter

This is the more consequential change. RFC 8446 removed weak or legacy options from the negotiable set entirely. Restricting the list to what the document itself states:

  • Static RSA and static Diffie-Hellman key exchange are gone. In the document's own words, «all public-key based key exchange mechanisms now provide forward secrecy». The property that a later leak of the server private key cannot retroactively decrypt past sessions stopped being an option and became structural
  • Compression is gone. There is no compression at the TLS layer
  • Custom ephemeral Diffie-Hellman groups and DSA signatures are gone. A server cannot supply arbitrary parameters; it picks from defined groups
  • The symmetric algorithm list was pruned. Everything classified as legacy was removed, and what remains is entirely AEAD. Old combinations such as CBC mode or RC4 do not exist among TLS 1.3's choices

A recurring confusion lives here: questions of the form «how do I disable CBC in TLS 1.3». There is nothing to disable. It was never on the menu. If you also expose TLS 1.2, the question is still valid over there, but that is a consequence of choosing to expose TLS 1.2, not a property of TLS 1.3.

The Meaning of a Cipher Suite Changed

The strings in old configurations looked like TLS_ECDHE_RSA_WITH_AES_128_GCM_SHA256. One line carries the key exchange, the authentication, the symmetric cipher and the hash. Combinations exploded, and picking the safe ones and ordering them was the operator's job.

RFC 8446 split that concept apart. In the document's phrasing, «the cipher suite concept has been changed to separate the authentication and key exchange mechanisms from the record protection algorithm». As a result, a TLS 1.3 suite name such as TLS_AES_128_GCM_SHA256 carries only the symmetric cipher and the hash. Key exchange and signature methods are negotiated through separate extensions.

The practical meaning is singular: the long suite list you copied out of an old document does not apply to TLS 1.3. The format is different, and there are only five to choose from anyway. Maintaining a long list is labor that survives only as long as you keep TLS 1.2 open, and even there, following a standard recommendation beats hand-picking. RFC 9325, from November 2022, offers four ECDHE-based AEAD suites as its recommended set for TLS 1.2. Anchoring on those four rots slower than enumerating twenty by hand.

Certificate Lifetimes Have Only Ever Moved One Direction

Separately from the protocol, one number has moved steadily in a single direction for the past decade: maximum certificate validity. That value is set not by the IETF but by the CA/Browser Forum's Baseline Requirements. And no table invalidates «what you learned back then» more precisely than this one.

The sequence runs like this. A maximum of 825 days applied from 1 March 2018. The next step arrived not through consensus among certificate authorities but through a unilateral browser decision. Apple announced that for TLS server certificates issued from roots preinstalled in its operating systems, anything issued on or after 1 September 2020 at 00:00 GMT must not have a validity period greater than 398 days. The notice added that connections to servers violating the requirement will fail. That episode shows where power sits in this field: the enforcer is whoever holds the trust store, not whoever holds the specification.

Then, on 11 April 2025, the CA/Browser Forum passed SC-081v3. Certificate issuers voted 25 in favor with none against, browsers 4 in favor with none against, and the intellectual property review closed on 13 May. What the ballot set was not a single number but a schedule. The effective dates as they now stand in the Baseline Requirements:

What 47 Days Actually Means

Read as numbers it just looks shorter, but the table carries an instruction to change how you operate. Doing the arithmetic makes it plain.

  • In the 398-day era renewal was once a year. That is a cadence a person can hold on a calendar
  • At 200 days it is twice a year. Still survivable
  • At 100 days it is four times a year. One handover of responsibility and somebody misses one
  • At 47 days it is close to eight times a year, and since you cannot renew a 47-day certificate at the last moment, the real cadence is shorter still

The right-hand column matters more. Validation data reuse drops to ten days in 2029. That means it is not enough to fetch a fresh certificate. The fact that you control the domain has to be proven again every ten days. No workflow where a human logs into a console and clicks a confirmation link can physically sustain that.

So what this table says is not «renew more often». It says «take the human out of this loop». Automated renewal moved from a convenience to a precondition.

Old Advice Against Current Advice

Knowledge in this field expires in a consistent way: the advice itself was not wrong, but the premise it stood on disappeared. Below is how a set of once-standard recommendations is treated now.

Where OCSP Used to Be - How Deprecation Actually Proceeds

The OCSP row above deserves its own look, because the full lifecycle of a feature actually disappearing is visible in it.

Sometimes a certificate has to be invalidated mid-life, because a private key leaked or the certificate was mis-issued. The problem is how a browser learns that. The Online Certificate Status Protocol was the answer: the browser asks the certificate authority whether a given certificate is still valid. That structure carries a defect on its face. The certificate authority learns your browsing. Which IP is trying to reach which site is exposed by the query itself.

Let's Encrypt named that privacy problem as its reason for ending the service. The shutdown ran in stages, and the notice published on 6 August 2025 summarizes the outcome: OCSP URLs had been absent from newly issued certificates for more than ninety days, so every certificate that contained one had expired, and revocation information would be published exclusively via CRLs from then on. The same notice records the scale — at peak, roughly 340 billion OCSP requests per month.

The browser side had already moved the same way. Chromium's documentation on CRLSets states that «online (i.e. OCSP and CRL) checks are not generally performed by Chrome». Revocation data is instead gathered from CA-published CRLs and shipped to the browser in advance. Enterprises can turn checking on by policy, but that is an exception, not the default.

The rules pointed the same direction. The title of the ballot the CA/Browser Forum passed in 2023 was itself «Make OCSP Optional, Require CRLs, and Incentivize Automation».

What to take from this is not OCSP's fate but the shape of deprecation. Nothing switches off one morning. The rules demote a feature to optional, a large operator announces a shutdown schedule, and the browsers reveal they stopped using it long ago. Meanwhile, somewhere, configuration files are still enabling it. Stale knowledge does not raise an error. It simply does nothing.

After ACME - When Automation Is the Default, Outages Change Shape

The counterpart to shrinking lifetimes is automation. ACME was standardized as RFC 8555 in March 2019. What it does is simple: it turns proving control of a domain and obtaining a certificate into JSON messages exchanged between machines with no human in the loop. Control is proven either by placing an HTTP resource (http-01) or a DNS record (dns-01).

The point to register is that this is not a story about convenience. The 47-day schedule in the previous section only holds because something like ACME exists. Rules and tooling prop each other up, which is why the option of not automating keeps narrowing.

The Server Now Tells You When to Renew

Once automation became widespread a new problem appeared. If everyone schedules renewal thirty days before expiry, renewal requests pile up at particular moments. And when a certificate authority has to revoke in bulk for some reason, it had no way to tell clients to renew right now.

ACME Renewal Information, ARI for short, solves both. It is standardized as RFC 9773. The server publishes a suggested renewal window per certificate, and the client picks a uniformly random time inside that window. The randomness is the design intent: it spreads load. If the chosen time has already passed, the client renews immediately.

In a notice dated 2 December 2025, Let's Encrypt laid out a schedule for cutting the default lifetime from 90 days to 45. The opt-in profile moves to 45 days on 13 May 2026; the default profile goes to 64 days with a 10-day authorization reuse period on 10 February 2027, then to 45 days with a 7-hour reuse period on 16 February 2028. The same notice gives an alternative for clients without ARI support: renew at roughly two thirds of the way through the certificate's lifetime. The shift from an absolute value like sixty days to a ratio is the substantive part.

The shorter end is already open too. A notice on 15 January 2026 announced general availability of a short-lived profile valid for 160 hours, just over six days. The same notice opened IP address certificates, which must be short-lived: because IP addresses change hands more often than domain names, validating more frequently was declared necessary.

Outages Change Shape

When automation is the default, the failure mode changes. Certificates used to expire because a person forgot. Now they expire because automation stopped. The latter is more dangerous for a specific reason: a person eventually realizes they forgot, while stopped automation makes no sound at all.

So the monitoring target has to move. An expiry countdown is now the last alarm to fire. The signals that should fire earlier look like this.

  • Last successful renewal timestamp. Watch for success stopping, not for failure appearing. A job that never runs produces no failure log
  • Expiry of the certificate actually being served. A renewed file with a process that never reloaded still serves the old certificate. That is why you check the port, not the disk
  • Health of the control-proof path. With http-01 a changed redirect rule breaks renewal silently; with dns-01 an expired DNS provider API key does the same
  • The intermediate chain. Renewing only the leaf while the chain stays old fails for some clients and not others. This is the family of bugs where browsers work but one language runtime does not
  • The inventory of where certificates are deployed. They live in load balancers, message queues, databases and internal tools, not only in front of the web server. Without an inventory, the blind spot in automation becomes the outage, and this is also where a retry storm hitting issuance rate limits begins

What People Still Have to Decide - Trust Stores, Private CAs, Internal Networks, Device Lifespans

It would be a mistake to read all this automation as less work for people. What automation removed is repetitive procedure; what is left is deciding where the boundaries go. The remainder is the harder half.

What Do You Trust

The entire public certificate system rests on a list that says «we trust this root authority». Operating systems and browsers each maintain that list, and the criteria for getting in or being removed are not purely technical. National, institutional and commercial interests are tangled in it. That thread is covered through one case in Korea's GPKI Root Certificate and the Firefox Root Store. Automation cannot make this call for you.

There is also machinery that watches issuance itself. Certificate Transparency logs record issued certificates in append-only public logs so that improper issuance can be detected. It began with RFC 6962, and RFC 9162 describes version 2.0.

Places a Public CA Cannot Serve

A name that is unreachable from the internet cannot be validated by a public certificate authority. Internal-only domains, management interfaces on private addresses, and equipment inside air-gapped networks all fall here. In those places standing up a private CA is the right answer, and what you have to decide then is:

  • Where the root key lives and who can reach it. That is not a technical decision but an authorization design
  • Which devices get the private root in their trust store. The moment it goes in, that device trusts every certificate that CA issues
  • Whether internal certificates also go short. Public rules do not apply here, so you set the number yourself
  • How you would withdraw the private CA if you had to. Retraction is far harder than distribution

Some Equipment Cannot Be Changed

The most concrete constraint appears here. Industrial controllers, medical equipment, payment terminals and aging network gear sometimes have a trust store baked into firmware with no update path. In front of such devices the current recommendations do not simply apply. The question to answer is not «how do I bring this up to date» but «how far do I isolate this device's territory». That device lifespans outlast protocol lifespans is itself a design condition.

After Quantum - Read Standardized and Deployed Separately

Few topics attract exaggeration and dismissal in equal measure like this one. Reading it accurately requires separating three stages: has the algorithm been standardized, has it been attached to the protocol, and is it used on real connections.

Standardization is settled. The United States National Institute of Standards and Technology finalized FIPS 203 on 13 August 2024. Its formal title is the Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism Standard, the algorithm it specifies is ML-KEM, and it defines three parameter sets: ML-KEM-512, 768 and 1024.

Deployment has begun as well. The Firefox 132 release notes state that support was added for «a post-quantum key exchange mechanism for TLS 1.3 (mlkem768x25519)», and that build shipped on 29 October 2024. As the name suggests it is a hybrid that uses classical X25519 together with ML-KEM-768. If either half breaks, the other remains, which is a reasonable posture before trusting a new algorithm alone.

That is the confirmed part. And here is the distinction people routinely skip: what has been deployed is key exchange, not certificate signatures. The two have different threat models.

  • Key exchange worries about ciphertext captured today and decrypted later. That is why a future threat justifies action now, and why it shipped first
  • Signatures only matter if forged in real time. Forging one later, after the connection is over, buys an attacker nothing, so there is comparatively more time
  • But the signature transition is far heavier. Roots, intermediates, leaves, validation libraries and trust stores all have to understand the new algorithm. The «equipment that cannot be changed» from the previous section is exactly what catches here

So the task at this moment is not swapping algorithms. It is holding an inventory of what runs where. When the transition arrives, what sets your speed is not algorithm knowledge but the accuracy of that inventory.

How to Keep This Knowledge Current - What to Follow and What to Ignore

A natural question by this point: if it changes this often, how do you keep up? The answer is you do not keep up with all of it. The changes that actually invalidate your configuration come from a fixed set of places.

Worth Following

  • IETF BCP 195. Today that number points at RFC 8996 and RFC 9325. Remember the number and you can check «what is currently recommended» in one lookup. RFC numbers change when documents are replaced; the BCP number does not
  • The Relevant Dates section of the CA/Browser Forum Baseline Requirements. Everything about to become mandatory is tabled with a date next to it. Every validity figure in this article came from there
  • Your certificate authority's announcements. This is where movement ahead of the rules and behind the rules both show up. An event like an OCSP shutdown is announced only on this channel
  • Browser root program policies and release notes. What the real enforcer cuts off, and when, appears here
  • Changelogs for your ACME client and server software. This is where rule changes get translated into actual behavior

Safe to Ignore

  • Individually branded vulnerabilities. A disclosure with a logo and a name gets attention, but most concern already-deprecated algorithms and do not apply once your floor is raised
  • Configuration snippet collections with a year in the title. Copy one and it starts rotting immediately. Save the document that justified the value, not the value
  • Predictions of when quantum computers will arrive. Standardization and deployment status are facts; timing predictions are not. Check the two stages instead of the forecast
  • Tuning done purely to raise a grade. A scanner grade is a useful signal but not a goal. Deciding to cut off older clients for a grade is a service decision, not a scanner decision

Check on Yourself Periodically

  • Have you verified the actual expiry of the certificate being served, directly on the port
  • Has the inventory of certificate locations been refreshed recently
  • Is the last successful renewal timestamp under monitoring
  • Is the negotiated floor TLS 1.2 or higher, and which of the remaining configuration entries still has a document behind it

What Could Not Be Confirmed

Items left out or narrowed in scope, gathered in one place. Leaving a gap empty beats filling it with an estimate.

  • Clause-level effective dates of the ballot that made OCSP optional. The ballot title and its 2023 passage were confirmed, but the differing per-clause dates could not be pinned to source text, so no date is used in the body
  • The ballot number and date that wrote 398 days into the Baseline Requirements. Only the browser policy effective date of 1 September 2020 was confirmed from a primary source, which is why that row cites browser policy
  • The exact Chrome version and date that enabled hybrid post-quantum key exchange by default. Firefox 132 was confirmed from release notes; the Chrome-side announcement body could not be opened, so no version number is stated
  • Any transition schedule for post-quantum certificate signature algorithms. Unlike key exchange, no confirmed public timeline was found
  • Short-lived certificate availability at issuers other than Let's Encrypt, and how domestic Korean CAs and hosting providers are handling the 200-day rule. One issuer's notice cannot stand for the industry, so the text stays within the named scope

Conclusion: Knowing the Rate of Decay Is Also Knowledge

Knowledge that lasts in this field and knowledge that rots have different characters. What rots are values. Validity in days, cipher suite strings, lists of things to enable and disable. Those go wholesale invalid on a scale of a few years. What lasts is structure. Why forward secrecy matters, how revocation information travels, where trust begins, and who the real enforcer is.

So the trick to staying current is not memorizing the latest values but remembering the shelf life attached to what you know. As of now: the protocol floor is TLS 1.2 and the recommendation is TLS 1.3, on the authority of BCP 195. Maximum certificate validity is 200 days from 15 March 2026, then 100 days in 2027, then 47 days in 2029. Renewal is performed by a program rather than a person, and the monitoring target is renewal success rather than expiry. Revocation checking leaves far less for a server operator to configure. Post-quantum is deployed in key exchange and not yet in signatures.

This list will expire too. What will not expire is knowing where the next list comes from. Remembering that lasts longer than memorizing the numbers.
