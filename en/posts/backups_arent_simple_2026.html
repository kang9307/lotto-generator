Backups Aren't Simple File Copies — Navigating Docker Permissions, DB Consistency, and Object Storage Traps

From the 3-2-1 Rule and RPO Spectrum to Fat-Tailed Incremental Snapshots, Borg, Restic, rsnapshot, ZFS Comparisons, and Semi-Annual Restore Drills

2026-09-18 Dev & Infra

One of the most pervasive misconceptions in systems engineering is the intuition that backing up data merely requires copying files to another drive or syncing directories across network boundaries. Engineers write straightforward periodic scripts, observe green checkmarks across automated monitoring dashboards, and conclude that operational data is securely guarded. However, when actual catastrophic incidents occur, this naive abstraction frequently unravels into unrecoverable disaster.

The imperative of restoration verification and the structural deceptive nature of return code zero were analyzed in depth in our earlier study, Having a Backup and Being Restorable Are Two Different Propositions, which focused on end-to-end verification pipelines. While that investigation addressed the downstream validation of completed archives, the present analysis focuses on the foundational upstream stage: the technical pitfalls encountered when assembling and capturing data from modern infrastructure layers.

In his essay titled Backups aren't simple, published on September 16, 2026, software engineer Aleksandar Filipovski articulates the nuanced failure modes that emerge when backups are treated as mundane byte transfers. He opens with an illustrative personal vignette: during his youth, his family gathered their digital photo archives onto an external drive to free up local disk space, only for the drive to be formatted without warning by a television set-top box. Storing data on a single physical medium without snapshot isolation brought their archive perilously close to permanent erasure.

The architectural lesson behind this event applies directly to enterprise infrastructure. Data loss is rarely confined to isolated disk drive head crashes; it propagates through user mistakes, unannounced reformatting, ransomware encryption, silent bit rot, memory buffer desynchronization, and permission mismatches. Robust backup engineering is not about blindly moving bytes from source to destination. It is a rigorous defense against the distinctive failure modes present across operating system kernels, container runtimes, database storage engines, and cloud object APIs.

Context — The Operational Chasm Behind Simple File Copying

A venerable maxim among systems administrators posits that there are only two categories of computer users: those who have experienced catastrophic data loss, and those who inevitably will. While engineers routinely assume their systems are adequately shielded, data destruction strikes through subtle vectors. Physical media gradually decays over time: magnetic orientations in hard disk platters slowly drift, and charge leaks from floating-gate NAND transistors in solid-state drives, precipitating silent bit rot. Above the hardware layer, human error, flawed automation scripts executing recursive deletes, and malicious ransomware payloads present constant hazards.

Aleksandar Filipovski correctly notes that basic live mirroring, such as a RAID 1 configuration, does not constitute a backup. Mirroring provides hardware redundancy against the physical failure of an individual disk drive, but it mirrors logical corruption in real time. If a file is mistakenly deleted, overwritten with zero bytes, or scrambled by an encryption Trojan, the mirrored drive executes the destructive write instantaneously. Redundancy ensures high availability; it provides zero defense against logical data loss.

True backup architecture mandates temporal separation: the ability to traverse backward in time to inspect and reconstruct uncorrupted snapshots. Yet attempting to capture and store discrete historical snapshots introduces immediate engineering friction. Uncontrolled storage consumption, container permission barriers, and asynchronous operating system caches quickly expose the inadequacy of simple file copying scripts.

Baseline Objectives — The 3-2-1 Rule and the RPO Spectrum

The architectural cornerstone of any resilient backup methodology is the 3-2-1 rule, emphasized prominently in Filipovski's analysis. This paradigm eliminates single points of failure through structural redundancy:

  • Maintain at least 3 copies of data: Keep the primary production dataset alongside at least two independent secondary copies to ensure recovery if one copy is damaged or compromised.
  • Utilize at least 2 distinct storage media types: Diversify hardware technologies and manufacturers (such as spinning magnetic drives, local SSDs, tape, or remote cloud block storage) to guard against systematic hardware-specific design defects or firmware bugs.
  • Keep at least 1 copy offsite: Store an isolated archive in a geographically distinct location to survive localized physical catastrophes, including structural fires, flooding, severe electrical surges, and equipment theft.

Coupled with the 3-2-1 rule is the Recovery Point Objective (RPO), which measures the maximum acceptable duration of data loss that an organization can tolerate during an outage. Filipovski underscores that RPO requirements span an enormous spectrum across different industries.

For mission-critical financial institutions handling high-frequency transactions, the RPO is constrained to under 30 seconds, demanding continuous synchronous replication and real-time write-ahead log streaming. Conversely, for small enterprises, internal utilities, and personal projects, an RPO of 24 hours or more is frequently acceptable. In his personal scenario regarding family archives, Filipovski reflects that a weekly backup cadenced to an RPO of roughly seven days would have comfortably averted catastrophe.

However, scheduling regular snapshots to satisfy an RPO introduces acute retention trade-offs. Preserving daily snapshots under a 24-hour RPO generates 365 distinct archive states annually. Granular daily revisions are critical for very recent work, but preserving daily increments from eleven months ago offers minimal operational utility. Operators therefore rely on rotation schemes like Grandfather-Father-Son (such as preserving 14 daily, 7 weekly, and 12 monthly snapshots), substantially escalating pipeline orchestration complexity.

Trap Layer 1: Containers and Permissions — Silent Root Ownership Failures

In modern containerized deployments managed with Docker or container runtimes, filesystem permissions introduce silent, severe points of failure. In traditional bare-metal environments, local system accounts managed service directories under well-defined permission policies. In containerized environments, however, user ID mappings between the host operating system and the container namespace frequently diverge.

Software packages running inside Docker containers routinely execute as root (UID 0) by default, writing runtime files and database journals to mounted host volumes under root ownership. Meanwhile, host-level backup processes, cron schedulers, or lightweight agents are deliberately executed under restricted, non-root system users to observe the Principle of Least Privilege. This structural asymmetry leads to critical breakdowns:

  • Permission Denied Failures: Unprivileged backup routines encountering root-owned container directories fail to read the files, resulting in aborted backup operations or unhandled exceptions.
  • Silent Partial Archives: If a backup shell script lacks strict error-checking flags (such as pipefail and exit-on-error), it will silently skip permission-denied directories, packaging only accessible configuration stubs and exiting with status code zero while omitting core data.
  • System-Wide Privilege Escalation Mandate: To reliably capture entire Docker volume mounts directly from the host filesystem, the backup process is forced to acquire complete, unrestricted root privileges over the host filesystem.

Granting blanket root privileges to a backup agent introduces substantial security liabilities, expanding the attack surface should the backup daemon or its network credentials be compromised. Conversely, remapping container process UIDs to align with unprivileged host accounts or configuring user namespaces introduces significant friction into container image maintenance and deployment automation. Simple file copy assumptions quickly collapse into complex access-control trade-offs.

Trap Layer 2: Database Consistency — Memory Flushing and Asynchronous I/O

Filesystem-level copying fails most catastrophically when applied to active relational and transactional databases. Production database engines (including PostgreSQL, MySQL, and SQLite) are engineered for high throughput and deliberately avoid immediate, synchronous disk writes for every transaction. Incoming modifications are buffered in volatile memory pools, while transactions are recorded in append-only write-ahead logs (WAL). The underlying database tables and indexes are flushed to disk asynchronously in batches.

If an external script attempts to copy raw database directories (such as `/var/lib/mysql` or `/var/lib/postgresql/data`) while the database process is running, the copied files will be torn and inconsistent. Because copying takes time, the utility reads physical disk blocks sequentially. In the interval between reading an index root block and reading a data leaf block, the database engine will have updated pointers, split B-tree pages, and modified transaction commit flags.

The resulting filesystem copy represents a fractured, impossible database state: table pages reference uncommitted memory transactions, index nodes point to obsolete disk offsets, and page-level checksums fail validation. When an operator attempts to restore this raw copy, the database engine will either refuse to start due to corrupted headers or attempt crash recovery that quietly rolls back valuable data.

Reliable database backups cannot be achieved by copying raw storage files. Operators must leverage native database dump utilities (such as `mysqldump` or `pg_dump`) or atomic storage engine snapshot APIs. These mechanisms interact directly with the database transaction manager, establishing consistent read views via Multi-Version Concurrency Control (MVCC) or brief read locks to export a mathematically cohesive point-in-time state.

Trap Layer 3: Object Storage — POSIX Metadata Loss and API Overhead

The rapid proliferation of cloud object storage (such as Amazon S3, MinIO, and Backblaze B2) has made offsite remote retention accessible and inexpensive. However, treating cloud object buckets as ordinary remote directories introduces two hazardous traps that frequently derail operations.

  • Loss of POSIX Filesystem Metadata: Object storage is a flat key-value store rather than a hierarchical filesystem. Objects hold data bytes and arbitrary HTTP headers, but they discard fundamental Linux metadata: numeric user IDs (UID), group IDs (GID), file mode permission bits, access control lists, symlinks, and extended attributes. Restoring files directly from object storage leaves them with generic ownership and broken permissions, preventing applications from executing correctly.
  • Exorbitant API Costs for Small Files: Cloud providers bill not only for raw gigabytes stored, but also per HTTP request (PUT, LIST, GET). If an infrastructure pipeline synchronizes a repository containing hundreds of thousands of small source files, icons, or session snippets directly to S3, the monthly PUT request charges can dwarf the actual storage bill by orders of magnitude.
  • Severe Latency Bottlenecks: Issuing separate HTTPS transactions for hundreds of thousands of small files introduces massive network round-trip overhead and TLS handshake latency, stretching the operational backup window across multiple hours and straining server resources.

Filipovski's recommended mitigation is to package filesystem hierarchies into 50MB tarballs prior to cloud upload. Archiving files into tar bundles encapsulates POSIX ownership, permissions, timestamps, and directory structures entirely within the archive stream. Simultaneously, combining hundreds of thousands of small files into consolidated 50MB chunks slashes S3 PUT API requests by several orders of magnitude, eliminating cost overruns and optimizing network throughput.

Backup Trade-offs — Hardlink Incrementals and Fat-Tailed Distributions

Once consistent data sources are established, the next architectural challenge is minimizing storage consumption. Executing full backups repeatedly wastes immense bandwidth and storage volume, making incremental snapshots indispensable for production systems.

Filipovski highlights the enduring utility of hardlink-based incremental architectures, historically embodied by Unix tools. When generating a new snapshot, the backup utility compares files against the prior snapshot. If a file remains identical, the utility creates a filesystem hardlink pointing to the existing inode, avoiding duplicate disk allocation. Only modified or newly created files consume fresh data blocks.

This design achieves remarkable efficiency because real-world file modifications exhibit a pronounced fat-tailed distribution. In practical systems, the overwhelming majority of stored files (operating system libraries, media assets, historical documents, and code packages) remain static over long horizons. Modifications cluster heavily around a small subset of files, such as dynamic database tables, application logs, and active session caches.

Nonetheless, hardlink-based incremental snapshots introduce operational trade-offs that engineers must account for:

  • Snapshot Dependency Chains: Because subsequent snapshots share underlying inodes and data blocks, filesystem corruption in an earlier snapshot can compromise the integrity of subsequent revisions.
  • Inode Exhaustion and Traversal Overhead: Creating millions of hardlinks places severe pressure on filesystem inode limits, and auditing large hardlinked trees demands substantial memory and CPU overhead from verification tools.
  • Incompatibility with Non-POSIX Targets: Hardlink structures cannot be mirrored directly onto filesystems lacking hardlink support (such as FAT32, exFAT, or standard object storage) without expanding into duplicate files, causing catastrophic storage expansion.

Tool Ecosystem Comparison — Architectural Goals of Borg, Restic, rsnapshot, and ZFS

To overcome these challenges, the systems ecosystem provides several specialized backup solutions, each optimized for specific architectural trade-offs.

The established utility rsnapshot relies on `rsync` and Unix hardlinks. Its paramount advantage is architectural transparency: snapshots reside as standard directory trees on the target filesystem. Operators can inspect, traverse, and retrieve files using standard command-line tools without specialized decryption or extraction software. However, rsnapshot lacks native encryption, offers no compression, and operates on whole files rather than variable chunks, forcing entire multi-gigabyte files to be duplicated if a single byte changes.

Modern utilities like BorgBackup (Borg) and Restic implement content-defined chunking, deduplication, and zero-trust encryption. These tools partition files into variable-length blocks based on content hashes. If a virtual machine image or large database dump changes partially, only the altered chunks are stored and transmitted. Archives are encrypted and authenticated with cryptographic checksums before leaving the client. Restic is particularly distinguished by its single static binary architecture and native support for modern cloud object storage backends, including S3 and Backblaze B2.

At the storage layer, ZFS resolves backup requirements directly within the filesystem. ZFS employs a Copy-on-Write (CoW) transactional architecture with end-to-end checksum verification. Snapshots take seconds regardless of dataset size by freezing pointer trees without copying data. Background scrubbing automatically detects bit rot and repairs corrupted blocks using parity data. Through `zfs send` and `zfs receive`, operators stream incremental block-level snapshots directly across networks with unmatched speed and mathematical integrity.

Operational Blind Spots — DST Transitions and Semi-Annual Restore Drills

Even with optimal tools, operational scheduling habits introduce subtle failure modes. Filipovski highlights a seemingly minor yet critical real-world heuristic: never schedule automated backup jobs at 2:00 AM or 3:00 AM.

In regions observing Daylight Saving Time (DST), clocks spring forward from 2:00 to 3:00 AM in the spring. A cron job scheduled for 2:30 AM simply never fires because that local clock value does not occur. In autumn, the clock falls back from 3:00 to 2:00 AM, causing the 2:30 AM window to execute twice, which can trigger concurrent repository access collisions and performance degradation. Production backups must run on Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) or be scheduled during stable morning windows well past potential transition gaps.

Finally, the entire backup pipeline culminates in regular restore drills. Filipovski strongly recommends conducting hands-on restoration exercises at least every six months. This reinforces an essential operational principle: the value of a backup system resides not in the creation of archive files, but in the verified ability to reconstitute production services from zero.

  • Decoupling Backup Success from Restore Viability: A successful zero return code indicates only that the backup tool completed writing. It provides zero guarantee that encryption keys are retrievable, archive headers are uncorrupted, or schema definitions match application dependencies.
  • Empirical Recovery Time Objective (RTO) Measurement: Restoring data into an isolated staging environment every six months allows teams to measure true recovery duration, ensuring actual recovery times align with business RTO constraints.
  • Credential and Documentation Validation: Restore drills expose expired repository passwords, invalid decryption keyfiles, and obsolete runbook procedures before an actual emergency takes place.
