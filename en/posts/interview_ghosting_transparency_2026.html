Interview Ghosting Goes on the Record

What a public ghosting tracker asks of hiring - what the law actually requires, and whether exposure can be the answer

Jul 27, 2026 Trends & Culture

Introduction: Silence Is Becoming Data

You had the interview. It went reasonably well, and the recruiter said you would hear back next week. Then that week passed, and the next, and a month. Your follow-up email goes unanswered. You are neither hired nor rejected - just suspended in time. The experience has had a name for years, ghosting, but it was usually metabolized as private grievance. The company posted a fresh req the following season as if nothing had happened, and the candidate rewrote a cover letter and blamed themselves.

Recently a different attempt has surfaced: turning that silence into a countable record. A service now collects anonymous reports about companies that cut contact after an interview without a clear rejection, moderates them, and publishes the aggregate. When it was introduced to the technology community, approved reports numbered in the high hundreds. Alongside the companies most reported for going silent, it also surfaces companies recognized for clear communication. It also makes a point of not collecting personal details about reporters or interviewers.

This piece is not a product review. It asks three things instead. First, how common is ghosting really, and how far should the published figures be trusted? Second, what does Korean hiring law actually compel, and what does it conspicuously leave alone? Third, can publishing records genuinely fix this, or does it create a second problem? The focus is on why a hiring process structurally manufactures silence - not on any particular company.

1. What a Ghosting Tracker Actually Does

1.1 The Mechanics - Reports, Moderation, Leaderboards

The structure is simple. A candidate anonymously submits the company name, a rough role, the date of last contact, and what happened afterward. A moderator screens out obvious spam and personal attacks, approves the rest, and the approved reports aggregate by company into a leaderboard. Two design choices carry most of the weight.

  • No personal identifiers - neither the reporter nor the interviewer is identified. That reads as a deliberate attempt to shrink the dispute surface.
  • Two-sided ranking - companies with clear communication appear next to those most reported for silence. That signals a reputation index rather than a pure pillory.
  • Pre-publication review - nothing posts automatically. It is slower, but it blunts the instant spread of fabricated reports.
  • Company-level aggregation - the display is cumulative counts, not individual stories. The emphasis is on pattern rather than personal venting.

1.2 Why It Is Landing Now

The idea is not new. Employer review platforms have existed for well over a decade. What changed is that the subject of the review moved from the experience of working somewhere to the hiring process itself. Only people who got hired can write an employment review. Ghosting, by contrast, happens to people who did not get hired - a far larger population. Giving the application stage its own record-keeping surface widens the addressable audience enormously.

The second shift is the collapsing cost of applying. Applications used to be effortful, which naturally capped volume. Now resume generation and one-click apply have exploded the number of submissions while response rates have fallen. From the candidate side, the information recovered per hour invested has become vanishingly small. Once that imbalance crossed a threshold, individual complaints turned into a collective demand for a paper trail.

None of it is official government data, and results swing hard on sampling and question wording. That is precisely why the numbers below range from 48% to 61%.

2.1 Response Rate Is the Colder Metric

More revealing than incidence is the response rate. A 2025 index report synthesizing dozens of studies flags wide sector gaps: roughly 5% in technology and just under 6% at small businesses, against about 20% in healthcare. The same report estimates the time candidates pour into hiring processes at an average of about 47 hours, a burden it labels the candidate time tax.

The implication is blunt. If response rates sit near 5%, silence is not the exception, it is the default. That matters practically for job seekers. Reading a non-reply as a verdict on your resume or your interview manner is usually over-attribution. What looks like a personal failure is frequently the system operating as designed.

2.2 Caveats When Quoting These Figures

  • Sampling bias - platform users are unusually active job seekers, which inflates incidence.
  • Fuzzy definitions - how many days of silence counts as ghosting differs from survey to survey.
  • The nature of index reports - synthesizing dozens of studies is convenient but flattens methodological differences. Check the underlying source where it matters.
  • National variance - nearly all of these figures come from English-speaking markets and map poorly onto Korean hiring norms.

3. Why It Happens Structurally - System Before Malice

Treating ghosting as individual rudeness leads nowhere. The real causes sit in process design.

3.1 Application Volume and Lopsided Automation

Applicant tracking systems are heavily invested in screening automation. Keyword filters, scoring, and automatic ranking are table stakes. Rejection automation, by contrast, often exists but sits switched off or unused. The reason is simple: screening automation cuts a recruiter's workload, whereas notification automation gives them nothing and can generate a new task in the form of replies to handle. When the benefits of automation flow in only one direction, silence is the result.

3.2 Overloaded Recruiters and Orphaned Pipelines

A single recruiter typically juggles many open reqs. When that person leaves, their candidate pipeline is the first thing to slide off the handover list. Contracts, payroll, and current-employee issues come first, and people who are not yet employees rank low. Add a reorganization or a hiring freeze and the req is left with no owner at all. Community discussion of ghosting cases returns to recruiter departures and paused reqs again and again.

3.3 Keeping Backups Warm

Another common driver is holding runner-up candidates in reserve. Explicitly rejecting the second and third choices before the first choice signs means losing them to other offers. So the notification is deferred, and once the first choice accepts, nobody bothers to close the loop. From the company's side it looks like a rational hedge. From the candidate's side it is simply silence. One sentence saying you are being held as a backup would resolve it, and that sentence is routinely omitted.

3.4 Avoiding Legal Exposure

The reason employers cite most often is that feedback becomes evidence. Written rejection rationales can be quoted back in a discrimination claim. That concern is not baseless. But the logic explains why a company might withhold detailed feedback; it does not explain withholding the fact of a decision. Telling someone a process has ended carries no evaluative content at all. At the point where risk avoidance is stretched to justify total silence, it stops being an explanation and becomes an excuse.

4. Ghosting Runs Both Ways

Fairness demands looking at the other direction. Employers are not the only ones who vanish. The same index cites 44% of job seekers admitting they ghosted an employer and 22% of accepted offers ending in a first-day no-show. Korea has its own data.

The Korean figures date from 2018 and 2019, so treat the timing accordingly. The lesson still holds. That the top reason for candidate no-shows was a poor company reputation discovered before the interview is especially telling: a large share of candidate disappearances are not rudeness but a rational response to information that arrived late. A structure that delivers information late produces defections on both sides.

Still, the two are not equivalent. The asymmetries are real.

  • Bargaining power - a company loses one of many candidates; a candidate has to rebuild an entire livelihood timeline.
  • Information access - companies log candidate histories in a system, while candidates historically had almost no way to learn a firm's notification habits in advance.
  • Direction of the record - a no-show candidate leaves a trace in the employer's database. The reverse never existed. Ghosting trackers aim at exactly that gap.
  • The shared principle - even so, disappearing without notice pushes process costs onto the other party. That part is symmetric.

5. What Korean Hiring Law Actually Compels

This is the crux. Online you will find both rejection notices are legally mandatory in Korea and that provision is toothless. Each is about half right.

5.1 Article 10 - Notification of the Hiring Outcome

The statute is the Act on Fair Recruitment Procedures. Article 10 requires an employer that has finalized its selection to notify applicants of the hiring outcome without delay. Permitted methods borrow from Article 7 paragraph 2 and include a website notice, text message, email, fax, or phone call. Individual outreach is therefore not the only compliant route - a posting on the careers site can satisfy it.

5.2 Three Layers of Limitation

The problem is that the duty is hemmed in three ways.

  • Restricted scope - the Act generally applies to workplaces with 30 or more regular employees. Applicants at smaller employers fall outside it. Some provisions, such as the ban on false job advertisements in Article 4, apply regardless of size.
  • A gap in the trigger - the duty fires only when a selection has been finalized. The most common real-world ghosting scenarios - paused reqs, cancelled hiring, indefinite review - involve no finalization at all. On the text alone, that space is empty.
  • No penalty - Article 10 does not appear in the list of provisions carrying administrative fines under Article 17. In other words, there is no fine for ignoring it, which is why practitioners describe it as merely exhortatory.

5.3 Which Provisions Have Teeth

Within the same statute, enforcement varies sharply.

Practical angle - the notification duty has no penalty, but the document return duty does. Under Article 11, when a rejected candidate requests the return of application documents, the employer must in principle send them within 14 days, and must retain those documents for a set period after the outcome is decided. Documents submitted via a website or email, and materials volunteered by the candidate, are excluded. Where paper documents went to a workplace of 30 or more employees, a return request becomes an indirect way to confirm that the process has closed.

5.4 If the Law Were to Be Amended

Making Article 10 real would take two changes. First, widen the trigger from a finalized selection to the closure or suspension of the process, which plugs the most common gap. Second, attach some minimal enforcement. Criminal liability or a heavy fine would be excessive, and that objection is fair. A staged approach - a small fine only after a correction order is ignored - looks more workable. Since notification is a low-cost bulk operation inside any ATS, the argument that compliance is burdensome does not hold up well.

6. The Value and the Danger of Exposure Platforms

6.1 The Value - Nudging the Asymmetry Back

The essential contribution of a ghosting tracker is not exposure but restored symmetry. Employers have logged candidates forever: application history, evaluation notes, no-show flags. Candidates had virtually no way to record the quality of an employer's process. That asymmetry is the root of the problem. If a company's notification habits are visible in advance, candidates can decide for themselves where to spend their hours. In market terms, that is the minimum condition for reputation-based self-regulation to function at all.

6.2 The Danger - Unverified, One-Sided Claims

The risks are equally clear. Listed coldly:

  • Unverifiability - no outsider can confirm the reporter actually applied, or that no notice was ever sent. Spam filtering is not fact-checking.
  • Negativity bias - satisfied people do not file reports. Only the aggrieved leave records, so the aggregate tilts negative by construction.
  • Attribution error - one team's behavior in one quarter becomes a company-wide trait. Large firms run dozens of separate hiring processes that the tally collapses into a single name.
  • Scale distortion - more applicants mean more absolute reports. A leaderboard ranked by count rather than rate penalizes high-volume hirers.
  • Frozen timestamps - a case from years ago sits on the board and obscures a process that has since improved.
  • Perverse incentives - a company avoiding the leaderboard may respond by blasting empty auto-rejections quickly. When a measure becomes a target, the measure degrades.

6.3 Legal Risk in the Korean Context

In Korea, negative posts about a company are hard to separate from defamation exposure. Article 70 of the Act on Promotion of Information and Communications Network Utilization and Information Protection provides that publicly disclosing facts online with intent to defame is punishable by up to 3 years imprisonment or a fine up to 30 million won, and false statements by up to 7 years imprisonment, up to 10 years suspension of qualifications, or a fine up to 50 million won. The belief that truth makes it safe is therefore unreliable.

What matters is that the offense requires intent to defame. Where the dominant purpose is public interest, that intent tends to be denied, and criminal law separately allows illegality to be negated for true statements serving the public interest. That is the ground employer review platforms stand on. Note also that a corporation can be the subject of a defamation claim, so posts targeting a company are not automatically safe. Practically, write like this:

  • State what happened and when, plainly, and avoid categorical claims about anyone's motives.
  • Do not use expressions that identify individuals, including a surname plus job title.
  • Skip insults, personal attacks, and mockery, which map directly onto intent to defame.
  • Include anything that went well. Balance supports a public-interest reading.
  • Confine the account to the process you experienced instead of generalizing to the whole company.

7. How Other Jurisdictions Handle It

7.1 The United States - Anonymity Is Not Absolute

The US has no general federal duty to give hiring feedback. If anything, discrimination-claim exposure has entrenched the habit of withholding detail. The friction instead sits between review platforms and defamation law. In a well-known case, a global toy company objected to anonymous reviews about it and sought to compel the platform to unmask the authors, and a US court granted the request. The decision drew attention partly because it was assessed against the defamation standard of the company's home jurisdiction. The takeaway is unambiguous: a platform being anonymous does not mean identity is permanently protected.

7.2 Europe - A Data Duty, Not a Reply Duty

Europe frames it differently. Under GDPR the question is how applicant data is handled, not whether you must answer. Supervisory guidance recommends retaining unsuccessful candidate data for roughly 6 to 12 months, with national practice varying - France allowing up to about two years from last contact, Germany recommending around six months after rejection. Keeping data longer for future openings requires separate consent, and commentators note that the power imbalance in hiring can undermine how freely that consent is given.

One common misconception deserves correcting. GDPR creates no duty to provide feedback. What it does create is a right of access to your own data, which incidentally opens a channel to ask what state your application is in. The impression that Europe has better response culture owes more to data rights and labor norms combined than to any legal mandate to reply.

7.3 Compared with Korea

Interestingly, it is Korea that has the explicit notification provision. Neither the US nor Europe commonly carries a general clause squarely requiring notice of a hiring outcome. The failure is not a missing statute but a statute that does not operate: no penalty, a narrow trigger, and consequently little presence in practice. It is a textbook case of adopting a rule early while never building the enforcement design around it.

8. Practical Moves for Job Seekers

8.1 Follow-Up Timing - Twice Is Enough

  1. Lock it in as you leave - ask directly when and how the decision will be communicated. That single answer becomes the baseline for every later judgment.
  2. Two or three business days past the date - first follow-up. Short and courteous, asking for a status update on the process.
  3. About a week later - final follow-up. Saying you would simply like to know whether the process has closed, regardless of outcome, slightly improves your odds of a reply.
  4. Then stop - a third message will not change the decision and only drains you. Mark it closed and move on.

8.2 Keeping Records

  • Follow up by email or text rather than phone. It leaves a record and lets the recipient answer when convenient, which also helps response rates.
  • If you had to take a call, send a short written summary afterward, noting the schedule you were given and the recruiter's team.
  • Keep an application log in one table. Company, role, applied date, interview date, promised decision date, follow-up date, current status is enough.
  • If you submitted paper documents to a workplace of 30 or more employees, consider requesting their return. The provision carries a penalty, so a reply is relatively more likely.
  • If you plan to post publicly, hold to the writing rules above - factual, non-identifying, balanced.

8.3 Managing the Emotional Cost

This is the part that matters most. Silence is not an evaluation. The existence of sectors with response rates around 5 to 6% shows that most silence is the default behavior of a process, unrelated to individual merit. People nonetheless read silence as a verdict on themselves. A few practices reduce that.

  • Run processes in parallel - do not pause other applications because you reached a final round. The point is never to build a structure where one outcome carries all your emotional weight.
  • Count only what you control - applications sent, preparation hours, feedback incorporated are controllable. Whether anyone replies is not. Do not use uncontrollable items as performance metrics.
  • Fix your checking rhythm - refreshing your inbox constantly feeds anxiety. Twice a day at set times works better.
  • Set an expiry - decide in advance how long you will hold hope for a given company, then close it out when that window passes.
  • Avoid isolation - a long search in isolation erodes judgment. Keep at least one regular point of contact, whether a study group, a community, or friends.

If you need support - when job search stress starts to affect daily life or sleep persistently, please do not push through it alone. Reach out to a crisis or counseling line in your country. If you are in Korea, the following lines were operating as of July 2026: 109 for suicide prevention counseling, which since January 1, 2024 has consolidated the previously scattered suicide prevention lines and runs 24 hours; 1577-0199 for mental health counseling, which kept its original number and role after that consolidation; and 1350, the Ministry of Employment and Labor customer counseling center, for hiring and working-condition questions, open on weekdays from 09:00 to 18:00.

9. Hearing Out the Employer Side

In fairness, the employer case has substance.

  • Application volume - generated resumes and bulk-apply tools have multiplied submissions. There is a genuine scale at which responding is itself a cost.
  • Risk in explaining rejections - documenting specific reasons can be quoted unfavorably in a dispute. Not a baseless worry.
  • Cancellations happen - when a budget freeze or reorganization kills a req, there is no conclusion to communicate.
  • Thin staffing - at small organizations hiring is often a side duty, so follow-up communication structurally slips.
  • One-sided records - reports accumulating without a right of reply are a real reputational risk for employers.

Yet these arguments largely explain why a company cannot offer detailed feedback. They do not explain failing to say the process is over. Most ATS products already ship bulk-send capability, and a closure notice with no stated reason carries essentially no legal exposure. Even a cancelled req is handled by one line: this opening has been withdrawn. What is left is not a cost problem but a priority problem - namely, where communication with people who are not yet employees sits on the organization's list.

Conclusion: Exposure Platforms Are a Symptom, Not a Cure

The verdict on ghosting trackers comes out like this: they were needed, they are useful, and they are not sufficient. Needed, because candidates had no recording surface at all. Useful, because knowing a company's notification habits in advance actually informs decisions. Insufficient, because a tally of unverified one-sided claims cannot adjudicate individual truth, carries distortions of scale and timing, and at worst incentivizes hollow, formulaic responses.

More fundamentally, the emergence of such a platform is a signal that the institution is not working. Korea already has a provision telling employers to communicate hiring outcomes. It just applies only to workplaces of 30 or more employees, triggers only on a finalized selection, and carries no fine when ignored. With the text on the books but inert, the market built its own substitute: a leaderboard. Before criticizing the leaderboard, it is worth asking why one became necessary.

Three realistic directions stand out. First, amend Article 10 to cover the closure or suspension of a process and attach staged enforcement; a small fine only after an ignored correction order keeps the burden light. Second, change ATS defaults so that automatic closure notices ship switched on - that alone resolves much of it. Third, have employers manage response rate as a hiring-brand metric in their own right. Seeing it on an internal dashboard is always better than seeing your name on an external board.

For job seekers, the closing note is simple. Silence is usually not a judgment of you but the default behavior of a system. Get the decision date, follow up twice, keep records, and keep moving in parallel. And when the process becomes more than you can carry alone, reach for the support lines noted above rather than absorbing it privately. The silence in hiring is not a burden for individuals to shoulder - it is a defect for whoever designed the process to fix.
