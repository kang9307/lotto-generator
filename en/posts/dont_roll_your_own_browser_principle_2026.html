The "Don't Roll Your Own" Principle 2026 - Why Web UI Should Stop Replacing Browser-Native Features

Stop Rebuilding the Browser - The 2026 Case for Using Native Web Platform Features

May 25, 2026 Tech & IT

A strange landscape - every website rebuilds the same components

Open one hundred websites at random and inspect their DOM. You will notice something odd. Almost every one of them rebuilds the same handful of components from scratch. Dropdowns. Modals. Accordions. Date pickers. File uploaders. Progress bars. Form validation. The browser already ships every one of these natively, yet more than ninety percent of production sites reinvent them in div elements wired together with JavaScript. If every desktop application reimplemented the operating system's native file dialog from scratch, we would call it inefficient. On the web, it is the default behavior.

The hada.io topic posted on May 24, 2026 - topic 6, "Don't Roll Your Own" Principle - put the right name on this pattern again. The principle started in cryptography in the late 1990s. "Don't roll your own crypto." Custom encryption algorithms tend to look secure but actually expose users to subtle, devastating bugs. From there the same warning spread to authentication, session management, HTML parsing, and date and time handling. In 2026 the same idea is returning to web UI, where browsers now ship a surprisingly complete set of components that almost nobody uses. This article is not a translation of the hada.io thread. It takes that thread as a starting point and extends it with my own analysis across twelve chapters.

Three questions drive the piece. First, exactly why is rebuilding browser-native features a bad default in 2026? Second, which native features have become mature enough to trust? Third, when is rolling your own still legitimately justified? Along the way we look at why GitHub, Stripe, and Linear - all top-tier UX shops - lean heavily on native HTML, how the React and Vue component-library ecosystem trains developers into bad habits, and how to negotiate this shift with designers and product managers who already approved a custom design system.

A short history of "Don't Roll Your Own" - from crypto to UI

Tracking the principle's origin matters. "Don't roll your own crypto" is the phrase Bruce Schneier and other security researchers have repeated since the late 1990s. A custom encryption algorithm gives users a false sense of safety. Standard libraries like OpenSSL or libsodium have been reviewed by hundreds of experts over decades. The trust gap between the two is enormous and almost always unjustified by the time saved building a custom one.

The second expansion of the principle came in authentication and session management. The years of homegrown PHP session code that shipped with subtle CSRF, session-fixation, and session-regeneration bugs eventually pushed the backend community to standardize on OAuth, OpenID Connect, and well-reviewed session libraries. The consensus settled: do not invent your own auth, take a battle-tested one and configure it.

The third expansion is happening now, in 2026 web UI. The "thing you should not roll yourself" is the set of native HTML elements the browser already provides: select, dialog, details, the many input types, progress, video, audio. These elements have been refined for decades against W3C and WHATWG standards. Yet developers still default to div plus JavaScript and rebuild them. The hada.io discussion is essentially a reminder: the same principle that saved cryptography and authentication should now save web UI.

Ten browser-native features you should stop rebuilding

My list of ten native features that are mature enough in 2026 that rebuilding them is almost always a mistake. For each I note why it gets rebuilt and why native is better today.

1) <select> dropdowns

The most-reinvented element on the web. Designers historically said "it cannot be styled," so teams rebuilt it in div. That excuse is expiring. Customizable Select - appearance: base-select with pseudo-elements like ::picker(select) - shipped in Chromium 130 in late 2024 and is stable in Firefox 135. A native select gives you keyboard navigation, type-ahead, mobile wheel pickers, and accessibility-tree registration for free, in one line.

2) <details> / <summary> accordions

Every accordion and toggle widget should now be details/summary. Zero JavaScript. The open attribute toggles, Enter and Space work, and content inside details participates in the browser's built-in find-in-page search. A typical React equivalent runs 100 to 300 lines and reimplements behavior you got for free.

3) <dialog> modals

Stabilized in every major browser since 2022. A single showModal() call gives you focus trapping, ESC to close, a generated backdrop, and accessibility-tree integration. The ::backdrop pseudo-element lets you style the dimmed area freely. Common mistakes in custom modals - like losing focus when Tab reaches the last element - simply do not exist with the native dialog.

4) <input type="date | time | datetime-local">

The React ecosystem still treats react-datepicker and MUI's DatePicker as defaults, but on mobile the native picker is typically faster and more familiar. The iOS wheel picker and the Android material calendar are what users already use in their banking and calendar apps. Pseudo-elements like ::-webkit-datetime-edit-fields-wrapper let you style the inline view, and the browser still keeps the rest accessible.

5) <input type="file"> uploaders

Even when you want drag-and-drop, the right pattern is a native input type=file with a styled drop zone on top, not a custom uploader from scratch. The multiple, accept, and capture attributes are the only way to reliably trigger the right camera or gallery flow on mobile - a step custom uploaders almost always skip.

6) <progress> / <meter>

A div with an animated width does not register in the accessibility tree. Screen readers just say "region." <progress value="40" max="100"> shows up as "40 percent." Adding ARIA manually to a div is more code than just using the element.

7) <video> / <audio>

video.js is powerful and useful for HLS, DRM, and ABR streaming. For ordinary playback the native video element with controls gives you picture-in-picture, captions, playback speed, and volume - all tuned per OS. Default to native and only adopt a library when you hit a feature gap.

8) input types color, range, search, tel, email, url

Choosing the correct type on an input changes the on-screen keyboard on mobile, enables built-in validation, and unlocks OS-level autocomplete and password manager integration. Shipping type="text" with a placeholder when you meant type="email" is leaving free UX wins on the table.

9) Form validation (required, pattern, minlength, maxlength)

HTML5 form validation is faster and more accessible than the equivalent JavaScript checks. The :valid and :invalid CSS pseudo-classes let you style state without scripting, and novalidate lets you opt out selectively. The 2026 best practice is to use native validation as the baseline and add JavaScript only for cross-field or async checks.

10) Scroll behavior (scroll-snap, scroll-behavior, overscroll-behavior)

Carousels and sliders no longer need a JavaScript library. CSS scroll-snap handles snap behavior, scroll-behavior: smooth handles smooth scrolling, and overscroll-behavior controls bounce. Native inertial scrolling on mobile is automatic. Stop importing a 30 KB carousel and let CSS do it.

The five real costs of rolling your own

The question after "why use native?" is "what exactly do I lose by rebuilding?" My five-cost framework:

  • Accessibility loss. Native elements ship ARIA roles, keyboard shortcuts, focus management, and screen-reader patterns by default. A div reimplementation typically needs 200 to 500 lines of JavaScript plus dozens of hours of a11y QA, and most still ship with subtle bugs.
  • Missing keyboard shortcuts. Type-ahead inside select, focus trapping inside dialog, Enter and Space inside details - these subtle keyboard behaviors are nearly always missing from custom builds. Power users notice, even if you never measure it.
  • Mobile compatibility regressions. Native input type=date renders the iOS wheel picker and Android material calendar. A custom datepicker is almost always worse on both. The same applies to file, color, and range.
  • Bundle size inflation. react-select is around 50 KB, react-datepicker around 40 KB, react-modal around 15 KB, react-dropzone around 30 KB. A single screen in an average SPA easily carries 150 to 300 KB of component libraries that native could replace at zero cost.
  • Maintenance debt. Libraries break on major version upgrades. The React 18-to-19 transition cost thousands of SaaS teams real engineering time updating react-select alone. Native APIs do not break - the standard is the standard.

Add it up and an average mid-size SaaS spends roughly 200 to 500 person-hours per year maintaining components that the browser already ships. That is a full-time designer or engineer salary, recurring annually. Migrating to native usually wipes most of that cost out within a single quarter.

When rolling your own is still justified - three exceptions

If the conclusion were "always use native, no exceptions," half the argument would be wrong. Three scenarios where building custom is the right call:

  • Interactions the browser does not support natively. Drag-and-drop sorting, virtualized infinite scroll, multi-column grids, code editors, canvas-based whiteboards. Native does not offer these, so a custom build or a vetted library is correct.
  • Brand-defining surfaces. The hero card on a checkout page, the signature component on a landing page, anything where the visual design is the value proposition. Here the constraints of native are reasonable to drop.
  • Native's structure does not match the requirement. Mega menus, virtual keyboards, virtualized dropdowns with tens of thousands of options. A native select rendering 10,000 options will freeze the browser. Virtualization is mandatory and only a custom build can deliver it.

The default heuristic: if native covers 70%, stay native and fill the 30% with CSS or a thin JavaScript layer. Discarding the 70% to chase the 30% is almost always a loss across accessibility, bundle size, and maintenance.

How native is leveling up in 2026 - View Transitions, Anchor Positioning, Popover

One reason native is suddenly attractive in 2026 is that the browser is shipping features that previously did not exist. Three landmark APIs:

View Transitions API

The days of importing framer-motion or react-spring purely for smooth SPA transitions are ending. document.startViewTransition() takes a snapshot of the DOM before and after a change and animates the difference. One line replaces a 50 KB animation library for the most common cases. Chrome and Safari support it; Firefox added stable support in early 2026.

CSS Anchor Positioning

Popper.js and floating-ui kept the web alive for tooltips and popovers. CSS Anchor Positioning replaces them. anchor-name and position-anchor let you say "place this next to the anchor, flip to the other side if off-screen" in pure CSS. Chrome shipped it stable in 2025; Firefox and Safari are stabilizing through 2026.

Popover API

The popover attribute combined with popovertarget gives you tooltips, dropdowns, and menus without JavaScript. Like dialog but non-modal and lighter. Combine it with Anchor Positioning and almost every floating UI element becomes native again.

Together these three APIs cover roughly 70 to 80 percent of cases that were "must be custom" in 2024. The Interop project, running since 2022, has aligned the four major browsers on a shared roadmap, so the pace of these wins is accelerating, not slowing.

Styling native with CSS variables and ::backdrop - design without abandoning the platform

The 2026 counter to "native looks ugly" is simple: CSS now lets you style nearly everything. Three patterns I use constantly:

First, the ::backdrop pseudo-element. Style the dialog or popover backdrop directly: backdrop-filter: blur(10px), background: rgba(0,0,0,0.5). Supported across all major browsers since 2024.

Second, customizable select. With appearance: base-select you get full control over the option list via ::picker(select) and option::checkmark. Your design system's typography, spacing, and color tokens drop straight in. The accessibility tree stays intact.

Third, color-scheme and accent-color. One declaration of accent-color: var(--brand) tints every native checkbox, radio, range, and progress on the page. Hundreds of lines of custom checkbox code are replaced by one line of CSS.

With these three techniques you can hit roughly 90 percent of a design system's visual requirements while keeping native semantics. The "design versus accessibility" dichotomy that dominated the 2010s is largely a historical artifact in 2026.

Why GitHub, Stripe, and Linear use native

Enough theory. The teams widely regarded as top-tier UX shops lean on native HTML. A closer look:

GitHub

GitHub's Primer design system formalized a "native first" policy around 2020. Sidebar toggles use details/summary. PR review modals use dialog. Searchable dropdowns sit on top of native select. The result: GitHub pages are noticeably lighter than peer SaaS, and keyboard shortcuts work everywhere because native already provided the foundation.

Stripe

The Stripe Dashboard leans on native form validation, input type=number, and input type=email. The checkout card field is isolated in an iframe for PCI-DSS reasons, but most other surfaces are plain native HTML, styled with the Stripe design tokens. Performance and accessibility scores at Stripe consistently top SaaS benchmarks.

Linear

Linear is a design-systems showcase, yet inside the app you find details, dialog, and native form elements doing most of the work. The pervasive keyboard shortcuts feel right because they sit on a native foundation. The lesson: a great-looking product does not need to abandon native.

The common thread across these three is a rejection of the "design versus native" framing. Inside each company, senior engineers and senior designers share the consensus that layering design on native is faster and more durable than rebuilding from scratch. That consensus is a cultural artifact that takes years to build but pays back every quarter afterward.

React and Vue ecosystem habits - shadcn and MUI are not automatic wins

Component libraries in React and Vue come in two flavors. Native-wrapping (Radix UI, Headless UI, Ariakit, parts of React Aria) keep native or shadow-DOM primitives underneath. Div-reimplementing (much of MUI, Ant Design, Chakra) build from scratch in div. Before installing anything, check which type you are getting.

shadcn/ui is built on Radix, so most of its primitives keep accessibility primitives. But a few components - the Select and Dropdown Menu in particular - are div-based, and they do not render the OS-native picker on mobile. Their keyboard behavior is close to native but not identical. Audit the components you actually use before you ship them in production.

MUI's Select is div-based as well, and behaves differently from native on mobile. MUI's DatePicker adds 50 KB or more to your bundle. For simple cases, native select and input type=date are smaller, faster, and more familiar to mobile users.

My working rule: reach for unstyled libraries like Radix, Ariakit, or Headless UI only when you genuinely need them; default to native otherwise. Component libraries that rebuild from scratch are always more expensive than native over a multi-year horizon.

Five things you can ship in your next PR

Five swaps you can apply tomorrow. Each one stands alone and delivers measurable wins.

  1. Swap one modal to <dialog>. Remove react-modal, save roughly 15 KB, and inherit focus trapping and ESC handling for free.
  2. Swap accordions to <details>/<summary>. Delete your custom toggle component. Typical removal is 200 to 300 lines of JavaScript.
  3. Swap date inputs to <input type="date">. If 70% of your traffic is mobile, you almost certainly improve usability. Remove react-datepicker and recover roughly 40 KB.
  4. Move form validation to native required and pattern first. Keep JavaScript validation only for cross-field rules and async checks.
  5. Build the habit of searching MDN for a native element before reaching for div + role. A year of doing this reshapes a codebase.

Each item is a developer decision that does not require designer approval. They land in PRs, they show up in Lighthouse scores, and they are easy to point at when arguing for broader adoption.

How to negotiate this with designers and product managers

The biggest organizational obstacle is the conversation with design and product. The pattern I have seen work in mature engineering teams:

First, scope the negotiation by component, not by philosophy. Do not propose "let us go native everywhere." Propose "let us swap five high-impact components - modal, dropdown, accordion, date, file - and keep the rest as is for now." Phased proposals pass far more often than total redesigns.

Second, translate the win into numbers. "Replacing react-datepicker with native input type=date shaves 700 ms off mobile time-to-interactive and 40 KB off the bundle." A designer cannot easily veto a 700 ms TTI improvement. "Native just looks better" is not a winning argument; measured deltas are.

Third, hand designers the controls. Expose CSS variables for color, spacing, typography, and component tokens, and let designers iterate without going through engineering. Once designers feel ownership of the output, resistance to native drops sharply.

For PMs, lead with mobile usage data and OS-native picker preference. The moment "users already know iOS and Android pickers" lands as a fact rather than an opinion, the conversation shifts from preference to data.

Conclusion and three actions you can take this week

One-sentence summary: the 2026 web platform is mature enough that "build it from scratch" is no longer the safe default - "use native and layer design on top" is. The browser is filling its gaps faster than any custom solution can keep up, CSS supplies the tools to design native elements, and the cost of rebuilding compounds year over year in bundle size, accessibility debt, and maintenance burden. Native's standard does not break; your custom build will.

Three actions for this week:

  1. Swap one modal in your codebase to native <dialog>. A one-hour PR that recovers roughly 15 KB and raises accessibility immediately.
  2. Audit your component library inventory. List every third-party UI library used in your SPA and mark the ones that are replaceable by native. The list itself becomes your migration roadmap for the year.
  3. Propose a "native first" policy at the next design-system meeting. Combine it with handing designers CSS-variable control over the output. The combination passes far more often than either alone.
