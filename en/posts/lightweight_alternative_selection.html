Before You Switch to a Lightweight Alternative - Six Axes of Light and the Hidden Costs

Telling apart lightness that came from removing features and lightness that came from building well

Aug 24, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: When someone tells you that thing is bloated

Someone sends you a link. That thing is bloated, switch to this. You open it and find a column of names, each with a license and a rating beside it. The list is persuasive. But there is one question it never answers: what exactly is bothering you right now. A directory cannot decide that for you.

This article is not a summary of any such list, and it does not tell you which program to use or avoid. It is about the procedure of the decision itself: what to measure first, and what you quietly give up in exchange.

The question of how software got heavy in the first place is covered separately. This piece picks up at the next step, when you have already concluded that something is too heavy and have to act on it.

1. Why these lists keep appearing

In August 2026 a directory called debloat.dev circulated through developer communities. It gathers open source projects that stand in for manufacturer apps and proprietary cloud services, sorted into about ten product categories, showing for each entry what it replaces along with its license, a user rating and a discussion count. It is not an editorially vetted list; it is a list that users submit to and users rate.

The reasons such lists get rebuilt every few years narrow down to a handful.

  • You install a manufacturer app to use one peripheral and it arrives with an account signup, a resident background process and an auto-updater. What you actually needed was to change one setting.
  • Work that could finish on the device routes through an account and a server instead. When that service shuts down, the feature shuts down with it.
  • When the sense that install size and resident usage are large relative to what the thing does repeats across several products, that sense stops targeting individual products and starts targeting modern software as a category.

So a list does not resolve the complaint. It supplies candidates, and candidates are not criteria. Criteria you have to build yourself, and the first slot in that framework is the next section.

2. Light is not one word - six separate axes

Light bundles six different things, and more importantly those axes often move in opposite directions. Precompute and store, and startup gets faster while install size grows. Hold a cache in memory, and CPU and network work drop. Process on the device, and the network stops mattering while CPU and battery start to. So picking the light option is not a well-formed task. The only well-formed task is picking the option that is light on the axis you are actually stuck on.

Three questions are usually enough to narrow the axis to one.

  • How many times a day do you open it? Many times means startup time is the axis; leaving it open all day means resident memory is.
  • When does the friction appear: on launch, on switching windows, on saving, or while you are doing nothing at all? The timing names the axis.
  • What are your power and connectivity conditions? A desktop on mains power and a laptop on a plane give different answers about the same program.

3. Why switching on impressions goes wrong - what to measure and how

The first day after a switch is almost always fast, and the route to that impression is predictable. The newly installed side has nothing in it: no extensions, no plugins, no signed-in account, no years of history. The incumbent is carrying all of it. On top of that, memory does not store averages, it stores the worst moment. That one afternoon when a backup was running hardens into the sentence that program has always been slow.

So the decision has to rest on values rather than impressions. The method is not hard; it is a matter of keeping the order.

  • Measure a task, not a program. Pick one action you actually repeat during the day and watch only that one.
  • Record the before value first. Without it, the number you get afterwards has nothing to compare against and remains an impression. This single habit settles half the decision.
  • Separate cold runs from warm ones. A first launch after a reboot and a relaunch right after closing are different values, and which one dominates your day varies by person.
  • Repeat it and read the worst value, not the average. What wears people down is the occasional long stall, not the mean.
  • Hold conditions equal: same machine, same data, same time of day, no background jobs. A single difference can produce the entire result.
  • Measure again once your real data is loaded. Empty-state comparisons tilt toward the fresh install, and the ranking usually changes on this second pass.

Where people begin to perceive delay, and why the tail matters more than the average, is laid out in the piece on performance expectations. Which memory figure to read is covered in more depth in the piece on language server memory.

4. Light because features were removed, light because it was built well

This is the distinction that matters most here. Lightness comes in two kinds. One is light because the scope of the work was narrowed; the other is light because the same scope is done with fewer resources. On day one the numbers look similar. The difference shows up later.

The narrowed kind comes back to you the day work falls outside its scope. I never use that feature is true today and false on the day a collaborator sends you that format, the day your employer requires it, the day your hobby takes one more step. At that point three options remain: return to the heavier tool, keep both installed, or drop the work.

This is not an argument against narrowed tools. A tool that states what it does not do and does well inside that boundary is honest, and usually better inside that boundary. The expensive error is mistaking a narrowed scope for an engineering advantage, because then you decide without pricing in the return trip.

Five questions separate the two.

  • Does the documentation state what it does not do? A project that leads with its limits is the narrowed kind and the honest kind at once.
  • Is a missing feature marked as not yet or as never? The roadmap and the issue tracker answer this. If nothing answers it, do not default to reading it as not yet.
  • Does the resource advantage vanish when you enable one setting? Then it is a default, not a design. The heavier tool can usually turn the same thing off.
  • Does the advantage survive once the same data is loaded? A gap that exists only in the empty state is usually the gap of having done nothing yet.
  • Where did the hard part go? If something that used to be automatic is now manual, the cost did not disappear, it moved to you. Your time is a resource even though it never appears on a usage graph.

5. The hidden costs of switching

The price of a migration is not the install time. The real invoice arrives later, usually on the least convenient day. Here are the parts you can inspect in advance.

CostWhen it is chargedWhat you can check beforehand
Data migrationThe day you moveWhether an export format exists and what it drops
Your collaborators formatThe day files are exchangedWhether you can produce what they open, and survive a round trip
Learning and muscle memoryThe first few weeksHow different the shortcuts, vocabulary and layout are
Security update cadenceThe day a vulnerability is publishedA documented reporting process and the record of past responses
Project longevitySeveral years outRecent activity, number of maintainers, release intervals
The way backThe day you admit it failedWhether export truly works and the old tool accepts that format
FootprintImmediately after switchingWhether this is a replacement or an addition
  • Having an export button and exporting correctly are different statements. Press it before you decide, then open the resulting file back in the original tool.
  • Whether the tool is yours alone changes the board. If files are exchanged, the format your counterpart opens is a requirement, and that item is not negotiable.
  • The most commonly missed item is the case where this becomes an addition rather than a replacement. Install the new one and fail to remove the old one, and resource usage goes up. If no date is set for removing the old tool, the switch is not a saving.
  • How many days it took to fix a published vulnerability is a matter of record. That cadence outlives any feature list in its effect on you.

6. What open source gives you and what it does not

Most entries on alternative directories are open source. Saying that this is good or bad in itself gets you nowhere; separating what it grants from what it does not is what actually helps.

The most frequently misunderstood item on the second list is warranty. Widely used permissive licenses state in their own text that the software is provided as is, without warranty of any kind, including merchantability, fitness for a particular purpose and noninfringement. No license manufactures a response time or a review process either.

The other common slip is reading public and reviewed as the same word. The possibility of being inspected is not the fact of having been inspected. A star rating on a community-curated list is likewise a record of popularity, not of vetting.

  • You gain — the ability to verify the data format and where things are stored. When a service shuts down, your data is not held hostage.
  • You gain — the option to fork if maintenance stops. Whether anyone actually will is a separate matter, but the road is not blocked.
  • You gain — a public record of the discussion. When a defect was reported and how it was handled becomes searchable history.
  • You do not gain — warranty or liability. When something breaks, the license leaves no counterparty to claim against.
  • You do not gain — a support desk or a response time. You can open an issue; nothing promises an answer.
  • You can check — whether it is still maintained, whether a security reporting process is documented, whether changes get human review, whether dependency updates are automated, whether releases are signed. The checks published by OpenSSF Scorecard are a catalogue of exactly these signals.

None of those items tells you whether a project is good. What they tell you is what you are able to check. What open source actually buys you is verifiability rather than assured quality, and verification does not happen on its own.

7. Accessibility and internationalization - what the lighter side drops first

One of the most common ways to reduce weight is to drop a shared UI framework and draw the interface directly. Everything the framework used to handle disappears at the same moment. Those things happen to be invisible on the author's own screen, which is why they take so long to arrive as bug reports.

W3C accessibility guidance explains that users who are blind rely on a screen reader that reads the information on a page aloud, and it places the requirement that all functionality be available from a keyboard among its basic principles. Hand-drawn widgets frequently carry no name, role or state, and then a button that is visible on screen does not exist in audio. If one interaction remains mouse-only, that feature is a missing feature for some users.

Korean splits at two further points. The first is composition input. Hangul assembles jamo into a syllable, and the input method reports the partially assembled syllable to the program during that process. Text fields that implement this step by hand produce characters that vanish, duplicate or leave the caret jumping. None of it reproduces in an English-only environment.

The second is line breaking and typesetting. The Unicode line breaking algorithm describes Korean as mixing space-based and character-based breaking, classifying Hangul syllables as ideographic by default while leaving room to tailor them toward alphabetic behavior depending on the document style. In a program that implements typesetting itself, the handling of line ends splits precisely there.

  • Have a screen reader read one screen aloud. Whether button names are announced, and whether the current selection is announced, is the first gate.
  • Put the mouse away for a day. The point where the keyboard alone cannot get you to the end is that tool's accessibility boundary.
  • Type in a non-Latin script for real. Start with search boxes, name fields and tag inputs, where a hand-built input widget is most likely to live.
  • Drop a long paragraph into a narrow window. Where lines break, and whether punctuation gets pushed to the start of a line, exposes the level of the typesetting implementation.
  • These items are not a yes or no; they change between versions. If something is absent today, check how many years the request has been open in the issue tracker. A long-open request means low priority.

8. Not switching is also a choice

It rarely appears in the comparison table, but leaving things as they are is one of the candidates, and it is the only candidate whose cost is zero. A migration always carries real expenses: time to move, time to learn, time spent preparing a way back, and the defects you meet for the first time in a new tool.

If any one of the following four holds, this is not the moment to switch.

  • The axis has not been identified. It is bloated is not a basis for a decision; it is somebody else's conclusion.
  • There is no before value. If you do not know what it costs now, you cannot know later whether it improved.
  • A deadline is close. The defects in a new tool always surface at the worst possible hour.
  • It will be an addition rather than a replacement. If both survive, the switch is an expansion, not a saving.

And between full replacement and the status quo there is usually a middle slot: turning off features you do not use, keeping something from staying resident, lengthening a refresh interval. Nothing moves in that slot, so it reverses easily and costs little when it fails. Put that column in the same table when you evaluate a switch, and the conclusion frequently changes.

9. A decision checklist

Ask in order and stop at the first no. Stopping is also a conclusion.

What could not be confirmed

  • No program's resource usage was measured for this article. The subject is the axes and the order of measurement rather than individual figures, which is why no size or memory numbers appear in the text.
  • The criteria by which a user-submitted directory accepts entries are not published. Ratings and discussion counts are visible, but nothing states what those values guarantee.
  • The state of accessibility and non-Latin script support differs per project and changes between versions. What is written here is where support tends to arrive late, not the current state of any particular project.
  • No public statistics on how often people switch back were found. The advice to verify the exit in advance comes from the cost structure, not from data.

Conclusion: the list gives candidates, you build the criteria

Alternative directories are useful. They collect names you would struggle to find alone and give a sense of what can stand in for what. But there are things a list does not do. It does not choose your axis, it has not loaded your data, and it does not know which format your collaborators open.

Only a short procedure substitutes for those three. Narrow to one axis, record the value before you switch, and ask whether the lightness came from removing work or from doing the same work with less. Pass those and the rest is mostly small verification. Fail them and not switching wins.

One last thing. Lightness is a budget, not a target. A tool that uses few resources has saved that much; a tool that uses many has bought something with it. Once you know what it bought, the next question gets much simpler: do I need that, or did I only believe I did.
