Introduction: Know in Advance, or Face a Dispute

A home built over a lifetime, a small shop, some savings. To many people these are ordinary assets, yet the moment they pass to the next generation, an unfamiliar process called tax arrives with them. It is easy to think inheritance and gift tax are only for the wealthy, but in Korea even owning a single apartment in the metropolitan area can be enough to face them. These two taxes are an area you can fully prepare for if you understand them early, and equally an area that turns into penalty taxes and family disputes if you pass through it unaware.

This article is an informational guide for ordinary readers meeting inheritance and gift tax for the first time. It covers how the two differ, who is taxed and on what, the progressive rate structure, the key deductions such as the lump-sum, spouse, and gift deductions, filing and payment deadlines, the mistakes people commonly make, and the basic direction of legitimate tax saving. It centers on the Korean system. Because rates and deduction amounts change with amendments, the figures here reflect the rules in force as re-checked against National Tax Service guidance on September 29, 2026, and reform proposals that have not yet passed the National Assembly are marked separately.

Inheritance Tax vs Gift Tax: What Differs

Both inheritance tax and gift tax are levied when property moves from one person to another without consideration, that is, for free. Because taxing freely received wealth is their shared essence, Korea governs both under a single law, the Inheritance Tax and Gift Tax Act, and their rate structures are identical. Yet one decisive difference remains: the timing of the transfer.

The Timing - After Death vs During Life

Inheritance tax applies when property passes to heirs because a person has died; it is an after-death tax. Gift tax applies when an asset owner transfers property to someone else while still alive, without consideration; it is a during-life tax. Give the same 100 million won to a child, and if the parent is alive it is a gift tax question, while if it passes after death it becomes an inheritance tax question.

A Different Taxable Unit

The two also differ in whose perspective the tax is computed from. Korea's inheritance tax is closer to an estate-tax method that first computes tax on the entire estate left by the deceased. Gift tax, by contrast, is computed on each recipient individually. Because of this, even the same total amount can carry a different overall burden depending on whether it passes at once as an inheritance or is split among several recipients as gifts.

The government gave advance legislative notice on March 19, 2025 of an amendment to the Inheritance Tax and Gift Tax Act that would switch this method to an inheritance-acquisition tax, which taxes the share each heir actually receives (Ministry of Economy and Finance press release). As of September 29, 2026, however, the National Tax Service's inheritance tax guidance still follows the current method of subtracting deductions from the deceased's whole estate, and no primary source confirming that the switch has passed the National Assembly and taken effect could be found. Read the inheritance-acquisition tax as a government proposal, not yet in force; this article explains the current estate-tax method.

In short: inheritance tax is based on the whole estate after death; gift tax is based on each recipient during life. Because timing and the taxable unit differ, the same wealth can carry a different burden.

Who Is Taxed, and on What

To understand any tax, first identify who pays it (the taxpayer) and what it is levied on (the taxable object).

What Property Is Taxable

The taxable object of inheritance and gift tax goes far beyond the cash and deposits people first imagine. Real estate (homes, land, commercial property), financial assets such as stocks and funds, cars, memberships, goodwill, parts of insurance proceeds and severance pay, and rights such as receivables - in principle every economic value that can be converted into money is included. Even something the recipient did not perceive as an asset can fall within the tax.

Who Pays

Gift tax is, in principle, paid by the recipient of the property. Inheritance tax is paid by the heirs and legatees who receive the property; when several inherit together, each bears a duty in proportion to their share, but a structure of joint and several liability can apply. In other words, if one heir fails to pay their share of tax, the burden can reach the other heirs, so it matters that the family sees the tax process through together to the end.

Resident Status Matters

Whether the deceased or the donor is a Korean resident or non-resident can change the scope of taxation (domestic assets only, or overseas assets too) and which deductions apply. According to National Tax Service guidance, when the deceased was a non-resident only the basic deduction applies and the other inheritance deductions are not available. Where there are overseas assets or family living abroad, the analysis grows especially complex, so do not conclude from general explanations alone; a professional check is needed.

The Progressive Rate Structure - The "50%" Myth

The most common fear around inheritance and gift tax is that half of the wealth is taken away. That fear comes from misreading the progressive rate.

What a Progressive Rate Is

Korea's inheritance tax and gift tax share the same five-band progressive rate that rises as the tax base grows. The rate table posted in the National Tax Service's inheritance and gift tax calculation flowcharts is as follows (checked September 29, 2026).

Tax baseRateProgressive deduction
Up to 100 million won10%None
Over 100 million up to 500 million won20%10 million won
Over 500 million up to 1 billion won30%60 million won
Over 1 billion up to 3 billion won40%160 million won
Over 3 billion won50%460 million won

The computed tax can be found in one step as "tax base × rate for that band − progressive deduction". The progressive deduction folds in the effect of taxing the lower bands at their lower rates.

The Top Rate Is Not Your Tax

The key is that a progressive rate does not multiply the whole amount by the top rate at once. The amount within lower bands is taxed at lower rates, and only the amount in the higher band is taxed at the higher rate. Using the table above, a tax base of 1.5 billion won gives 1.5 billion × 40% − 160 million = 440 million won, about 29 percent of the tax base. As a result, the ratio actually borne against total assets, the effective rate, is often lower than the nominal top rate. Remember firmly: reaching the 50 percent band does not mean paying half your wealth.

The Tax Base Is Not the Whole Amount Received

Another vital point is that the tax base, the amount the rate is applied to, is not the entire property received. From the received property you subtract debts, funeral costs, and the various deductions explained below, and the rate is applied only to what remains. So when deductions are large enough, it is not unusual for the tax actually due to be zero even when there are assets.

Note: the rate table above shows the current values confirmed against National Tax Service guidance on September 29, 2026. Rate bands can change through amendments, so at the time of filing re-check the then-current standard through the National Tax Service Hometax auto-calculator or a tax professional.

Inheritance Tax Deductions - Lump-Sum and Spouse

In inheritance tax, the single most important factor in the real burden is not the rate but the deductions. Korea's inheritance tax offers several that greatly ease the burden on ordinary households.

Basic and Lump-Sum Deductions

When an inheritance begins, a basic deduction of 200 million won applies, along with personal deductions for children, minors, the elderly, and persons with disabilities. National Tax Service guidance describes the rule as applying the larger of "basic deduction plus other personal deductions" or the lump-sum deduction of 500 million won. Most ordinary households choose the lump-sum deduction because it is simpler and often larger. Thanks to these deductions, the National Tax Service states that in general no inheritance tax is due on an estate valued up to 500 million won (1 billion won if the deceased's legal spouse survives).

The Spouse Inheritance Deduction

One of the most powerful deductions is the spouse inheritance deduction. Under National Tax Service guidance, if the spouse actually inherits nothing or less than 500 million won, 500 million won is deducted; if the spouse inherits 500 million won or more, the amount actually inherited is deducted, up to the statutory limit and no more than 3 billion won. To claim more than 500 million won, the spouse's share of the estate must be divided within nine months from the day after the inheritance tax filing deadline. This is why a split design - inherit to the spouse first, then pass to children later - is often discussed. However, when the spouse later dies, that property can be subject to inheritance tax again, and prior gifts the spouse received before the inheritance began can affect the limit calculation, so careful judgment is needed.

Other Deductions

  • Financial asset deduction: a deduction within a set limit for net financial assets such as deposits and stocks. Financial assets held in another person's name that were not reported by the filing deadline are excluded.
  • Co-residence home deduction: when a child who meets certain conditions and owns no home inherits a house long shared with a parent, 100% of the home's value is deducted up to 600 million won.
  • Family business and farming inheritance deductions: a qualifying family business may receive an additional deduction with a limit of 30 billion to 60 billion won depending on how long the deceased ran it, and farming inheritance up to 3 billion won (demanding requirements and post-management conditions).
In short: the key to inheritance tax is deductions, not the rate. The 500 million won lump-sum deduction and the spouse deduction (at least 500 million won) alone can bring the tax to zero up to a considerable size. The requirements must be met, and the amounts can be amended.

Gift Tax Deductions - The Gift Deduction

Gift tax has a gift deduction that subtracts a set amount based on the relationship between the recipient and the donor. It is a device that frees gifts within the family from tax up to a certain range.

Limits by Relationship

The gift deduction limit depends on whom you received the gift from (the donor group), and each limit applies on a ten-year cumulative basis. The amounts in National Tax Service guidance are as follows (checked September 29, 2026).

Donor (relationship to the recipient)Deduction limitUnit
Spouse600 million won10-year cumulative
Lineal ascendant (parent, grandparent, etc.)50 million won (20 million won if the recipient is a minor)10-year cumulative
Lineal descendant (child, grandchild, etc.)50 million won10-year cumulative
Other relatives10 million won10-year cumulative
Marriage or childbirth (from a lineal ascendant, on top of the above)100 million won combinedLifetime total

Donors in the same group are counted together. For example, even if you receive separately from your father and your grandmother, both belong to the lineal-ascendant group, so they share one 50 million won limit (20 million won for a minor) over ten years.

The Ten-Year Aggregation Is Crucial

The point most often missed is that this limit applies not once but cumulatively over ten years. According to National Tax Service guidance, if gifts received from the same person within ten years before the gift date total 10 million won or more in taxable value, they are added back and the tax is recomputed. The idea that splitting gifts to give often creates a fresh limit each time is simply wrong. Also, when a gift skips a generation - for example a grandparent giving directly to a grandchild rather than to a child - the computed tax is surcharged by 30% (40% if a minor receives more than 2 billion won).

Marriage and Childbirth Gift Deduction

For gifts made on or after January 1, 2024, a marriage and childbirth gift deduction applies. Property received from a lineal ascendant within two years before or after the marriage registration date, or within two years from a child's birth (for adoption, the adoption registration date), can be deducted separately from the basic 50 million won deduction, with a combined limit of 100 million won for marriage and childbirth. You cannot claim 100 million won each from parents and grandparents; all lineal ascendants together share 100 million won, and it does not apply retroactively to property received on or before December 31, 2023 (Korea Policy Briefing, National Tax Service guidance). Re-check the detailed requirements with the National Tax Service and a tax accountant at the time of the marriage or birth.

Filing and Payment - Miss the Deadline, Pay Penalties

Inheritance and gift tax are taxes you compute, file, and pay yourself. Miss the deadline and penalty taxes can sharply raise the burden.

Filing Deadlines

Under National Tax Service guidance, inheritance tax must be filed and paid within six months from the end of the month containing the date the inheritance began (the date of death) (nine months if the deceased or an heir has an address abroad), and gift tax within three months from the end of the month in which the gift is received. If the last day falls on a public holiday or Saturday, the deadline moves to the next day. For example, a gift received on June 10 has a gift tax filing deadline of September 30.

Voluntary Filing Benefit and Penalties

Submitting the return within the statutory deadline earns a 3% filing tax credit. Conversely, failing to file, under-reporting, or paying late adds non-filing and under-reporting penalty taxes plus a late-payment penalty. Penalties are added on top of the principal tax and are by no means small, so the idea of paying only if caught later is very dangerous.

Installments and Multi-Year Payment

There are ways to spread a large bill. Under the National Tax Service's inheritance tax guidance, if the tax due exceeds 10 million won, it can be paid in installments within two months after the filing and payment deadline. If the tax due exceeds 20 million won, deferred annual payment can be requested; for inheritance tax the period is set within up to ten years (five years if the inheritance began on or before December 31, 2021), with each annual installment above 10 million won. Deferred payment carries an additional charge, and payment in kind with real estate or stocks is also possible when requirements are met. The deferred-payment period for gift tax differs from inheritance tax, so check it separately. In an inheritance short on cash, whether to use these measures matters, so review them with a professional at the filing stage.

Common Mistakes and Family Disputes

Inheritance and gifts are not only about money but an area entangled with family relationships, so a mistake easily becomes a dispute.

  • Omissions that "I didn't know" won't excuse: large sums a parent moved to a child's account during life, or property title transfers, can later be treated as gifts and taxed retroactively.
  • Nominee or roundabout deals: borrowing another family member's name, or selling below market, can be deemed a gift or challenged as an improper act.
  • Forgetting the add-back of prior gifts: property the deceased gave to heirs within ten years, or to non-heirs within five years, before the inheritance began is added back to the estate (National Tax Service calculation flowchart). Planning without knowing this can erase the expected effect.
  • Disputes over dividing the estate: if heirs cannot agree on who gets what, the filing itself is delayed, leading to penalty taxes, and the division deadline needed for the spouse deduction can also be missed.
Tip: gathering the family while a parent is alive to map out assets and debts together, keeping records and evidence for large transfers, and filing within the deadline can by itself greatly reduce later disputes and tax risk.

The Basics of Tax Saving (General Principles)

Tax saving is entirely different from tax evasion. Evasion is illegally hiding or deceiving; saving is lawfully using the deductions and timing the system permits and reporting all of it in full. The following is general direction only, and whether it applies depends on personal circumstances.

Spreading Through Lifetime Gifts

In a progressive structure, a single large tax base meets higher bands. So splitting some assets through lifetime gifts to spread the base can lower the overall burden. The recipient must then file gift tax within three months, and filing even when the gift is within the deduction limit leaves a record of the amount and date. The add-back of prior gifts seen above and the cash cost at the time of gifting must be weighed as well.

Valuation Timing and Asset Type

Assets expected to rise in value may be better transferred early while the valuation is low. Conversely, complex assets like real estate or a family business can cause a loss if moved carelessly, as other taxes such as acquisition tax pile on.

Maximizing Deductions

Preparing the requirements for each deduction - spouse, lump-sum, financial asset, co-residence home, marriage and childbirth - in advance can lawfully cut the burden a great deal. The key is to confirm, before the inheritance or gift occurs, which deduction is available under which requirements, and to file within the deadline.

Important: a simple formula like "gifting in advance always wins" is dangerous. You must weigh asset type, family structure, timing, the add-back of prior gifts, and other taxes; a wrong design can cause a loss. Always consult a tax professional.

Real Estate, Family Business, and Other Cautions

For ordinary households, the large share of inherited and gifted property is usually real estate. With real estate, market valuation easily becomes the issue, and even the same home can produce a different tax base depending on the valuation method. Gifting real estate also triggers acquisition tax separately from gift tax, so do not judge by gift tax alone.

Family business inheritance offers a large deduction (a limit of 30 billion to 60 billion won depending on how long the deceased ran the business) when a child takes over a family-run enterprise, but the requirements on industry, size, and keeping employment, and the post-management condition of maintaining the business for a set period after inheritance, are very demanding. Failing to meet them can claw back the deduction received, so business succession must be designed with a professional over the long term.

The guidance page for the 2026 tax reform plan, announced by the Ministry of Economy and Finance on August 3, 2026 and finalized as the government proposal on September 1, lists "redesigning the family business inheritance deduction (adjusting eligible industries, rationalizing deduction requirements, etc.)" and "new tax support for business succession to third parties" as agenda items. These are a government proposal, not yet in force. The specific requirements and figures could not be confirmed from the original text this time, and statutory changes take effect only after the National Assembly passes them, so judge by the current family business deduction requirements.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Will I really get caught if I don't file inheritance tax?

The National Tax Service can review financial data, property registrations, and various filings broadly, so large transfers are often confirmed after the fact. Not filing can lead to the principal tax plus penalties being assessed, and the 3% filing credit is lost, so even when it is unclear whether tax applies, it is safer to check with a professional.

Q. Is helping with a parent's medical bills or living costs a gift?

Ordinary support for socially accepted purposes such as care, education, or treatment is generally understood not to be treated as a gift. But moving large assets under that label can be viewed as a gift, so if the amount is large, keep evidence and confirm with a professional.

Q. Where can I try a calculation?

The National Tax Service Hometax offers an inheritance and gift tax auto-calculator, and for an accurate filing it is safer to go through a tax accountant or tax firm. The rate table here lets you gauge the size of the computed tax, but results vary with asset valuation and deduction requirements, so it must not be used as the basis for an actual filing.

Conclusion - Understand the Structure, Plan Early, With a Professional

Inheritance and gift tax are easy to put off because they feel complex and frightening. Yet as this article shows, the core is surprisingly simple. First, inheritance tax is after death and gift tax is during life, and their timing and taxable units differ. Second, the rate is a five-band progressive structure from 10 to 50 percent, so the top rate is not your tax, and the real burden is governed by deductions. Third, understanding deductions such as the 500 million won lump-sum, spouse, and gift deductions and preparing their requirements in advance can lawfully cut the burden a great deal. Fourth, missing the filing deadline (six months for inheritance, three for gifts) or the add-back of prior gifts brings penalties and disputes.

And the most important final note. This article is general guidance for information and education only, not tax or legal advice. Rates, deduction amounts, aggregation periods, and deadlines can change with amendments, and reforms not yet in force, such as the switch to an inheritance-acquisition tax, are under discussion. The accurate calculation and design for your case vary greatly by asset type and family situation. When you actually need to file, pay, or plan, always consult the National Tax Service Hometax, your district tax office, or a licensed tax accountant or tax firm about your own case. Knowing in advance, preparing in advance, and working with a professional - that is how inheritance and gifting turn from a burden into an orderly process.

## 1. Rate table (shared by inheritance and gift tax)

| Tax base | Rate | Progressive deduction |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 100 million won | 10% | None |
| Over 100 million up to 500 million won | 20% | 10 million won |
| Over 500 million up to 1 billion won | 30% | 60 million won |
| Over 1 billion up to 3 billion won | 40% | 160 million won |
| Over 3 billion won | 50% | 460 million won |

Computed tax = tax base × rate − progressive deduction
Example: tax base 1.5 billion won → 1.5 billion × 40% − 160 million = 440 million won (about 29% of the base)
