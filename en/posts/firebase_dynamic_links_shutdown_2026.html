After the Firebase Dynamic Links Shutdown: Rebuilding Deep Links From Scratch

Universal Links, AASA, and KakaoTalk sharing - what it took to replace FDL with our own infrastructure

Jul 10, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: What We Learned Where FDL Used to Be

On August 25, 2025, Google fully shut down Firebase Dynamic Links (FDL). From that day on, every previously issued FDL link - whether hosted on a page.link subdomain or a custom domain - started returning an HTTP 404 for any new request. Links printed on QR codes, links already sent through KakaoTalk or SMS, and short links baked into marketing campaigns all died overnight. As of July 2026 it has been nearly a year, but the homework this shutdown left behind is still very much unfinished. Teams that delayed migration, and teams that removed FDL but missed edge cases like deferred deep links or KakaoTalk sharing, are still discovering problems late.

I came back to this topic for a simple reason. The FDL shutdown was not just one service disappearing - it broke the lazy assumption that "deep linking is just an SDK you bolt on." This piece was prompted by a Korean developer's own migration story, shared on GeekNews, about rebuilding this infrastructure from scratch. Rather than summarize that story, I want to lay out, in my own words, the underlying concepts of deep linking and the concrete problems you actually hit when you build this yourself - why it is hard, and what you need to prepare.

1. What Exactly Broke - Three Roles FDL Was Quietly Carrying

When people hear FDL is gone, the first reaction is usually "oh, short links stopped working." In reality FDL was quietly carrying three distinct roles at once, and all three collapsed together.

1.1 Routing Hidden Behind a Short Link

The most visible role was issuing short, clean URLs. But the real value sat behind that: a single link would send an iOS click to the App Store or the app, an Android click to the Play Store or the app, and a desktop click to a web page - a routing layer that auto-detected platform and picked a destination. Losing that routing logic is the first break.

1.2 Store Redirection When the App Is Missing

The second is the fallback that sends users without the app to a store gracefully. This matters more than it sounds for marketing: if a click lands on a blank page or an error because the app is missing, the user simply leaves. FDL handled this branch automatically; now someone has to write that logic by hand.

1.3 Deferred Deep Links - Carrying Click Context Across an Install

The hardest of the three is deferred deep linking: sending a user who clicked a link to a specific product or event page - without the app installed - to that exact screen on the very first launch, after they go through the store and install it. All three roles rode on FDL alone, so when FDL disappeared, deep links, store fallback, and deferred deep linking all broke at the same time.

2. Rebuilding the Basics - Universal Links and App Links

Before building anything yourself, you need to understand the underlying mechanics precisely, because you are now doing by hand what a tool like FDL used to do for you.

2.1 iOS Universal Links and AASA

Universal Links on iOS route an ordinary https:// URL straight into the app. The core piece is the AASA (apple-app-site-association) file. It must be hosted at /.well-known/apple-app-site-association on your domain, over HTTPS, with no redirects, and under 128KB. On the app side, you register the domain in the Associated Domains entitlement with an applinks: prefix - a two-way trust relationship where both sides must point at each other for verification to hold. A detail teams commonly miss: since iOS 14, Apple caches the AASA file on its own CDN, so a fix on your server can lag behind for hours before Apple's edge picks it up. During development, the ?mode=developer entitlement bypasses that cache and fetches directly from your server, which makes debugging far less painful.

2.2 Android App Links and assetlinks.json

The Android counterpart is App Links, verified through /.well-known/assetlinks.json. This file must declare your app's package name and the SHA-256 fingerprint of its signing certificate. The single most common failure in practice is a mismatch caused by Play App Signing: the fingerprint you registered in assetlinks.json does not match the fingerprint of the actually distributed, Play-re-signed build. This file must also respond with HTTP 200 and an application/json content type with no redirects, and verification after install runs asynchronously, taking roughly 20 seconds to complete.

2.3 Why Custom Schemes Still Matter

Even with Universal Links and App Links in place, you cannot fully retire a custom URL scheme like myapp://. In environments where OS-level routing does not behave as expected - the KakaoTalk in-app browser being the prime example in Korea - a custom scheme can actually be the more reliable path. In practice, teams end up with Universal Links as the default and a custom-scheme fallback running in parallel for specific entry points.

3. Deferred Deep Linking, the Hardest Piece

3.1 Why It Is Uniquely Hard

Universal Links and App Links assume the app is already installed. The problem is what happens when it is not. The moment a user is routed to the store to install, the parameters from the original clicked link are gone - the store does not automatically hand "which link this user clicked" back to the app after install completes. Deferred deep linking therefore becomes a problem your server has to stitch together separately: the click-time context and the first-launch context.

3.2 The Limits of Android's Install Referrer

Android at least has an official channel: the Google Play Install Referrer API. On first launch, an app can retrieve which referrer parameters were attached at install time through this API. The catch is that this only works for installs that went through the Play Store - it does not help with sideloading or other app stores.

3.3 On iOS, You End Up With Probabilistic Matching

iOS has no equivalent official channel at all. Choosing to self-build means falling back on fingerprint-based probabilistic matching: storing device information, IP address, timestamp, and User-Agent at click time, then comparing that same combination against what is observed at first launch, and inferring "this install probably came from that click." This is inherently approximate, and mis-matches can occur when multiple users click from the same network around the same time. Part of why platforms like Branch and Adjust charge what they do is that they push this probabilistic-matching accuracy up using large-scale device graphs.

4. What You Need to Rebuild This Yourself - A Checklist

If you are replacing FDL with your own infrastructure, you need at minimum these four pillars in place.

4.1 A Domain and Static File Hosting

Secure a domain (or subdomain) dedicated to deep linking, and serve AASA and assetlinks.json reliably from its /.well-known/ path. The key requirement is that these files must be served with no redirects, always from the same domain, with caching accounted for. If you put a CDN in front, you need a cache-invalidation strategy alongside it.

4.2 Server-Side Routing Logic

You need server logic that reads the User-Agent, distinguishes iOS, Android, and desktop, and routes each to Universal Links, App Links, or a web page respectively. On top of that, given the fundamental uncertainty of not knowing in advance whether the app is installed, you must prepare both a fallback for the no-app case (store redirect) and handling for the app-present case (custom scheme or Universal Link).

4.3 Short Link and Parameter Design

To avoid the risk of another external service's end-of-life, it is safer to manage the short-link generation and resolution pipeline yourself, on your own domain. Keep a store that maps short codes (base62 encoding, for instance) to the original parameters, and restore the full parameters at click time to drive routing. It also helps to prepare a long fallback URL carrying the full parameters, so that if the shortening step fails or lags, the system degrades gracefully rather than breaking outright.

5. A Korean Wrinkle - The KakaoTalk Sharing Trap

5.1 An In-App Browser That Defeats the Standards

This is where things go beyond what the iOS and Android official docs alone can tell you. A link shared through KakaoTalk opens inside its built-in in-app browser, not the user's default browser. In that environment, OS-level interception for Universal Links and App Links commonly fails to trigger as expected. In other words, you can implement the iOS and Android standards perfectly and still see deep links silently fail on traffic coming through KakaoTalk.

5.2 Branching on executionParams

One practical workaround is to use parameters like executionParams in the KakaoLink (KakaoTalk share SDK) message. By attaching execution-time parameters when the message is sent, you can design a branch - on the app side and the server side - so users who already have the app open it instantly via a custom scheme, while users without it are routed to a dedicated web landing page. The original author called this problem the "anatomy of a dead short link" (see references) - a phrase that captures well how a domestic messenger ecosystem can introduce a wrinkle that no amount of standard-spec compliance alone resolves.

6. Commercial SaaS vs. Building It Yourself

6.1 Comparing the Cost Structures

Google's officially suggested alternatives after the FDL shutdown are commercial deep-linking and attribution platforms - Branch, Adjust, AppsFlyer OneLink, and Kochava. Here is a rough comparison of the cost structures.

6.2 How I Would Decide

My own rule of thumb is straightforward. If your deep-linking needs are mostly simple share-link routing at modest traffic, the upfront investment of building it yourself can beat a recurring SaaS subscription on total cost of ownership. If large-scale marketing attribution, fraud detection, or sophisticated cross-store deferred deep linking is directly tied to core business metrics, a proven commercial platform's accuracy and reduced maintenance burden are usually worth paying for. The real lesson of this shutdown, in my view, is that many teams never actually made this trade-off consciously - because FDL was free, the decision was never forced.

7. A Migration Checklist

  • Confirm whether existing link and click data was exported before the FDL cutoff - if not, plan the redesign assuming it is unrecoverable
  • Secure a dedicated deep-linking domain and prepare to host /.well-known/apple-app-site-association and /.well-known/assetlinks.json
  • Update app code for iOS Associated Domains entitlement and Android App Links intent filters
  • Build server-side routing logic - branch iOS/Android/desktop by User-Agent and implement the store fallback
  • Confirm whether you actually need deferred deep linking - if not, the scope of a self-built solution shrinks significantly
  • Test traffic that goes through the KakaoTalk in-app browser separately, and check whether an executionParams-style branch is needed
  • Build a short-link pipeline (creation, resolution, redirect) on your own domain, or choose a commercial service
  • Get a total-cost-of-ownership quote for Branch, Adjust, or AppsFlyer OneLink and compare it against building it yourself
  • Plan deployment and rollback with AASA/assetlinks.json caching delays in mind (Apple's CDN, Android's ~20-second verification)
  • Check whether old links already printed on QR codes, sent via SMS, or embedded in emails need a redirect handled

Conclusion: The FDL Shutdown Is Not a Warning, It Already Happened

As of July 2026, the FDL shutdown is no longer "a future to prepare for" - it is "a past that already happened." Even so, it stays relevant because the habit of treating deep-linking infrastructure as a single SDK you bolt on still lingers on many teams. Universal Links and App Links are a trust relationship your domain and your app verify with each other; deferred deep linking is fundamentally a gap your server has to fill probabilistically; and a domestic messenger environment like KakaoTalk layers yet another exception on top. Once you understand these three layers, whether you choose a commercial SaaS or build it yourself, you can at least decide with a clear picture of what you are buying and what you are taking on. My own recommendation: build it yourself if your traffic is modest, and lean on a commercial platform if attribution is directly tied to revenue. Whichever you choose, it is worth walking through this migration checklist yourself at least once.
