After the Stuck Time Disappeared - Where Skill Was Actually Being Made

Practice scores go up while exam scores go down - how learning and performance come apart, what is different from the calculator debate, and a rule for when to struggle and when to delegate

Aug 25, 2026 Trends & Culture

Introduction: When the answer takes three seconds

You paste the error message and three seconds later the fixed code comes back. You apply it, watch it run, move to the next thing. Nothing about that is wrong. The trouble is that what used to sit inside those three seconds is very hard to see afterwards.

What used to sit there was thirty minutes. Reading the error again, naming two or three candidate causes, ruling out two of them, landing on the third. The output of those thirty minutes was fixed code, but not only fixed code. What also stayed behind was knowing where to look first the next time a similar symptom appeared.

What the original argument says

An essay putting this head-on circulated in developer communities recently: AI Coding will Prevent Expertise by Lars Faye. Its thesis is that a way of working which puts code generation ahead of understanding blocks the formation of the next generation's expertise altogether. The author calls this the skilled orchestrator paradox: directing AI agents well requires being able to judge their output, and that judgement is precisely what fails to develop if you keep handing the work to the agent. His prescription is a slower, more deliberate path — verify what you get against official documentation, human peers and real trial and error.

To be fair to it, this is an argument rather than a proposition proven by data. And the part that carries the weight is not the conclusion but the premise — that skill is made out of friction. Whether that premise holds, where exactly a tool removes the friction, and whether any of it has actually been measured is what follows.

1. Skill is made in the stuck time

Cognitive psychology already has a name for this place. Robert Bjork coined desirable difficulties in 1994: conditions that feel distinctly harder while you practise, yet produce learning that lasts.

  • Spacing — leave gaps instead of massing practice. Retrieving something you have half forgotten is harder, and that difficulty is what strengthens storage
  • Retrieval practice — pull from memory instead of rereading. Rereading gives you the feeling of knowing; retrieval tells you whether you actually do
  • Interleaving — mix problem types instead of blocking them. You have to choose the method first each time, which slows you down, but real situations always arrive mixed
  • Generation — produce the answer yourself instead of receiving it. Even a wrong attempt changes how you take in the explanation that follows
  • Varying conditions — practise only in one environment and you build memory that is attached to that environment

The heart of the concept is not the list but the distinction under it. Learning and performance are different things, and they sometimes move in opposite directions. The conditions that make practice performance look best can produce the least durable learning. It is the same shape as cramming, which lifts the score the night before and leaves nothing a month later.

Manu Kapur's productive failure work shows the same direction experimentally. His 2012 study was a pre-post quasi-experiment with 133 ninth graders at a Singapore public school. The same teacher taught both conditions, four fifty-minute periods each. One group wrestled with problems without guidance before being taught the concept; the other received direct instruction first. The group that struggled first did better on the post-test, and notably did better on higher-order items the lesson had never covered. The reading is not that failing first supplied the answer, but that it exposed the gaps in their own knowledge first, so the explanation landed differently.

2. Practice goes up, the exam goes down

Two results can be read as that same separation reappearing with AI tools. The populations and tasks differ, but they point at the same spot.

StudySample and designMeasuredResult
Turkish high school maths experiment (Bastani et al.) Roughly 1,000 students randomly assigned to three arms — a plain chatbot, a tutor-style interface built with teacher input to give hints rather than answers, and a control arm with only textbook and notes Performance on practice problems and later exam scores, measured separately On practice problems the tutor arm was 127 percent better and the plain chatbot 48 percent better. On the exam, the plain chatbot arm scored 17 percent below control. The tutor arm essentially erased that harm, but no positive effect was observed
Coding skill formation experiment (Anthropic, released January 2026) 52 participants, mostly junior. Each had used Python at least weekly for over a year and was new to the target library. Randomly assigned to an AI condition or a hand-coding condition A quiz immediately after implementing two features, covering debugging, code reading, code writing and conceptual understanding AI condition averaged 50 percent, hand-coding 67 percent (effect size 0.738, p=0.01). The difference in completion time was about two minutes and not significant

The maths numbers get flattened in retelling. The 127 percent is practice performance, not an exam score. What the tutor arm accomplished on the exam was removing the damage the plain chatbot caused; it did not make students better than the control arm. That distinction is the learning-performance split itself. Performance while holding the tool nearly tripled, while capability after putting it down did not rise to match.

The coding experiment has the same shape. Completion times were roughly equal, so performance in the moment did not diverge. What diverged was the quiz taken once the tool was gone. The researchers state the limits plainly: the sample was small and measurement happened right after the task. Whether an immediate score predicts long-run skill development is not something this design can answer.

3. What disappears when the tool clears the raw material

This is the axis of the whole piece. When the answer arrives instantly, the stuck stretch disappears, and so does whatever was being built inside it. The catch is that the product of that stretch was never the code. Code was the visible result; the real output accumulated somewhere you cannot see.

  • Restating the problem in your own words — being stuck forces you to rewrite the problem, and rewriting it changes what you should have been asking in the first place. Pasting skips the step entirely
  • Forming a hypothesis and confirming it is wrong — you have to delete three wrong hypotheses before the fourth means anything. To someone who never deleted any, the fourth is just a memorised answer
  • A symptom-to-cause lookup table — the sense that this error usually comes from over there. It does not transfer through explanation and accumulates only in proportion to encounters
  • A causal model of the system — the picture of what moves over here when you touch there. It forms only in people who poked around while stuck
  • The this-will-not-work signal — the unease that arrives before the reasoning does. It is what actually earns its keep in review, and it scales with the volume of failures you have lived through
  • Naming what you do not know — building a search query is the act of putting a name on your own ignorance. If throwing raw symptoms produces an answer, the naming step gets skipped
  • A feel for the cost of failure — how far back you can undo and where undo stops. Usually learned once, expensively

What fades is not only capability. Your read on your own capability blurs along with it. A randomised trial METR published in July 2025 shows the structure. Sixteen experienced open-source developers were assigned 246 tasks with AI tooling allowed or blocked by condition, working in repositories they had maintained for around five years on average. Completion time in the AI-allowed condition was 19 percent longer. Beforehand the same developers expected a 24 percent speedup, and afterwards they reported a 20 percent speedup. Perception and measurement had opposite signs.

With sixteen participants and an early-2025 tool stack, the speed figure should not be lifted into a general law. What earns its place here is the fact that perception diverged from measurement at all. If it diverged for experienced developers, then someone without a baseline has no way to detect the divergence. The feeling that you are getting better is not evidence that you are.

4. Calculators, compilers and search heard the same warning

This is the point to stop and check. The shape of this worry is not new, and the earlier cases mostly did not play out as feared. Laying the comparison down before asserting anything is the honest move.

ToolThe worry at the timeWhat was actually observedWhat is different now
Calculator Mental and written arithmetic collapses A 1986 meta-analysis of 79 studies found that at every grade except fourth, students using calculators alongside instruction did better on average at paper-and-pencil computation and word problems, with better attitude and confidence. But sustained use in grade four hindered basic skill development for the average student Narrow output range, deterministic, and sanity-checking by estimation costs almost nothing
High-level languages and compilers You lose machine-level and memory intuition Most work genuinely did move up a layer. The places needing low-level intuition narrowed to performance and memory problems, but did not vanish Same input, same output. When it is wrong, usually it will not compile at all
Search engines Memory moves outside and you know less yourself —note Results arrive with sources and with several documents that disagree. The choosing stays with the person
AI coding tools The path by which skill forms is severed A handful of immediate measurements. See the next section on long-term tracking —

note No conclusive material on the long-term outcome for the search-engine generation turned up. It is listed in the section on what could not be confirmed.

The part of the calculator result worth staring at is the exception, not the average. Harm appeared at exactly one stage: where the basic skill was still forming. For grades where written arithmetic was already automatic, the calculator removed drudgery and let attention go to problem structure. For the grade where it was not yet automatic, it spent the chance to make it automatic. Same tool, opposite result. The thing to worry about was never the tool but when the tool arrived.

Here is what is the same. In all three cases the ability did not vanish; the layer of ability that mattered moved upward, and the upper layer attached better to people who had passed through the lower one once. What is different is the nature of the output. Calculators and compilers are deterministic, and search hands you sources and leaves the choosing to you. AI coding tools are none of those. Their output range is wide, probabilistic, and arrives already looking finished. The cost of noticing it is wrong is far higher than with earlier tools, and the capacity to pay that cost is precisely the capacity under dispute.

5. The state of the evidence: nobody has watched three years

The fact most often dropped from this debate is simple. Everything we have is a short measurement.

  • What exists — immediate measures: a quiz right after the task (52 participants), a unit exam (about 1,000 students), completion time (16 developers, 246 tasks). All taken directly after the tool was removed
  • What exists — short neural and behavioural measures: the 2025 MIT Media Lab EEG study on essay writing is widely cited. Fifty-four participants were split across chatbot, search engine and no-tool conditions for three sessions each, with conditions swapped in a fourth. The chatbot condition showed the lowest engagement across neural, linguistic and behavioural measures. But only 18 people took part in the fourth session, and the task was essay writing, which does not transfer straight to coding
  • What barely exists — long-term tracking: studies following the same people for one, three or five years and measuring skill are hard to find. A 2026 longitudinal study of developers exists (two surveys six months apart, 158 at the first wave, 101 at the second, 95 answering both), but what it measures is perception of task focus, developer experience and productivity, not skill formation
  • What does not exist — comparison across tool shapes: no design comparing whether autocomplete, chat and agents act differently on learning. The three differ completely in where the human intervenes, yet the debate treats them as one lump

So the honest conclusion is neither side. Collapse is not settled and neither is no harm. What is settled is narrow — performance while holding the tool and performance immediately after putting it down do not move together. Whether that gap closes or widens with time has not been measured by anyone. In that state, the reasonable move is not to wait for a verdict but to pick habits that cost little whichever way it turns out.

6. The same tool acts differently on different people

Educational psychology describes the expertise reversal effect: instructional methods that work for novices lose effect and can become harmful for learners with more knowledge. The classic case is the fully worked example. Low-knowledge learners do better studying a well-built example than struggling through the problem alone, and as knowledge grows, solving it themselves becomes the better activity.

That result rebuts two common claims at once. Pulling tools away from beginners is not the answer, and the line that tools erase the beginner's barrier to entry does not hold as stated either. The split is not tool or no tool but whether the answer gets processed.

  • Someone with a model puts the AI answer into the verification slot automatically. They snag on the parts that do not fit, and their own model updates where they snagged. For them the tool works more like tightening the interval on retrieval practice
  • Someone without a model puts the same answer into the correct-answer slot. With no standard to check against, no checking happens, and with no checking there is no model to update. This is not the tool being bad; it is the absence of anything to compare with
  • People in the middle are the largest group, and this is where outcomes split. Treat the answer as an example and explain it back to yourself, and the worked-example effect attaches; paste it straight in and it does not. Same tool, same answer, different result
  • The tutor arm of the maths experiment demonstrated that difference by design. Switching from answers to hints made the harm disappear. The shape of the tool changed the outcome

This piece is about the path along which skill forms. The conditions under which an already-skilled person produces good output today run on a different axis, and that one is handled separately in Why the Same Model Gives Different Results, with verification cost as the axis.

7. When to do it yourself and when to delegate

At the individual level the criterion narrows to one question. Is there anything left to gain from this task? If yes, do it. If no, delegate it. Below is that test unfolded by situation.

SituationDefaultWhy
A concept or library you are meeting for the first timeYourself, inside a time boxGenerating your own attempt cannot be recreated after you see the answer. The order only runs one way
Chasing the cause of a bugHypotheses yourself, checking delegatedLinking symptom to cause is the substance of the skill. Receive the cause and there is no room for it to form
Routine work you have done many timesDelegateRepetition has nothing left to give. Time saved here becomes the budget for the two rows above
Mapping an unfamiliar domainDelegate, but take only the starting pointEntry cost itself was what blocked learning. Take the map and walk it yourself
Changes that are hard to reverseYourself, tool as reviewerThe cost of failure is too high to be used as learning material
Work that is genuinely urgentDelegateYou do not have to learn from every task. Just revisit it once afterwards

The time box in the first row is the hard part in practice. Fifteen minutes or twenty, the number itself has no evidence behind it. The point is to put a boundary at the spot where stuck slides into give up. Without one, people usually land on an extreme — moving on in three seconds, or burning three hours.

  • Write your own answer before you get one. A single line is enough. Written down, the gap between yours and theirs becomes visible, and that gap is the thing to learn. Unwritten, the received answer feels like it was your idea all along
  • Explain why the working code works. Out loud or in a comment. The point where your explanation stalls is the point you still do not understand
  • The second time you meet the same problem, do not open the tool first. The second encounter is where retrieval practice attaches. Open the tool every time and even what you gained the first time never sets
  • Periodically spend a day with the tools off. Not to measure ability but to check the gap between how it feels and how it goes. As METR showed, that gap appears in experienced people too

8. What organisations can do, and why a ban is not it

Individual habits do not close this on their own, for a simple reason: it is hard to stay stuck alone inside an organisation that counts stuck time as waste. The answer organisations usually reach for is a ban, and it mostly does not work.

  • There is no way to check. An artefact does not reveal how it was produced. Only the people following the rule slow down, and the gap widens in the wrong direction
  • It locates the problem wrongly. The real reason learning fails to happen is not the tool but a process that never asks for an explanation. If review passes artefacts, nobody learns whether a person or a tool wrote the code
  • What gets measured stays the same. Under metrics that only count throughput, people will find another way to skip understanding even with the tool banned. The place to copy from used to be somewhere else

The process is what you can change. Below are devices that restore stuck time without blocking the tool.

  • Ask for reasoning, not results, in review. Why this approach, why not the alternatives, where it would break first. One rule that code you cannot explain does not merge is stronger than any ban. Running review itself is covered separately in Code Review Is a Trained Skill
  • Extend what pairing covers. Instead of two people on one screen, one person works with the tool while another watches the session. Where they accept and where they push back becomes visible
  • Name tool-free stretches inside onboarding exercises. That is design, not prohibition. Deciding this part is done without tools turns a rule into a curriculum
  • Count stuck time as work. Create a place to share what people got stuck on and what they ruled out. Deleted hypotheses that stay in the organisation stop the next person burning the same ground
  • Put senior time ahead of design rather than into review. When tools raise output, the bottleneck piles into review. Trying to catch everything there burns seniors out and leaves juniors receiving only results

9. The other side: paths the tools opened

Writing only one side would make this piece wrong. The same tools genuinely created learning paths that did not exist before.

  • Feedback delay went to nearly zero. Getting an opinion on your own code used to mean waiting on a reviewer's calendar, and by then you had forgotten what you wrote and why. Now you can ask while writing
  • Areas you could not enter at all are now enterable. Another language ecosystem, undocumented legacy, an unfamiliar domain. Plenty of these were abandoned before the first day was out, and abandonment means zero learning
  • Asking has no social cost. A great deal used to go unlearned because asking the same thing a third time was uncomfortable. The tool answers the same way however many times you ask
  • There is evidence that changing the design removes the harm. The maths arm switched from answers to hints erased the negative effect. But that arm was not better than control either. Guardrails blocked the damage; they did not manufacture more learning

Which is why using the tool Socratically is a genuinely valid prescription. Instead of asking for the answer, ask where your hypothesis would be wrong if it were. Instead of receiving code, ask which cases you have missed. One condition comes attached: you need a hypothesis to ask about first. Building that hypothesis is exactly the capacity the earlier sections were about.

10. What could not be confirmed

  • Long-term tracking — no study following the same developers for one, three or five years while measuring skill turned up. Nothing in this piece fills that hole
  • Long-term outcomes for the search-engine generation — the blank in the comparison table above. Experiments on externalised memory exist, but nothing conclusive on long-run outcomes was confirmed
  • The scale of any expertise supply-chain collapse — no hiring or career data confirming that senior supply is actually shrinking. For now it is an argument, not an observation
  • Data from any single national market — the experiments cited here involve Turkish high schoolers, Singaporean secondary students, and developer samples whose nationality is not specified. Local conditions may differ

Conclusion: not a question of which tool, but of what order

In the calculator meta-analysis, harm was located in timing rather than in the tool. It appeared only where the basics were still setting and the tool spent that chance instead. The current debate has the same shape. The question is not whether to use AI coding tools but what to pass through once yourself before you do.

It is worth admitting outright that this judgement has to be made on thin evidence. Still, the handful of short measurements point consistently at one thing — performance while holding the tool and capability after putting it down do not move together. That gap may close. If it does not, the loss is much larger.

So the habit reduces to a single sentence. Write your own answer before you get one. One line is enough and being wrong is fine. With that line, the answer you receive becomes something learned; without it, it is something that passed by. Designing the delegation itself is handled separately in Delegating Work to AI.
