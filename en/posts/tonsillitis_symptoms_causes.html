Tonsillitis: Symptoms and Causes

Why viral and bacterial cases look identical, when antibiotics are actually needed, and which signs mean go now

Aug 11, 2026 Health & Wellness

Introduction: there are white patches on your tonsils

Every swallow feels like tearing. There is a fever. You open your mouth in front of a mirror and the two lumps either side of the uvula are angry red with white and yellow patches stuck to them like stains. Almost everyone reaches the same conclusion at this point: it is bacterial, I need antibiotics.

That inference is wrong more often than people expect. Exudate on the tonsils is not a bacterial signature; it is a sign that the tonsil is fighting hard, and viruses produce the identical picture. So the central question in tonsillitis is not how bad it hurts but what is causing it, and that question cannot be settled by looking. This is precisely why clinical scores and laboratory tests exist.

The article follows that order. First, where the tonsils are and what they do, and what separates acute from recurrent from chronic disease. Then the viral-versus-bacterial split, the structural reason the two are hard to distinguish, and how far the Centor score and rapid antigen and culture testing can take you. Red flags and peritonsillar abscess are pulled forward deliberately. Finally, when antibiotics are indicated and why the course has to be finished, tonsil stones, the criteria used when recurrent infection raises the question of surgery, recovery care, and complications.

If your main symptom is a changed or lost voice rather than pain on swallowing, the problem may sit at the vocal cords instead. That axis is covered separately in Laryngitis: Symptoms, Causes, and When to See a Doctor.

Medical disclaimer
This article is general health information and does not replace medical diagnosis, prescription or treatment. Nothing here should be used as a basis for starting or stopping any medication on your own. In particular, do not take antibiotics on your own initiative and do not use leftover antibiotics from a previous illness. The choice of antibiotic, its dose, its duration and when to stop are decided by the clinician who examined you. This article neither recommends nor discourages tonsillectomy; that decision must be made with a specialist. Children, pregnant and breastfeeding people, older adults, people who are immunocompromised and people with chronic illness may need different advice from the general adult case. If you cannot swallow your own saliva, cannot breathe comfortably, or cannot open your mouth properly, do not wait — call emergency services (911 in the US, 999 in the UK, 119 in Korea) or go to the emergency department.

1. Where the tonsils sit and what they do

There is more than one tonsil. Korea's national health information portal, run by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency, defines tonsillitis as inflammation of the tonsils and the tissue around them, and notes that what people usually mean by the tonsil is the palatine tonsil, the round structure on each side of the uvula, alongside the adenoid (pharyngeal tonsil) behind the nose, the lingual tonsil at the base of the tongue, and the tubal tonsils. Only the first of those is visible in a mirror.

The tonsils mount an immune response to bacteria and viruses arriving through the mouth and nose. Put differently, a swollen tonsil is not a broken organ; it is evidence of a fight at the gate. That framing is useful because it explains why swelling alone tells you nothing about which organism is responsible.

Separating the sites makes everything downstream easier.

  • Tonsillitis — inflammation confined to the palatine tonsils. Severe pain on swallowing, enlarged tonsils, exudate on the surface.
  • Pharyngitis — inflammation of the pharyngeal lining generally. It occurs when the tonsils are not prominent, and in people whose tonsils have been removed.
  • Tonsillopharyngitis — both together. This is the commonest presentation in practice and the reason medical references treat the two as one topic.
  • Laryngitis — inflammation of the larynx, where the vocal cords are. Voice change leads rather than pain. Covered in the laryngitis article.

A sore throat with a cracked voice may well mean inflammation in two places at once. Even so, which symptom came first and dominates usually decides which axis to work along.

2. Acute, recurrent, chronic — the line is drawn by frequency, not duration

Many respiratory conditions split acute from chronic using a time threshold such as three or twelve weeks. Tonsillitis is different. Korea's national portal defines recurrent acute tonsillitis as six or more episodes in one year, five or more per year for two consecutive years, or three or more per year for three consecutive years. The axis that matters here is not how long one episode lasts but how often it comes back.

The three forms, side by side:

  • Acute tonsillitis — sudden inflammation from bacterial or viral infection. Sore throat, high fever and chills, sometimes with headache, general weakness and joint pain.
  • Recurrent acute tonsillitis — repeated episodes meeting the frequency thresholds above. Each episode looks acute, but the pattern itself is the diagnosis.
  • Chronic tonsillitis — persistent tonsillar inflammation causing discomfort without acute symptoms. The commonest complaint is persistent sore throat, sometimes with difficulty swallowing or bad breath.

The practical consequence is direct. If every visit is treated as a fresh cold, the recurrence pattern never gets written down. Every tonsillectomy criterion set you will meet later requires documented episodes, so people who get tonsillitis often are better served by keeping their care in one place. This is not about collecting paperwork in advance; it means being seen while you are actually ill so the episode enters the record.

3. Virus or bacteria — the numbers

The broad picture first. Most sore throats and most tonsillitis are viral, and bacteria account for the smaller share. The exact proportion differs between sources, and that variation is not error — it reflects different populations.

  • US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention clinical guidance attributes 20 to 30 percent of pediatric and 5 to 15 percent of adult health care visits for sore throat to group A streptococcus.
  • Korea's national health information portal states that in children under three years the cause is mainly viral, while in those five and older 30 to 40 percent are bacterial.
  • When the cause is bacterial, beta-haemolytic streptococcus is the commonest organism, though staphylococci, pneumococci and others also occur.
  • On the viral side, influenza virus, parainfluenza virus and coxsackievirus are among those named.
  • Transmission is by respiratory droplets, which is why one case in a household is so often followed by others.

What matters in these figures is not the exact percentage but the fact that age moves the probability enormously. Group A strep is uncommon under three, peaks in school-age children, and falls again in adults. That is exactly why the Centor score, coming up shortly, awards points for age.

One more thing. A low proportion is not the same as a negligible one. Even if most adult sore throats are viral, missing that 5 to 15 percent opens the door to peritonsillar abscess or acute rheumatic fever later. Testing exists not to be right about the majority but to avoid missing the minority.

4. Why looking does not settle it

The 2012 Infectious Diseases Society of America guideline on group A streptococcal pharyngitis puts the problem in one sentence: clinical features alone do not reliably discriminate between group A strep and viral pharyngitis. Exudate, high fever and severe pain belong to neither camp exclusively.

The opposite direction, however, carries real information. The same guideline advises against testing for strep when cough, runny nose, hoarseness or oral ulcers are present, because those features point towards a virus. In other words there is no finding that confirms bacteria, but there are findings that make bacteria unlikely. The table below sorts them by direction.

Look again at the row for tonsillar exudate, where both columns say the same thing. The finding people trust most is in fact the weakest. That is exactly where the inference in the introduction goes wrong.

The classic look-alike is infectious mononucleosis. Caused by Epstein-Barr virus, it brings fever, tonsillopharyngitis and lymph node swelling with thick exudate that is hard to separate from bacterial tonsillitis. Give an aminopenicillin such as amoxicillin in that setting and a measles-like rash commonly appears days later — which in this context is frequently not a true penicillin allergy. That phenomenon alone explains why treating first and asking later is a poor strategy.

5. The Centor score — what it counts and what it cannot do

Since looking does not work, clinicians count instead. The Centor score, and the McIsaac modification that adds age, tally a handful of findings to stratify the probability of group A strep. The structure is simple.

  • Tonsillar exudate or swelling — plus 1 if present
  • Tender or swollen anterior cervical lymph nodes — plus 1 if present
  • Temperature above 38C — plus 1 if present
  • Absence of cough — plus 1 if absent (0 if cough is present)
  • Age — 3 to 14 years plus 1, 15 to 44 years 0, 45 and over minus 1

What counts is not the number but what the number is for. The IDSA guideline treats clinical scoring systems as useful for identifying low-risk patients but not precise enough to diagnose without laboratory confirmation. The score's real job is not to declare a bacterial infection; it is to sort people into tested and not tested.

Korea's antibiotic stewardship guidance for acute upper respiratory infection follows the same architecture: at a modified Centor score of three or more, perform a rapid antigen detection test on a throat swab and treat if positive, with empirical antibiotics considered at three or more only where rapid testing is unavailable. Note the ordering — testing first, empirical treatment as the fallback.

Three limitations

  • Its window is narrow. It was designed for acute pharyngitis of three days' duration or less. Applying it to someone whose throat has been scratchy for two weeks is using the wrong tool.
  • A high score is not a diagnosis. Plenty of people scoring 4 do not have strep. A high score means testing is worthwhile, not that bacteria are present.
  • It performs less well in children. Findings overlap more in paediatric patients, so score and test result diverge more often. That is part of the reason testing and culture backup are emphasised more strongly in this group.

In short, the Centor score is a sorter, not a diagnostic instrument. It was never designed as a self-assessment tool for deciding whether to take antibiotics.

6. Rapid antigen testing and culture — thirty minutes versus three days

The answers come from testing, and two tests work as a pair.

  • Rapid antigen detection test (RADT) — a throat swab detecting group A streptococcal antigen. Korea's national portal describes results within 30 minutes and specificity above 95 percent.
  • Throat culture — growing the organism to confirm it. The same source puts this at at least three days.

The difference in character between the two shapes the whole pathway. Rapid tests are highly specific but less sensitive. The IDSA guideline puts sensitivity broadly at 70 to 90 percent, and that combination means the following.

  • A positive result is trusted. Specificity is high enough that confirmatory culture is unnecessary; the decision moves straight to treatment.
  • A negative result in a child or adolescent gets a culture. The sensitivity gap can miss real infection, so IDSA strongly recommends backup culture after a negative rapid test in this age group.
  • Adults are not routinely given backup culture. The guideline's reasoning is the lower prevalence of group A strep pharyngitis and the lower risk of acute rheumatic fever in adults.

The point of this section is that a negative result is not simply the end; whose negative it is determines the next move. There are also cases where not testing is the better choice. IDSA advises against testing when viral features such as cough, runny nose, hoarseness or oral ulcers are prominent, and against routine testing under three years of age because acute rheumatic fever is rare there. Testing indiscriminately turns carriers into apparent infections and sends out antibiotics that were never needed.

7. Red flags — past this line it is no longer self-care

This is the section to read first. The signs below warrant immediate assessment regardless of how bearable the pain feels.

Call emergency services now
You cannot swallow your saliva and it is drooling out / you are struggling to breathe or your breathing sounds abnormal / you cannot open your mouth properly / one side is severely swollen and your voice has gone muffled. That combination suggests infection has spread beyond the tonsil or that the airway is narrowing. Children have narrower airways than adults and deteriorate far faster from the same amount of swelling. If a child is floppy and unresponsive, changing colour around the lips or skin, or unable to take any fluid at all, do not wait — call 911 in the US, 999 in the UK, 119 in Korea.

8. Peritonsillar abscess — one side swollen and a jaw that will not open

All of those red flags have been pointing at a single condition. Peritonsillar abscess occurs when suppurative infection of the tonsil breaks through the tonsillar capsule and pus collects around the tonsil. It is a local complication of acute bacterial tonsillitis and usually affects one side.

The pattern is recognisable.

  • Severe pain concentrated on one side with earache on the same side
  • Trismus — inflammation reaching the pterygoid muscles causes spasm so the mouth will not open
  • Difficulty swallowing — in severe cases saliva cannot be swallowed and drools out
  • Muffled voice — classically described as speaking with a hot potato in the mouth
  • Uvular deviation — the swollen uvula is pushed towards the unaffected side
  • Persistent fever with chills and general weakness

Treatment differs in kind from tonsillitis. Pus is not something a drug dissolves; it is something that has to come out. Seoul National University Hospital's medical information describes careful nursing, adequate hydration, intravenous antibiotics and incision and drainage, with tonsillectomy considered when abscesses recur. The consequences of leaving it are stated just as plainly: supraglottic oedema can obstruct the upper airway and require emergency tracheostomy, and progression to necrotising fasciitis can be fatal.

The practical conclusion is therefore singular. If the pain has localised to one side and your mouth will not open properly, you need to be seen that day. This is not a situation where waiting to see if tomorrow is better works.

9. When antibiotics are indicated and when they are not

Antibiotics target bacteria. In viral tonsillitis there is no target. That sentence is the whole of this section; everything else is detail built on top of it.

Korea's national portal frames the conservative management of acute tonsillitis as drinking enough fluid and resting, with mouth rinses helping to keep the oral cavity clean. Antibiotics come after that. The portal specifies that bacterial acute tonsillitis should be treated with appropriate antibiotics under specialist care, and names three purposes.

  • Reducing suppurative complications — preventing collections of pus such as peritonsillar abscess
  • Preventing complications — the non-suppurative ones, acute rheumatic fever and glomerulonephritis
  • Lowering infectivity — reducing the chance of passing the organism to others

Notice that the first purpose is not pain relief. Feeling better on antibiotics is a side effect, not the reason for the prescription. It is the same logic behind the NHS statement that you do not normally need antibiotics for a sore throat, with analgesia and self-care placed first.

Situations leaning away from antibiotics

  • Prominent viral features such as cough, runny nose, hoarseness or mouth ulcers
  • A negative rapid antigen test in an adult, where backup culture is not required
  • Children under three, where group A strep infection and acute rheumatic fever are both uncommon
  • Symptoms already turning clearly in the direction of recovery

Situations leaning towards antibiotics

  • Group A streptococcus confirmed on rapid testing or culture
  • A high modified Centor score where testing cannot be performed promptly (the empirical-treatment judgement)
  • A suspected or confirmed suppurative complication such as peritonsillar abscess
  • Individual risk factors such as a history of acute rheumatic fever

Either way, the decision belongs to the clinician who examined you. This list exists so you know what is being weighed in the consultation, not so you can reach the conclusion yourself. Taking leftover antibiotics from the cupboard is not advisable under any circumstances — the dose will not match, the course cannot be completed, and the result obscures the testing that would otherwise guide care.

10. Why the course has to be finished

This is the rule that breaks most often. Two days in, the throat feels far better, and the remaining tablets go into a drawer.

Underneath this sits a simple fact: symptoms and bacteria run on different clocks. The IDSA guideline sets the duration likely to eradicate the organism from the pharynx at usually 10 days, and Korea's national portal likewise describes courses of about 10 days depending on the agent. The moment pain disappears is not the moment the organism does.

More importantly, consider what the drug was prescribed to prevent. In group A strep infection the primary aim of antibiotic treatment is preventing non-suppurative complications such as acute rheumatic fever. The IDSA guideline notes that treatment within 9 days of the onset of illness is effective in preventing acute rheumatic fever, and Korea's pharyngotonsillitis page uses the same nine-day figure. That preventive effect was established on the assumption of a completed course. There is no evidence that three days of treatment delivers the same protection.

Concretely, stopping early causes problems on four fronts.

  • Relapse — surviving organisms multiply and symptoms return within days
  • Uncertain prevention — the premise behind protection from rheumatic fever and glomerulonephritis is removed
  • Transmission — you return to normal life while still infectious enough to pass it on
  • Confused follow-up — partial treatment makes later testing and diagnosis harder to interpret

One distinction has to be drawn clearly. Finish the course does not mean keep taking it no matter what. If a rash, severe diarrhoea or breathing difficulty appears, do not simply stop on your own — tell your clinician straight away and let them judge. As the amoxicillin rash in infectious mononucleosis shows, the meaning of a rash depends entirely on the setting. Starting and stopping both belong to the prescriber.

11. Tonsil stones — the white lumps and the smell

A perennial companion search to tonsillitis. Korea's national portal describes tonsil stones as forming when bacteria and food debris accumulate in the tonsillar crypts and harden into stone-like lumps under bacterial action. The symptom description is unusually concrete: they can cause sore throat or a foreign-body sensation, and patients report bad breath and having coughed up foul-smelling white or yellow lumps.

The key to understanding them is the crypt. The tonsil surface is folded and grooved, and repeated chronic inflammation deepens those grooves. Debris settles in the deepened crypt and bacteria harden it. A stone is therefore closer to a result than a cause. Remove one stone and, if the crypt remains, another forms.

  • Do not dig them out. Scraping with cotton buds, toothpicks or fingernails injures the mucosa and can cause bleeding and secondary infection.
  • Drink water regularly and keep the mouth clean after meals. Korea's portal also advises mouth rinsing to maintain oral hygiene.
  • If they recur and interfere with daily life, an ENT clinician can remove them.
  • If stones persist alongside chronic tonsillitis, the conversation may move to surgery — but that judgement belongs to a specialist.
  • If one side suddenly becomes severely painful and swollen, this is not a stone problem. Go back to the red flag table above.

It is also worth saying that not all bad breath is tonsil stones. Oral hygiene, periodontal disease and postnasal drip are all separate axes. If you have never actually produced a lump from the tonsil, it makes sense to look elsewhere first.

12. Recurrent infection and tonsillectomy — the fact that criteria exist

This section neither recommends nor discourages surgery. What is worth carrying into a consultation is that this is not decided by intuition or by how much you can tolerate; it is a judgement made against stated criteria, and those criteria differ between sources.

The point of the table is that the three rows disagree. None of them is wrong; they diverge because the target age group, the underlying literature and the health system differ. Which is why arriving with a number found online and announcing that you meet the threshold rarely works. What actually counts is the episodes recorded in your notes.

The detail worth noting in the AAO-HNS guideline is that it strongly recommends watchful waiting when the thresholds are not met. Its reasoning is that operating on children below those thresholds does not produce clinically meaningful improvement and exposes them to surgical risk for no return. The criteria are designed as a door that opens and a door that closes.

Beyond frequency, Korea's portal lists other situations in which surgery is discussed.

  • Chronic tonsillitis — persistent halitosis, sore throat, tender inflamed cervical nodes
  • Severe snoring or sleep apnoea from tonsillar enlargement — in children this bears directly on growth and sleep quality
  • Unilateral tonsillar enlargement — where malignancy has to be excluded
  • Recurrent peritonsillar abscess — as noted above, surgery is considered when abscesses return

The same document is equally clear that surgery carries a price. Pain, bleeding and breathing difficulty can follow, as can velopharyngeal insufficiency, nasopharyngeal stenosis and taste disturbance. The argument that tonsils must never be removed because they are immune tissue, and the opposite argument that frequent illness settles the matter, both ignore this balance. Weighing benefit against risk on your own particulars is work to be done with a specialist.

13. Recovery care — fluids, pain relief, rest

Whatever the cause, recovery care looks much the same. The backbone of conservative treatment in Korea's national portal is plenty of fluid and rest, plus keeping the mouth clean with rinses. NHS self-care advice for sore throat runs in the same direction.

  • Fluids, often and in small amounts. Painful swallowing reduces intake, drying the mucosa and worsening the pain — a loop worth breaking early.
  • Cool or soft food. The NHS recommends cool drinks and soft foods. Hot and spicy food amplifies the pain.
  • Warm salt water gargles. Offered by the NHS as self-care, with the explicit caveat that it is not for young children.
  • Pain relief. The NHS notes that a pharmacist can help with paracetamol or ibuprofen and with medicated lozenges containing a local anaesthetic, antiseptic or anti-inflammatory. Choice, dose and combinations depend on your circumstances, so ask the pharmacist.
  • Rest. Cutting activity while febrile shortens the recovery.
  • Avoid tobacco smoke. Both smoking and second-hand smoke slow mucosal healing.
  • Humidity. Korea's portal recommends keeping indoor humidity at 40 to 50 percent in its prevention section.
  • Hand washing and tooth brushing. Since the infection spreads by droplets, these are practical measures against passing it around the household.

For a sense of timing, the NHS is useful: tonsillitis symptoms usually go away after three to four days but can last longer, and you should see a doctor if symptoms persist beyond a week or throat infections keep recurring. A sore throat on day one rarely warrants an emergency department visit, but a week is a reasonable ceiling on waiting.

On folk remedies, one line will do. This article does not present unproven remedies as treatment. Habits that make a throat more comfortable and treatments that resolve an illness are different categories.

14. Complications — suppurative and non-suppurative

Antibiotics are not used because tonsillitis is a frightening illness. They are used because a small number of cases head somewhere bad. Complications split into two families.

Once you see the timing of the non-suppurative complications, the antibiotic rule makes far more sense. Acute rheumatic fever arrives one to five weeks after the infection; glomerulonephritis one to two weeks. Both begin after the throat has recovered. That is why stopping the drug because the pain has gone is a risky call: the event you are trying to prevent has not happened yet.

Be honest about frequency too. Korea's portal puts acute rheumatic fever at 0.3 to 3 percent and glomerulonephritis at under 1 percent. It will not happen to most people. The reason these numbers still drive clinical guidelines is the weight of the outcome. Acute rheumatic fever can leave permanent damage to heart valves. When an unlikely outcome is also irreversible, the balance tips towards prevention.

The signs listed for glomerulonephritis are worth remembering: oliguria, defined as total daily urine output falling below 400cc, and oedema. If your urine output drops noticeably or your eyelids and feet swell one to two weeks after a sore throat, mention the connection explicitly when you are seen.

15. Common misconceptions

  • White patches mean bacteria. They do not. Viral infections, especially infectious mononucleosis, produce thick exudate too. Exudate is not a bacterial signature.
  • Severe pain means antibiotics are needed. Pain intensity and the presence of a bacterial pathogen are independent. Testing and examination decide.
  • Two days and I felt fine, so I stopped. Symptom relief is not eradication. Clearing the pharynx generally takes about 10 days, and the complications being prevented arrive one to five weeks later.
  • I have leftovers from last time, I will use those. The dose will not match, the course cannot be completed, and the result confounds testing. Not advisable.
  • I can score myself with Centor and decide. The score sorts who gets tested; it is not a diagnostic tool, and it was designed only for illness of three days or less.
  • A negative rapid test is the end of it. In children and adolescents a negative result is recommended for backup culture, because sensitivity is not 100 percent.
  • Tonsil stones just need digging out. The crypt remains, so they return, and scraping risks bleeding and infection.
  • Frequent infections mean it is better to remove them. Below the thresholds, watchful waiting is strongly recommended. Surgery carries its own risks including pain and bleeding.
  • Removing tonsils wrecks your immunity. The tonsils are one component among many immune tissues, and the decision is a benefit-versus-risk judgement made with a specialist.
  • Adults do not get tonsillitis. The proportion caused by group A strep is lower in adults, but tonsillitis itself still occurs — and peritonsillar abscess is not rare in young and middle-aged adults.

16. What is not settled here

The items this article deliberately does not put numbers on, gathered in one place.

  • National caseload and age distribution — figures on how many people are treated for tonsillitis in a given health system were not included.
  • Prevalence of tonsil stones — no reliable figure was identified, so no frequency language is used.
  • Adult tonsillectomy criteria — the national row in Table 3 is consumer guidance from Korea's health information portal; the adult thresholds in a society guideline document were not confirmed.
  • Coverage and cost of rapid antigen testing — this varies by country, institution and time, so it is not stated.
  • The place of a single steroid dose — some sources mention dexamethasone for symptom relief in throat infection, but standard national guidance on this was not confirmed and it is not covered.
  • How the adult no-backup-culture recommendation transfers — IDSA's reasoning rests on US prevalence and rheumatic fever risk. Whether it transfers unchanged to other health systems was not confirmed.
  • Likelihood ratios for individual findings in Table 1 — only direction is given; the quantitative diagnostic value of each finding is not.

These gaps are left empty rather than filled with estimates. Judgements that need numbers are better made in a consultation, alongside your own particulars.

17. Frequently asked questions

Q. Do white patches on the tonsils mean bacterial tonsillitis?

Not on their own. White and yellow exudate is a classic finding in acute tonsillitis, but viral infections produce the same appearance, and infectious mononucleosis in particular mimics it closely with thick exudate. The IDSA guideline states that clinical features alone do not reliably discriminate between the two causes. The reverse direction is useful though: when cough, runny nose, hoarseness or mouth ulcers are present, testing for strep is not advised.

Q. Do I always need antibiotics for tonsillitis?

No. Antibiotics target bacteria, so in viral tonsillitis there is nothing to hit. The NHS states you do not normally need antibiotics for a sore throat, and Korea's national portal restricts them to bacterial acute tonsillitis under specialist care. A common pathway uses a modified Centor score of three or more to trigger a rapid antigen test, treating those who test positive. Taking leftover antibiotics on your own is never advisable.

Q. My throat felt fine after two days. Can I stop the antibiotics?

Feeling better and clearing the organism are separate events. The IDSA guideline sets pharyngeal eradication at usually 10 days, and Korea's portal describes about 10 days. In group A strep infection the primary aim is not pain relief but prevention of late complications such as acute rheumatic fever, and that benefit was shown with completed courses. When to stop is your prescriber's decision; if side effects appear, do not simply stop but contact your clinician immediately.

Q. Can I dig out tonsil stones myself?

It is not a good idea. Scraping with a cotton bud or toothpick can tear the mucosa and cause bleeding and secondary infection, and because the crypt remains the stone returns quickly. Drinking water regularly and rinsing after meals is the lower-risk approach. If it keeps bothering you an ENT clinician can remove them, and if chronic tonsillitis coexists, surgery becomes a specialist conversation. Sudden severe one-sided pain and swelling is not a stone problem and needs same-day assessment.

Q. If tonsillitis keeps coming back, should the tonsils come out?

That decision comes from a consultation. What is worth knowing is that criteria exist. The AAO-HNS guideline on tonsillectomy in children uses at least 7 episodes in the past year, at least 5 per year for 2 years, or at least 3 per year for 3 years, requires documentation of each episode, and strongly recommends watchful waiting below those thresholds. Korea's national portal publishes different numbers and adds snoring, sleep apnoea and one-sided enlargement. That the numbers differ between sources is itself the point: this is a judgement, not a calculation.

Conclusion: it narrows to two questions

Tonsillitis is common and usually settles on its own. The reason information still matters comes down to two forks in the road.

First, is this an emergency right now? If you cannot swallow saliva, cannot breathe comfortably, cannot open your mouth, or one side has swollen severely and your voice has gone muffled, this is not a situation for counting days. A peritonsillar abscess is an illness that requires drainage, and if it progresses it can obstruct the airway. Children have narrower airways and deteriorate faster from the same swelling. At that point you call emergency services — 911 in the US, 999 in the UK, 119 in Korea.

Second, what is causing it? That question does not yield to inspection. Exudate appears in both, and clinical features alone do not reliably separate them. The Centor score sorts who gets tested; the answer comes from rapid antigen testing and culture. And if the answer was bacterial, the course ends on the scheduled day, not on the day your throat felt better — because the event being prevented arrives one to five weeks after recovery.

Recurrent tonsillitis and surgery sit one layer further out. What to remember there is not the numbers themselves but that criteria operate on the medical record. If you get tonsillitis often, be seen while you are ill so the episodes are documented. That record becomes the material for the conversation later.

Medical disclaimer, once more
This article is general health information and does not replace medical diagnosis, prescription or treatment. Because the right course depends on your symptoms, age, underlying conditions and current medication, decisions about testing, antibiotic use and surgery must be made with your clinician. Do not start or stop antibiotics on your own, and do not take leftover medication. See a doctor if symptoms last beyond a week or infections keep recurring, and if you experience inability to swallow saliva, breathing difficulty, trismus, severe one-sided swelling with voice change, or persistent high fever, seek emergency care without delay (911 in the US, 999 in the UK, 119 in Korea).
