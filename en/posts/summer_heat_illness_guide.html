Heat Illness Symptoms and Emergency Response - How to Tell Heat Exhaustion from Heat Stroke

Work through it one question at a time and decide what the person in front of you actually needs

Jul 26, 2026 Health

🚨 Do This Now - The 30-Second Summary

  1. Get them out of the heat. Shade or air conditioning. Stop the activity immediately.
  2. Loosen clothing and start cooling. Spray water or drape wet cloths, then fan hard. Put ice packs at the neck, armpits and groin.
  3. Only if they are fully alert, let them sip water or an electrolyte drink slowly.
  4. If they are confused, slurring, seizing or vomiting repeatedly, call emergency services now. Do not force fluids into them.
  5. Keep cooling while you wait. Cooling is the first aid, not an optional extra.

The most dangerous moment in a heat wave is the one where someone shrugs and says they are just a bit overheated. Heat illness spans a wide range - from relatively mild forms like heat cramps, heat syncope and heat edema, through heat exhaustion, where fluid and salt losses drain the body, all the way to heat stroke, where temperature regulation collapses outright and organ damage becomes possible. The problem is that the two ends of that range can look similar from the outside. Both bring dizziness. Both bring weakness. Both can leave someone drenched in sweat.

So this article moves through questions rather than explanations. Someone who has to make a call right now does not need a lecture on thermoregulation; they need a one-line answer to whether this person needs an ambulance. What follows reorganizes the principles that Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency, Korea Occupational Safety and Health Agency, the US CDC and OSHA, and the WHO all converge on, arranged by situation. For scale: Korea runs an emergency-room heat illness surveillance system from mid-May through the end of September each year, and in 2025 it logged heat illness patients in the four-thousand range with dozens of suspected deaths. This is not a rare event.

One thing must be clear up front. This is general health information and does not replace diagnosis or treatment. In a real emergency, the instructions of an emergency dispatcher and the judgment of medical staff outrank every sentence here.

Q1. It is hot, I feel dizzy and I am sweating hard. Is this heat illness?

Possibly. In a hot environment, dizziness, headache, nausea, intense thirst, muscle cramps and a faster-than-usual pulse are read as signs the body is losing its fight with heat. The first move is not to work out which specific condition it is - it is to get away from the heat source.

The classic mistake here is deciding to push through a little longer. Heat illness does not always worsen in neat steps; it can accelerate. Caught at the heat-exhaustion stage, most people recover with proper rest and cooling. Left in the heat, the same person can tip into heat stroke. The more ambiguous the symptoms, the stronger the case for stopping.

Move into shade or an air-conditioned room, lie the person down, and raise the legs slightly above heart level. Loosen clothing. If they are fully alert, let them sip water or an electrolyte drink slowly rather than gulping.

Q2. How do I actually tell heat exhaustion from heat stroke?

Three things matter: mental state, temperature and sweating. Of the three, mental state decides it.

FactorHeat ExhaustionHeat Stroke
Mental stateClear. Converses normally, recognizes peopleAltered. Confusion, strange speech, slurring, seizure, unconsciousness
Body temperatureNormal to moderately elevatedVery high, rising rapidly
Skin and sweatingHeavy sweating; skin pale, clammy, coolSweating mechanism often fails, so skin is hot and dry (exertional cases may still sweat)
PulseFast and weakFast, often strong
NatureExhaustion from fluid and salt lossFailure of thermoregulation and a true medical emergency
ResponseMove to cool place, cool, rehydrate, observeCall emergency services now and keep cooling until help arrives

You do not need to memorize the table. The field test is one line: does this person answer you the way they normally would? If the answer is off, treat it as heat stroke and call.

One trap deserves naming. Reasoning that someone cannot have heat stroke because they are still sweating is dangerous. Hot, dry skin is the textbook finding, but heat stroke that develops during exertion can still present with sweat. Mental state outranks sweating every time.

Q3. What is the most effective way to cool someone?

The core of heat stroke first aid is not getting fluids in - it is bringing the core temperature down fast. Emergency medicine consistently emphasizes that faster cooling means better outcomes in heat stroke, to the point that a cool-first, transport-second principle is actively discussed for exertional cases.

3-1. Cooling, in order

  1. Move - into shade or air conditioning. Hot asphalt keeps pouring heat in on its own.
  2. Expose - remove or loosen outer and constricting clothing so heat has somewhere to go.
  3. Evaporate - spray cool water on the skin or drape wet cloths, then fan. Evaporation carries heat away.
  4. Conduct - place ice packs or cold bottles at the sides of the neck, the armpits and the groin, where large vessels run near the surface.
  5. Continue - do not stop until help arrives. Do not stop at looks a bit better.

3-2. Details people miss

A fan alone is weaker than water plus airflow. Blowing hot air across dry skin can actually deliver more heat. Wet cloths also warm up to body temperature quickly, so they only help if you keep swapping them out.

Avoid pressing ice directly against bare skin for long stretches. Keep a layer of cloth between, and rotate the placement.

Q4. What must you never do?

⛔ Four Things to Avoid

  • Do not put fluids into someone who is confused or unresponsive. With swallowing reflexes impaired, liquid can enter the airway and cause choking or aspiration. If they are not fully alert, skip the water and call for help.
  • Do not try to bring the temperature down with fever medicine. Antipyretics work by lowering a set point the body itself raised, as in infection. They are not considered effective against a temperature driven up by environmental heat, and can be harmful in some situations, so self-medicating is not advised.
  • Do not rehydrate with alcohol or heavily caffeinated drinks. They are widely flagged as poor choices for rehydration. Use water or an electrolyte drink.
  • Do not go straight back to work once symptoms ease. Cramps stopping is not recovery; resuming immediately invites relapse.

One more thing: do not leave a suspected heat stroke patient alone. Their condition can change within minutes, and once mental state declines they cannot call for help themselves.

Q5. How much should I drink, and what?

One precondition: only when fully alert.

Drinking only when thirsty tends to fall short in extreme heat. Thirst is a signal that arrives after dehydration has already begun, and older adults in particular are known to feel it less. The common recommendation for outdoor activity is therefore to take a few sips every 15 to 20 minutes even without thirst. Spacing intake out beats chugging, both for absorption and for stomach comfort.

If sweating has been heavy, water alone does not replace lost salt, and an electrolyte drink can help - though some contain a lot of sugar, so the label is worth a look. That said, anyone with kidney disease, heart disease, or medical fluid and salt restrictions should not increase intake on their own and should follow their doctor. Individual variation here is too large for blanket advice. If a bottle you actually carry helps you drink more, our guide to choosing a tumbler or water bottle may be useful.

Q6. Who is most at risk?

The high-risk groups that the WHO and other bodies converge on:

  • Adults over 65 - reduced thermoregulation and blunted thirst, often layered with chronic conditions and medications. Living alone and rationing air conditioning is a particularly dangerous combination.
  • Infants and young children - a higher surface-area-to-weight ratio means surrounding heat reaches them faster, and they cannot describe their own symptoms. Never leave a child alone in a parked car, not even briefly. Interior temperatures can climb to dangerous levels in a short time.
  • People with chronic conditions - cardiovascular and respiratory disease, diabetes, kidney disease. Some medications affect sweating or fluid balance, so hot-weather precautions are worth discussing with a doctor in advance. If blood pressure is the concern, our guide on lowering blood pressure naturally is a useful companion read.
  • Outdoor workers - construction, agriculture, logistics, delivery. Long exposure plus physical exertion compounds risk, and incidents cluster in the first days of a heat wave and on the first day back, before the body has acclimatized.
  • Pregnant women and people without reliable access to cooling are also singled out.

Q7. How do I prevent it?

7-1. Avoid the hours

The window from roughly noon to 5 p.m. is generally flagged as the most dangerous. Push outdoor work and exercise out of that band, lower the intensity, and where it is unavoidable, break it up with short, frequent rests.

7-2. Change your clothing and your environment

Light-colored, loose, breathable clothing, a wide-brimmed hat and shade are the basics. Indoors, maintaining adequate cooling beats aggressive energy saving. If cost is the concern, look at efficiency before switching the cooling off - our summer electricity saving guide covers that ground. If the heat is wrecking your sleep, the guide to choosing summer cooling bedding may help too.

7-3. Track the body signals

Darker urine or less frequent urination can indicate dehydration. On outdoor jobs, working in pairs and checking on each other is genuinely effective, because the one thing you cannot notice yourself is your own mental state slipping.

Q8. Are there lasting effects after recovery?

Heat exhaustion generally resolves with adequate rest and rehydration. Heat stroke is the harder case. Prolonged high core temperature can affect the brain, kidneys, liver and muscle, and reports describe fatigue, reduced concentration and increased heat sensitivity persisting for a period after recovery. When heat stroke develops during intense exertion, complications related to muscle breakdown are also discussed.

For that reason, after heat stroke it is advised not to self-clear yourself back to normal activity but to return in stages under medical guidance. It is also commonly noted that having had a heat illness once can leave a person more vulnerable to heat afterward.

Call Emergency Services Without Hesitating When

📞 Immediate Emergency-Call Criteria

  • Confusion, failure to recognize people or place, slurred speech, or strange behavior
  • A seizure, or loss of consciousness
  • Skin that is hot and dry with an apparently very high temperature
  • Repeated vomiting, leaving them unable to take fluids
  • No improvement, or worsening, after 30 minutes of rest in a cool place
  • Any of the above in an older adult, infant, or person with a chronic condition

Keep cooling after you call. When the judgment call is close, calling is the safer error.

Conclusion - Only Three Things to Remember

This has been a long walk through heat illness, but what your hands actually need to remember is short. First, mental state is the decision rule. If they answer you normally, treat it as heat exhaustion, cool them somewhere shaded and watch. If the answer is off, treat it as heat stroke and call. That single check comes before sweating, before any number on a thermometer.

Second, the core of first aid is cooling. Getting fluids in is a secondary measure that only applies to people who are fully alert; what decides outcomes in heat stroke is how fast the core temperature comes down. Move them, loosen clothing, wet the skin and fan it, chill the neck, armpits and groin. Do not stop until help arrives.

Third, not doing the wrong thing matters as much as doing the right one. No forced fluids into someone who is not fully alert. No trying to fix it with fever medicine. No rehydrating with alcohol or heavy caffeine. No walking straight back into the heat because things eased up for a minute.

Heat waves come every year, but heat illness is mostly a preventable accident. Avoid the worst hours, sip before thirst arrives, and check on the older adults and children near you one extra time. Those three habits cut summer risk substantially on their own. And in any moment where the call is genuinely unclear, emergency services are always the right answer.
