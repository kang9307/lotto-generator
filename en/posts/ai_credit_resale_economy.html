AI Credits as an Intermediate Currency - Burn Rates, Resale Markets, and Breakage

How a vendor can raise the real price without touching the price tag, and what to check before you pay

Aug 17, 2026 Dev & Infra
Scope note — This article explains the structure of credit-based pricing. It does not assert what any particular vendor policy currently says, and the regulations cited reflect what was confirmed in August 2026. Rates, burn tables and terms change often, so verify the official documents at the moment you buy. The accounting section is conceptual and is not accounting, tax or legal advice. This article also does not explain how to resell or buy relayed access. It covers only why that market exists and what it puts at risk.

Introduction: the unit price disappears at checkout

Open the pricing page of almost any AI product today and something is missing compared to a few years ago. The dollars-per-million-tokens figure is hard to find. In its place sit lines like 5,000 credits per month or a 20,000-credit starter pack. You can see how many credits you get. What you cannot see, anywhere on the same screen, is how much work those credits actually buy.

That is not a cosmetic difference. Pricing in credits inserts an exchange rate the vendor controls between your money and the compute you receive. The first rate, dollars to credits, is printed on the plan page for everyone to see. The second rate, credits to compute, usually lives in a separate document or nowhere at all, and the vendor can adjust it. When the second rate moves, the first one does not have to. Put plainly, it is a structure that lets the effective price rise without a price increase.

A second property compounds this. Credits are typically prepaid, time-limited, and bound to an account. Prepayment creates inventory, expiry makes the inventory perishable, and account binding blocks the normal way of moving it. Whenever those three conditions hold together, markets respond the same way every time: brokers appear. A secondary market forms between people sitting on balances they cannot use and people who want access below list price.

This piece walks the whole picture. Why the credit layer exists at all, where exactly it destroys a buyer ability to compare, what conditions produce resale and what that trade actually puts at risk, how unused balances are treated on the issuer books, and what to look for before you press the buy button. The short version fits in one line: whoever issues the currency also sets the exchange rate.

1. Why the credit layer exists

The decision to price in credits is rarely a marketing preference. It comes out of the cost structure. Three motives account for most of it.

1.1 It absorbs price volatility

Model rates move constantly. A new generation ships and the previous one drops, a lightweight tier appears and the price band splits again. A service that exposes token rates directly has to rewrite its pricing page, its contracts and its sales collateral every time upstream costs shift. With a credit layer, the vendor only adjusts the burn rate and the visible plan stays intact. From the vendor side that is entirely reasonable. The catch is that the same convenience works in both directions. Nothing obliges anyone to lower burn rates when upstream costs fall.

1.2 It bundles features with different unit economics

Modern AI products do not sell text alone. Image generation, video, speech synthesis, web search, code execution and file analysis live inside one product, and their underlying costs differ by orders of magnitude. Publishing a per-feature rate card turns the pricing page into a six-table quotation. Credits convert incompatible cost structures into a single number and make the plan look simple. That simplicity is genuinely useful at first glance. It is also purchased by removing information.

1.3 A balance holds people in place

A prepaid balance carries real switching friction. If thirty dollars of credits sit in your account, moving to a better alternative feels wasteful even when the alternative is clearly better. You decide to burn the balance first, and while you do, your workflow settles deeper into that product. Add expiry and a second force appears: unused credits vanish, so you find reasons to use them. Consumption rises and the next purchase follows naturally.

Put together, credits do the following for the issuer.

  • Deferred pricing power — the published price stays fixed while the effective price can be tuned later
  • Bundling — cheap and expensive features can be sold at a blended average
  • Cash in advance — money arrives before any service is delivered
  • Retention — the balance functions as a switching cost
  • Breakage — a portion is sold but never delivered

That last item gets its own section later. To a buyer it is just money left on the table. On the issuer books it is a line item with a name.

2. Compute per credit is not a fixed quantity

The most common misreading of credit pricing is treating a credit as a unit, something that denotes the same quantity everywhere the way a kilogram or a gigabyte does. It does not. A credit is closer to a table of variable exchange rates than to a unit.

The compute behind one credit moves along at least these axes.

AxisWhat actually happensVisible to the buyer
Model tierFrontier and lightweight models can differ by several to dozens of times per callUsually documented in a table
Feature typeText, image, video, speech, search and code execution each deduct at different ratesSometimes only partially published
Context lengthAttaching long documents means re-reading them every turn, so deductions growRarely surfaced
Reasoning lengthWhen the model thinks longer, output grows and so does the deductionRarely surfaced
Tool call countEvery agent round trip recomputes the input sideRarely surfaced
Retries and failuresSome designs still charge for what was processed before a failureDepends on policy
Priority processingFast queues commonly deduct at a multipleUsually stated

The right-hand column is the important one. Many of the factors that drive burn rate are values a buyer cannot compute at the time of purchase. No amount of careful document reading tells you what your own daily workload costs in credits. You find out by running it, and by then you have already bought the credits.

So there is exactly one question credit pricing can answer honestly: how many credits did one pass of my own workload consume? Not an absolute unit price, but your own conversion rate. If you never record that number, you lose the only baseline that would let you detect a change later.

3. Where comparison breaks, and how to notice a change

From a consumer-protection angle, the real problem with credits is not that they are expensive. It is that you cannot tell whether they are expensive. Comparison breaks in three specific places.

3.1 Cross-vendor comparison does not hold

Five thousand credits on service A and five thousand on service B are not the same quantity to begin with. The moment you put the two numbers side by side and conclude that A gives you more, the comparison is already wrong. A genuine comparison would require running the same workload on both and measuring consumption, which means paying for both. The cost of comparing is designed to exceed the cost of buying.

3.2 Comparison over time does not hold either

This one is nastier. If the plan still offers 5,000 credits for the same money, buyers read that as a stable price. But if the burn rate was adjusted in the meantime, the compute those credits actually buy has shrunk. That is an increase that does not look like one. Where the terms carry no notification duty, it can happen without any announcement at all. Silently swapping the default model for a lighter one produces the same outcome from a different direction: credits unchanged, quality down.

3.3 Coarse usage reporting makes verification impossible

Plenty of services show a monthly total such as 3,120 credits used and nothing per request. That makes it impossible to know which task consumed what, and equally impossible to detect a burn rate change. The granularity of the usage screen is not a convenience feature, it is your verification instrument.

Given that structure, the buyer side response is fairly settled. It does not need to be elaborate.

  • Fix a benchmark workload — pick two or three tasks you genuinely repeat and freeze them
  • Record the initial consumption — right after purchase, run them and write down the credits deducted
  • Re-measure periodically — monthly is enough. A widening gap means the effective price moved
  • Pin the model — with automatic model selection on, burn changes and model changes blur together
  • Check export — if usage history exports to CSV or an API, you have evidence if a dispute ever arises

Once that habit sticks, you stop evaluating services by the number on the pricing page and start evaluating them by your own conversion rate. Under credit pricing, that is the only metric that means anything.

4. Where the secondary market comes from

Once prepaid credits reach a certain scale, brokers follow. There is nothing novel about it. The same thing happened with airline miles, mobile gift vouchers, in-game currencies and reserved cloud instances, and the cause was identical every time: inventory with a non-transfer clause attached always grows a back door.

AI credits satisfy those conditions unusually well.

  • Large free grants — startup programmes, cloud marketplace commitments and early partnerships distribute far more credits than the recipient needs
  • Rigid commitment sizes — annual prepaid commitments are hard to unwind when plans change
  • Expiry — a remaining balance is worth zero the day it lapses, and a ninety percent discount still beats zero
  • No refunds — when the legitimate route to cash is closed, an illegitimate route develops a price
  • Price discrimination — when the same compute costs different amounts by region, programme or contract size, arbitrage appears

One more structural detail decides the shape of the trade. Credits are usually bound to an account, so the balance itself cannot be handed over. The transaction therefore takes the form of relayed access rather than a sale of credits: the seller pools keys behind a relay, and the buyer receives an endpoint that looks and behaves like the original provider interface. On the surface it is simply a cheaper API.

Nobody knows the true size of this market, because no public statistics exist. One observation write-up published in August 2026 estimated the volume visible in broker listings across several channels at tens of millions of dollars, with advertised discounts running from roughly 30 to 80 percent off list. That is an estimate scraped from public listings, not settled transaction value. The direction is clear enough, though: there is sufficient supply for brokering to graduate from one-off deals into standing platforms.

5. What travels with you through a broker

Judged on price alone, brokered access looks attractive. But price is not the only thing that changes hands in that trade. The contractual relationship and the data path change too. Breaking down where those land makes the decision straightforward.

5.1 Contracts — terms violations and account suspension

Provider terms of service commonly restrict account sharing, credit transfer and resale of access. The exact wording and exceptions vary by vendor so nothing can be asserted categorically, but the restriction itself is widespread. From that, several consequences follow.

  • Suspension is not limited to the seller. The account receiving relayed traffic can be actioned too
  • Once suspended, a remaining balance is often treated as forfeited rather than refundable
  • If the broker disappears, there is no basis to recover what you paid, and consumer dispute mechanisms may not apply
  • For companies this becomes a procurement and audit finding: unauthorised purchasing channel, unverifiable licence compliance

5.2 Data — your requests transit a third party

This matters more. In a relay arrangement the request body necessarily passes through the broker servers. Consider what is inside those requests and the size of the problem becomes obvious: customer enquiries, draft contracts, source code, internal metrics, spreadsheets containing personal data. All of it crosses third-party infrastructure with no verified contractual relationship.

  • Retention and logging — whether requests and responses are stored, for how long and who can read them is effectively unverifiable
  • No contract inheritance — data processing agreements, no-training commitments and security certifications signed with the original provider do not transfer to a broker
  • Processor disclosure — if personal data is involved, processing delegation and cross-border transfer must be disclosed, which is impossible when you cannot identify the processor
  • Provenance of responses — you cannot readily verify that the model you requested is the model that answered. Routing to something cheaper is not immediately visible
  • Billing basis — the broker generates the usage numbers, and there is no provider dashboard to reconcile against

In a corporate setting the last three lines end the evaluation on their own. A path where response provenance and billing basis cannot be verified will not survive an audit no matter how much it saves, and once personal data is in transit the saving stops being the comparable quantity at all.

5.3 What this section deliberately omits

Which channels exist, how to reach them, and which arrangements evade detection are not covered here. Those are operating instructions for conduct that likely breaches provider terms. What is useful is not the method but why the market formed, and that answer sits in the previous section. Block rollover and refunds, and the inventory does not disappear. It relocates.

6. The issuer side — prepaid credits and breakage

To a buyer, unused credits are simply wasted money. On the issuer books they are a named line item, and that name explains the incentive structure behind this pricing model. What follows is conceptual and is not accounting advice.

When credits are sold, the cash is not revenue yet. No service has been delivered, so it is recorded as a contract liability, or deferred revenue. As the buyer spends credits and compute is actually provided, that amount moves into revenue. So far this is identical to gift vouchers or stored-value balances.

The interesting part is the portion never spent. In accounting this is a customer unexercised right, and practitioners generally call it breakage. IFRS 15, the standard on revenue from contracts with customers, treats it along two broad paths.

  • Proportional recognition — if the entity expects to be entitled to a breakage amount, it recognises that amount as revenue in proportion to the pattern of rights exercised by the customer
  • Recognition on expiry — if the breakage amount cannot be reliably estimated, revenue is recognised when the likelihood of the customer exercising the remaining rights becomes remote

One structural observation follows. Shorter expiry pulls breakage recognition forward. With no expiry, judging when the likelihood becomes remote is difficult and the liability lingers. With an expiry date, it resolves on that date. In other words, a clause that disadvantages the buyer points in a direction that flatters the issuer financial statements. Claiming that any specific company designs its terms for that reason would be an unfounded assertion, but the incentive genuinely leans that way, and that is worth naming.

One more wrinkle. Promotional credits handed out for free involve no cash received, so they are not deferred revenue at all. Yet when they are spent, real compute cost is incurred. How large free-grant programmes flow into a company cost base is not something outside observers can read off the disclosures, which is why announcements of hundreds of millions in credit support are hard to weigh from the outside.

7. Where regulation sits, using Korea as a worked example

Korean buyers have an established reference point, because rules on prepaid balances already exist along two separate tracks. What matters here is that AI credits do not clearly fall inside either. The same gap exists in most jurisdictions, so the example generalises.

7.1 The standard terms for electronic gift vouchers

The Korea Fair Trade Commission publishes standard terms covering mobile, electronic and online gift vouchers. As confirmed in August 2026, the broad shape is that even after the stated validity period, a substantial share of the purchase price remains refundable within the five-year commercial prescription period; value-denominated vouchers carry a minimum validity of at least one year; and once a set proportion has been spent, the remaining balance can be redeemed. The specific percentages and thresholds have been revised over time, so exact figures should be read from the source.

Hold AI credits against that benchmark and the gap is wide. Validity that resets monthly, no refund clause for unused balances, and forfeiture on cancellation are all common. That said, standard terms are a recommended template rather than a rule binding every operator, and whether AI credits count as gift vouchers is a separate question. There is a strong argument that they are a service entitlement instead.

7.2 Prepaid electronic payment instruments

The stored-value track carries stronger rules. The amended Electronic Financial Transactions Act, effective 15 September 2024, obliges prepaid issuers to segregate customer float through trust, deposit or payment guarantee insurance, so customer money does not mingle with operating funds.

That regime is unlikely to reach AI credits, though. The statutory definition of a prepaid electronic payment instrument presumes an instrument usable with third parties beyond the issuer, whereas AI credits are generally spendable only inside the issuing company own service. Self-issued instruments of that kind frequently fall outside the definition. The practical consequence is that customer prepayments may not be segregated at all, and if the company shuts the service down, the balance sits in the position of an ordinary unsecured claim.

The summary is uncomfortable but simple. Rules governing prepaid balances clearly exist, yet AI credits are not clearly inside the circle those rules draw. Protection therefore comes from the individual terms of service, not from statute. Reading the terms is not a formality here. It is the only line of defence you actually have.

8. Subscription, pay-as-you-go and credits compared

No model is universally better. What is clear is which risk each one transfers to the buyer.

Monthly spend predictabilityFixedUncertainCeiling fixed, exhaustion date uncertain

Visibility of price changesAn increase is obviousShows up as a rate revisionBurn rate changes are easy to miss

If you want a single decision rule, use usage variance. For someone whose consumption is similar every month, credits are unnecessary complexity; a subscription is cheaper and easier to manage. For a pattern where one month is ten times another and the next is zero, pay-as-you-go is the honest choice. Credits sit between the two as a compromise tilted toward the issuer. If you still pick a credit product, the reason should be a capability only that product has, not the price.

9. Six things to check before you pay

The list of items to hunt for in the terms and pricing docs is finite. And where an item is absent, that absence is itself information.

What to checkWhy it mattersIf it is not stated
Expiry and forfeiturePurchased and granted credits often expire on different clocksAssume the shortest window and start small
RolloverWith a monthly reset, whatever is left is a monthly lossAssume a reset and defer upgrading the tier
Refund policyMid-term cancellation is where treatment diverges mostAssume no refund and avoid large prepayments
Published burn ratesWithout them you cannot compute an effective unit priceSubstitute measurement and keep the records
Usage reportingPer-request detail and export become evidence in a disputeKeep your own logs
Change notificationHow many days of notice precede a burn rate change is the cruxAssume unannounced changes and shrink the commitment

Depending on your situation, a few items get added to that list.

  • Transfer and sharing clauses — check whether several people using one team account is already a breach. It sometimes is, even internally
  • Auto top-up — find the default setting for automatic recharge when the balance runs low
  • Default model substitution — a clause letting the vendor change the default model moves quality and burn rate together
  • Training use — some products apply different input-data training policies by plan tier
  • Shutdown handling — check whether notice periods and balance treatment on service termination are written down
  • Payment method for business buyers — invoiced contracts usually leave you better positioned in a dispute than a prepaid card charge

One practical habit is worth adding: make the first purchase the smallest pack available. Even if the bulk tier has a lower headline rate, that discount is unquantified until you have measured your own burn. Run a month, derive your conversion rate, then scale.

10. What could not be confirmed

Items left blank because the evidence was not there, collected in one place.

  • Current burn rates and expiry terms by vendor — no company-specific figures were pinned to a date here. These values change without notice, and hard-coding them into an article makes it wrong quickly
  • Actual transaction volume in the broker market — no public statistics exist. The tens-of-millions figure cited above is an estimate based on observing public listings, not verified turnover
  • Legal classification precedents — no published regulatory interpretation or court decision was located on whether AI credits are gift vouchers or service entitlements
  • Per-issuer breakage policy — breakage is rarely broken out as a separate disclosure line, which makes company-by-company comparison impractical

11. Conclusion: whoever issues the currency sets the rate

There is no reason to call credit pricing bad in itself. For a product with many different capabilities it is a reasonable way to keep a rate card readable. The problem is that the simplicity is bought by removing information, and that all control over the removed information sits with the issuer.

So a buyer defence narrows to two things. First, measure and record your own conversion rate. The number on the pricing page is not a basis for comparison. Second, keep prepaid balances small. A balance is not a convenience, it is exposure: it can expire, it is hard to recover if the service closes, and it holds you in place when something better appears.

The resale market is a by-product of that structure. It is only the newest instance of an old fact, that inventory without a legitimate exit will find an illegitimate one. Anyone tempted by the discount should remember that opening that door also changes the contractual relationship and the data path. The saving is visible immediately; the risk you accepted stays invisible until something goes wrong.

What to want from issuers is equally clear. Publish per-model and per-feature burn rates on the same screen as the plan, commit to a notice period before adjusting them, and write unambiguous sentences about rollover and refunds. None of the three is a difficult request. If a service already does all three, that fact alone is a better reason to choose it than price.
