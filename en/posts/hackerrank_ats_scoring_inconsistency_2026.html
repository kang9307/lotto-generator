Same Resume, Scores 90 Then 74 Then 88 — AI Hiring's Nondeterminism Problem

LLM-based ATS produces different scores for identical input — why unreproducible hiring evaluation is a fairness and legal crisis

2026-07-01 Trends & Culture

Introduction: A Hiring Screener That Rolls Dice

In June 2026, a developer submitted his resume to HackerRank's open-sourced Applicant Tracking System. First run: 90 points. Second run, same resume, no changes: 74 points. Third: 88 points. A subsequent analysis found that running the same resume 100 times produced scores spread from 66 all the way to 99.

The question this raises is not narrow. If an identically qualified applicant gets filtered in or out depending on which probabilistic path an LLM happened to take that millisecond, can we call that a fair evaluation? This piece works through the technical cause, the legal exposure, the fixes available, and what applicants and employers should actually do — as of June 2026.

A note on position: I am not arguing AI ATS is inherently bad. Manually reading thousands of resumes is not realistic, and well-designed AI tools can reduce certain human biases. But a system that cannot reproduce its own scores is a different category of problem. It is not making hiring decisions based on merit — it is running a lottery dressed as an evaluation.

1. What HackerRank's Open-Source ATS Revealed

1.1 How the System Works

When HackerRank open-sourced its AI hiring agent code in early 2026, analysts could read exactly what happens under the hood. The system parses the resume PDF to text, then calls an LLM a minimum of six times to extract structured data: basics, work history, education, skills, projects, and awards. Each extracted chunk gets a sub-score. The final score is a weighted sum of those sub-scores.

1.2 Why the Score Fluctuates

LLMs are probabilistic generative models. When temperature is above zero, the model samples different tokens on every inference call even with identical input. The HackerRank ATS calls the LLM at least six times per resume, so variance from each call compounds into the final score. As one analyst put it, asking an LLM to decide whether a candidate's experience deserves 18 points or 24 points is a "vibe-check" — as long as the rubric is not defined numerically, the LLM reinterprets it differently each time.

At a systems level, this ATS lacks idempotency. An idempotent system guarantees identical output for identical input. In a high-stakes domain where the output determines career opportunities, the absence of idempotency is not a technical quirk — it is a structural injustice.

1.3 What Score Variance Means in Practice

Consider a company that sets its first-round cutoff at 80 points. A resume that scores 66 on one run and 99 on the next will pass or fail depending entirely on execution timing. The candidate's qualifications did not change. What changed was which random path through the probability distribution the LLM followed. That is not merit-based screening — it is a draw from a lottery.

2. Nondeterminism in LLM Evaluation — Beyond Hiring

2.1 LLM-as-Judge Is Broadly Unreliable at High Stakes

The same nondeterminism problem exists wherever LLMs serve as evaluators: essay grading, code review, medical document classification. Academic frameworks for high-stakes AI evaluation set reliability thresholds at ICC (intraclass correlation coefficient) ≥ 0.90. Many LLM-based evaluation pipelines do not meet that bar. Hiring ATS sits at the extreme end of consequence severity — a failed eval can cost someone months of job search, not just a grade.

2.2 Bias and Nondeterminism Compound Each Other

Nondeterminism (different scores for the same resume) and bias (systematically lower scores for certain demographic groups) are distinct problems, but they interact in the wild. Multiple 2025-2026 studies found that LLM-based resume screeners score resumes with Black- or female-associated names lower, even when all other content is identical. When the scoring system is highly nondeterministic, that bias manifests inconsistently — making it harder to detect in standard audits. The two problems hide each other.

2.3 Parsing Errors Amplify Score Variance

Before any LLM judgment happens, the resume must be correctly parsed from PDF to text. Resumes with tables, multi-column layouts, or image-embedded text generate parsing errors that vary across runs. A mis-parsed employment date or dropped skill section can shift the sub-score significantly — and that mis-parse itself may or may not occur on a given run. Score instability originates in both the parsing layer and the LLM reasoning layer.

3. Legal Risk — Unreproducible Scores Are a Regulatory Target

3.1 EEOC Disparate Impact Doctrine

The U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission has made algorithmic fairness a named priority in its 2024–2028 Strategic Enforcement Plan. The operative legal theory is disparate impact: even without discriminatory intent, employers are liable when an AI hiring tool produces statistically lower selection rates for protected classes (race, gender, age, disability). A system whose scores fluctuate wildly weakens the employer's ability to demonstrate consistent, lawful criteria — exactly what disparate impact defense requires.

3.2 The Cases Shaping 2026 Enforcement

In February 2026, a federal court authorized class notice in Mobley v. Workday, the most-watched AI hiring discrimination case, alleging that Workday's AI screening software unlawfully filtered out applicants based on age, race, and disability. Earlier, the EEOC secured a $365,000 settlement from iTutorGroup after its AI screening tool automatically rejected women over 55 and men over 60 — the first major EEOC enforcement action targeting an algorithmic hiring tool for age discrimination.

At the state level: New York City mandates annual independent bias audits for any automated employment decision tool (AEDT) and requires candidate notification at least 10 business days before use. Colorado's AI Act, effective mid-2026, classifies employment AI as high-risk and requires impact assessments and candidate notifications. Illinois Human Rights Act amendments effective 2026 explicitly prohibit discriminatory AI use in hiring.

3.3 The Reproducibility Audit Problem

When a hiring discrimination case goes to court, the employer must demonstrate what criteria led to a specific rejection. A system that produces different scores on every run makes that demonstration nearly impossible. "Our system scored this applicant 74 points" is a weak defense when the same resume scores 90 on a different run. Nondeterminism is simultaneously a technical failure and a legal liability.

4. Making LLM Hiring Evaluation More Reliable

4.1 Temperature Control

The most direct fix is setting temperature to 0 or very close to 0 (below 0.1). At temperature 0, the model always selects the highest-probability token, producing near-deterministic outputs. Research on LLM reproducibility commonly uses temperature ≤ 0.3 as a practical reproducibility threshold. Note that even at temperature 0, minor variance can arise from floating-point arithmetic differences across hardware runs — but the range collapses from 33 points to fractions of a point.

4.2 Ensemble Scoring

Running the same resume N times (e.g., 5–10) and using the mean or median as the final score significantly reduces variance. Using multiple different LLMs in parallel and combining outputs is an even stronger ensemble. Research on RAG-based resume screening frameworks found ensemble methods consistently outperform single-LLM approaches on both accuracy and consistency. The cost is N-fold inference spend — in hiring, where a wrong filter has real consequences, that cost is often justified.

4.3 Specify Rubrics Numerically

Abstract rubric criteria like "strong backend experience" force the LLM to interpret — and it will interpret differently each time. Replacing vague descriptors with explicit point ranges ("5+ years backend = 20 pts; 3–5 years = 15 pts; under 3 years = 10 pts") collapses the interpretive space and reduces variance. Rubrics themselves can encode bias, so the design process must include fairness review — but a specific rubric is a prerequisite for any meaningful consistency.

4.4 Separate Parsing from Scoring

Parsing the resume into structured JSON should be validated as a distinct step before scoring. Storing the parsed intermediate representation lets you audit what the LLM actually saw, re-score from the same parsed input for reproducibility, and isolate parsing errors from scoring errors. Forcing structured output (JSON schema) from the LLM rather than free text reduces format-level variance.

5. Human Review Is Not Optional

5.1 Why AI Cannot Replace Final Human Judgment

Even with ensemble scoring and low temperature, routing final hiring decisions entirely through an automated score is a mistake. Hiring involves dimensions that do not compress into a point total: team culture fit, role-specific context, potential that is not yet on a resume. Compression always destroys information. The higher the stakes, the more that lost information matters.

5.2 Use Scores as a First Filter, Not a Verdict

AI ATS scores should narrow the candidate pool to a manageable size for human review — not automatically confirm or reject. Rather than a hard cutoff score, using "top N% proceed" or "top N candidates proceed" reduces the impact of nondeterminism at the margin. Candidates near any threshold should be flagged for mandatory human review rather than auto-filtered by a score that could be 74 or 90 depending on when the model ran.

5.3 Audit Logs and Explainability

Every scoring run should log: the LLM version used, temperature setting, prompt version, and the full scoring breakdown by sub-category. When a discrimination claim arises, the employer needs to reconstruct exactly what happened. Explainable score breakdowns ("this candidate scored 12/20 on experience because the parsed tenure was 2.5 years against a 5-year target") give applicants transparency and give auditors something concrete to verify.

6. What Applicants and Employers Should Do Now

6.1 For Applicants

An AI ATS score is a probabilistic estimate of your qualifications — not a verdict. The same resume submitted on a different day could pass or fail. Practically: include exact keywords from the job description, quantify achievements with numbers and timeframes ("reduced response time from 200ms to 50ms", "grew revenue 30% YoY"), and use clean text layouts without heavy tables or graphics. These practices reduce parsing errors and improve LLM extraction accuracy. A rejection may reflect the ATS lottery as much as your actual qualifications.

6.2 For Employers

Before deploying any AI ATS, run an idempotency test: submit the same resume 100 times and measure the score range. If the range exceeds 5–10 points, demand temperature controls, ensemble scoring, and rubric specification from your vendor. Conduct regular bias audits: measure acceptance rate differences by gender, race, and age group. In jurisdictions with active regulations (NYC, Colorado, Illinois, and growing), independent annual audits are now a legal requirement. Never use AI scores as automatic rejection triggers — require human review at every final stage.

6.3 For Developers Building ATS

An ATS directly affects people's careers. Idempotency testing — measuring score variance across 100 identical inputs — must be part of your release checklist, not an afterthought. Pin LLM versions to prevent score drift over time, use fixed random seeds where the API supports them, and version-control your prompts as strictly as your code. A high-temperature default may make scores feel more "natural" but it is a reproducibility illusion. Document and expose your temperature and rubric configuration so customers can audit it.

7. Conclusion — A Score Is an Estimate, Not a Verdict

A system that scores 90, then 74, then 88 is not evaluating — it is estimating. Estimation is not inherently wrong; all assessment involves uncertainty. The problem is when probabilistic estimates function as definitive verdicts, filtering human careers without anyone acknowledging the lottery embedded in the process.

The technical fixes exist: temperature control, ensemble scoring, explicit rubrics, structured parsing. The regulatory pressure is building: EEOC enforcement, state laws, class actions. The business case for reliable AI hiring is clear. What is missing, in most deployments, is the will to spend the extra inference cost and engineering time to make the system reproducible.

The question is no longer "should we use AI in hiring?" That ship has sailed — Fortune 500 companies have already moved in this direction. The question is "are we using AI that we can stand behind when a rejected candidate, a regulator, or a court asks us to explain the score?" If the honest answer is "the score might have been different if we ran it again," there is work to do.
