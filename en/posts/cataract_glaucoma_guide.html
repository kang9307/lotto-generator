Cataracts vs Glaucoma - Complete Guide to Symptoms, Causes, Treatment and Prevention

Cloudy lens vs damaged optic nerve - an evidence-based guide to two of the most common eye diseases

Jun 17, 2026 Health & Wellness
Medical disclaimer. This article is for educational and informational purposes only and is not medical advice. Diagnosis of eye conditions, decisions about medication, laser therapy, or surgery must be made with a licensed ophthalmologist. The information here is a general summary based on sources including the American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO), Mayo Clinic, and the National Institutes of Health (NIH), and does not substitute for professional evaluation of your individual condition. If you experience sudden eye pain, sudden vision loss, or see halos around lights with nausea, seek emergency eye care immediately.

Introduction: two diseases, two completely different mechanisms

Cataracts and glaucoma are often confused - both affect vision and both become more common with age. But their underlying mechanisms, symptoms, and treatments are fundamentally different. Cataracts occur when the eye's natural lens becomes cloudy, blurring light before it reaches the retina. Glaucoma damages the optic nerve, usually because of elevated pressure inside the eye.

The single most important difference for patients: cataract surgery removes the cloudy lens and replaces it with a clear artificial one, restoring vision. Glaucoma damage to the optic nerve is irreversible with current medicine. Treatment slows progression but cannot restore what has been lost. That is why early detection matters so much for glaucoma.

This guide draws on the AAO, Mayo Clinic, NIH, and the Glaucoma Research Foundation to explain how each disease works, how to recognize it, how it is treated, and what you can do to protect your eyes.

2. Cataracts - what you need to know

2.1 What is a cataract?

A cataract forms when proteins in the eye's natural lens break down and clump together, making the lens cloudy. The lens sits behind the iris and pupil and is responsible for focusing light onto the retina. When it clouds over, light scatters rather than focusing cleanly, producing the characteristic blurry, glary vision. According to the Mayo Clinic, most people who live long enough will develop cataracts; by age 80, most people either have cataracts or have had cataract surgery.

2.2 Causes and risk factors

  • Aging: the dominant cause - protein breakdown accelerates after age 40
  • Diabetes: elevated blood sugar promotes lens protein glycation
  • Smoking: increases oxidative stress in the lens
  • UV radiation: cumulative UV-B exposure damages lens proteins
  • Long-term steroid use: especially associated with posterior subcapsular cataracts
  • Eye injury or surgery: trauma and some surgical procedures can accelerate cataracts
  • High blood pressure, obesity, excess alcohol: indirect risk factors
  • Family history: genetics play some role

2.3 Symptoms

  • Blurry, hazy, or foggy vision
  • Faded or yellowed colors
  • Increased sensitivity to glare from lights or sunlight
  • Halos around lights at night
  • Difficulty reading fine print even with glasses
  • Frequent changes in eyeglass prescription (myopic shift)
  • Poor vision in dim light or at night

2.4 Diagnosis

An ophthalmologist uses a slit-lamp examination to inspect the lens directly. A dilated eye exam (using drops to widen the pupil) allows a better view of the degree of clouding. Visual acuity tests, glare tests, and intraocular pressure measurements are usually performed at the same visit.

2.5 Treatment

In the early stages, stronger reading glasses, anti-glare lenses, and better lighting can help manage symptoms. When cataracts begin to interfere with daily life - reading, driving, working - surgery is the definitive treatment.

Phacoemulsification is the current standard: a small incision is made, an ultrasonic probe breaks the cloudy lens into fragments for aspiration, and a foldable intraocular lens (IOL) is inserted in its place. The procedure typically takes 15-30 minutes and is performed under local anesthesia on an outpatient basis. According to the Mayo Clinic, it is one of the most commonly performed surgeries worldwide with a high success rate. Individual outcomes vary; discuss risks, IOL options, and expected recovery with your surgeon.

3. Glaucoma - what you need to know

3.1 What is glaucoma?

Glaucoma is a group of eye diseases that damage the optic nerve - the cable of more than a million nerve fibers that carries visual information from the eye to the brain. In most cases, damage is driven by elevated intraocular pressure (IOP), though glaucoma can also occur at normal pressure (normal-tension glaucoma). The AAO identifies glaucoma as one of the leading causes of blindness worldwide. Because early-stage open-angle glaucoma produces no symptoms, millions of people have it without knowing.

3.2 Types of glaucoma

  • Open-angle glaucoma: the most common type. The drainage angle between the iris and cornea remains open, but the trabecular meshwork drains too slowly, so IOP rises gradually. No pain, no obvious symptoms until peripheral vision is lost.
  • Angle-closure glaucoma: the drainage angle is suddenly blocked, causing a rapid IOP spike. Symptoms include sudden severe eye pain, headache, nausea, vomiting, blurred vision, and rainbow halos around lights. This is an ocular emergency requiring immediate treatment.
  • Normal-tension glaucoma: optic nerve damage despite IOP within the normal range; exact mechanism is incompletely understood.
  • Secondary glaucoma: caused by another condition (diabetic retinopathy, uveitis) or medication (corticosteroids).

3.3 Causes and risk factors

  • Elevated IOP: impaired aqueous humor drainage is the most important risk factor
  • Age: risk rises sharply after 60
  • Family history: having a first-degree relative with glaucoma increases risk 2-3 times
  • High myopia: structural features of the highly myopic eye increase susceptibility
  • Diabetes and high blood pressure: associated with vascular risk to the optic nerve
  • Long-term corticosteroid use: eye drops or systemic steroids both count
  • Previous eye injury or surgery

3.4 Symptoms

Open-angle glaucoma typically produces no symptoms for years. Peripheral (side) vision is lost first; because the two eyes compensate for each other and the brain fills in gaps, patients often do not notice until damage is advanced. Warning signs requiring prompt attention:

  • Gradual loss of peripheral vision (noticed late)
  • Tunnel vision in advanced disease
  • Acute angle-closure: sudden severe eye pain, headache, nausea/vomiting, rainbow halos around lights - EMERGENCY

3.5 Diagnosis

A comprehensive glaucoma evaluation includes: tonometry (IOP measurement), optic disc examination, visual field testing, gonioscopy (to view the drainage angle), and optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the optic nerve fiber layer. Because symptoms arrive late, regular eye exams are essential for at-risk individuals.

3.6 Treatment

The goal of all glaucoma treatments is to lower IOP enough to prevent further optic nerve damage. Lost vision cannot be recovered.

  • Eye drops: typically the first-line treatment. Classes include prostaglandin analogues (most commonly prescribed), beta-blockers, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, and alpha-agonists. Drops must be used consistently as prescribed - skipping doses allows IOP to rise.
  • Selective laser trabeculoplasty (SLT): research presented at the AAO 2024 meeting found glaucoma progressed 29% more slowly in patients treated with laser versus eye drops alone, and SLT is increasingly considered a first-line option for open-angle glaucoma. It works by improving drainage through the trabecular meshwork.
  • Surgery: trabeculectomy or minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) creates a new drainage pathway or implants a device to lower IOP when drops and laser are insufficient.

The target IOP and treatment pathway are individualized based on the degree of optic nerve damage, rate of progression, age, and comorbidities. Never stop glaucoma drops without medical advice.

4. Can you have both at the same time?

Yes. Cataracts and glaucoma often coexist, especially in older adults. An advanced or long-neglected cataract can cause the lens to swell or release material that blocks aqueous drainage, triggering elevated IOP (phacolytic or phacomorphic glaucoma). Conversely, some glaucoma surgeries can accelerate cataract development. Managing both conditions simultaneously requires close coordination with your ophthalmologist.

5. Prevention and protective habits

No lifestyle change has been proven to completely prevent cataracts or glaucoma. However, the following measures are supported by evidence as risk-reducing and as aids to early detection:

5.1 Regular eye examinations

The single most important protective habit, particularly for glaucoma. Guidelines from the AAO and other bodies recommend:

  • Adults 40-54 with no risk factors: every 2-4 years
  • Adults 55-64: every 1-3 years
  • Adults 65 and older: every 1-2 years
  • High-risk individuals (family history, diabetes, high myopia): annually or as advised by a doctor

5.2 UV-blocking sunglasses

Wearing sunglasses that block UV-A and UV-B radiation is associated with reduced cataract risk. A broad-brimmed hat adds protection.

5.3 Not smoking

Smoking increases oxidative stress in the lens and reduces blood flow to the optic nerve, raising risk for both conditions.

5.4 Managing systemic conditions

Controlling blood sugar in diabetes and keeping blood pressure within normal range help protect both the lens and the optic nerve's blood supply.

5.5 Diet and exercise

A diet rich in leafy green vegetables (lutein and zeaxanthin) and antioxidant vitamins C and E may support eye health. Regular moderate aerobic exercise has been shown in some studies to modestly lower IOP. Evidence for specific supplements preventing glaucoma or cataracts remains limited; discuss options with your doctor before starting.

5.6 Caution with corticosteroids

Both topical and systemic steroids can raise IOP and accelerate cataracts with prolonged use. Do not use steroid eye drops without a prescription or beyond the prescribed duration.

6. Emergency warning signs - seek care immediately

  • Sudden severe pain in one eye
  • Sudden onset of rainbow halos around lights
  • Blurred vision accompanied by nausea or vomiting
  • Sudden loss of part or all of your vision in one eye
  • Red eye with severe headache

These may indicate acute angle-closure glaucoma or another vision-threatening emergency. Untreated acute angle-closure can cause permanent vision loss within hours.

7. FAQ

What is the main difference between cataracts and glaucoma?

Cataracts cloud the lens - vision loss is reversible with surgery. Glaucoma damages the optic nerve - vision loss is irreversible. Treatment aims to slow progression, not restore sight.

Can glaucoma have no symptoms at all?

Yes. Open-angle glaucoma, the most common form, is often symptom-free until advanced stages. Regular eye exams are the only reliable way to catch it early.

When is cataract surgery recommended?

When visual impairment from cataracts interferes with daily activities. Discuss timing with your ophthalmologist - there is no single right answer.

Do glaucoma drops have to be used every day indefinitely?

Usually yes. Glaucoma is a chronic condition. Stopping drops without advice allows IOP to rise and damage to accelerate. If side effects are problematic, ask your doctor about alternatives.

Which foods are best for eye health?

Leafy greens (spinach, kale) rich in lutein and zeaxanthin, and foods high in vitamins C and E are commonly recommended. No single food is proven to prevent cataracts or glaucoma, but a balanced diet supports overall eye health.
