What an AI Governance Document Actually Stops, and What It Cannot

A policy with no named approver is just a document - on the location of decision rights and the ability to reconstruct events afterwards

2026-09-05 Tech & IT

Most organizations already have an AI usage policy. It typically runs three or four pages, lists prohibitions alongside recommendations, and closes with a section on consequences for violations. And yet incidents still happen in organizations that hold such a document. The shape of the incident is always similar. Somebody pasted a customer list in to have it summarized. Somebody quietly swapped the model. Somebody handed work to a partner firm, and nobody knows which tools that work passed through.

The cause is not the absence of a policy. It is that the policy records prohibitions but not approvals. A prohibition says do not do this. An approval says if you want to do it, go to this person. A document with only the first kind of sentence achieves nothing at the moment of execution, because nobody has been designated to stop anything.

What follows summarizes statutory and standards requirements at a general level. It is not legal advice about any particular organization, and it does not decide case-specific questions such as whether a given contract constitutes consignment or which transfer exception applies. Real-world application should go through the guidance of the competent authority and a review by qualified legal counsel.

The trigger - what an investment firm asked of CEOs

On 19 August 2026, the venture capital firm Bessemer Venture Partners published a piece on AI governance through its own content channel, and a summary circulated in Korean developer communities. The fact that the publisher is an investment firm is a condition to place on the table before reading. An investor has a direct interest in reducing the regulatory exposure of its portfolio companies, and writing of this kind is generally aimed at those companies and their boards. So it is more accurate to read what follows not as fact but as the claims of that piece.

The piece argues that AI governance is a responsibility of the CEO and the board that cannot be delegated to legal or to the information security function alone. Its reasoning is that conventional cybersecurity was designed around blocking access, whereas with AI the influence that contaminated data exerts on a model judgment also becomes an object of management. It therefore proposes supplementing the familiar triad of confidentiality, integrity and availability with three other axes: sensitivity, criticality and compliance. Beyond asking whether the data can be protected, it argues, an organization must ask whether the data should be used to influence an intelligent system.

It then lists six principles of chain of custody: identifiable origin, documented possession, tamper evidence, integrity verification, access control logs, and preservation. And it sets out seven things a CEO should do: designate an accountable AI lead, require a data provenance audit of existing AI systems, validate acceptable use policies, verify that the cybersecurity program has adapted to AI, require independent audits and traceability of data, develop and approve a formal AI ethics framework, and build chain of custody requirements into vendor contracts. On boards it is blunter still. The sentence in that piece reads that if those policies have not been reviewed by the board, they do not function as governance.

That is the extent of the original claims. It is also worth being explicit about what the piece does not contain. There is no mention of circumvention through personal accounts, which is to say shadow AI. There is nothing on behavior shifting because a vendor swapped the model. There is no separate treatment of partner firms and sub-consignment paths. It does not reference the framework from the US standards body or any international certification standard. It does say to put requirements into vendor contracts, but it does not address the situation in which the counterparty to that contract changes its own terms. So this article does not summarize the original; it fills in the next column.

The three things a document really does stop

None of this means governance documents are useless. There are things only a document can do. Three of them.

First, advance agreement on the boundary. If you decide in advance which class of data may go into an external model, then when an incident happens you can tell whether it was a misjudgment or a rule violation. Without that distinction every incident gets filed as individual carelessness, and the same incident recurs next quarter.

Second, a basis for attributing accountability. If the document names the person who was supposed to review an output, the organization can discuss improvement when the output turns out to be wrong. Without it, the discussion becomes a hunt for a culprit.

Third, requirements that carry over into contracts. A requirement written in an internal document can move into procurement language and contract clauses. This is very nearly the only route by which a document exerts force outside the organization.

All three come with one condition attached. The approver has to be written down as a person. Not a job title, not a team, but the seat where that decision is made. If the seat is empty, all three effects vanish. What ethical principles themselves demand of an organization is covered separately in the Korean AI ethics principles, so it is not repeated here. This article looks at the arrangement of decision rights that sits underneath those principles.

Four decision rights - who approves what

The points in an AI rollout that genuinely need a sign-off are fewer than people expect. They reduce to four. Pile all four onto one person and approval becomes a formality; attach them to nobody and the policy becomes a notice board.

DecisionWhat actually gets settledWhat happens when the approver seat is empty
Model selectionWhich vendor and which model attach to which workload, including whether a swap requires re-approvalEvery department wires in a different model, and after an incident nobody can pin down which model it was
Data egressWhich class of data may cross the organizational boundary, including consent to training useClassification falls to individuals. The person in the biggest hurry sends out the most sensitive data
Outbound call boundaryWhich system may call which external endpoint, including the tool permissions of agentsIntegrations grow quietly, and afterwards nobody can count which boundaries the data crossed
Output accountabilityWho checks and signs when an AI-produced result goes to a customer or outside the companyReview turns into ritual. When a wrong output ships, accountability shifts onto the tool

The reason to attach these four to different seats is mutual restraint. If the same person signs off on both model selection and data egress, data classification gives way to performance arguments. If the same person holds both the outbound boundary and output accountability, both open when a deadline is tight. How an approval prompt goes blunt at the level of an individual device is set out in the conditions under which an approval gate is a defense. The same erosion happens at the organizational level, and the cause is usually an arrangement in which the approver does not bear the consequences of the approval.

Attaching decision rights and observable signals to each risk

Settling decision rights does not make the risk disappear, because what happens outside the approval procedure remains. So for each type of risk you have to write down both who approves and what you can look at to know. The last column of the table below is the point of this article. That column does not close with a document.

Risk typeDecision ownerObservable signalWhat a document cannot stop
Sensitive data leaving for an external modelPrivacy officerClass tags on outbound payloads, per-endpoint volume at the proxy, share of requests with no class tagText a person summarized and pasted in by hand carries no class tag at all
Circumvention through personal accountsInformation security leadUnregistered domains in DNS and proxy logs, external sign-ups in the identity provider, personal subscriptions in expense reportsPersonal devices on personal connections never appear in company logs
Changes to vendor terms and data policyProcurement and legalThe mailbox receiving change notices, contract renewal dates, the policy section of the vendor status pageThe gap between notice date and effective date, and quiet additions to the sub-processor list
Behavior drift from a model swapEngineering leadModel identifier and version string, output variance on a fixed input set, refusal rate and latencyWeights changing behind an endpoint of the same name cannot be detected from outside
AI use by partners and outsourced workBusiness unit and procurementGeneration traces in deliverables, sub-consignment notifications, activity hours on access accountsPersonal account use inside a partner firm is addressed only by contract and is never observed

Automated judgments affecting individualsLegal and the business unitCount of automated decisions, rate of human reversals, number of explanation requests receivedWhether the impact is significant is itself disputed after the fact

Put the two right-hand columns side by side and one thing becomes clear. Most observable signals come out of logs the organization already holds. It is faster to look at the proxy, the identity provider and the expense system before buying a new tool. And the last column remains no matter how well the policy is written. Knowing that it remains is very different from not knowing.

Shadow AI - banning it does not remove it, it hides it

In organizations that ban personal account use aggressively, what actually declines is not the usage but the reporting. The work is still there and the deadline is still there, so when the approved tool is slow or blocked, people do the same job on their own phone. At that moment the organization loses both control and observation. Before the ban, at least a trace landed in the corporate proxy.

So it pays to shift the objective from blocking to making visible. Below are the places where signals appear with no new budget.

# Shadow AI signals from places the organization already has
proxy / dns_log      : unregistered model service domains, request distribution by hour
idp / sso_log        : external accounts created with a corporate address, first sign-up time
saas_admin_console   : unapproved app integration consents, scopes granted
expense_report       : subscriptions paid on personal cards, recurring billing cycles
code_repository      : external API keys in commits, endpoints in config files
helpdesk_ticket      : failed pastes, length limits, access blocked inquiries

Not one of those six lines is an AI-specific tool. It is simply reading logs from systems already in operation at a different angle. Gather the signals and the conclusion is usually the same. The workload most often circumvented is the workload that needs an approved path first. Treat circumvention as something to punish and this information disappears; treat it as a demand signal and it becomes next quarter adoption priority.

And a part remains that a document never closes. Use over a personal device on a personal connection does not appear in company logs at all. What is left there is contract and training rather than observation, and both work only probabilistically. A policy written without acknowledging this limit underestimates the real risk.

Vendor terms and model swaps - behavior changes without your consent

Internal systems have change management. Deployments carry approvals, there is a rollback plan, and the change history is preserved. External models sit outside that procedure. When the vendor changes something, it changes without your approval. Two kinds of things change.

One is terms and data policy: whether input data is used for training, retention periods, the list of sub-processors, the processing region. These items are premises on which internal policy rests, and the moment they change has nothing to do with your schedule. There are three places to observe. The account receiving the notice email should be a shared mailbox rather than an individual inbox, the policy sections should be re-read at contract renewal, and changes to the sub-processor list should be tracked as a maintained list.

The other is the swap of the model itself. When weights change behind an endpoint of the same name, the output distribution shifts. Summary length changes without anyone touching the prompt, and requests that used to be refused are performed, or the reverse. There is no way to establish this from outside. What you can do instead is keep a fixed input set so that the change itself becomes visible. Pick twenty representative inputs per workload, send the same requests on a schedule, and record output variance, refusal rate and latency; when the numbers bend one day, you can infer a swap. That is an inference, not proof.

There is an easily missed point here. The decision to change models also happens inside the organization without approval, because it takes a developer one line in a config file. If the model identifier is not on the deployment approval checklist, you will miss internal swaps long before you have to worry about external ones. How internal documents and knowledge drift out of sync over time is covered in the ways an internal knowledge base goes stale, and model swapping is a different axis from that. Documents go stale because nobody updates them; models change because somebody else updates them.

Partners and outsourcing - down to the sub-consignment

Managing only the AI an organization uses directly leaves half the surface empty. When an outsourced development shop writes code, when a call center consignee produces call summaries, when a translation vendor processes documents, the tools those flows pass through are unknown to you unless the contract says so. And your data is riding on those flows.

Seen through Korean requirements, this is the sharpest point. Article 26 of the Personal Information Protection Act requires that consignment of personal data processing to a third party be effected through a document containing the prohibition of processing beyond the consigned purpose, technical and administrative safeguards, and other matters prescribed by Presidential Decree. Paragraph 6 of the same article provides that a consignee must obtain the consent of the consignor before sub-consigning the entrusted work, and paragraph 4 imposes on the consignor a duty to train the consignee and to supervise it, including inspection of processing status. Paragraph 7 provides that, as to liability for damages arising from the consignee violating the Act in connection with the entrusted work, the consignee is deemed an employee of the personal information controller.

Translated into operational language, that reads as follows. If a partner attaches an AI tool without your knowledge, that can become a sub-consignment question, and the resulting damage comes back to you. So what the contract language needs is not one prohibition clause but a list.

  • Give advance notice of the name and processing region of any AI service used to perform the work
  • Obtain prior consent before adding or replacing such a service
  • Mark the scope of AI-generated content where a deliverable contains it
  • Warrant that your data is not used for model training, and produce the documents supporting that warranty
  • Fix the notification deadline and the scope of log provision in the event of an incident
  • Fix the data destruction procedure at contract termination and the form of the evidence for it

Of those six, the first two create observation and the remaining four create after-the-fact response. How the open source ecosystem handles whether a contribution used a tool is covered in why an AI contribution policy conditions on the warranty rather than the tool. Commercial contracts follow the same principle. Whether a tool was used cannot be verified, so the only verifiable thing is what was warranted.

Auditability - the minimum unit of reconstruction

When an incident happens, the question an organization has to answer collapses into one. Which model produced which output from which input, and who ran it under which approval. If that sentence cannot be reconstructed afterwards, every other countermeasure is a declaration. The investor piece points in the same direction. It argues that every interaction with training data and model artifacts must be tied to an identity, human or machine, with timestamps and action records, and that who accessed it, when, what they did with it and which version was affected must remain on record.

Carried into a single operational record, the minimum unit looks roughly like this.

# Minimum fields to reconstruct a single AI call after the fact
request_id       : request identifier. the key an investigation pivots on
actor            : human account or service account
approval_ref     : identifier of the decision that permitted this use
purpose_code     : pre-registered purpose code
model_id         : vendor, model name, version string
prompt_hash      : hash rather than the raw input
prompt_class     : data class contained in the input
output_hash      : hash rather than the raw output
tool_calls       : list of external tools the model invoked
egress_target    : boundary and region the data crossed
reviewer         : person who checked the output. blank if unreviewed
retention_until  : expiry date for this record

The pivot of this shape is approval_ref. Without an approval identifier in the record, the log tells you what happened but not whether it was permitted. That is why settling decision rights and designing the audit log are effectively the same task. If the trace of approval is not attached to the trace of execution, both are half a record.

The second pivot is prompt_class. Recording which class of data went into the input is what lets you scope an incident. Class alone supports an estimate of blast radius without the raw text, and it is safer than accumulating the text.

What belongs in the log and what must not

Loading every prompt and response in full in the name of auditability is a common design and mostly a mistake. That log becomes a new repository of personal data in its own right. Data that was access-controlled in the source system frequently ends up open to every operator in the log store, and the retention period is usually longer than the original.

FieldWhy it is keptThe form it must not take
Input bodyEstimating incident scopeFull raw retention. Replace with hash, class, length and attachment type
Output bodyTracing the deliverableFull raw retention. Replace with hash and a reference to where the output is stored
Acting principalAttributing accountabilityCollapsing everything into one shared account. Separate human and service accounts
Model versionTracking behavior driftRecording only the alias. Keep the version string as returned in the response
Approval basisDeciding whether it was permittedRecording only the title of the approval document. Keep an identifier with a change history
Data classSizing the blast radiusAllowing it to be absent. Better to count absence itself as an exception signal
Retention periodPreventing over-retentionIndefinite. Put an expiry on each record and automate the expiry handling

There are segments where raw retention genuinely is required, for instance work whose output accuracy may later be disputed. Even there, raw retention should be designated rather than blanket, and it is safer to set the access list and retention period separately. Holding to one principle, that a log inherits the class of the highest-class data it contains, already changes the design considerably.

Korean requirements - where privacy law binds

From here the treatment stays at the level of requirements and makes no determination about whether any particular case falls within them. The governing statute is the Personal Information Protection Act, in the version recorded by the Korea Law Information Center as effective 2 October 2025 under Act No. 20897, partially amended on 1 April 2025.

ProvisionWhat the provision requiresWhere it lands in an AI rollout
Article 26 (1)Consignment of processing must be effected by a document covering the prohibition of processing beyond the consigned purpose and technical and administrative safeguardsWhere processing work rides on an external AI service, the items of the contract language are fixed
Article 26 (6)A consignee must obtain the consignor consent before sub-consigning the entrusted workThe route by which a partner adds an AI tool on its own initiative lands here
Article 28-8 (1)Provision, consignment of processing and storage abroad are prohibited in principle with five exceptions, and provision expressly includes cases where the data is queriedCalls to overseas endpoints touch the concept of transfer in that section. Which exception applies is a separate determination
Article 28-8 (2)When taking separate consent, the data items, the country and timing and method, the recipient, the purpose and retention period, and the method of refusal must be disclosed in advanceIf the consent route is chosen, the items on the notice screen are fixed by the provision
Article 37-2Where a decision made by a fully automated system significantly affects rights or obligations, the data subject may refuse it and demand an explanation, and the criteria and procedure must be disclosedSystems applying artificial intelligence technology are named in the provision. The point of human intervention has to be designed in

The cell practitioners most often miss is the third. Article 28-8 (1) puts provision, consignment of processing and storage together within the scope of cross-border transfer, and states in parentheses that provision includes cases where the data is queried. In other words, a design that calls out without moving and storing data does not automatically end the discussion. The five exceptions are separate consent; a special provision in a statute or treaty; where consignment or storage is necessary to conclude and perform a contract with the data subject, with either disclosure in the privacy policy or individual notice; transfer to a recipient holding a certification designated by the Protection Commission where all the required measures have been taken; and a country or international organization recognized by the Commission as having a level substantially equivalent to that of the Act. Which route to take depends on the contractual structure, and that determination is outside the scope of this article.

Article 37-2 also lands directly on AI adoption. The provision states in parentheses that a fully automated system includes a system applying artificial intelligence technology, and it gives the data subject a right to refuse the decision and to demand an explanation. Absent justifiable grounds, the controller must not apply the automated decision or must take necessary measures such as reprocessing with human involvement or providing an explanation. Building the point of human involvement in from the start costs far less than bolting it on later.

What the AI Framework Act sets as the level of duty

From 2026 there is one more source. It is the Framework Act on the Development of Artificial Intelligence and Establishment of a Foundation of Trust (인공지능 발전과 신뢰 기반 조성 등에 관한 기본법), shown on the statute text screen of the Korea Law Information Center as effective 21 July 2026 under Act No. 21311, partially amended on 20 January 2026. The duties in this Act attach to an AI business operator. Article 2 (7) divides that into developers who develop and supply AI, and users who provide products or services using AI so supplied. An organization that uses AI only for internal work and an organization that sells a product built on it fall on different sides of this definition.

The other axis that determines the weight of duty is high-impact AI. Article 2 (4) defines it as a system that significantly affects or risks harming human life, physical safety and fundamental rights, and that is used in one of the enumerated domains. The enumerated domains include energy supply, drinking water production, the healthcare delivery system, medical and digital medical devices, nuclear facilities, analysis of biometric information for criminal investigation or arrest, judgments or evaluations that significantly affect rights and obligations such as hiring and loan screening, the principal operation of transport means and systems, decisions of state agencies such as eligibility determinations for public services, and student evaluation in early childhood, primary and secondary education. That hiring and loan screening are named explicitly is the part that reaches ordinary companies immediately.

  • Article 31 - a product or service based on high-impact or generative AI must notify users of that fact in advance, generative outputs must be marked as generated, and outputs hard to distinguish from reality must be notified or marked in a way users clearly recognize
  • Article 32 - a system whose cumulative training compute exceeds a threshold set by Presidential Decree must identify, assess and mitigate risk across the lifecycle, run a risk management system that monitors and responds to safety incidents, and submit the results to the Minister of Science and ICT
  • Article 33 - an operator must review in advance whether its AI falls under high-impact AI, and may request confirmation from the Minister where necessary
  • Article 34 - when providing high-impact AI, an operator must establish and operate a risk management plan, an explanation plan covering final results and the principal criteria and an outline of training data, a user protection plan, human management and supervision, and the preparation and retention of documents evidencing those measures
  • Article 36 - an operator without a domestic address or place of business that meets thresholds on user numbers and revenue must designate a domestic representative in writing and report the designation

Two items on that list overlap precisely with the axis of this article. Human management and supervision under Article 34 (1) item 4 is a question of how decision rights are arranged, and the preparation and retention of documents evidencing the measures under item 5 of the same paragraph is a question of auditability. What the provision asks for is not a policy declaration but a form that can be checked later. That is where the record design of the previous section meets this requirement.

The skeleton a standard provides - what GOVERN writes down

If statutes set requirements, standards supply a skeleton. The AI Risk Management Framework from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology carries the document number NIST AI 100-1 and was issued on 26 January 2023, and the text itself states that the framework is intended to be voluntary, rights-preserving, non-sector-specific and use-case agnostic. That sentence matters, because it means that having followed the standard does not substitute for compliance with law. The same agency generative AI profile is NIST AI 600-1, issued on 26 July 2024.

Of the four functions, GOVERN is the one that meets this article axis. The framework describes GOVERN as cultivating a culture of risk management, outlining the processes and documents and organizational schemes that anticipate and identify and manage risk, incorporating processes to assess potential impacts, providing a structure that aligns risk management with organizational principles, and addressing the full product lifecycle including legal issues concerning third-party software and hardware and data.

ItemOutcome the text asks forWhere it meets this article axis
GOVERN 1.1Legal and regulatory requirements involving AI are understood, managed and documentedThe place where domestic statutory requirements are maintained as a list
GOVERN 1.6Mechanisms are in place to inventory AI systems, resourced according to risk prioritiesAnything absent from the inventory is shadow AI. The inventory itself becomes an observation tool
GOVERN 1.7Processes exist for decommissioning and phasing out AI systems safelyThe handling path for data and logs at model swap and service sunset
GOVERN 2.1Roles and responsibilities and lines of communication are documented and clear to staffThe demand to name approvers exists in the standard as well
GOVERN 2.3Executive leadership takes responsibility for decisions about risks in AI development and deploymentThe same direction as the claim in the triggering piece, expressed more narrowly
GOVERN 3.2Policies define and differentiate roles, responsibilities and oversight for human-AI configurationsThe place where the locus of output accountability is fixed
GOVERN 6.2Contingency processes exist for failures or incidents in third-party data or AI systems deemed high-riskAlternate paths for vendor outages and policy changes

Read the table and it is clear the standard is not asking for anything new. It writes the same three things, decision rights, inventory and record, in a different language. So rather than trying to transplant the standard document wholesale, it is faster to write the name of the responsible person in your organization next to each of those seven lines. The line where you cannot write a name is the line that will break first.

Conclusion - four names on one page

Standing up a governance program is a long piece of work. What can be done this quarter is short. It is writing four names on a single page: the person who approves model selection, the person who approves data egress, the person who approves the outbound call boundary, and the person accountable for outputs. They should be different people, and each should receive reporting on the consequences of their own decisions.

Next comes the log. If a single call is recorded with its approval identifier, model version and data class attached, then when an incident happens the organization can turn events into a sentence. If it cannot, the argument proceeds on circumstance and memory.

Finally, write down what cannot be stopped as something that cannot be stopped. Use on personal devices, unilateral vendor changes, and judgments made inside a partner firm do not close with a document. Filling that column with what you can look at to know, instead of leaving it blank, is both the honesty and the effectiveness of a policy. Why the narrative of replacing headcount with AI so often fails inside organizations is treated separately in the problem with the replacement narrative. Adoption without decision rights is another name for that failure.

The summary above carries provisions and items of statutes and standards at a general level and is not legal advice on the facts of any particular organization. Statutes are amended and effective dates can differ by provision, so the text and supplementary provisions in force at the time of application, along with the notices and guidance of the competent authority, should be read together. Final judgments on contracts and processing structures belong with qualified legal counsel.

What could not be confirmed

The items below were left out of the body because the primary sources could not be reached. Nothing was filled in by assumption.

  • ISO/IEC 42001 — the standard page at the International Organization for Standardization refused access on two attempts. The formal title, publication year and certifiability could not be fixed from a primary source, so the standard is not cited in the body
  • Provisions of the EU AI Act — the triggering piece mentions it as an instance of codifying this into law, but the text of the act was not opened, so none of its content was carried into the body. Only the fact of the mention is recorded
  • Per-provision effective dates of the AI Framework Act — the statute text screen shows an effective date of 21 July 2026 while the provision screen shows 22 January 2026, and the two disagree. Effective dates can differ by provision under the supplementary provisions, so the timing for any individual provision has to be read there
  • Concrete thresholds delegated to Presidential Decree — the cumulative compute threshold in Article 32, the user and revenue thresholds in Article 36, and the safeguards under Article 28-8 of the privacy statute are all delegated to Decree and notices. The enforcement decree and notices were not opened, so no figures are given
  • The regulatory convergence claim in the triggering piece — the statement that three US agencies are converging on a single expectation of proving data provenance is that piece claim, and it was not cross-checked against primary documents from each agency
