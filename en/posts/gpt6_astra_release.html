Scores Measure Capability, Products Follow Policy - How to Read the GPT-6 Astra Launch

Why a 100 percent score and a refusal sit in the same announcement, and what to check next time you read a benchmark table or a price sheet

2026-09-05 Tech & IT

Every model launch produces a table. Benchmark names on the left, numbers on the right, and the numbers are generally high. That table then travels for a few days, getting thinner with each repetition. Every time it moves, one line of context falls off the bottom, and what survives at the end is a number and a model name.

This particular launch shows that thinning process unusually well, because the same announcement contains a sentence saying the model reached 100 percent at a certain capability and a sentence saying the shipped product refuses to do exactly that. Put those two side by side and it becomes very clear what a benchmark score is actually measuring.

Every figure here was verified in a published source, and the measurement conditions are printed alongside it. This is not an article about which product is better. It is about the conditions a published number has to be read with. Anything that could not be verified was collected near the end rather than filled in by estimate.

The trigger - 100 percent and a refusal in one document

In early September 2026 OpenAI announced GPT-6 Astra. Summaries went around quickly, and they were full of eye-catching numbers. Two of those numbers sat next to each other. One said the model reached 100 percent on an evaluation that measures the ability to write attack code. The other said that the production model as actually deployed refuses to write advanced exploits.

These two statements are not in conflict. They are simply about different things. The first measures what the model can accomplish inside an evaluation environment. The second is the result of deployment policy deciding which parts of that capability are allowed out. Training creates capability; alignment and policy create behavior. Both come from the same model, and they are not the same number.

Most of the time this distinction is invisible, because on most benchmarks capability and product behavior overlap almost entirely. A model that is good at math problems is also good at math problems when you use it. But in a domain like offensive security, where higher capability leads directly to narrower deployment, the two split apart in plain view. That is what makes this case a teaching example. The fact that a benchmark score and the behavior of the product you will actually use were always separate quantities has nowhere to hide here.

What knowledge-style and behavior-style evaluations each fail to capture is covered separately in the blind spots of LLM evaluation, so it is not repeated here. This article adds one layer on top of that: there is a policy layer between the evaluation and the product.

Before any interpretation, the facts get pinned down. Every item in the table below was verified on the model page in OpenAI's developer documentation. Do not skim past the last two rows. The unsupported list is on the long side. Embeddings, moderation and realtime voice are all heavily used axes in real services, and their absence means this single model cannot unify a stack. Deciding which segment of an existing configuration is being replaced has to come before any cost estimate. The absence of fine-tuning is another condition that forces a rethink if domain specialization was part of the plan.

62.7 and 99.9 from the same model on the same benchmark

The most instructive item is ARC-AGI-3. ARC Prize, which operates the benchmark, published its own evaluation results, and two different scores for the same model sit next to each other in that post.

AspectStandard harnessProvider adapter harness
Score62.7%99.9%
Reasoning effort settingmaxhigh
Total evaluation cost26,098 dollars18,817 dollars
State between requestsOnly the notes the model chose to keep carry forwardOpaque reasoning state is preserved, with compaction as conversations grow
Reuse of prior workOnly as far as those notes goAvailable

This table inverts intuition twice. First, the run with the higher reasoning effort produced the lower score. Second, the run that scored higher cost less. Spending 26,098 dollars produced 62.7, and spending 18,817 dollars produced 99.9. What raised the score was not additional computation but the way the model was run.

There is exactly one difference. The adapter harness carries the model's internal reasoning state across the boundary between requests. Under the standard harness that state disappears each time, and the model can only forward the notes it decided to write down for itself. On tasks that require exploring an environment over many rounds and inferring its rules, that difference is decisive, because not losing what you already worked out is itself a large part of the capability.

So how should these two numbers be treated

Both are genuine. Neither is manipulated, and the organization running the benchmark published both. The problem is transmission. Someone who reads a sentence carrying only the 99.9 will wire this model up as a stateless single-shot API call and expect a comparable result. That expectation will meet a reality much closer to the 62.7.

ARC Prize also drew its own line around the interpretation. It wrote in the same post that saturating the benchmark would not represent proof of achieving general intelligence, and that the evaluation environments have deterministic, closed-ended mechanics and goals which do not represent the complexity and open-endedness of the real world. That caveat, attached by the people who built the benchmark, is the first sentence to disappear in transmission. How quickly benchmarks age and why is examined separately in a look at the lifespan of evaluation methods.

What the 100 percent counted - two security evaluations

The 100 percent on ExploitBench deserves the same dissection. As reported, that 100 is an aggregate capability-coverage score rather than a proportion of individual tasks passed. In other words it is closer to a breadth measure of which classes of technique the model can handle. That is a metric structure in which a value of 100 is relatively easy to reach.

ExploitGym, which appeared in the same launch, has a different character. There the result was 42.4 percent, and that was with the six-hour limit removed. A 42.4 that needs the limit lifted and a 100 built from coverage aggregation differ by more than a factor of two while measuring the same domain. The point is not which one is correct but that the two are answering different questions.

The announcement attached two qualitative facts on top of that. During evaluation the model produced exploits against hardened browsers and operating systems, and it found two previously unknown vulnerabilities in a widely used JavaScript engine. On the mathematical side there was a claim of narrowing a known bound on prime gaps from 240 to 186. These statements are different in kind from scores. A score states a relative position on a standardized problem set, whereas two newly discovered vulnerabilities are absolute events. And it is the absolute events that move deployment policy.

The trend of companies building their own evaluations once standard benchmarks become hard to trust has been running for several years now. That background and the Goodhart's law dimension are laid out in a case of building an in-house coding benchmark, and how paper-grade figures collapse under reverification is covered in a reverification case study.

Capability and deployment are decided on different layers

Back to the original pair. Why does a model that scored 100 percent refuse in production?

OpenAI maintains its own preparedness framework for classifying a model's risk level. This model was reported as the first the company has classified at the top cybersecurity tier of that framework. As a consequence of that classification, advanced requests amounting to attack code authoring and vulnerability discovery were blocked from general access. Defensive organizations that need the capability reach it through a separate vetted program.

That gives three points of intervention.

  • Training — where capability itself is created. What benchmarks measure is largely the output of this layer
  • Alignment — where the disposition to answer certain requests certain ways is set. The same capability produces a different response after this step
  • Deployment policy — where access itself is divided by tier and vetting. Regardless of whether the capability exists, for some users the feature is simply absent

A benchmark table sees only the first layer. What a user encounters is the result of passing through all three. A high number in the table is therefore not a guarantee that you can get the model to do that job. The gap between table and reality widens specifically in domains where deployment constraints bind hard, such as security, medicine, law and finance.

None of this structure is inherently bad. Whether it would be desirable for the ability to independently discover unknown vulnerabilities to be open to everyone without conditions is a separate question. The point is that whoever reads the table needs to know this layer exists. Why security as a field is structurally difficult in the first place is written up in a separate article.

The five lines to look for in any benchmark table

Here is the practical takeaway. Whatever model's benchmark table you read next, first check whether five lines are printed next to the number.

  • The exact benchmark revision — when one name has branches such as 2.0 and v2-offline, the figures are not comparable
  • The execution harness and whether state persists — the single variable that separated 62.7 from 99.9 in this case
  • The reasoning budget or effort setting — the same model behaves like a different tier depending on this
  • Attempts and scoring method — one shot or best of several, pass rate or coverage aggregate
  • The cost or time it took to produce the score — which, as this case shows, can even run backwards

The same applies to citation. When a score has to be copied into your own document, this shape is worth keeping. A number passed along without conditions conveys nothing to the next reader, and later fails to serve as evidence even for the person who wrote it down.

[bad citation]
  ARC-AGI-3 99.9%

[usable citation]
  ARC-AGI-3 Semi-Private 99.9%
    harness    : provider adapter (reasoning state preserved, compaction when long)
    setting    : reasoning effort high
    cost       : 18,817 dollars
    not the same condition : standard harness 62.7% / effort max / 26,098 dollars
    source     : published by the organization running the benchmark
    caveat     : that organization states saturation is not proof of general intelligence

Pricing - 10 and 50 dollars are the baseline

The second axis is price. The widely circulated figures are 10 dollars for input and 50 dollars for output per million tokens. Those are correct. They are, however, not the whole rate card but a baseline, with multipliers attached above and below.

CategoryPer million tokensApplies when
Input10 dollarsBaseline
Cached input1 dollarThe portion covered by prompt caching
Output50 dollarsBaseline
Long input surcharge2x on input and cache, 1.5x on outputRequests whose input exceeds 272,000 tokens. Applied to the entire request, not the overage
Fast response mode2x the applicable ratesThis 2x is a multiplier on price, not on speed
Batch and flex processing50% discountWork that does not need an immediate response

One frequent headline distortion lives right here. Saying the fast mode is 2x means the price is doubled. It does not mean the speed is doubled. Copying the multiplier without its subject makes it easy to read the opposite of what is meant.

The 272,000 boundary also bites sharply in practice. A 1.05 million token context window does not make filling 1.05 million tokens a natural usage pattern. The moment the boundary is crossed by a single token, the rate rises for the entire request. Below is a plain comparison of handling similar volume just inside and just outside that line.

Assume: 270K input tokens, 10K output tokens, no cache

Inside the boundary (272,000 or below)
  input   270,000 x 10 dollars / 1,000,000 = 2.70 dollars
  output   10,000 x 50 dollars / 1,000,000 = 0.50 dollars
  total                                      3.20 dollars

Same job, a little more context added, boundary crossed
  input   280,000 x 20 dollars / 1,000,000 = 5.60 dollars   (2x rate)
  output   10,000 x 75 dollars / 1,000,000 = 0.75 dollars   (1.5x rate)
  total                                      6.35 dollars

Input grew 3.7 percent and the bill roughly doubled

How a list price becomes an invoice

The same list price produces quite different actual bills. At least four items sit in between.

  • The tokenizer — how many tokens the same sentence is split into differs by provider. Korean, Japanese and Chinese are languages where this gap opens especially wide. Unit price comparison does not hold until token counts have been measured on your own real text
  • Output length — when the output rate is five times the input rate, output dominates the total. The same problem answered verbosely costs several times more, and the reasoning effort setting feeds directly into this
  • Cache hit rate — for a structure that repeatedly sends a long system prompt or a fixed document, caching determines most of the total. On this model a cache hit drops the input rate to one tenth
  • Retries and failures — calls repeated because a format did not validate, and stretches rewound because a tool call tangled, are all billed. The more agentic the usage, the larger this share becomes

The number that actually matters when evaluating adoption is therefore not the list rate but the average billed amount from running 100 real items of your own workload. That figure takes a day to obtain, and staring at a rate card will never produce it.

What a 1.05 million token window changes and what it does not

A context window of 1,050,000 tokens is an attention-getting number. What it solves and what it does not have to be kept apart.

  • What changes — a long codebase or a large document bundle can go into one request without being split. The chunking logic and the boundary errors it creates go away
  • What does not change — the ability to pick the right passage out of a very long input is a separate property from window size. A bigger window does not by itself guarantee it
  • What can get worse — crossing above the 272,000 token boundary raises the rate. Being able to use a large window and being better off using it are different statements
  • Output is separate — the maximum output is 128,000 tokens. However large the input, one response cannot exceed that ceiling

In practice, selecting only the needed sections and warming a cache on the fixed prefix tends to be both cheaper and steadier than pushing an entire document through. A large window widens the options; it does not do the design for you.

Where a computer-operating model runs into trouble

The weight of this model sits on looking at a screen and operating a computer and browser. The evidence offered was 72.6 percent on OSWorld 2.0 and an average time per task falling from 75 minutes in the prior generation to 40. On the time axis that is a reduction of about 47 percent.

Figures in this family call for a different sort of caution. Unlike problem solving with a single correct answer, computer operation is a domain where what got touched along the way matters as much as the result. Benchmarks generally score on whether the final state was reached, so what was deleted or clicked en route does not register. And when this is attached to a real working environment, it is usually that intermediate portion that causes the trouble.

  • Permission boundaries — the model, not a person, ends up deciding what is accessed under which account. Without least privilege cut in advance, after-the-fact audit does not work
  • Irreversible actions — deletion, sending, payment and approval deserve their own confirmation step. A 72.6 percent success rate also means the remaining 27.4 percent exists
  • Screen dependence — when the target software's interface changes, it fails quietly. The success rate is itself a function of the target environment
  • Audit trail — without a record of what was clicked and why, incident analysis is impossible. Log design has to be settled before rollout

What to settle before evaluating adoption

The most common mistake at a model launch is to decide from the benchmark table and then fit the workload to that decision. The order is backwards. Five things come first.

  • What is being replaced — embeddings, moderation and realtime voice are on the unsupported list. The whole stack cannot move, so decide which segment changes first
  • Whether state can be maintained — as this case demonstrated, state persistence swings the outcome heavily. If the plan is stateless single-shot calls, scores from a stateful condition must not be the expectation
  • Whether prompts sit near the 272,000 token boundary — a design that drifts across that line makes the bill jump per request. Pinning it below the line is more predictable
  • Whether fine-tuning was planned — it is not supported on this model, so domain specialization has to be rebuilt around prompting and retrieval
  • Whether security work is in scope — requests amounting to vulnerability analysis or attack code are blocked from general access. Regardless of the evaluation score, plan as though that capability is absent

The tier structure of the prior generation and the selection criteria that applied then are written up in the previous generation launch summary and the tier selection guide. The framework for weighing price difference against performance difference between tiers does not change much across generations.

Rereading the five headlines

Finally, the most widely repeated sentences from this launch, restored to their conditions. None of the five is false. All five, however, lost a line somewhere in transmission.

Restoring those five lines does not take long. Yet the impression left afterwards is considerably different. That is the whole of the attitude a benchmark table requires — not doubting the numbers, but recovering the lines that used to sit next to them.

What could not be verified

The following items failed verification and were left out of the body. None were filled in by estimate.

  • The official announcement page itself — OpenAI's announcement page refused access on two attempts. Statements appearing only in that announcement were included only as far as they could be cross-checked against the developer documentation and the benchmark operator's published material, and where they could not, the source is named in the text
  • The OSWorld revision label — the launch summary says OSWorld 2.0 while other write-ups say V2-Offline. Whether these are one thing under two names or two different revisions could not be settled from primary material. If they are different revisions, the 72.6 percent is not a comparable figure
  • The definition of ExploitBench — the description of it as a capability-coverage aggregate was verified in secondary write-ups, but the benchmark's own specification document was not opened. Exactly what the 100 percent is 100 percent of requires that specification to settle
  • The scope of the access gate — whether the vetted program gates only the offensive security capability or the model as a whole is described inconsistently across sources. Since it coexists with descriptions of a staged rollout to paid ChatGPT tiers, the former reading is the natural one, but it could not be confirmed
  • Benchmarks with unverified conditions — for GPQA Diamond, BenchCAD Vision2Code, DeepSWE v1.1 and Terminal-Bench Science only the scores were verified; attempts and settings were not. Those condition cells were left blank in the table above
