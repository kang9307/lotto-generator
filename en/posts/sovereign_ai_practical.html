What the Word Own Actually Bundles - Reading Sovereign AI as Four Separate Layers

Weights, training data, inference location, and policy control each move on their own. A question list for deciding how far your organization needs to go

2026-09-05 Tech & IT

When the sentence 「we need to own our model」 lands in a meeting room, the five people around the table picture five different things. One imagines downloading a weights file. Another imagines racking GPUs in the building. A third imagines a model trained on the company's own data. A fourth imagines a state in which the vendor refuses a request and the company allows it anyway. The fifth simply imagines a contract that says domestic region.

All five pictures go by the single word ownership. And yet they are independent of one another. Acquiring one of them leaves the other four exactly where they were, and in most organizations that is precisely what happens. This is also where budgets tend to go wrong: the money is allocated before anyone has said which layer is being bought.

The license clauses and hardware figures quoted below come from each model's license text and model card, and the statutory text from the Korean government legal information portal. Claims made by the piece that prompted this article are attributed as claims, not restated as fact. Items that could not be grounded were not filled in with estimates; they are collected in a section near the end.

The trigger - a word that was not in the original title

On 19 August 2026 Sequoia Capital published a piece titled Own Your Intelligence: A How-To Guide. The author is Sonya Huang. When it was carried into the Korean developer community, the headline became 「Own your intelligence: a practical guide to sovereign AI」. The first half is a direct translation. The second half was added in transit.

The original does not use the word sovereign. It does not mention data sovereignty, national AI, or cross-border transfer. More striking still, it contains no discussion of deployment location at all. Neither on-premises nor self-hosting appears anywhere in the argument. What the piece is about is not where the servers sit but who runs the learning loop.

So reading it as an argument for keeping servers in-country is reading a claim it never made. One headline moved the axis of the whole argument.

Where the author stands

The author is a venture capital firm. If more companies build their own intelligence stacks, the parties funding that market benefit. The piece cites Harvey, Mercor, LangChain, Trajectory and Fireworks as examples, and carries no disclosure of whether those companies are portfolio investments of the fund. Accordingly, the statements below are rendered as claims the piece makes rather than as established facts.

The core of the argument runs like this. The contest at the AI application layer is not only about UI, workflows or go-to-market; it is a fight for the intelligence layer itself, and that is where the newest battleground lies. It compresses this into a slogan: not your weights, not your product. As criteria for choosing between owning and renting, it lists four: cost, speed, proprietary data, and control. If inference costs scale directly with usage, it argues, owning the model is the best way to protect your margins.

That is the piece. The trouble begins immediately after. The slogan names weights, yet the thing the piece actually tells readers to own is not weights but the loop that runs from evaluations to the harness to post-training. Within a single sentence, two different objects are already sharing one word. Which is why the work has to start by splitting the layers.

Own is one word pointing at four different things

In practice, at least four distinct things get bundled under ownership. Set out side by side, with what each one enables and what it still leaves out of reach, they look like this.

One thing is visible immediately in that table. None of the four layers implies any of the others. Acquire the first and the second still belongs to someone else. Acquire the third and the fourth may remain exactly as the vendor set it. Yet one sentence in a meeting room sounds like a promise of all four at once. And when the approval request circulates saying only 「model ownership」, the person signing it does not know which layer the money bought either. An approval request that names no layer cannot be checked afterwards against what was actually delivered.

Layer 1 · Weights - what the license actually permits

What lands in your hands the moment you download weights is a file. What you may do with that file is set by the license. Licenses differ by model, and the single phrase open weights covers contracts of quite different character.

The general point that open weights and open source are not the same phrase is already laid out in State of Open Source AI, so it will not be repeated here. What matters here is the wording at the clause level. Set out exactly as written in each license, the conditions read as follows.

ModelLicenseConditions as written
Llama 4Llama 4 Community LicenseAbove 700 million monthly active users you must request a separate license from Meta, and the rights are not exercisable until Meta grants it · a prominent 「Built with Llama」 notice on websites, user interfaces and documentation · derived models must carry Llama as a name prefix · a copy of the license and the copyright notice must travel with any redistribution · compliance with the acceptable use policy and trade control laws
Kimi K2Modified MITOne clause added to standard MIT. A commercial product or service exceeding 100 million monthly active users, or exceeding 20 million US dollars in monthly revenue, must prominently display 「Kimi K2」 in its user interface. All other grants and disclaimers remain as in MIT
gpt-oss-120b and 20bApache 2.0Described on the model card as a permissive license allowing you to build freely without copyleft restrictions or patent risk

What to read out of this table

All three rows amount to 「you may obtain the weights」, yet the contracts sit at different levels. Apache 2.0 is a standard open source license. The other two are standard licenses with conditions bolted on, and those conditions trigger on scale.

  • Scale conditions bite later, not now — 700 million or 100 million monthly active users looks irrelevant to almost every organization. But a license follows a product for its whole life, and the moment a threshold is crossed the arrangement becomes renegotiable. The structure is: succeed, and the clause fires
  • Attribution requirements land in product design — Built with Llama and Kimi K2 notices are not filing-cabinet obligations. They are elements that ship on screen. A white-labeled product hits this constraint first
  • Name prefixes constrain branding — publish a derivative in the Llama line and the model name carries the Llama prefix. Harmless for internal use; a different conversation if you plan to supply models to third parties
  • The acceptable use policy is a separate document — reading the license body is not the end of it. The referenced policy carries the actual prohibitions

In short, ownership at layer 1 is a scope, not a boolean. The sentence 「we have the weights」 says nothing about that scope. It becomes information only when the license name and its trigger conditions are written next to it.

Layer 2 · Training data - the layer that rarely opens

This is the hardest of the four to obtain. Plenty of organizations publish weights; very few publish the pre-training corpus as it stands. So even with an open-weight model in hand, what that model read and learned from remains invisible from the outside.

That asymmetry creates three concrete problems in practice.

  • Auditing is impossible — asked whether a particular copyrighted work or personal record entered training, there is no basis on which to answer. Holding the weights changes nothing about this
  • There is no mechanism for deletion requests — when someone asks that their data be removed, you cannot identify what to act on if you do not know whether it went in at all
  • Nothing reproduces — the model cannot be rebuilt, so when something goes wrong the cause cannot be traced back into the training stage

Realistically, the data an organization ends up owning sits on the post-training side: instruction data from its own domain, preference data, failure cases, and the evaluation sets assembled from all of it. This is in fact closer to what the piece means by ownership. It cites Harvey as an example: more than 1,200 agent tasks across 24 legal practice areas, evaluated against more than 75,000 expert-written rubric criteria. That is not weights. It is data, and specifically post-training and evaluation data.

So any claim to own layer 2 needs a qualifier: which side of the data. Owning pre-training data is the name of a different business. Owning post-training data is something most organizations can start on today. Where the line runs for putting internal data into a model at all is set out separately in Can company data go into an LLM.

Layer 3 · Where inference runs

The third layer is the physical question of where the compute happens: an in-building rack, a domestic region, the vendor's shared endpoint, or a managed inference provider. And it is independent of weight ownership.

Downloading open weights and then calling them through a managed inference provider's endpoint is a common arrangement. In that configuration layer 1 is secured and layer 3 is not. The reverse also exists: running a closed model through a vendor's dedicated deployment inside your own account secures much of layer 3 while layer 1 remains entirely absent.

What layer 3 actually changes is one data path. Inputs stop crossing the organizational boundary, latency and throughput come under direct control, and the system keeps working when the external network drops. What it does not change is everything else. What the model refuses, which biases it carries, and what data it was built from have nothing to do with where the server sits.

And an invoice follows this layer, because availability moves onto your side of the line. How far the deployment-infrastructure analogy holds is examined in the Kubernetes moment, and the axes that actually decide hardware selection are laid out in what actually decides inference hardware.

Layer 4 · Who decides what gets refused

The fourth layer is recognized last and missed most often. It is the question of who decides which requests the model refuses, in what form it answers, and which subjects it backs away from.

With a closed API, this layer belongs to the vendor. If the vendor changes policy, work that succeeded yesterday can fail today, and that change usually arrives without notice. In security, medicine and law, where refusal rules are dense, this layer sets the functional boundary of the product directly.

One misconception recurs here: that moving on-premises brings this layer along. It does not. Open-weight models also carry refusal dispositions installed during post-training, and those dispositions live inside the weights. Running them on your own servers changes nothing automatically. Genuinely taking layer 4 means designing your own system prompts and filters and, where necessary, adjusting the disposition itself through post-training. Which means layer 4 only arrives once part of layer 2 has been secured.

Taking layer 4 also brings the responsibility with it. Once your organization is the party deciding what is permitted, your organization is the party that has to keep a record of the reasoning. What axes an internal ban on a specific AI tool actually runs along is recorded as a case in an internal usage ban.

The four layers move independently

With the layers split, real configurations can be scored against them. Five shapes commonly met in practice, marked across the four layers, come out like this.

The second and third rows make the point of this article on their own. The second row holds no weights whatsoever and secures the location. The third row secures the weights and holds no location at all. Both go by the word ownership, and the problems they solve do not overlap.

The last row fills every cell, but the resources that row consumes are an order of magnitude away from the four above it. Ownership as actually debated in practice is almost always a choice among the middle three rows. And none of those three is simply a superset of another. An organization that needs the second row and one that needs the third are not solving the same problem in the first place. What gets compared is not configuration against configuration, but your own requirements against how each row fills in.

The price - not skipping the API, doing the work

Deciding to take layers 3 and 4 means the work the vendor was quietly doing comes across intact. This list is what the sentence 「we will not use an API」 conceals.

What you take onWhat it concretely involvesWho does it when you use an API
Weight storageA single file for a large model runs to hundreds of gigabytes. Integrity verification, access control, backups and generation management come with itThe vendor
Tracking updatesEvery new checkpoint is a judgment call about whether to adopt, and adopting means running the regression suite againThe vendor swaps it for you. Which is exactly how reproducibility breaks quietly
Security patchingThe inference server, the runtime, the accelerator drivers and the container images are each a vulnerability surfaceThe vendor
Hardware and powerAccelerator procurement lead times, rack space, power, cooling. Procurement can slip by quartersThe vendor
AvailabilitySomeone has to get up when the inference server dies at four in the morningThe vendor's SLA
EvaluationBuilding and maintaining the yardstick for whether a change was an improvementNobody does this for you

Evaluation is your job either way

The last row differs in kind from the rest. The first five are work that arrives when you own something, whereas the evaluation set is your work whether you own anything or not. An organization on a closed API still has to measure for itself whether a vendor's model update made its own work better or worse.

Invert that order and everything else becomes meaningless. Own a layer without an evaluation set and you arrive at a state in which you cannot say what improved after you bought it. That is presumably why the piece puts evaluations first among its four steps. The places where evaluation design actually catches are collected in evaluation practice.

How to read hardware numbers

Start examining layer 3 and hardware numbers arrive immediately. A number without conditions attached is unusable here, because the same model demands several times the equipment depending on precision, throughput and concurrency.

The gpt-oss model cards are an example where conditions were published alongside the figures. As written on the cards:

Itemgpt-oss-120bgpt-oss-20b
Total parameters117B21B
Active parameters5.1B3.6B
Weight precisionMXFP4 quantization applied to the mixture-of-experts weightsSame family configuration
Memory conditionStated to fit into a single 80GB acceleratorStated to run within 16GB of memory
LicenseApache 2.0Apache 2.0

What fits into a single 80GB accelerator does not say

That sentence is a statement about memory residency, not about throughput. What the card asserts is that the model fits inside that memory. How many tokens per second come out, how many concurrent requests it withstands, and how much more the KV cache consumes under a long context are separate values entirely.

So the figure splits cleanly into judgments it supports and judgments it does not.

  • Supported — whether this model can be put on a single-card machine for experimentation, and whether the 20b variant can be run on a developer workstation
  • Not supported — how many machines are needed to serve a given daily request volume, and whether that configuration costs more or less per month than API pricing
  • Additional conditions required — target concurrency, target response latency, mean input and output length, quantization level, batching policy. Without those five, a cost comparison does not hold together
  • The active-parameter trap — 5.1B active out of 117B reduces the compute per token but does not reduce the memory requirement. The full weights still have to be resident

The procedure for testing cost claims themselves is set out step by step in reading cost claims, so it is not repeated here.

Regulation is a separate axis

「Regulation is why we need our own model」 is the most frequently offered justification in the meeting room and the sentence that most frequently skips a layer. Regulatory requirements are not a fifth axis standing beside the four layers; they are a different kind of demand altogether.

Under Korean rules the most common trigger is cross-border transfer of personal information. Article 28-8 of the Personal Information Protection Act does not prohibit transferring data abroad. It requires one of five grounds.

SubparagraphGroundWhat using it requires
1Separate consent from the data subjectConsent specifically covering overseas provision, entrustment or storage. Bundling it into another consent does not qualify
2A special provision in law, treaty or international agreementIdentifying the provision that serves as the basis
3Entrustment or storage necessary to conclude or perform a contractAn account of why it is necessary to performance, together with the notice the statute prescribes
4Certification held by the recipientCertification designated by the Protection Commission, plus the safeguards, rights-protection measures and the steps needed to implement them in the destination country
5Recognition of an equivalent protection levelA Commission finding that the destination country or international body offers substantially equivalent protection

Set that table beside the four-layer table and the mismatch is visible. Nowhere among the five grounds is there a requirement to own model weights. All five concern legal basis, documentation and recognition, and none of them is satisfied by layer 1 or layer 2.

Layer 3 is different. If personal information never leaves the country in the first place, the article's scope is simply not engaged. So the only layer wired directly to regulation is location, and even then it works by preventing the requirement from arising rather than by satisfying it. The broader terrain of the open-weight regulatory argument is mapped in the regulation debate.

When ownership answers a rule and when it does not

So when should a requirement be solved by ownership and when by something else? It splits on the character of the demand.

Character of the requirementIs ownership the answerWhy
Certain data must not physically leave the countryYes. Solve it at layer 3Removing the path is simpler than establishing a ground, and it shortens audit responses
A legal basis for the transfer must existNoConsent, contract, certification and equivalence findings are matters of paperwork and process. Moving servers does not create a basis
Our data must not be used for trainingUsually notThis is generally handled by provider terms and contract. If it can be locked down contractually, the case for buying a layer weakens
The model must not refuse a particular taskYes. Solve it at layer 4Policy is not the sort of thing a contract can change. Though part of layer 2 is needed alongside it
We must keep operating if the vendor sunsets the serviceYes. Solve it at layer 1Holding the weights is the only real hedge. Confirm in advance that the license permits that continued use
We must explain in an audit what the model learnedLayer 1 will not do itUnless the pre-training data opens, holding weights gives you nothing to explain from

One rule runs through the table. Solving a contractual requirement with infrastructure raises the cost by an order of magnitude. Conversely, papering over a requirement that contracts cannot reach means the debt returns all at once during an audit or an incident. So classify the requirement first, and choose the layer afterwards.

Mapping the four steps onto the layers

The piece lays out a four-step build process for those who have decided to own. Placing each step onto the layers above reveals which layer it was really talking about.

  • Step 1 · Evaluations — build a set of tasks that measures whether the system does the work well, including prompts, context and graders. This is layer 2, on the post-training side
  • Step 2 · Harness and context engineering — routing, retrieval, tools, memory, fallbacks, tracing. The main job of the harness, the piece quotes, is to bring context to the model at the right point in time. This is not any of the four layers; it is a separate axis, application design
  • Step 3 · Post-training — choose among supervised fine-tuning, preference tuning, reinforcement learning and distillation. Pick the lightest method that moves your eval, the piece says. This is layer 2, and it consequently affects layer 4
  • Step 4 · Online learning — collect trajectories in production, then route failures into evaluations, gaps into context, and errors into harness fixes. This is the operating loop that keeps filling layer 2

Not one of the four steps requires layer 1. Not one requires layer 3 either. The slogan names weights, while the execution plan actually offered is entirely about data and application design.

The piece also draws a contrast about the shape of performance: closed models give you a high floor with a lower ceiling, whereas your own stack starts from a lower floor but raises the ceiling. The contrast is plausible but it is not a verified proposition, and it is worth reading alongside the fact that it comes from a party that benefits when the own-your-stack market grows. And the way to find out whether the ceiling actually rose leads straight back to step one. You need an evaluation set to measure a ceiling.

How far do you need to go - a question list

Applying all of the above to your own organization comes down to ten questions. The order matters. If the earlier question has no answer, the later ones are meaningless.

  • 1. What actually must not leave the building — write the list. If it comes out empty, layer 3 is not needed
  • 2. Is the basis for that judgment law, contract, or habit — if law, drill down to the article and the subparagraph. If habit, it is due for review right now
  • 3. If the basis is law, is one of the grounds available to you — if any of the five in Article 28-8 can be established, the problem is solved without buying layer 3
  • 4. Do you hold data nobody else has — if not, owning layer 2 produces the same model everyone else has. Most plans catch on this question
  • 5. Can that data become an evaluation set — if it cannot, improvement is unmeasurable regardless of what you own
  • 6. Is there work the current model refuses to do — write down the actual cases. If there are none, layer 4 is premature
  • 7. Does inference cost scale directly with usage — if cost stays nearly flat as usage grows, the economic case for ownership is weak
  • 8. If the vendor withdraws this model tomorrow, what breaks and how fast — if nothing breaks, layer 1 is an expensive insurance policy
  • 9. Does any candidate license have a clause that blocks your scale or sector — measure against your size three years out, not today
  • 10. Who gets up when the inference server dies at night — if no name emerges, the organization cannot yet carry layer 3

Answer those ten and the layers you need narrow on their own. And the answers have to be written down to serve as a basis later. Recorded on one page in the form below, they save the next meeting from relitigating the same argument.

[OWNERSHIP LAYER DECISION RECORD]
  Workload    : first-pass summarization of customer inquiries
  Decided on  : 2026-09-05

  Requirement classification
    Legal       : cross-border transfer -> ground available under Art. 28-8(3)
    Contractual : no-training-use clause secured
    Operational : no work currently blocked by refusals
    Availability: under 3 seconds during business hours

  Per-layer decision
    L1 weights  : do not buy (alternate path exists if vendor sunsets)
    L2 data     : secure post-training and evaluation data only (720 items)
    L3 location : do not buy (requirement resolved via subparagraph 3)
    L4 policy   : do not buy (no work blocked)

  Re-review triggers
    - monthly call volume exceeds 5x current
    - 3 or more refusal-blocked tasks reported in a quarter
    - candidate license scale threshold comes within reach

If you do decide to buy a layer, record the license checks the same way before you buy. A license is a condition that follows the product for its entire life.

What could not be confirmed

  • License terms for Qwen, DeepSeek, GLM and Kimi K3 — secondary summaries carry various descriptions, but the license texts themselves were not opened and the clauses could not be settled. In an article working at clause level, unsettled values do not belong in the table, so those models were left out of it. Anyone evaluating a specific model should open the LICENSE file in its repository and start with whether a scale clause exists
  • The full text of Article 26 of the Personal Information Protection Act — entrustment of processing is a separate axis governed by a separate article, but the statutory page renders through script and the text could not be retrieved. So the specific obligations under Article 26 were not asserted in the body; only the existence of the axis was noted
  • Sector-specific rules in finance and healthcare — regulations such as the electronic financial supervision rules or healthcare-sector guidance may impose domestic processing requirements independently of the Personal Information Protection Act, and those were not checked. The statement that the law does not generally require domestic storage is scoped strictly to Article 28-8
  • Investment relationships between the companies cited and the author's fund — the original carries no disclosure and the portfolio listing was not cross-checked. So the body notes only the possibility of an interest and asserts no relationship
  • Actual throughput of the gpt-oss family — the model cards state memory residency conditions and nothing else. Tokens per second by concurrency level, KV cache consumption under long contexts, and the effect of batching policy are absent from the cards and could not be used in a cost comparison
  • The Llama 4 clause on outputs used for retraining — the attribution obligations for distributing derived models were established, but whether a separate clause restricts using outputs to train models in other families could not be settled. The wording varies by version, so no assertion was made
