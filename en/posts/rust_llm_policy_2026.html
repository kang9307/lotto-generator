Introduction: not whether AI was used, but who answers for it

When an open source project publishes an AI policy, the reaction usually splits two ways. Either it is a retreat from the present, or it is long overdue. Both rest on the same mistake: the assumption that the policy is about a tool.

Read the LLM policy Rust adopted for rust-lang/rust clause by clause and that assumption falls apart quickly. The policy does not forbid models. It explicitly permits using them, and for some purposes it reads closer to encouragement. What it does is draw a line at exactly one point. Did a model originate the artifact that ends up in a public place? If the answer is yes, most doors close. If no, most stay open.

That distinction is far more practical than it looks. Open source collaboration runs on something other than code. It runs on the premise that a person can explain their own contribution — that when a reviewer asks why a particular choice was made, an answer comes back. Break that premise and review stops being verification and becomes guesswork. What the policy protects is not code quality. It is that premise.

So this article does not rule on whether the policy is right. It sets out, on both sides, what each clause was meant to solve and what it gave up in exchange, and separates what actually changes for contributors from what changes for maintainers. Whether company data may be fed to a model is a different axis entirely; that one is covered in Can Company Data Go Into an LLM. Here the subject is narrower: the rules that apply when you push your output into someone else's project.

1. The boundary in one sentence - handling versus creating

One sentence runs through the whole policy. It is fine to use an LLM to answer questions, analyze, distill, refine, check, suggest, and review. Not to create. Nearly every other clause is that sentence applied to a specific situation.

1.1 What is permitted

The things the policy explicitly allows share an obvious trait: either the output never becomes public, or a person rewrote it from scratch.

  • Asking a model questions about an existing codebase. For getting oriented in an unfamiliar module there is no restriction at all
  • Summarizing other people's comments privately, on the condition that the summary is not reposted publicly
  • Having a model privately review your own code or prose
  • Building personal developer tooling with a model
  • Using a model to generate candidate solutions, learning from them, and then writing something from scratch in your own style. The policy document lists this flow directly as an allowed case
  • Clearly experimental pull requests, where the character is disclosed through an S-experimental label or a [PERF] title

The fifth item shows the policy's character best. Same model, same problem: submitting the output is forbidden, while reading it, learning from it, and rewriting is allowed. The results may end up looking similar, but appearance is not what the policy examines.

1.2 What is prohibited

The other side uses the same test. If it lands in a public place and a model originally created it, it is blocked.

  • Comments posted from a personal account that were originally created by an LLM. Issue bodies and pull request descriptions count here
  • Documentation. Non-trivial source comments are treated as documentation too, with doc-comments, safety comments, and multi-paragraph comments given as examples
  • Compiler diagnostics. A model may conditionally assist with the logic around a diagnostic, but it must not be used to create the message itself that a user sees
  • Policies that would require an LLM to execute them. A rule a person cannot read and apply is not functioning as a rule
  • Substituting an LLM review for a human review decision. Assisting a review and replacing one are different jobs

The point most often misread. Editing generated output afterward does not fix it. The test is not the final form but who originated the content. A workflow where a model drafts and a person polishes does not cross over into permitted territory no matter how heavy the polishing. To cross over, the direction has to reverse: the person writes and the model checks.

2. Why the narrow scope is not an accident

Summarize this as Rust banned AI and two things are wrong: it is not a ban, and it is not Rust as a whole.

2.1 One repository, five teams

The policy document confines itself to the rust-lang/rust repository and names the teams: compiler, libs, types, rustdoc, and bootstrap, along with their subteams. It also spells out what falls outside.

  • Other rust-lang repositories are not covered. Tooling and ecosystem repositories decide for themselves
  • The lang team and the edition team are excluded
  • Documentation of the language itself, the style guide, and lint naming are out of scope as well
  • In effect the policy binds only the path by which code and documentation enter the compiler proper

That the exclusions were listed at all is information in itself. It says the scope was contested, and that narrowing it is what made adoption possible.

2.2 Why it could not go project-wide

An attempt at a project-wide policy did happen, and it did not reach consensus. The discussion itself ran over a month and produced something on the order of 3,000 chat messages. The announcement does not paper over the disagreement either: it states plainly that there is no consensus within the project — and likely never will be — about when, how, and where AI-based tools are acceptable. The person who assembled the policy wrote that they do not think every rule in it is wholly good.

  • The objections are on the record. One held that this is worse than no policy at all and would harm potential contributors, and that the bar set by the exception reads as a lack of trust
  • Another questioned whether the judgment should rest on how something was made rather than how good it is — whether a concise, genuine improvement would be turned away purely because a model was involved
  • There was also a call to treat this as provisional rather than final
  • The document does in fact carry several provisions making it easier to amend later than it was to adopt, and creating a subteam dedicated to LLM policy is under consideration

That changes how the text should be read. This is not a finished norm but an experiment with no fixed end date. The circuit breaker clause below only makes sense on that assumption.

3. The stated reasons - and the number 1,281

Judging a policy starts with what it was trying to solve. Three reasons were given, and none of them is about the quality of AI code.

3.1 The signal of effort broke

The first is that a polished pull request no longer reliably indicates the author's effort and understanding. Short sentence, heavy consequences.

  • Open source review has long used form as a signal. Tidy commits and a thorough description were themselves evidence of care
  • That signal rested on a cost structure: producing that much polish took time. Remove the cost and the signal goes with it
  • Losing the signal means reviewers must spend more time reaching the same judgment, because the stage that filtered on appearance is gone
  • The people who lose here are not only contributors using AI. A careful newcomer's hand-written pull request draws the same suspicion

3.2 Reviewing is made of decisions

The second is bandwidth. The figure of 1,281 open pull requests was given, and the sentence next to it is the crux: reviewing is made of decisions. It is not the work of catching bugs but of judging whether the direction and the approach are right.

  • Automation can help with bug detection. It cannot help with judgment
  • So while generation tools raise the supply of pull requests, review throughput stays flat. The bottleneck does not widen — the inlet does
  • A longer queue becomes a cost of its own. Old pull requests need rebasing, and rebased pull requests need reviewing again
  • The influx of low-effort pull requests was described with the word slop. That word says a lot about the temperature of the discussion

3.3 Using people as relays

The third is the most concrete. An author receives review feedback, pastes it into a model, and pastes the answer back into the pull request. That pattern was called a waste of everyone's time.

  • The reviewer believes they are talking to a person while actually talking to a model. The premise of the conversation collapses
  • There is also no way to tell whether the feedback landed. When the same mistake reappears next round, nobody can say why
  • The pattern does not reduce round trips; it multiplies them. One reviewer's single comment turns into several verification passes
  • This is why the policy blocks comments and pull request descriptions before it blocks code. The judgment is that collaboration breaks at the conversation, not at the diff

Lay the three side by side and the policy's nature is visible. This is not a claim that AI code is bad. It is a rule for allocating a finite resource called review. Why the slower, more deliberate approach to AI-assisted coding works at all is laid out separately in Using AI to Write Better Code Slower.

4. The code exception - what the five conditions really ask for

This section is the evidence that the policy is not a ban. There is a path for LLM-authored code. It is simply a narrow one.

4.1 The five conditions

  1. Pre-arranged. A reviewer agrees ahead of time. You do not throw it over the wall; the person receiving it is settled first
  2. Non-critical. The area must be extremely unlikely to cause a soundness regression. Territory like the trait system or MIR building does not qualify
  3. High quality. It clears the same bar as any other code. There is no gentler standard
  4. Well tested. Coverage must extend to all edge cases
  5. Well reviewed. Both author and reviewer must fully understand the code and be able to explain it

The procedure is set out too. The pull request carries an ai-assisted label and is posted to an internal chat channel for the organization. On top of that, LLM-generated code changes are held to a higher bar than human-authored changes, must come with tests, and soundness-critical changes are off limits unless the author is already a domain expert.

4.2 Why the first condition is pre-arrangement

If only one of the five survived, it would be the first. The other four are quality standards; the first is a cost allocation rule.

  • Without pre-arrangement, the review cost falls entirely on the receiving side. When the sender's cost approaches zero, that asymmetry turns straight into queue
  • Pre-arrangement reverses it. Only as much comes in as a reviewer said they would take
  • At the same time the condition is a barrier. Someone who can talk to a reviewer in advance is probably already inside the project
  • This is exactly where the lack-of-trust criticism lands. To an outsider it reads as a demand to become an insider first
  • Both readings are true. A device that protects the queue is simultaneously a device that narrows new entry — that is the precise character of the clause

What the fifth condition is really asking. Requiring that both author and reviewer can fully explain the code is something good review has demanded since long before AI. What changed is that enough contributions now fail to meet it that it had to be written down. The clause is less a new rule than a tacit expectation moved onto paper.

5. The circuit breaker - 50 percent and ten days

One clause here is rare in comparable project documents: a ratio-triggered stop.

The policy states that if more than half of the pull requests merged in a six-week window were LLM-created, merging of new LLM-created pull requests is disallowed until the share falls back below 50 percent, with a minimum cooldown of ten days.

  • The criterion is the overall mix, not individual quality. However good the pull request, it stops when the ratio tips
  • The purpose is stated as well: to collect information about whether this experiment is working
  • So the clause is not a quality gate but an instrument and a safety valve, closer to a mechanism that halts automatically when things drift outside expectation
  • For contributors it introduces an unfamiliar property. Whether your pull request can merge depends partly on the ratio of contributions you had nothing to do with
  • The ten-day minimum keeps the policy from flapping on and off near the threshold. A bare threshold oscillates at the boundary

The value of this clause lies less in the numbers than in making the experiment behave like an experiment. A policy that writes down its own stop and reversal conditions is easier to fix later than one that does not. The burden is equally plain: unless there is a defined way to count what half means, the clause does not fire.

6. What the disclosure requirement actually demands

There is a second axis. LLM output that lands in a public place must, in principle, be disclosed as such. A handful of items are exempt; everything else falls under the rule.

6.1 The test is origination, not final form

  • Disclosure uses the same test as everything else: did a model originate it
  • So I changed it a lot, it is mine now does not hold under this policy. Making the degree of editing the question would make the test unenforceable in the first place
  • That the test buys decidability at the price of relying on honesty is something the policy itself concedes, which is why the enforcement clauses in the next section treat dishonesty as the gravest breach
  • There is also a provision for non-English contributors: contributing in your native language is welcomed and translating into English is not a requirement

6.2 The four things you can simply disclose

The policy carves out a middle ground that is neither prohibited nor freely permitted. These only require saying so.

  • Machine translation, on the condition that the original message is not posted alongside it
  • Trivial changes. Typo fixes, markdown links, synonym swaps, and type signatures are the examples given
  • Bug discovery using a model, provided you verified it yourself. Passing it along unverified simply transfers the review cost
  • Review bots, under three conditions: a separate account, the ability to block them, and no power to block a merge

Those three bot conditions are a small piece of good design on their own. The separate account keeps people and machines distinguishable, blockability gives an unwilling contributor a veto, and the non-blocking rule stops the bot from becoming a gatekeeper. It is a three-line recipe for adopting automation without handing it decision rights.

7. How violations are handled - the clause against playing detective

A policy's real force shows up in enforcement. And these enforcement clauses have more to say about accusers than about violators.

  • Breaching the honesty requirements is treated most severely: warnings go out and bans are possible
  • Minor violations are handled by requesting compliance and pointing at the specific clause
  • Major violations mean closing the pull request or issue
  • The policy states outright that it is not your job to play detective. Do not investigate someone accusingly on the basis of writing style alone
  • If you have concerns, the designated path is to report to the moderation team rather than to accuse publicly
  • Harassing people who use LLMs is separately forbidden

Why this structure is needed becomes obvious if you imagine its absence. Attach a vigilante corps to an undetectable rule and the corps does more damage than the rule prevents. Style-based judgment is unreliable, and the people it misfires on are usually non-native speakers or newcomers who worked hard on presentation. By admitting the limits of its own enforceability and funneling concerns into one channel, the policy did not loosen the rule — it capped the size of the side effects in advance.

That is also this policy's largest structural weakness. As long as the test depends on honesty, the outcome can be that only the honest are bound by it. The policy does not hide this.

8. Where other projects stand

Placing this policy requires comparison. Responses across open source fall into roughly three groups.

8.1 Outright refusal and the DCO argument

The simplest answer is not to take it. QEMU is the clearest case. Its policy is to decline any contribution believed to include or derive from AI-generated content — note that suspicion, not proof, is the trigger.

The reasoning is what deserves attention. It is not quality but the Developer Certificate of Origin. To satisfy the DCO, a contributor has to fully understand the copyright and license status of the content they are contributing, and for generated content that understanding does not hold.

  • The argument never asks whether the code is good. It asks only whether a legal assertion can be made
  • So better models do not weaken it. It is entirely decoupled from the quality debate
  • The cost is rigidity. QEMU's policy does leave a channel open for exceptions, directing contributors to bring specifics about the tool, the model, and the use case to the mailing list
  • The document states that the project welcomes discussion on exceptions to the policy or more general revisions

The game engine world reached a similar conclusion, and that story is covered in Godot Bans AI-Authored Code Contributions.

8.2 Permission under supervision

At the other end are projects that accept contributions on condition of human supervision and disclosure. Their shared argument is different: whatever the tool, the submitter is accountable, and the existing responsibility structure already covers it.

  • Adoption cost is low because it reaffirms an existing structure instead of inventing a new rule
  • The biggest advantage is that it does not narrow the flow of contributors
  • The review bandwidth problem, however, remains untouched. Nothing in the policy stops a surge of low-effort contributions
  • And where disclosure is the only requirement, there is effectively no way to filter contributions that were not disclosed

Read the table across and the fork appears. It is what the project values most.

  • To keep license risk near zero, refusal is the simplest route, and the adjudication cost is low
  • To protect the flow of contributors, disclosure costs less — but the bandwidth problem has to be solved some other way
  • When review bandwidth is already at its limit and contributor breadth must be preserved too, what remains is Rust's route of splitting the clauses by use case. Complexity is the price
  • Whichever route is taken, two things are needed in common: a decidable test, and a path for when the decision turns out wrong

9. What changes for contributors starting tomorrow

Clauses alone do not convey much. Translated into actual working order, this is what shifts.

  • You write the pull request description yourself. The description trips the rule before the code does. A model-written PR body fails the clause no matter how good the diff is
  • You write review replies yourself. The paste-in, paste-back round trip is closed off, which also means feedback you do not understand cannot be answered until you do
  • Landing code means finding a person first. Submitting LLM-authored code requires arranging it with a reviewer beforehand. The order of operations changes
  • You check the area before you start. Soundness-sensitive territory is not eligible for the exception, and you cannot begin without knowing which parts those are
  • Tests stop being optional. The exception explicitly requires coverage of edge cases
  • Translation actually gets easier. Machine translation only needs disclosing, and contributing in your native language is welcomed
  • Experiments are handled with labels. Performance work and exploratory attempts just need their character disclosed via an S-experimental label or a [PERF] title
  • Small fixes remain easy. Typos and link corrections are not blocked. Disclose and proceed
  • The tools are not taken away. Reading, understanding, and exploring options with a model stays fully open. What changed is the step immediately before submission

Compressed into one line: the policy opens the model wide as a learning tool and closes it narrowly as a submission tool. The people who actually lose out are not those who use models, but those who send along what a model produced without reading it.

10. What maintainers gain and what they pay

10.1 What they gain

  • Grounds for declining become a document. Being able to point at a clause instead of a preference cuts the emotional cost of reviewing considerably
  • The premise of conversation is restored. Once comments and PR descriptions are written by people, a reviewer can tell whether feedback actually landed
  • Cost allocation flips. With pre-arrangement in place, only as much arrives as a reviewer agreed to take
  • There is a way to stop. The ratio-based circuit breaker pre-builds a route to halt without a fresh argument if things go badly
  • No vigilante corps forms. Forbidding detective work and designating the moderation path heads off community fracture

Put them together and what shows is that none of the costs was avoided for free. So success here rests not on the polish of the clauses but on how quickly the project notices which of the six costs bites first, and fixes it. The amendment provisions and the subteam discussion are the preparation for exactly that.

11. What could not be confirmed

Collected in one place, the items without solid grounding.

  • A stated effective date. The adoption announcement is dated 5 August 2026, but the policy document itself contains no sentence fixing when it takes effect
  • Whether the two documents name the same five teams. The announcement says five teams without listing them. The policy names five teams in scope, so the correspondence is natural, but no sentence confirming they are the same five could be found
  • How the circuit breaker counts. What an LLM-created pull request is counted from — labels, self-declaration, or something else — could not be confirmed
  • Operational detail of the ai-assisted label and the internal channel. Posting scope, who can read it, and how tallies are kept could not be confirmed
  • Enforcement history. How many warnings or bans have actually issued is not available this soon after adoption
  • Metric movement after adoption. It is too early to say how the open pull request count has responded
  • Primary policy text for other projects. Apart from QEMU, individual project policy documents were not read directly. The classification in the table above rests on a secondary summary and may have changed since
  • Detailed procedure in the Rust contributor guide. That the development guide separates guidance for writers from guidance for reviewers was confirmed, but the specific wording inside was not. What is stated is only that where the two conflict, the policy document governs

12. Frequently asked questions

Did Rust ban AI-made contributions outright?

No. The line the policy draws is not between banned and allowed but between handling and creating. Using a model to answer questions, analyze, distill, refine, check, suggest, and review is permitted. What gets blocked is a model originating the artifact that lands in a public place. Even code is not fully closed off. It is accepted as an experimental exception when five conditions are all met: the pull request was pre-arranged with a reviewer, the change sits in a non-critical area extremely unlikely to cause a soundness regression, it clears the same quality bar as any other code, it is well tested across edge cases, and both author and reviewer can fully explain it. In short, this is conditional permission with a disclosure requirement layered on, not a ban.

Does the policy cover every Rust repository?

No. It applies to the rust-lang/rust repository alone, and only to the compiler, libs, types, rustdoc, and bootstrap teams along with their subteams. Other rust-lang repositories, the lang team, the edition team, documentation of the language itself, the style guide, and lint naming are explicitly out of scope. That narrowness is less a design choice than the shape of what could be agreed on. An attempt to write a project-wide policy did not reach consensus, so the effort started where review load is heaviest and the stakes are clearest. The policy document also carries several provisions that make it easier to amend later than it was to adopt.

Why did this policy become necessary now?

Three reasons were given. First, a polished pull request no longer reliably signals the author's effort and understanding. Neat formatting used to be evidence of care because it cost time; once that cost disappears, the signal disappears with it. Second, review bandwidth. With 1,281 open pull requests, reviewing is described as being made of decisions about direction and approach rather than bug hunting, and that kind of judgment does not scale with automation. Third, the round trip where an author pastes review feedback into a model and pastes the answer back. The human becomes a relay and the reviewer ends up talking to a model. None of the three is an argument about the quality of AI code. All three are about trust and time, and that sets the character of the whole policy.

What is the 50 percent circuit breaker?

If more than half the pull requests merged in a six-week window were LLM-created, merging of new LLM-created pull requests stops until the share drops back below half, with a minimum cooldown of ten days. What makes it unusual is that the trigger is a ratio rather than a quality threshold. However good an individual pull request is, the gate closes when the overall mix tips, which makes this a composition control rather than a code quality control. Its stated purpose is to gather information about whether the experiment is working. In practice it behaves like a spending cap. For a contributor it introduces something new: whether your pull request can land depends partly on the contribution mix of people you have never met.

What have other open source projects chosen?

Roughly three paths. The first is outright refusal. QEMU is the clearest case, and its reasoning is not quality but the Developer Certificate of Origin: to satisfy the DCO a contributor has to fully understand the copyright and license status of what they submit, and that understanding does not hold for generated content. The second is permission under supervision, which usually pairs a disclosure requirement with the submitter carrying final responsibility. The third is where Rust stands, splitting the rules by use case and leaving code generation as a narrow exception. Rather than one being correct, the fork in the road is what a project values most. If you want license risk near zero, refusal is simpler. If you want to protect the flow of new contributors, disclosure costs less.

Conclusion: three things to take from this policy

First, what this policy protects is not code but explainability. Follow the clauses one by one and they all arrive at the same place: can the person who submitted this explain it? With that premise standing, review is verification; without it, review is guesswork. That is why comments and pull request descriptions were closed off before code was. Collaboration actually breaks at the conversation, not at the diff.

Second, review bandwidth does not grow with tooling. The sentence set beside those 1,281 open pull requests was that reviewing is made of decisions. Automation helps with bug detection; judgment about direction and approach does not follow. If generation tools raise supply while throughput holds flat, what grew is not contribution but queue. This policy sits between acknowledging that asymmetry and writing allocation rules for it, and refusing to acknowledge it and holding on.

Third, a policy that writes down its costs is easier to fix. Pre-arrangement protects the queue while narrowing new entry. The origination test makes adjudication possible while leaning on honesty. Forbidding detective work reduces side effects while lowering enforcement. None of it is free. The circuit breaker with its pre-written stop conditions, the provisions that ease amendment, and the subteam discussion are all markers that the bill will be settled later.

One last thing. The sentence most likely to outlast this debate is not a clause but a diagnosis: polished output is no longer evidence of effort. That change is not specific to Rust, nor to code. Every form of collaboration that used form as a proxy for trust is standing in the same spot, and each is now looking for a different signal. The signal Rust picked was explainability.
