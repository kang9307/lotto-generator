Varicose Veins Complete Guide - Symptoms, Causes, Treatment, and Prevention

Appearance and severity do not track each other - five conditions to rule out, the signs that need a doctor, and the evidence level behind each measure

Decisions about diagnosing varicose veins and choosing management - compression therapy, sclerotherapy, radiofrequency or laser ablation, surgery - belong with the clinician who has actually examined your leg and reviewed your ultrasound. In particular, if one leg suddenly swells and hurts, a varicose vein bleeds, you have redness and tenderness with fever, or a wound below the knee has not healed within two weeks, do not wait - seek care now.

Introduction: Appearance and Severity Do Not Track Each Other

Varicose veins are leg veins that have stretched and become tortuous enough to bulge visibly beneath the skin, usually appearing blue or purple. That is the familiar picture, but judging this condition by appearance alone fails in both directions. Some people have thick, prominent veins and no discomfort whatsoever, while others have legs that look perfectly normal yet feel heavy every evening with ankles that swell. What happens inside the vein and what shows on the skin match each other far less closely than most people assume.

The cause is the valves. Leg veins contain a series of one-way valves that keep blood moving toward the heart. When those valves weaken or become damaged, blood refluxes downward and pools, pressure inside the vein rises with the weight of that standing column of blood (venous hypertension), and sustained pressure stretches the vessel wall until it loses its elasticity. The bulging vein you can see is the product of that process, not its cause. This is precisely why removing only the visible veins can leave the leg symptoms exactly where they were.

This guide, current as of August 2026, covers the anatomy and causes, the functional symptoms that appear before anything is visible, the five conditions most often mistaken for varicose veins, the signs that warrant a clinic visit, diagnosis and the CEAP classification, compression therapy and the range of procedural options, the evidence level behind each piece of lifestyle advice, and three persistent myths. It does not recommend any particular procedure and does not rank procedures against each other. Which approach fits depends on where the reflux is, how far it extends, and your overall health - and that judgement belongs to the clinician who has seen the scan.

1. What Are Varicose Veins - Vein Valves and Reflux

1.1 How a Healthy Vein Works

Leg veins must move blood against gravity, back up toward the heart. Multiple one-way valves inside the veins make this possible. Each time the calf muscles contract, they squeeze the veins and push blood upward; the valves then open and close to prevent blood from flowing back down. This mechanism is often called the "calf muscle pump."

1.2 From Valve Failure to Varicose Veins

When valves weaken or become damaged, blood cannot be pushed fully upward, and some of it flows backward and pools in the vein. Over time, this sustained pressure stretches the vein wall, causing it to lose elasticity and twist into the bulging shape recognized as a varicose vein. This most commonly develops in the legs - particularly the calves and inner thighs - because standing and walking place more pressure on leg veins than on veins elsewhere in the body.

1.3 Superficial and Deep Veins Are Different Layers

Leg veins run in two broad layers: the superficial system just under the skin (the great and small saphenous veins and their tributaries) and the deep system that runs between the muscles. Almost every varicose vein you can see belongs to the superficial layer. A clot in the deep system - deep vein thrombosis - produces something quite different: a whole leg that swells without any prominent surface veins, and a genuine emergency. Keeping these two layers separate in your mind makes the differential discussion further down much clearer.

2. Causes and Risk Factors

2.1 Age and Genetics

As people age, vein valves wear down and weaken, so risk rises with every decade. Having a parent with varicose veins raises your own likelihood, and genetic factors clearly influence the strength of vein walls and valves. Family history shifts the odds, however; it does not settle them.

2.2 Sex, Pregnancy, and Hormones - Where Folk Wisdom and Surveys Diverge

The idea that varicose veins are a women's condition is widespread, but population surveys that randomly sample people and examine them in person do not line up behind it. The Edinburgh Vein Study examined 1,566 adults aged 18-64 and found the age-adjusted prevalence of trunk varices was 40% in men and 32% in women, with chronic venous insufficiency at 9% in men and 7% in women. Other cohorts, such as the Bonn Vein Study, put women slightly ahead. The surveys disagree because they define and grade varicose veins differently - and because who ends up in a clinic is not the same question as who has the condition.

None of which makes hormonal factors irrelevant. During pregnancy, blood volume rises and the enlarging uterus presses on pelvic veins, loading the leg circulation. Varicose veins that appear for the first time during pregnancy often improve substantially after delivery - though not always completely, and less completely with each subsequent pregnancy. The accurate summary is narrow: this is not a women's condition, and hormonal risk in women is not imaginary either.

2.3 Lifestyle Factors - Prolonged Standing, Obesity, and Sedentary Habits

Standing still or sitting for long stretches puts the calf muscle pump out of action, which lengthens the time blood spends pooling in the leg. That is why retail work, healthcare, and teaching show up repeatedly in prevalence figures. Carrying extra weight raises abdominal and leg venous pressure together. In the second round of the Bonn Vein Study, multivariate analysis of who had worsened over 6.6 years left age, obesity, and arterial hypertension standing as the main risk factors for progression.

The commonly cited risk factors, gathered in one place:

  • Age: ageing of valves and vein walls, the most consistently confirmed factor of all
  • Family history: the odds climb further when both parents are affected
  • Pregnancy, particularly repeated pregnancies: higher blood volume plus pelvic vein compression
  • Obesity: confirmed as a risk factor for progression, not merely for onset
  • Occupations spent standing: long periods with the muscle pump switched off
  • Previous deep vein thrombosis: secondary varicose veins after valve damage

3. Symptoms - What You Feel Before Anything Shows

3.1 Functional Symptoms Come First

Remember this condition by its appearance and you will miss the early signals, because what people notice first is not a vein but how the leg feels. Fine in the morning, heavy and tight by mid-afternoon; a sock elastic that leaves a deep mark by evening; a calf cramp that wakes you at night. These functional symptoms share one telling feature - they vary across the day. They worsen the longer you stand and ease after you elevate the leg or sleep. Pain that is worst on waking and improves as you move is pointing somewhere other than the veins.

  • Heaviness and a tight, dragging sensation in the leg - the most common early complaint
  • A daily pattern: worse through the afternoon and evening, lighter in the morning
  • Swelling around the ankle; sock marks that stay visible for a long time
  • Dull aching or throbbing, and leg fatigue after prolonged standing
  • Calf cramps that wake you at night
  • Itching around the veins, tingling, or burning
  • Twisted, bulging veins that appear blue or purple
  • In longstanding cases, brown discolouration or eczema on the inner ankle

3.2 Why Symptoms and Appearance Come Apart

A separate analysis from the same Edinburgh cohort showed the association between leg symptoms and trunk varices is weaker than expected: even in people who had varicose veins, most leg symptoms probably had a non-venous cause, and removing the visible varices was therefore unlikely to relieve them. Turn that around and you get the practical rule: reflux can be present with nothing visible on the surface, and a prominent vein may not be the reason your leg hurts today. Which is exactly why the reflux gets confirmed on ultrasound rather than by looking.

3.3 Spider Veins vs. Varicose Veins

Spider veins are thin, web-like red or blue vessels immediately under the skin, smaller than varicose veins and usually painless. Varicose veins are thicker, visibly raised, tortuous, and more often accompanied by heaviness or aching. The CEAP system grades them separately - telangiectasias and reticular veins as C1, varicose veins as C2. The two frequently appear together, and reflux in a larger vein underneath can hide beneath what looks like nothing more than spider veins.

4. Is This Really Varicose Veins? Five Conditions to Rule Out

A leg that swells and aches is not automatically a venous leg. The five conditions below are easily confused with varicose veins yet demand completely different urgency. The first row is an emergency.

5. When to See a Doctor

Varicose veins themselves are rarely a matter of hours. The signals below are a different category. The UK NICE guideline (CG168) names most of them as criteria for referral to a vascular service, and it sets bleeding apart as an immediate referral.

  • A varicose vein that bleeds: a vein has ruptured beneath thinned skin. This needs care immediately. Control it by pressing on the wound with the leg raised above heart level.
  • Sudden swelling and pain in one leg: think deep vein thrombosis. Accompanying breathlessness or chest pain makes it an emergency.
  • Changes in skin colour: brown staining at the inner ankle, eczematous change, or skin that feels hardened all signal that chronic venous insufficiency is already established.
  • A wound below the knee that has not healed in two weeks: that is the threshold for a venous ulcer. A healed ulcer scar also warrants referral.
  • Redness and tenderness with fever or chills: possible infection, and more urgent still if an ulcer is present.
  • Pain or heaviness that interferes with daily life: this is not something to endure as a cosmetic complaint. Symptomatic varicose veins are a clinical category.

6. Diagnosis - Doppler (Duplex) Ultrasound and the CEAP Classification

Assessment starts with looking at the leg and palpating it. Then comes Doppler ultrasound (duplex ultrasound), which images the vessel while simultaneously measuring the direction and speed of flow, identifying which segment refluxes and for how long. There is no pain and no radiation. The reason this matters is the mismatch described earlier: appearance cannot tell you which vein is responsible, and without knowing the source segment there is no rational basis for deciding what to do.

Findings from examination and ultrasound are recorded using the CEAP classification. In the 2020 update, the clinical class runs C0 (no visible or palpable signs), C1 (telangiectasias or reticular veins), C2 (varicose veins), C3 (oedema), C4 (skin changes - C4a pigmentation or eczema, C4b lipodermatosclerosis or atrophie blanche, C4c corona phlebectatica), C5 (healed venous ulcer), and C6 (active venous ulcer), with an "r" appended for recurrent varicose veins and recurrent ulcers. If someone in a clinic has said "you are a C2", this is the scale being used. A higher number is not the same as greater urgency, but C4 and above means the skin is involved, and that changes the weight of the management plan.

7. Treatment 1 - Compression Therapy: These Are Medical Devices

This is where most of the confusion sits. A compression stocking is not a tight sock; it is a medical device with a defined pressure class. Graduated compression - strongest at the ankle, weaker as it rises - narrows the vein's cross-section, reduces the refluxing volume, and improves the efficiency of the muscle pump. The sensation of a lighter, less swollen leg comes from that.

The strength of the evidence, however, varies enormously by purpose. For an active venous leg ulcer, compression aiming at an ankle pressure of at least 40 mmHg carries the highest grade of recommendation in the European Society for Vascular Surgery (ESVS) 2022 guidelines for improving ulcer healing. For simple varicose veins without ulceration, by contrast, the Cochrane review updated in 2021 concluded there is insufficient high-certainty evidence to say whether stockings work as a sole and initial treatment. The NICE guideline goes further, stating that compression hosiery should not be offered to treat varicose veins unless interventional treatment is unsuitable. In short, the role of relieving symptoms and the role of reversing disease are two different claims.

Pressure class (mmHg), length, and wearing schedule depend on the state of the leg and the goal, so do not decide these yourself - have them prescribed after a consultation. The usual routine is to put them on in the morning before swelling develops and remove them before bed; there is no rule requiring overnight wear. Compression is also not universally safe. The following situations require assessment before any compression is applied.

  • Peripheral arterial disease: the ESVS guidance treats an ankle-brachial index of 0.5 or below as an absolute contraindication to any form of compression, with modified pressures needed in the 0.5-0.8 range.
  • Decompensated heart failure: returning pooled fluid to the circulation abruptly can add load the heart cannot take.
  • Acute skin infection or open lesions: compression does not simply go on over cellulitis or a weeping wound.
  • Reduced sensation, such as diabetic neuropathy: pressure injury can develop without the wearer feeling it.
  • The wrong size: a garment that grips the ankle and gapes at the calf behaves like a tourniquet. Limbs get measured before garments get chosen.

8. Treatment 2 - Sclerotherapy

Sclerotherapy involves injecting a special solution, either liquid or foam, into the affected vein, causing inflammation of the vessel's inner wall that leads it to gradually close. The closed vein is reabsorbed by the body over time, and blood naturally reroutes through healthy veins. It is commonly used for smaller varicose veins and spider veins, while ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy, performed with real-time imaging to track the vein's location, is also used for somewhat larger veins. The procedure is typically done quickly in an outpatient setting.

9. Treatment 3 - Radiofrequency and Laser Vein Ablation

For thick veins such as the great saphenous vein, which is often the starting point for an entire network of varicosities, a catheter is passed into the vein to close the vessel itself. Some approaches use heat; some do not.

  • Radiofrequency ablation (RFA): a thin catheter delivers radiofrequency energy that denatures collagen in the vein wall and damages the inner lining, closing the vein.
  • Endovenous laser ablation (EVLA/EVLT): a laser fibre passed through a catheter coagulates the vein wall. Dilute anaesthetic is infiltrated around the vein - tumescent anaesthesia - as part of the procedure.
  • Non-thermal approaches: medical adhesive, or chemical and mechanical stimulation, can close the vein without heat. The ESVS 2022 guidelines treat this family in its own section.

What they share is a change of principle: rather than pulling the vein out, they close it in place and let the body absorb it. That is not the same as being free of side effects. Bruising, post-procedure tightness and pain, and superficial thrombophlebitis are relatively common; nerve irritation and skin burns are rare but reported. Sclerotherapy or phlebectomy is sometimes performed alongside.

10. Treatment 4 - Surgery, and the Question of What Comes First

Traditional vein stripping removes the problem vein through an incision; ambulatory phlebectomy extracts prominent varicosities through very small ones. Minimally invasive procedures have reduced how often open surgery is used, but anatomy and general health still leave it on the list of options.

Guidelines do place these options in an order. NICE puts endothermal ablation first when intervention is indicated, then ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy if ablation is unsuitable, then surgery if foam sclerotherapy is unsuitable. The ESVS 2022 guidelines likewise give endovenous thermal ablation their highest grade for great saphenous vein incompetence. That ordering, though, is a recommendation about populations, not a prescription for an individual. The location and length of the refluxing segment, vessel calibre and tortuosity, skin condition, comorbidities, and anaesthetic considerations can all move the conclusion. This article recommends none of them. That several options exist, and that choosing between them is done with the clinician who has read your scan, is as far as the honest version goes.

11. Prevention and Lifestyle Management - With the Evidence Level Attached

Lifestyle advice for veins is listed in much the same way everywhere, but the items do not carry equal weight. "It cannot hurt" and "it was confirmed in controlled trials" are different statements, so here they are separated.

MeasureWhat you can expectEvidence level
Leg elevation Drops venous pressure immediately, easing swelling and heaviness Mechanism is clear, controlled trials of long-term benefit are thin. Reasonable to advise for symptom relief only
Walking and calf exercise Engages the muscle pump and assists venous return The Cochrane review (updated 2023) found insufficient evidence to judge exercise in non-ulcerated chronic venous insufficiency. Cardiovascular benefits stand on their own footing
Weight management Reduces venous pressure load and progression risk The Bonn Vein Study identified obesity as an independent risk factor for progression (observational). No trial of weight loss itself was located
Compression (symptom relief) Eases heaviness and swelling Widely reported subjectively, but as a sole initial treatment for non-ulcerated varicose veins the Cochrane review judged the evidence insufficient
Compression (ulcer healing) Improves healing of an active venous leg ulcer ESVS 2022 gives compression at 40 mmHg or more at the ankle its highest grade. The strongest entry in this table
Changing position, avoiding tight clothing Shortens the time blood spends pooling General advice. No plausible harm and no cost, but no controlled-trial evidence either

Translated into practice:

  • Elevate the legs: above heart level when sitting or lying. Two spells of 15-20 minutes in the evening is enough to feel the difference.
  • Walk, and move the ankles: raising and lowering the toes at your desk is enough to switch the muscle pump on.
  • Manage weight: one of the few progression risk factors you can actually change.
  • Change position: if you work standing, break up the time spent fixed in one spot.
  • Avoid tight clothing: garments that grip the waist or groin obstruct venous return.
  • Compression: if you are at risk or symptomatic, agree the pressure class with a clinician and put the garment on in the morning.

13. What Happens If You Leave It - Progression and Complications

Left long enough, varicose veins can tip into chronic venous insufficiency. The skin stains brown, venous eczema appears, and lipodermatosclerosis hardens the skin and the tissue beneath it. The most severe form is a venous ulcer, typically an indolent wound on the inner ankle. An infected ulcer can become cellulitis and, rarely, something deeper. Superficial thrombophlebitis can also develop within a varicosity, leaving a hard, tender cord you can feel along the vein.

For a sense of the pace, the second round of the Bonn Vein Study is useful. Among participants who had varicose veins only (C2) at baseline, the proportion that had moved to a higher class 6.6 years later was 19.8% for non-saphenous and 31.8% for saphenous varicose veins. Over the same interval, 13.7% developed new varicose veins, and age, obesity, and hypertension remained the risk factors for progression. Read the other way round: most people do not deteriorate sharply within a few years, but roughly one in three or four moves up a class. Not a reason to rush, and not a reason to ignore it either.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. What are varicose veins?

Varicose veins occur when the valves inside leg veins fail to function properly, allowing blood to flow backward and pool, which stretches and twists the veins so they bulge visibly beneath the skin. Varicose veins most often appear in the legs, particularly the calves and thighs, because standing and walking place greater pressure on leg veins.

Q2. How are spider veins different from varicose veins?

Spider veins are small, thin, web-like red or blue veins just beneath the skin and are usually painless. Varicose veins are larger, bulge more visibly, appear twisted, and are more often accompanied by pain, heaviness, or swelling. The CEAP system grades them separately as C1 and C2, and reflux in a larger vein underneath can hide beneath what looks like spider veins alone.

Q3. What's the difference between sclerotherapy and radiofrequency/laser ablation?

Sclerotherapy injects a solution into the vein to close it gradually and is commonly used for smaller veins, while radiofrequency and laser ablation use a catheter to deliver heat energy that closes larger veins. Neither can be declared better in general terms - the choice shifts with the location of the reflux, the calibre and tortuosity of the vessel, and your overall health. Make that decision with the clinician who has read your ultrasound.

Q4. Will compression stockings alone make varicose veins go away?

No. Compression stockings are a tool for easing heaviness and swelling, and for non-ulcerated varicose veins the Cochrane review judged the evidence insufficient to say whether stockings work as a sole initial treatment. They are medical devices with defined pressure classes, so the class and the wearing schedule should be agreed with a clinician; the usual routine is to put them on in the morning and remove them before bed. They can be contraindicated in peripheral arterial disease (ankle-brachial index 0.5 or below) and decompensated heart failure, among other situations.

Q5. What happens if varicose veins are left untreated?

They can progress to chronic venous insufficiency, skin discolouration, venous eczema, lipodermatosclerosis and, in severe cases, venous ulcers. In the second round of the Bonn Vein Study, 19.8-31.8% of people who had varicose veins only had moved to a higher class 6.6 years later. If your skin colour changes or a wound will not heal, get it seen.
