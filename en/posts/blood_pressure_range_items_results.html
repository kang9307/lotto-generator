Blood Pressure Ranges and How to Read Your Numbers - Systolic, Diastolic, Home Readings

What 120/80 actually means, which band 130/85 falls into, why the same reading gets a different verdict once it crosses a border, and why the number at home rarely matches the one at the clinic - reading the two numbers properly

2026-08-09 Health & Wellness
Read this first: this article is general health information about how blood pressure numbers are classified. It does not replace medical diagnosis, prescription or treatment. Every figure below is a classification threshold set by a professional society or public health body, not a personal target. Nothing here recommends starting, stopping or adjusting any medication, and it must never be used as grounds for changing a prescribed dose on your own. Dose changes belong to the clinician who prescribed them. If you are pregnant, being treated for kidney disease, diabetes or cardiovascular disease, or if the reading belongs to a child, an adolescent or a frail older adult, the general adult table does not transfer directly.

Introduction: you are looking at two numbers, but probably reading only one

Say the monitor shows 128/84. The first number is under 130, so it gets waved through. Yet that reading is neither normal nor merely elevated under any current guideline. The 84 on the right has already moved it into a different band.

This is where blood pressure gets misread most often. The two numbers measure two different events, and classification follows whichever of them is worse. Reading only the first number is reading half the result.

Then a second problem lands on top. Search for blood pressure thresholds and you will find hypertension starts at 130/80 and hypertension starts at 140/90 stated with equal confidence. Both are correct. The first is the American line; the second is what Korea, Europe, Japan and China use. Without knowing which table you are looking at, you cannot know which band your reading belongs to.

This article is not about lowering blood pressure. Diet, exercise and lifestyle are covered separately in how to lower blood pressure naturally. What follows is the step before that: what systolic and diastolic each measure, which table your reading belongs to, why home and clinic values diverge, how to take a reading that a table can legitimately be applied to, and when to be seen. Every threshold below is current as of August 2026 and changes when the societies revise their guidelines.

1. What systolic and diastolic each measure

1.1 The first number - peak pressure as the heart pushes

The heart contracts and drives blood into the aorta. The peak pressure on the arterial wall during that moment is systolic blood pressure, the upper or top number.

It depends heavily on how much blood is ejected per beat and on how readily the large arteries expand to absorb it. As the aorta stiffens with age, the same volume produces a higher pressure spike. That is the mechanism behind the slow upward drift of systolic readings from middle age onward.

1.2 The second number - pressure that never fully releases

While the heart relaxes and refills between beats, arterial pressure does not fall to zero. The lowest pressure sustained during that interval is diastolic blood pressure, the lower or bottom number.

It mostly reflects how narrowed the small peripheral arteries are, that is, total systemic vascular resistance. This is why a diastolic-only rise turns up in younger adults. Pressure that stays high even while the heart is resting means the vessels are carrying a load continuously.

1.3 Which one matters more depends on age

Asking which number counts more brings age into the question. The broad pattern looks like this.

  • After roughly age 50, systolic pressure carries more of the explanatory weight for cardiovascular and stroke risk, because arterial stiffening produces high systolic with normal or even low diastolic values.
  • In younger adults, diastolic pressure is frequently the one that rises first. With the systolic number sitting comfortably in range, the pattern often passes unnoticed.
  • At every age, classification follows the higher band of the two. A reading of 118 over 92 is not normal; it sits in the hypertensive range.
  • The unit mmHg means millimetres of mercury. Modern electronic monitors contain no mercury, but the unit set by the old mercury manometers has stayed in place.

2. The American table - normal through hypertensive crisis

The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline keeps the categories introduced in 2017. Before reading it, note the same caveat that applies to every table in this article: this one is written for readings taken in a clinical setting. Home readings are judged against a different line, covered further down.

Category Systolic (mmHg) Logic Diastolic (mmHg)
Normal Under 120 and Under 80
Elevated 120-129 and Under 80
Hypertension stage 1 130-139 or 80-89
Hypertension stage 2 140 or above or 90 or above
Hypertensive crisis Above 180 and/or Above 120
Isolated systolic pattern At or above the threshold while Stays below it
Isolated diastolic pattern Stays below the threshold while At or above it

2.1 The and/or column is what flips the verdict

The column people trip over is the third one. Normal and elevated are joined by and, while stage 1 and stage 2 are joined by or.

  • Systolic 115, diastolic 78 - both conditions hold, so this is normal.
  • Systolic 115, diastolic 84 - the systolic number is fine, but 84 falls in the 80 to 89 band, so this is stage 1 in the US and pre-hypertension in Korea. Reading only the first number would have called it normal.
  • Systolic 125, diastolic 76 - elevated, and not yet stage 1.
  • Systolic 145, diastolic 82 - systolic has crossed 140, so stage 2, and with diastolic under 90 the shape of it is isolated systolic hypertension.
  • Systolic 132, diastolic 94 - diastolic has crossed 90, which is stage 2 under US rules and stage 1 hypertension under Korean rules, in the isolated diastolic shape.

In short, the worse of the two numbers sets the band. If either value reaches a higher row, the whole reading moves up to it.

2.2 Isolated diastolic hypertension became a named category in 2026

One notable change in the 2026 Korean Society of Hypertension guideline is that isolated diastolic hypertension now stands as its own category: systolic below 140 with diastolic at 90 or above.

  • It is seen relatively more often in younger adults.
  • Because the systolic figure sits inside the normal range, it is easy to skim past on a check-up printout.
  • The same guideline kept the diagnostic line itself at 140/90. Korea did not follow the United States down to 130/80.
  • Treatment targets are split by risk. For high-risk patients with diabetes, chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disease or prior stroke, the target is below 130/80; for low and moderate risk patients and older adults with hypertension, it is below 140/90.
  • Treatment targets apply to people already under treatment. They are not the same thing as diagnostic thresholds, and confusing the two changes the meaning of a reading entirely.

3. Why the same 130/85 is hypertension in one country and not in another

Blood pressure thresholds differ by country, and the difference is not a rounding detail. It is large enough to change the verdict outright. Placed side by side as of August 2026, the major guidelines look like this.

Guideline Hypertension threshold (office) Bands below it Where 130/85 lands
United States (AHA/ACC 2025) 130/80 or above Normal under 120/80, elevated 120-129 with diastolic under 80 Stage 1 hypertension
Korea (Korean Society of Hypertension) 140/90 or above Attention 120-129 with diastolic under 80, pre-hypertension 130-139/80-89 Pre-hypertension
Europe (ESH 2023) 140/90 or above Normal 120-129/80-84, high-normal 130-139/85-89 High-normal blood pressure
Japan (JSH2025) 140/90 or above High-normal 120-129 with diastolic under 80, elevated 130-139/80-89 Elevated blood pressure
China (2024 revision) 140/90 or above High-normal 120-139/80-89 High-normal blood pressure

3.1 Why the United States moved the line down

The American Heart Association and the American College of Cardiology lowered the diagnostic line from 140/90 to 130/80 in 2017, and the 2025 revision kept 130/80 in place. The reasoning runs along two tracks.

  • Evidence accumulated that cardiovascular and stroke risk is already rising through the 130 to 139 over 80 to 89 band, rather than switching on at 140.
  • A lower line starts risk assessment and lifestyle intervention earlier. Lowering the diagnostic threshold is not an instruction to medicate everyone inside it, and the 2025 guideline directs clinicians to use a risk-estimation equation when deciding on drug therapy.
  • The arithmetic consequence was a large jump in the share of US adults classified as hypertensive. The same person can acquire or shed the label simply by crossing a border.

3.2 Why Korea, Europe, Japan and China held at 140/90

The other guidelines declined to move. Their logic is equally explicit.

  • Lowering the line sharply increases the population carrying a diagnosis. The judgement is that the medical resource burden and treatment side effects may outweigh the incremental risk reduction.
  • Instead, that band is named separately - pre-hypertension, high-normal, elevated - and flagged for lifestyle intervention. The message is not that it can be ignored, but that it is managed without a formal diagnosis attached.
  • The Chinese guideline retains a grade 3 hypertension band at 180/110 and above, citing the fact that more than 8 percent of hypertensive patients in China fall into it. A threshold table reflects the patient distribution of the country that wrote it.
  • Japan took a hybrid route in JSH2025: the diagnostic line stays at 140/90, while the treatment target was unified at below 130/80 across all ages. Diagnosis and target are handled as two separate questions.

3.3 Where app and smartwatch verdicts stop being reliable

Consumer devices generally colour their readings against the American table, which is why 130/82 can trigger a red Stage 1 Hypertension banner.

  • That label is correct within the American framework, but it may differ from the verdict a clinician outside the United States would record.
  • The reverse mistake is just as easy: reading the same value as merely pre-hypertension and treating it as nothing. Every guideline flags that band for intervention.
  • The practical rule is short. Check which country wrote the table the colour is based on, and apply the local table where you are treated.
  • What travels well is not the label but the raw numbers and the conditions they were taken under. Record the values and the time, and any table can be applied to them later.

4. Why the number at home differs from the number at the clinic

Home readings coming out lower than clinic readings is extremely common. The gap is not error; it demonstrates that a different measurement environment needs a different threshold. That is precisely why guidelines publish separate cut-offs.

Measurement setting Threshold, US (AHA/ACC) Threshold, Korea and Europe What it captures and what to watch
Office reading 130/80 or above 140/90 or above A standardised snapshot; the diagnostic tables were built on it
Home reading Follows the office line down to 130/80 135/85 or above Morning and evening values across several days; never judge it with the office number
Ambulatory, 24-hour mean Lower than the daytime line 130/80 or above A full day including sleep, so the low night values pull the mean down
Ambulatory, daytime mean Aligned with the office line 135/85 or above Waking hours only; the same line as home monitoring
Ambulatory, night-time mean Lowest of the set 120/70 or above During sleep; failure to fall at night is a separate warning sign

4.1 White coat hypertension - high only in the clinic

White coat hypertension means office readings cross the hypertension threshold while home or ambulatory readings stay in range. The medical setting itself triggers a sympathetic response.

  • Reported frequency varies widely with study design. Around 20 percent has been reported among people with raised office readings, and close to half in some analyses restricted to the stage 1 range.
  • It does not mean risk is zero. A proportion of these patients progress to sustained hypertension over time, so they stay under follow-up.
  • Confirming it requires measurement outside the clinic. Repeating the reading in the same clinic room does not separate the two.

4.2 Masked hypertension - normal only in the clinic

The mirror image is masked hypertension: office readings look fine while readings at home or at work cross the threshold. The name is literal, since the clinic conceals it.

  • Figures around 10 percent of the general population have been reported. That is not a rare phenomenon.
  • It is the more dangerous direction in practice, because routine visits never surface it and untreated hypertension simply proceeds.
  • It is described as more common with smoking, alcohol use, workplace stress, night shifts and sleep apnoea.
  • If office readings are normal but home readings repeatedly exceed 135/85, that log is itself clinically meaningful material.

4.3 Where the 135/85 home threshold comes from

The 135/85 figure was not chosen arbitrarily. It was derived as the home equivalent of an office reading of 140/90 - the value that expresses the same level of risk in a home setting.

  • So a home reading of 138/88 is under 140 on the office table, yet already inside the hypertensive range on the home table.
  • Japan's JSH2025 goes a step further and pairs its targets explicitly: below 130/80 in the office and below 125/75 at home.
  • In the United States the office line is itself 130/80, so the home line moves down with it. Think of the whole set of thresholds as shifted one notch lower.
  • The rule underneath all of it: the setting a reading came from and the threshold applied to it must always be matched as a pair.

5. Technique alone can change the verdict - how to take a reading

Blood pressure is unusually sensitive to measurement conditions. A single postural error pushing systolic up by 10 to 20 mmHg is routine, and that is enough to move a reading a whole band.

5.1 The thirty minutes before

  • Avoid caffeine, smoking and exercise for 30 minutes beforehand. A coffee and a cigarette each push the number up on their own.
  • Empty the bladder first. A full bladder raises the reading.
  • Sit quietly and rest for at least five minutes immediately before measuring. Measuring the moment you sit down reads high.
  • During that rest, do not scroll a phone or hold a conversation. Talking alone lifts the value.
  • Cold exposure, a recent meal or ongoing pain all produce readings unlike your usual ones. Note the circumstance in the log so it can be interpreted later.

5.2 Posture and arm position

  • Sit in a chair with your back supported. Sitting unsupported tends to raise the diastolic value.
  • Keep both feet flat on the floor. Crossed legs raise the systolic value.
  • Rest the arm on a desk or support so the cuff sits at heart level. An arm below heart level reads high; above it reads low.
  • Do not tense the arm. Holding it unsupported in the air adds muscular contraction and lifts the reading.
  • Do not talk and do not move during the measurement itself.
  • On the first occasion, measure both arms, then use the arm with the higher reading consistently from then on.

5.3 Cuff size - the most common source of error

When home readings look persistently strange, the cuff is often the culprit. A cuff is not a one-size accessory.

  • The bladder inside the cuff should cover roughly 80 percent of arm circumference in length and about 40 percent in width.
  • A cuff that is too small for the arm reads higher than the true value. This is the situation for people with larger arms using the standard cuff supplied in the box.
  • A cuff that is too large reads lower than the true value.
  • Wrap the cuff on a bare arm or over thin clothing. Rolling a thick sleeve up so it grips the upper arm distorts the reading by itself.
  • The lower edge of the cuff should sit 2 to 3 cm above the crease of the elbow, snug enough to admit one finger underneath.
  • Wrist monitors are more sensitive to position and angle. If you use one, the wrist has to be held at heart level, and an upper-arm device is easier to keep consistent for record keeping.

5.4 How many readings, how far apart

  • Take two or more readings at least one minute apart and record the average. A single reading is not used as a basis for judgement.
  • If the two differ substantially, take a third and average them.
  • Home monitoring is built on morning and evening sessions. Measure in the morning within an hour of waking, after emptying the bladder, before the morning medication and before breakfast.
  • Measure in the evening before going to bed, in a settled state.
  • For a record intended to inform diagnosis or assess treatment, collect at least five to seven days. One day of readings is not decision-grade material.
  • First-day readings tend to run high, and some protocols exclude them from the average. Confirm how yours should be handled at your appointment.
One rule for choosing a monitor

Start with an upper-arm device that has passed a recognised validation protocol. Measure your arm circumference and check that the supplied cuff actually fits; larger arms need a large cuff bought separately. Taking your own monitor to an appointment and measuring alongside the clinic device shows how far the two drift apart.

6. Readings that differ every time are not a malfunction

Almost everyone who starts home monitoring is unsettled by the same thing: two readings five minutes apart differing by 15 points. That is not a device fault; it is the nature of the measurement.

  • Blood pressure changes with every heartbeat. Breathing, posture shifts, conversation and even mental effort register immediately.
  • It also cycles through the day: lowest in the small hours, rising quickly around waking, fluctuating with daytime activity, and falling again during sleep.
  • Season contributes too. Peripheral vessels constrict in cold weather, which tends to raise readings in winter.
  • The unit of judgement is therefore an average across several days, not a single value. Taking comfort from one good reading and taking fright at one high reading are the same mistake.
  • That said, a persistent difference between arms, or repeated readings under identical conditions still differing by more than about 20 points, is worth reporting at your next visit.

7. Making a home log actually useful

Home monitoring is not about collecting numbers; it is about producing material a clinician can use. How you record determines whether it works.

  • Record five things: date, time, systolic, diastolic and pulse. Leaving them in device memory hides the trend; writing them out reveals it.
  • Hold the conditions constant. Same time of day, same arm, same posture, same device, or the values cannot be compared to each other.
  • Do not delete the high readings. A curated log distorts the decision it is meant to inform.
  • If you take medication, measure before the dose as the default. A reading taken right after the morning tablet has the drug effect folded into it.
  • Note unusual circumstances briefly. A cold, poor sleep, heavy drinking, a painkiller or a stressful schedule explain outliers that would otherwise look inexplicable.
  • When you have an appointment, bring the last one or two weeks of raw readings. The original numbers are far more useful than a summary such as mostly around 130.
  • If measuring more often starts feeding anxiety or compulsive checking, treat that as something to raise at the visit. Repeating measurements many times a day rarely helps.

8. Pulse pressure, morning readings and night-time readings

There are more than two numbers in the data. Several further indicators come out of the same measurements, and they are interpretive aids rather than diagnostic criteria.

8.1 Pulse pressure - the gap between the two numbers

Pulse pressure is systolic minus diastolic. A reading of 140/70 gives a pulse pressure of 70.

  • It largely reflects large-artery elasticity. As arteries stiffen, systolic rises while diastolic falls, and the gap widens.
  • A gap of 60 mmHg or more is generally treated as a marker suggesting arterial stiffness. It is a reference indicator, not a diagnostic threshold.
  • This explains why high systolic with low diastolic is common in older adults; pulse pressure widens as a natural consequence.

8.2 Morning readings - the surge around waking

Blood pressure climbs rapidly around the time of waking, and an exaggerated version of that rise is examined separately.

  • Home morning readings that repeatedly reach 135/85 or above are handled as morning hypertension.
  • A pattern confined to the morning cannot be seen from evening readings alone. That is why both morning and evening sessions exist.
  • It can also appear when the duration of action of a medication does not reach into the following morning. Assessing and adjusting that is entirely a clinical decision, and changing your own dosing time is unsafe.

8.3 Night-time readings - does pressure fall during sleep

Normally blood pressure drops during sleep relative to daytime values. This is one of the things a 24-hour ambulatory study looks at.

  • The usual pattern is a night-time mean roughly 10 to 20 percent below the daytime mean.
  • Failing to fall adequately at night, or rising instead, is treated as a distinct warning sign.
  • A home monitor cannot really assess this. It requires 24-hour ambulatory monitoring, and whether to order it is decided in consultation.
  • Sleep apnoea can alter the night-time pattern, so heavy snoring or marked daytime sleepiness is worth raising at the same visit.

9. When to be seen

Reading the numbers exists to support this decision. It splits in two directions.

9.1 Signs that warrant booking an appointment

  • Home averages repeatedly reaching 135/85 or above.
  • An office reading confirmed at 140/90 or above, or at 130/80 or above where the American table applies. One reading does not make a diagnosis, but it is grounds to start the confirmation process.
  • Normal systolic values with diastolic repeatedly at 90 or above.
  • Readings inside the normal range but alongside diabetes, kidney disease, abnormal lipids, smoking or a family history of early cardiovascular disease. The same number carries a different risk calculation.
  • Already on treatment but not reaching the target range, or developing symptoms such as dizziness or light-headedness on standing.
  • Pregnancy or planning a pregnancy. Blood pressure in pregnancy has its own thresholds and management.
  • A persistent large difference between the two arms.

9.2 Emergency department or emergency services, without delay

Situations that need immediate help

A systolic reading of 180 or above or a diastolic reading of 120 or above, accompanied by any of the following, means you should call emergency services or go to an emergency department rather than spending time repeating the measurement.

  • A severe headache unlike your usual ones
  • Chest pain or pressure, or shortness of breath
  • Blurred vision or sudden loss of vision
  • Slurred speech, weakness in one arm or leg, or drooping on one side of the face
  • Severe vomiting, seizures, or any change in alertness
  • In pregnancy, a rise in blood pressure with headache, upper abdominal pain or swelling

A high number with no symptoms is not automatically safe either. Rest for at least five minutes, measure again, and if the level holds, arrange to be seen the same day. Do not add or increase your own medication in the meantime. Dropping blood pressure too quickly can itself cause harm, which is why dose decisions belong to a clinician.

10. What this article could not confirm

In any article about thresholds, the real hazard is an unverified number sitting among verified ones. The following could not be pinned down, and so were not stated as figures above.

  • Exact prevalence of white coat and masked hypertension is not a single number fixed by any society guideline. The ranges given above come from individual studies and shift substantially with the population and measurement method used.
  • The full US correspondence table for home and ambulatory thresholds tracks the 130/80 office line downward, but the individual cells were not verified against the primary document, so the comparison table describes them in relative terms rather than as figures.
  • Japan's home blood pressure sub-bands in JSH2025, meaning the home ranges corresponding to grade I, II and III, could not be confirmed. Only the 135/85 diagnostic line and the 125/75 target are stated.
  • The 60 mmHg pulse pressure marker is widely referenced clinically, but whether any national guideline names it as a formal diagnostic criterion was not confirmed. It is presented only as a reference indicator.
  • Paediatric and adolescent thresholds are defined by percentiles for age, sex and height, and the adult table here does not transfer to them. That is a separate subject.
  • Thresholds get revised. Everything above is current as of August 2026 and will change as societies update their guidelines.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. What exactly counts as a normal blood pressure reading?

Under the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline, normal means a systolic reading below 120 and a diastolic reading below 80, measured in a clinical setting. Both conditions have to hold at once. Between 120 and 129 systolic with diastolic still under 80 is called elevated blood pressure, 130 to 139 systolic or 80 to 89 diastolic is stage 1 hypertension, and 140 or above or 90 or above is stage 2. Korea, Europe, Japan and China keep the diagnostic line at 140/90 and label the 130 to 139 band pre-hypertension or high-normal instead. Two further points matter. That table is written for office readings, and home readings are judged against a different line. And being inside the normal band does not mean cardiovascular risk is absent, because age, smoking, diabetes, lipids and family history all enter the calculation. Bring the numbers to a clinician rather than deciding on your own.

Q2. My home readings are lower than the ones at the clinic. Which should I trust?

Neither gets discarded. The clinic is the environment the diagnostic tables were built around, while home monitoring captures many readings closer to what your pressure does across an ordinary day. The gap between them is itself information. High only at the clinic and normal at home is white coat hypertension. Normal at the clinic but high at home or at work is masked hypertension, and that direction is easier to miss because routine visits never reveal it. This is why guidelines ask for out-of-office confirmation rather than a single office value. One caution: the home threshold sits lower. Internationally it is 135/85, and in the United States, where the office line is 130/80, the home line follows it down. Applying the office number 140 to a home reading will misclassify it. Take the raw log to your clinician and let the interpretation happen there.

Q3. My readings swing by 10 to 20 points every time. Is the monitor broken?

Usually not. Blood pressure changes with every heartbeat, with breathing, and with every shift in posture. It dips overnight, climbs sharply around waking, and moves through the day with activity. Technique stacks on top of that. Sitting without back support, feet dangling, an arm below heart level, or talking during the measurement all push the number up. A cuff that is too small for the arm reads high, and one that is too large reads low. That is why a single reading is never the unit of judgement: take two or more readings at least a minute apart and average them. Once the conditions are held constant, the spread shrinks noticeably. If a large difference between arms persists, or if repeated readings under identical conditions still differ by more than about 20 points, write it down and show the log at your next visit.

Q4. My systolic number is fine but the diastolic is above 90. Does that matter?

Classification follows the worse of the two numbers. A systolic reading under 130 does not rescue a diastolic reading of 90 or more, which lands in the hypertensive range in every major guideline. The Korean Society of Hypertension made this explicit in its 2026 guideline by naming isolated diastolic hypertension as its own category, meaning systolic below 140 with diastolic at 90 or above. It shows up more often in younger adults, and because the systolic number looks reassuring it is easy to skim past on a check-up printout. The opposite pattern appears with age: as the large arteries stiffen, systolic pressure rises while diastolic pressure falls, which is isolated systolic hypertension. Either way, do not settle it from one reading. Collect several days of home readings and take them to a clinician.

Q5. My reading was over 180. Should I go to the emergency room right now?

The deciding factor is whether symptoms are present, not the number alone. A systolic reading of 180 or above, or a diastolic reading of 120 or above, is treated as requiring immediate attention in every guideline. If it comes with a severe or unusual headache, chest pain, shortness of breath, vision changes, slurred speech, weakness on one side of the body, severe vomiting or any change in alertness, do not spend time repeating the measurement. Call emergency services or go to an emergency department. With no symptoms at all, sit quietly for about five minutes, measure again, and if the level holds, arrange to be seen the same day. What you must not do is add or increase your own medication. Driving pressure down too quickly can itself cause harm, so any change in dosing belongs to the clinician who prescribed it.

A normal range is not a safety certificate

Every figure in this article is a classification threshold derived from population data. A reading below the line does not by itself establish that cardiovascular risk is low. Individual risk emerges only when age, sex, smoking, glucose, lipids, kidney function and family history are calculated together. The converse also holds: one high reading is not a diagnosis of hypertension. A number is grounds to start checking, not a conclusion.

Conclusion

The two numbers on the display measure different events. The first is the peak pressure on the arterial wall as the heart pushes; the second is the pressure that remains while the heart is resting. Classification follows the worse of the two, and that is exactly what the habit of reading only the first number misses.

It also matters that there is more than one table. As of August 2026, Korea, Europe, Japan and China treat 140/90 and above as hypertension in the office, while the United States uses 130/80. The same 130/85 is stage 1 hypertension in America and pre-hypertension in Korea. That is why an app warning and a clinic verdict can disagree without either being wrong; they are reading different tables.

The environment-dependent thresholds are the more practical issue. The office line is 140/90, home is 135/85, and the 24-hour ambulatory mean is 130/80. Looking at a home reading of 138/88 and concluding that it did not reach 140 applies the wrong table to it.

So the actual task is simple. Use a validated upper-arm monitor, rest five minutes, sit with your back supported, keep the cuff at heart level, take two readings a minute apart and record the average. Collect five to seven days of morning and evening readings, keep the high ones in, and take the log as it stands to your appointment. What that record means, whether further testing is needed and whether treatment is warranted are questions for a clinician who can see your history and your other results. The practical side of lowering blood pressure is covered separately in how to lower blood pressure naturally. A number is a signpost pointing in a direction, not a diagnosis.

Medical disclaimer: this article is intended as general health information and does not replace medical diagnosis, prescription or treatment. Every figure in it is a classification threshold set by a professional society or public body, or a value used in research, and none of them is a personal target or recommendation. Nothing here recommends starting, stopping, increasing or reducing any medication, and it cannot be used as a basis for adjusting a prescribed dose on your own. What your readings mean, and whether further testing or treatment is needed, should be decided in consultation with a physician or other qualified clinician. If you experience a severe headache, chest pain, shortness of breath, vision changes, slurred speech, weakness on one side of the body or any change in alertness, call emergency services or go to an emergency department immediately. If you are pregnant or planning a pregnancy, are being treated for kidney disease, diabetes or cardiovascular disease, or if the reading belongs to a child, an adolescent or a frail older adult, do not apply the general adult table directly and seek clinical advice first.
