How to Read a Seasonal Outlook - A Winter Forecast Is Not a Forecast of Your Winter

What an El Nino Advisory actually states, the exact meaning of a 40 percent chance, why the 30-year normal keeps moving under your feet, and which decisions an August headline can and cannot settle

Aug 17, 2026 Life & Practical

Introduction: Why winter headlines arrive in mid-August

The heat has not broken yet and your feed is already running winter cards. Record-breaking super El Nino. Coldest winter in decades. Prepare now. Outside it is still well over 30 degrees Celsius and the ad below the card is selling a heated blanket.

These stories almost always trace back to one document: the monthly ENSO diagnostic discussion from NOAA's Climate Prediction Center (CPC). That document does not contain a sentence about your winter. It contains sentences about sea surface temperature in the equatorial Pacific and about the probability that the event reaches a given strength.

What happens in between is the subject of this piece. A sentence written as a probability becomes a claim, and a sentence whose subject was the tropical Pacific acquires a new subject: your region. Two substitutions are enough to turn greater than 90 percent chance of a very strong event into 90 percent chance of a brutal winter here. Completely different statements, but the number survives the trip, which makes the second one look sourced.

What follows is how to read the original in its own grammar. This is not an argument that seasonal outlooks are worthless. Once you know precisely what they claim, a short list of real decisions falls out of them. That list is at the end.

1. What is actually on the record right now

These are the agency statements available as of August 17, 2026, with subjects and dates preserved.

1.1 NOAA Climate Prediction Center

The ENSO Diagnostic Discussion dated August 13, 2026. The ENSO Alert System Status remains El Nino Advisory.

  • July monthly index values: Nino 3.4 at +1.4C, Nino 3 at +1.7C, Nino 1+2 at +2.9C
  • Subsurface anomalies reaching +10.0C at depth, with a deeper-than-average thermocline
  • Stated in the discussion: a greater than 90 percent chance of a very strong event during Northern Hemisphere fall and winter 2026-27
  • Also stated: during October to December 2026, a 69 percent chance of an event exceeding the strength of previous El Nino events dating back to 1950
  • Next diagnostic discussion scheduled for September 10, 2026

Do not let the word advisory mislead you. It shares vocabulary with hurricane and severe weather products but not their function. An El Nino Advisory is a status marker meaning the event is underway. It is not a warning that anything is imminent for any particular place.

1.2 Japan Meteorological Agency

El Nino Monitoring Report No. 407, dated August 10, 2026. JMA states that El Nino conditions have persisted since spring, puts the July NINO.3 monitoring region sea surface temperature anomaly at +2.5C, and gives a 100 percent probability that El Nino continues through winter.

The American 90 percent and the Japanese 100 percent are not in conflict, because they measure different quantities. One is the probability of reaching very strong intensity; the other is the probability of continuation. Asking which agency is right is not a well-formed question.

1.3 China and Korea

China's National Climate Center was reported in early August 2026 as describing continued strengthening through June and July, projecting an eastern-type strong to super-strong event peaking in autumn and winter and decaying through spring and summer 2027. That reached me through press coverage rather than the agency's own bulletin.

The Korea Meteorological Administration has issued no winter outlook at this point, because of how its calendar works.

  • One-month outlook: every Thursday
  • Three-month outlook: around the 23rd of each month
  • Seasonal climate outlook: around the 23rd of February, May, August and November

So a seasonal outlook covering winter is due in late November. Any August article attributing a winter forecast to KMA is either citing something else or has attached the wrong source.

Put the four side by side and one thing stands out: none of these documents contains a national winter temperature outlook. They are all about the state and intensity of the tropical Pacific. Regional temperature belongs to the separate seasonal outlooks each national agency issues on its own schedule.

2. El Nino is not a story about the ocean alone

The full name gives it away. The phenomenon is El Nino Southern Oscillation (ENSO): the first part is the ocean name, the second part is the atmospheric name. They were observed separately before anyone realized they were one coupled system, and the compound name is the fossil of that history. The coupling is the whole thing.

2.1 The Pacific in its usual state

  • Near the equator, trade winds blow from east to west
  • Those winds pile warm surface water toward the western Pacific
  • In the east, cooler water rises to replace what was pushed away, a process called upwelling
  • Deep convection and heavy rainfall concentrate over the warm pool in the west

2.2 How an El Nino latches on

  • When trade winds weaken, warm water that had been pushed west sloshes back east
  • A warmer eastern surface supports convection farther east than usual
  • The shifted convection deforms the Walker circulation and weakens the trades further
  • Weaker trades warm the east more. That feedback is what grows the event
  • Oceanic Kelvin waves carry warm water eastward beneath the surface. The +10.0C subsurface anomaly in the August discussion is describing exactly this

The last point matters most. The ocean changes the atmosphere and the atmosphere changes the ocean back. Because it is a feedback loop rather than one-way causation, an event persists for months once it engages, and that persistence is what creates any seasonal predictability at all. La Nina is the same loop running with the opposite sign.

The loop then exports a signal beyond the Pacific through upper-atmosphere wave trains. That export is called a teleconnection. As the next sections show, saying a teleconnection exists is a long way from saying it decides your winter.

3. What do super and record-breaking actually measure

These are the two most common words in the headlines and the two least verifiable.

3.1 Super is not an official category

CPC uses four strength labels: weak, moderate, strong and very strong. In its probability tables, very strong corresponds to the band at or above 2.0C. There is no super bin. The World Meteorological Organization does not use the term. A climatologist at Australia's Bureau of Meteorology has said flatly that super El Nino is not a scientific term.

It does have an origin. In 2003, CSIRO researchers in Australia used it for events where the Nino 3.4 departure reached at least 3C. Most current media usage applies a 2C threshold instead. The same word is circulating with at least two different cutoffs.

Working rule: when you meet the word super, check whether the index and the threshold it crossed appear in the same paragraph. If they do not, the word is an adjective, not information.

3.2 A record of what, exactly

For the word record to mean anything, three things must be pinned down: which index, over what period of observation, and in which seasonal window. Drop any one and the claim becomes unfalsifiable.

The CPC 69 percent sentence is a useful control. It specifies dating back to 1950 and it specifies October to December. That makes it a statement someone can later score as right or wrong. A sentence that says only record-breaking cannot be wrong regardless of what happens.

The same standard is why several index values quoted in the original blog coverage do not appear in this article. Figures I could not match against an agency bulletin are not reproduced here.

4. A seasonal outlook is not a forecast - the grammar of terciles

This is the core. A statement that it will rain tomorrow and a statement that this winter will be cold are not the same kind of sentence.

4.1 Three boxes

Seasonal outlooks divide the recent 30-year distribution into thirds, cutting at the 33.3rd and 66.7th percentiles to create below normal, near normal and above normal. With no information, each box holds 33.3 percent. CPC marks that baseline state EC, for Equal Chances, and publishes it wherever forecast skill does not beat climatology for that region and season.

4.2 What a 40 percent chance means precisely

A below-normal value of 40 percent means the outlook has tilted 6.7 percentage points off the 33.3 percent baseline. In common CPC practice the near-normal box stays near 33.3 percent and the opposite box is reduced by the same amount the favored box gains, so 40 below implies roughly 33.3 near and 26.7 above.

Which makes all of the following misreadings.

  • Forty percent of the season will be cold - it is not a fraction of time
  • It will be 40 percent colder than usual - it is not an intensity
  • Cold is far more likely than not cold - the other 60 percent is still in near and above
  • There will be a cold snap in mid-January - no date is being named

Agencies publish this structure differently. Korea's KMA issues the same three-box logic as three numbers rounded to multiples of ten, as in the precipitation line of its winter 2025 outlook, which read 40 for below, 40 for near and 20 for above in December. If you quote only the first number, half the information is gone. Check the convention your own agency uses before comparing figures across borders.

4.3 It forecasts the mean, not the extremes

This is where the mismatch bites in daily life. A seasonal outlook is about a three-month average. Winters whose average lands above normal still produce cold snaps, because a few sharp days barely move a three-month mean.

Skipping pipe insulation because the outlook leans warm is a misuse of the product. Reading a cold-leaning outlook as a promise of snow in a specific week is the same error with the sign flipped.

5. Normal is a moving target, not a fixed value

In the phrase above normal, the word people question least is normal. That value changes every ten years.

  • Climate normals are 30-year averages beginning in a year ending in 1 and ending in a year ending in 0
  • The current baseline for NOAA seasonal outlook climatology is 1991 to 2020
  • Korea's KMA adopted the same 1991 to 2020 baseline in 2021
  • The prior baseline was 1981 to 2010

Two consequences follow. First, an identical temperature can be classified differently on either side of a baseline update, because the dividing lines moved. Second, normal in an old article is a different yardstick than normal today, so a departure quoted in 2018 should not be lined up against one quoted now.

5.1 When the baseline falls behind: RONI

This became an operational problem, not just a pedantic one. In February 2026 CPC changed its primary ENSO monitoring index from the Oceanic Nino Index to the Relative Oceanic Nino Index (RONI). The stated reason is precise: a departure from a 30-year average struggles to keep pace with anomalous changes in tropical sea surface temperature itself, and that is worst in real time when the climatology lags.

RONI subtracts the tropics-wide anomaly, averaged across 20 degrees south to 20 degrees north, from the Nino 3.4 anomaly. It asks how much warmer that Pacific box is relative to the whole tropical belt, which reduces dependence on the base period. One implication is worth carrying: comparing the ranking of a current event against historical events is now a more delicate operation than it looks.

6. Why one region's winter is not decided by El Nino alone

Teleconnections are statistical tendencies. A tendency existing and a tendency determining the outcome are different claims, and the gap is measurable.

Korea makes an unusually clean case study because the relationship there is weak. Research from Ewha Womans University, reported in November 2025, examined 1920 through 2023 and found a correlation of 0.28 between the El Nino index and the Korean winter temperature anomaly. Statistically significant, but limited: a correlation of 0.28 accounts for roughly 8 percent of the variance. The other 92 percent comes from elsewhere.

In the same work, only 34 percent of El Nino and La Nina years produced a winter clearly above or below normal. Two thirds of event years had a winter that landed near normal anyway.

KMA's climate information portal makes the same point in its own words: each El Nino and La Nina has its own characteristics, so their impacts appear in varied forms, which is why a single blanket application of the signal is difficult.

6.1 Every event has a different shape

This is where China's National Climate Center specifying an eastern-type event carries information. Whether the warm anomaly is centered in the eastern or the central Pacific changes where convection sets up, which changes the path of the wave train leaving the tropics. The teleconnection pattern is therefore not a fixed stencil applied to every event.

6.2 The factors with short lead times

The high-correlation factors in that table share a property: they are not knowable in August.

  • Arctic Oscillation phase flips on timescales of weeks
  • Siberian High strength depends on conditions including autumn snow cover
  • Sudden stratospheric warming events resist being pinned to a date a season ahead
  • Jet stream meander patterns update on something close to real time

So the drivers that move winter temperature the most are not observable in summer. August winter stories fixate on El Nino because it is the only piece visible that early, not because it is the most important one.

8. The three points where probability becomes assertion

Knowing where information leaks in transmission makes reading much faster. There are three, in order.

8.1 The hedge disappears in the headline

The body says likely or projected; the headline does not. Character limits are often the honest reason, but the effect is the same, because most readers only ever see the headline.

8.2 The subject gets swapped

This is the decisive one. The original subject is the equatorial Pacific; the article's subject becomes your country. The probability figure is carried over intact, so the claim looks sourced. Asking this 90 percent is a probability of what, exactly catches it every time.

8.3 The tense shifts

Projected to peak in October to December becomes already the strongest on record. A projection is promoted to an observation. If the projected peak is months away, the value is not there yet.

Four-line check on any seasonal story: is the issuing agency named, is the issue date given, is the probability quoted in its original form, and what geographic area does that probability describe. If two or more are missing, the article is unlikely to contain new information.

Watch the citation chain too. An agency bulletin cited by an international outlet cited by a local outlet cited by a forum post is three steps from the source, and each step adds an adjective. Opening the original bulletin usually takes about as long as reading the summary of it.

9. What a probabilistic outlook can and cannot settle

That was reading. This is use. The conclusion that seasonal outlooks are useless is wrong; the class of decision they can settle is simply bounded.

The test is a single question: can the decision be reversed, and if you are wrong, which direction costs more? Reversible decisions with low preparation cost can be made on probabilities. Irreversible decisions tied to a specific date cannot.

What is genuinely worth doing in August is the preparation you will not regret whichever way the outlook moves.

  • Book the annual boiler or furnace service before the rush
  • Inspect pipe insulation, since one cold night is enough in an otherwise mild winter
  • Check window and door gaps, which pay off in cooling efficiency right now too
  • Verify the safety condition of heating appliances, including cable insulation and thermal cutoffs
  • Budget winter costs as a range rather than a single figure

Cutting heating cost is a different axis and is not covered here. Indoor temperature settings, insulation and tariff options are in how to cut winter heating costs, and appliance selection criteria are in choosing a fan heater or electric heater.

11. Conclusion: August settles very little

To summarize: what is established is that a strong El Nino is underway in the tropical Pacific and that agencies place high probability on it reaching very strong intensity in fall and winter. Multiple independent bulletins point the same direction on that.

What is not established is which way that pushes temperature in any given region this winter. In the long record for Korea the correlation was 0.28 and two thirds of event years landed near normal anyway. The factors that move winter temperature more are not observable right now.

So an August headline settles very little. Three things survive it. First, the habit of reading probability statements in their original form, which means looking at all three boxes and comparing against the 33.3 percent baseline. Second, the habit of distrusting the word normal, a value refreshed every decade in a monitoring system that just changed its primary index. Third, doing only the preparation in August that holds up either way.

If you want a serious look at winter, wait for your own agency's late-autumn seasonal outlook. By then the lead time is shorter and the factors that actually drive winter, from the Arctic Oscillation to the continental high, are starting to come into view. An August winter story was written at a moment when none of that could be seen.
