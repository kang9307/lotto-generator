Revised Rules of Engineering Leadership - Rewriting Principles for the AI Era

Delegation, trust, and psychological safety haven't changed - but how leaders put them into practice has changed completely

Jul 2, 2026 Trends & Culture

Introduction: The Rules Haven't Changed, So Why Rewrite Them

A post recently made the rounds on GeekNews - a veteran engineering leader reflecting on how their leadership principles evolved over time (GeekNews discussion; the original is Will Larson's Revised rules of engineering leadership). What makes it interesting is that none of the underlying principles - delegate, build trust, create psychological safety - actually changed. What changed is how those principles get executed.

Rather than restating the original rules, this piece reconstructs those insights from my own vantage point, grounded in how these principles need to be rewritten now that AI coding tools have genuinely embedded themselves in day-to-day team operations, as of July 2026. Where the original centers on personal experience with migrations, development harnesses, and decision speed, this piece reorganizes that thinking around the practical axes of leadership: delegation and trust, psychological safety, technical debt and decision-making, and the trio of code review, junior mentorship, and productivity measurement.

1. Delegation and Trust - The Object Shifted from "People" to "People Plus Agents"

Traditional delegation was simple. A leader hands a task to a team member and trusts their competence and judgment, usually built on a track record.

1.1 A Double-Delegation Structure

Once AI coding agents entered daily workflows, delegation gained an extra layer. Team members now delegate portions of their work to AI agents, and leaders must decide whether to trust the result. The problem is that leaders now need to evaluate trust on two levels at once: the team member's competence, and the reliability of the automation workflow that person built. A leader who misses this structure keeps evaluating only the person, while the real risk sits in the automated pipeline they designed.

1.2 The Unit of Trust Has Gotten Smaller

In my observation, trust in the AI era needs to be broken down from a broad "I trust this person" into a much narrower "should this person be allowed to automate this specific class of work to this degree." As the original piece notes, even complex migrations can now be substantially completed by an individual - but that same leverage means expanding delegation scope without incremental verification also expands the blast radius of failure. Leaders should keep a staged approach: build verified trust at a small scope before expanding it.

2. Psychological Safety - The Faster Things Move, the More the Brakes Matter

Psychological safety is an environment where team members believe they can admit mistakes, raise disagreement, or ask questions without penalty. Research consistently links a lack of trust in leadership to burnout, underscoring how tightly trust and psychological safety are intertwined.

2.1 Can Your Team Challenge AI Output the Same Way They'd Challenge a Colleague's Code?

AI coding tools can produce code that looks plausible but is wrong, and they do it fast. The real question is whether your team can challenge AI-generated code with the same rigor they'd apply to human-written code. If an implicit assumption creeps in - "the AI wrote it, so it's probably fine" - psychological safety actually erodes in the face of AI output. A leader's job is to explicitly sanction skepticism and re-verification of AI output as a normal, expected part of the job.

2.2 A Culture That Surfaces Failure Fast

The faster an organization moves, the more expensive it becomes to hide problems. Because AI agents now handle a volume of changes that used to take a person far longer, a defect caught late likely means far more code has already been contaminated by the time it's found. In this environment, psychological safety isn't just a culture slogan - it functions as a real safeguard that surfaces defects early enough to stop them from spreading.

3. Technical Debt and Decision-Making - The Bottleneck Moved From Writing Speed to Comprehension Speed

AI tools have dramatically increased the speed of writing code. But multiple studies point to a consistent side effect: technical debt rising roughly 30-40% within a year of AI tool adoption.

3.1 The Bottleneck Shifted From "How Fast You Write" to "How Fast You Understand"

Old-style technical debt was usually a deliberate shortcut taken under time pressure. Today's version has a different character - it arises when code an AI generated in seconds gets merged before the human who has to review and maintain it truly understands it. Some discussions call this "cognitive debt": the gradual loss of a team's grasp on why the code is structured the way it is. I consider this more dangerous than traditional debt, because traditional debt at least starts from a known "who did this and why" - cognitive debt is fuzzy from the very starting point.

3.2 Decisions Need to Be Both Faster and More Durable

One point the original piece emphasizes is that fast, durable decision-making is a prerequisite for meaningfully benefiting from AI. As execution speed accelerates, a slow decision process becomes the new bottleneck instead of execution itself. In practice, I've seen two responses work: first, concentrate decision authority in one person or a small group to reduce consensus-seeking delays; second, separate reversible decisions from irreversible ones, prioritizing speed for the former and reserving careful review for the latter.

3.3 Shift the Prioritization Metric From Output to Robustness

The question a leader should ask about technical debt isn't "how fast did we build this," but "how quickly could someone new understand this codebase" and "how well does our development harness - tests, CI - actually guarantee this change is correct." Using generation speed itself as the prioritization criterion is a fast track to losing control in the AI era.

4. Code Review, Junior Mentorship, and Productivity Measurement - Three Axes That Changed

The most tangible day-to-day shifts in the AI coding era show up across three axes: code review, junior mentorship, and productivity measurement.

4.1 Code Review - The First Filter Moved From Human to Machine

Human review used to be the first and only filter. Now, a well-built development harness - automated tests, linting, static analysis, AI review assistants - catches most obvious errors before, and faster than, a human ever could. That doesn't make human review obsolete; it shifts what human reviewers are for. Instead of catching typos and style violations, reviewers now judge whether code fits the system's broader context and design intent. Multiple practitioner reports consistently note that AI-generated code can be syntactically correct and pass tests while violating codebase conventions or hiding subtle logical errors. Review checklists need to be redesigned around exactly that risk.

4.2 Junior Mentorship - The Old Learning Path Through Repetition Has Broken

Junior developers traditionally built pattern recognition and design judgment by writing repetitive implementation work - CRUD operations, standard API integrations, boilerplate. AI tools have accelerated exactly that category of work the most, creating a paradox: the hands-on experience juniors used to learn from is shrinking. Left unaddressed, I believe this risks producing a generation of engineers who can't make design judgments without AI. The practical fix is clear: don't let juniors simply accept AI-generated code as-is. Deliberately assign them the role of reviewing and critiquing that output, and require them to explain the design rationale. Shift mentorship from training implementation to training judgment.

4.3 Productivity Measurement - Output Metrics Are Especially Dangerous Now

Commit counts, lines of code, and PR counts were already poor proxies for productivity, and AI tools make the distortion worse - these metrics can be inflated almost instantly. My recommendation is to pair output with reliability of outcomes: post-deploy rollback frequency, incident rate, the volume of comments that bounce back during review, and the decision speed and quality the original piece emphasizes. Measuring speed alone pushes a team to optimize for speed alone, while technical and cognitive debt quietly accumulate underneath.

5. Traps Good Leaders Should Avoid

Watching this shift unfold, I've identified three traps leaders should be especially wary of.

5.1 Mistaking Delegation for Abdication

Once AI agents start handling a large share of the work, some leaders assume the oversight responsibility that came with delegation has automatically transferred too. It hasn't. Delegation still presumes the leader remains accountable for the outcome - whether an agent or a human wrote the code, final quality responsibility sits with whoever approved it.

5.2 Mistaking Speed for Performance

More code shipping faster because of AI doesn't automatically mean the team is performing better. As the original piece notes, actually benefiting from AI requires decisions that are both fast and durable. Weak decisions just mean speed carries the team in the wrong direction faster.

5.3 Underestimating Team Continuity

Because AI tools boost individual productivity so much, some organizations assume they can reshuffle teams more freely. But domain context isn't something AI builds for you. I've repeatedly seen long-tenured teams absorb new AI techniques faster precisely because of the tacit domain knowledge they've accumulated together. Team continuity remains a scarce asset in the AI era - if anything, it's scarcer because of it.

Conclusion: The Principles Stay the Same, the Execution Has to Be Rewritten

As of July 2026, the skeleton of good engineering leadership - delegate, build trust, create psychological safety, manage technical debt, and make fast, durable decisions - hasn't changed from the pre-AI era. What changed is the execution detail that fills in that skeleton. Delegation now requires trusting a double structure of people and agents; psychological safety requires a culture that challenges AI output with the same rigor as human output; technical debt needs to be managed by comprehensibility rather than generation speed. Code review, junior mentorship, and productivity measurement all need to shift from output-centric to judgment-and-reliability-centric.

Ultimately, good leaders don't discard principles just because the tools changed. They keep rewriting the execution so those principles still hold on top of the new tools. Auditing your own organization against the five axes covered here - delegation and trust, psychological safety, technical debt and decisions, the three operational axes, and the common traps - is the most concrete first step to take this week.
