How a Fund Makes Money Decides What a Founder Is Offered - Reading Management Fees and Carry in the Actual Clauses

A management fee accrues regardless of outcome; carry only begins above a hurdle. Where that difference shows up on a term sheet

2026-09-05 Finance & Economy

A founder holding a first term sheet usually looks at the valuation first. What are we worth, and how much of the company goes out the door. Yet that number was not set by the company alone. How the fund carrying the money makes its own money had already settled about half of it.

In early September 2026 the American technology writer Anil Dash published an essay on how the character of venture capital has changed, and it circulated through Korean developer communities as well. The argument is forceful: a handful of very large investment firms, he says, can no longer properly be called venture capital at all. Whether or not one accepts that conclusion, there is a structure inside it that can be checked against actual written clauses. A fund earns from two separate streams, and those two streams push a person in different directions.

This article is written for general information and does not recommend investment in any particular product or company. The calculations below are simplified hypothetical illustrations meant to show a structure, not real contract terms. Interpretation of any individual agreement belongs with a lawyer, an accountant or the relevant authority.

The occasion - what the essay argues, and what is not in it

The original is titled 「Cancer Capital」, and it reached Korean readers under a headline meaning 「VC is no longer VC」. The author argues that as regulation loosened, a small number of venture firms grew into do-everything funds, and that in the process they stopped legally being venture capital firms at all.

At the centre of the argument sits a single line of arithmetic. When you collect 2 percent a year to manage 50 billion dollars, a billion dollars lands in your pocket annually whether or not any company you funded lives or dies. To this the author attaches the question of political money. He writes that one large firm contributed 115.3 million dollars in the 2026 midterm cycle, nearly double its 63 million dollars in 2024, and that this accounted for more than 20 percent of political giving from the cohort of crypto and artificial intelligence companies being tracked. The conclusion is an inversion of power. Where founders once built companies and investors went looking for opportunities, now, he argues, investment firms publish manifestos and founders are the people selected to carry parts of them out.

One thing should be stated plainly about the nature of this text. It is an essay. It is an argument, not an empirical study. Its sentences therefore travel as claims, not as facts. What is absent from the original is set out separately further down.

Strip away the political discussion, though, and the line of arithmetic remains. The management fee accrues regardless of the outcome. That single line is what this piece takes from the essay, and it is enough on its own to explain why the terms a founder receives change.

A fund earns from two separate streams

A venture fund is not a company but a partnership. The side putting money in and the side deploying it are separated. Article 50 paragraph 3 of the Korean Venture Investment Promotion Act sets out the composition: one or more general partners who execute the business of the partnership and bear unlimited liability for its debts, and limited partners who bear limited liability up to the value of their contribution. The former are commonly called the GP, the latter the LP. In a publicly offered venture investment association there is exactly one general partner.

The money the deploying side receives comes in two streams.

  • Management fee - payment for operating the fund. It accrues at an agreed rate regardless of performance, and it covers fixed costs such as salaries and offices.
  • Carried interest, or performance fee - a share taken from returns above a threshold agreed in advance. It is tied to the outcome.

The two have opposite risk profiles. The management fee behaves like fixed income; carry is a subordinated success payment. Which of the two dominates changes what the person running the fund attends to. When carry is effectively the only reward, everything rides on making exits large. When the management fee alone sustains the organisation, continuing to raise larger funds becomes the stable choice.

How Korean rules write these two fees down

The model partnership agreement for venture investment associations published by the Korean Venture Capital Association, together with its drafting guidance, contains the clauses verbatim. The rate fields are left blank, but the structure is fully exposed.

Management fee - the life of the fund splits in two

Article 27 paragraph 1 of the model agreement divides the management fee into two periods.

  • Item 1 - from the date of registration with the Ministry of SMEs and Startups until a stated number of years later, an annual percentage of total committed capital.
  • Item 2 - from then until dissolution, an annual percentage of the remaining invested balance, measured as the average balance at each quarter end or the daily average balance.

That division is the heart of it. In the first period the base is the committed total. Whether the money actually reached a company, and what happened to it after it did, does not enter the calculation. Only in the second period does the base shift to the invested balance that remains. As for payment, item 1 is settled at each quarter end, while item 2 is in principle paid after the partners meeting approves the annual accounts, with up to half payable in advance.

The management fee is borne by the partnership. Article 26 paragraph 1 item 7 places it on the list of partnership operating expenses, and paragraph 2 of the same article provides that any operating cost not on that list falls to the general partner.

Carried interest - only above the threshold

Article 28 paragraph 1 provides that where the internal rate of return exceeds the hurdle rate during the life of the fund, an agreed proportion of investment profit is paid to the general partner as a performance fee, subject to approval by the partners meeting. The hurdle rate itself is likewise left blank in paragraph 2.

The agreement defines its own terms. Investment profit is the appraised value of partnership assets less contributed capital and partnership operating expenses. The internal rate of return is the discount rate that equalises the present value of the money the partnership deploys with the present value of what the partners recover. The hurdle rate is the return that serves as the threshold above which a performance fee may be paid.

Paragraph 3 sets out an early payment route. Where three years have passed since registration, the investment period stated in the agreement has elapsed, and the deployment obligation has been met, a performance fee may be paid before final settlement provided a sequence is followed. First, distributions to the partners continue until the cumulative amount reaches the sum of their contributed principal and the amount corresponding to the hurdle rate. Then, within whatever remains, the performance fee is paid. Anything left after that is divided in proportion to contributions. Paragraph 4, following the enforcement decree, requires the general partner to pass the performance fee it receives on to the lead fund manager and other staff as incentive pay, within the amount received.

The blank rate fields are not an omission. The guidance attaches a note to both the management fee and the performance fee reading, in effect, that since no separate regulation fixes them they are set by agreement among the partners. The rate, in other words, is not a figure handed down by law but a field the LP and the GP negotiate and fill in. Any explanation along the lines of 「the law sets carry at 20 percent」 is therefore not supported by this document.

The life of the fund works the same way. Article 14 sets it as a stated number of years from registration, extendable by special resolution of the partners meeting, with a note citing the enforcement decree for a floor of at least five years. The management fee accrues across that entire period.

What scale does to the management fee

At the same rate, a different size produces a different result. What follows is a simplified hypothetical illustration meant to show the structure. Taxes, interim realisations and the shift of calculation base between periods are all ignored, and the committed capital base is applied across the whole life.

[Assumptions] Management fee 2% a year, fund life 8 years

Fund A - committed capital 100 billion won
  Annual management fee = 100bn x 2%   =   2.0 billion won
  Cumulative over 8 years              =  16.0 billion won

Fund B - committed capital 1 trillion won
  Annual management fee = 1,000bn x 2% =  20.0 billion won
  Cumulative over 8 years              = 160.0 billion won

The rate is identical. Only the size changed.

What deserves attention is that costs do not scale with size. A fund ten times larger does not need ten times the investment staff, because the work of evaluating a single deal is not proportional to the amount invested. So as size grows, what is left from the management fee after keeping the organisation running grows with it.

The 50 billion dollars and 2 percent in the original essay aim at exactly this point. That figure, however, is an illustration the author chose to make his case, not the actual contract terms of any particular fund. Both the amount and the rate vary by period, by jurisdiction and by agreement.

What matters is not the amount but the direction. Once the management fee comfortably exceeds what it costs to keep the organisation running, making the next fund larger becomes a rational choice in its own right. Carry is settled only after realisations are complete, while the management fee begins arriving the quarter after the fund closes.

The arithmetic that forces a large fund to need a large exit

Size does not only change the management fee. It changes which companies fit the portfolio. This too is a simplified hypothetical illustration.

[Assumptions] The target is to return three times capital to the LPs.
              Of 30 investments, meaningful realisations come from 3.
              Ownership at the point of exit is 15%.

Fund A - 100 billion won
  Total realisations needed = 300 billion won
  3 successes  -> 100 billion won each
  15% ownership -> enterprise value about 670 billion won

Fund B - 1 trillion won
  Total realisations needed = 3 trillion won
  3 successes  -> 1 trillion won each
  15% ownership -> enterprise value about 6.7 trillion won

At the same target multiple, the size of company required grows in proportion to the fund. There is nothing good or bad in this arithmetic. It is simply arithmetic.

Seen from the founder side, though, the consequence becomes clear. A company with 10 billion won of annual revenue and steady profit is an excellent business. Inside Fund B, however, it is an asset that disappears on rounding. Not because the company is poor, but because there is no room for it in that fund arithmetic. The very same company is a meaningful exit candidate inside Fund A.

So 「you are not a fit for our fund」 is usually not an assessment of the company but a statement about arithmetic. On hearing it, there are cases where the answer is not to fix the company but to change who is across the table.

This is where a founder terms diverge

The arithmetic so far surfaces in three places on a term sheet. The basics of the rounds themselves, meaning the distinction between seed and Series A, B and C, how valuation is computed, SAFEs and the cap table, are set out in The Complete Guide to Startup Funding Rounds. What follows looks only at the incentives layered on top of them.

Growth pressure is structural, not a matter of taste

An investor leading with growth rate is not doing so out of temperament. The size demanded by the arithmetic above already fixes how many times over a company must grow within the time remaining. The fund life is at least five years and in practice longer, but the further into that life a fund sits, the less time is left. For the very same company, a freshly closed fund and a fund near the end of its term ask for different speeds. Which is why the vintage of the fund across the table is itself part of the terms.

The next round is assumed into the structure

The valuation of the current round is generally built on the assumption that the next one prices higher. Where the assumption holds, everyone benefits. Where it breaks, the round becomes a down round, and dilution and clauses move together at that moment. It is better, then, to work out how each clause behaves if the next round prices lower while reading the sheet. In the good scenario most clauses stay quiet.

Liquidation preference sets the order

This is the clause deciding who takes how much first when a company is sold or wound up. In Korean venture investment the common instrument is redeemable convertible preferred stock, RCPS for short. As the name says it carries a redemption right and a conversion right, and it ranks ahead of common stock in both dividends and the distribution of residual assets. The weight of the clause shows itself in numbers in the next section.

Putting liquidation preference into numbers

Liquidation preference splits into two forms. Participating takes the preference amount first and then shares in what remains, in proportion to ownership. Non-participating ends at the preference amount, although with a conversion right the holder will convert to common and take the pro rata share whenever that is larger. What follows is again a simplified hypothetical illustration.

[Assumptions] An investor puts in 2 billion won for 20% in preferred stock.
              Liquidation preference is 1x invested capital.
              No other rounds and no transaction costs.

Sale price 20 billion won
  Non-participating : max(2.0bn, 20bn x 20% = 4.0bn) -> investor 4.0bn / common 16.0bn
  Participating     : 2.0bn + (18bn x 20% = 3.6bn)   -> investor 5.6bn / common 14.4bn

Sale price 10 billion won
  Non-participating : max(2.0bn, 10bn x 20% = 2.0bn) -> investor 2.0bn / common  8.0bn
  Participating     : 2.0bn + (8bn x 20% = 1.6bn)    -> investor 3.6bn / common  6.4bn

Sale price 3 billion won
  Non-participating : max(2.0bn, 3bn x 20% = 0.6bn)  -> investor 2.0bn / common  1.0bn
  Participating     : 2.0bn + (1bn x 20% = 0.2bn)    -> investor 2.2bn / common  0.8bn

Laid out as a table, one thing becomes sharp. The lower the sale price, the heavier this clause becomes. At a sale of 20 billion won, whether the preference participates moves the common share by roughly a tenth; at 3 billion won, what is left to common falls below a third.

This is where the clause joins up with the fund arithmetic from earlier. The larger the fund, the greater the share of its deals that end as 「small realisations」, and so the probability of landing in the range where this clause actually bites rises with it. Accepting terms while looking only at the good scenario means signing without having calculated the range in which the clauses do their work.

Real agreements are more involved than this. The preference multiple may not be one times, participation may come with a cap, and where there are several rounds it must be settled separately whether later rounds rank ahead or share the same seniority. Terms differ from contract to contract, so these are not values that generalise.

The places Korean rules block in advance

To the question of what happens when a fund collects a management fee without deploying capital, Korean rules have an answer. There are two mechanisms.

The first is the minimum deployment ratio. Article 30 of the model agreement and its accompanying notes cite Article 51 of the Venture Investment Promotion Act, Article 35 of its enforcement decree and Article 7 of the registration and management rules for venture investment associations. A blind fund or project fund must invest at least 20 percent of committed capital through recognised investment methods within three years of registration. For secondary and merger and acquisition funds the figure is 60 percent over the same period. The universe of targets is not open either. Article 30 paragraph 1 confines it to unlisted startups, venture firms, and technology-innovative or management-innovative small and medium enterprises.

The second is the return of capital never deployed. Article 35 paragraph 2 of the model agreement allows contributed capital still uninvested after three years from registration and after the investment period stated in the agreement to be distributed back to the partners by special resolution of the partners meeting. A proviso requires contributed capital to remain at or above 2 billion won after any such distribution.

Acts serving the manager own interest are blocked by clause as well. Article 52 paragraph 1 of the Act requires the general partner to execute the business of the partnership with the care of a good manager, and paragraph 2 prohibits using partnership assets for the benefit of the general partner or a third party, borrowing, giving guarantees or providing collateral, investing in companies belonging to conglomerates subject to cross-shareholding restrictions, and acquiring non-business real estate. Partnership assets are held and administered separately by a custodian.

The source of the capital also differs. On 23 January 2026 the Ministry of SMEs and Startups announced its first regular commitment round for the year, targeting 2.1 trillion won of fund-of-funds commitments to form 4.4 trillion won of venture funds. By ministry the figures were 1.63 trillion won from SMEs and Startups, 499 billion won from Culture, Sports and Tourism, and 15 billion won from Oceans and Fisheries. A substantial share originates in public finance, which is why deployment obligations and distribution rules of the kind above travel with it.

The American situation the essay takes aim at therefore does not transfer to Korea unchanged. The texture of regulation is different. What is the same, however, is the underlying structure in which the management fee accrues regardless of outcome. Korea has simply fitted brakes on top of it, in the form of a minimum deployment ratio and the return of undeployed capital.

Other routes - what you give up and what you get

Where the arithmetic of a venture fund does not suit a company, there are options that change the funding method itself. Each gives something up and gets something in return. None is superior in the abstract; the question is which meshes with the company growth curve.

Revenue-based financing is not yet a familiar name in Korea. The structure described in the materials published by overseas providers runs as follows. Capital arrives up front and is repaid each month as a fixed share of revenue, with a cap on the total repaid; once the cap is reached, the obligation ends. The cap is frequently described as falling in a range of 1.2 to 1.5 times the principal, and terms are stated as a total repayment multiple rather than an interest rate. Because repayment tracks revenue, faster growth ends it sooner and slower growth later, so there is no fixed maturity date. In exchange it requires revenue and a track record, which rules it out before revenue exists, and the amount available is bounded by the size of that revenue.

Money coming through government and public institutions has a different character again. Amounts that must be repaid and amounts that need not be are mixed together, so the order in which an announcement is read has to be sorted out first; that part is set out separately in Policy Funds Are Not Free Money. The routes by which individual investors reach this asset class, and their risks, are covered in The Complete Guide to Crowdfunding and P2P Lending Investment.

Where to read a term sheet first

Valuation catches the eye first but is the last number to settle. There are places that should be read before it. Working through the order below exposes the incentives a term sheet stands on.

Once all eight have been read, one task remains. Run the same term sheet through two scenarios. One where the next round prices higher, and one where it prices lower or never happens at all. In the good scenario most clauses are quiet. The character of a clause appears only in the bad one.

Who is across the table is part of the terms too. Vintage, fund size and remaining life can usually be established by asking, and those three settle the arithmetic from the earlier sections. The decision-making problem covered in Most Startups Have a Decision Problem interlocks with this structure of terms, because when funding conditions force a growth rate, that rate in turn eats into the room available for decisions.

What the original essay does not cover

To keep the citation accurate, the scope of the original is worth recording. Dash writes about the American situation, and his centre of gravity is political money and the structure of power. That part lies outside the territory covered here.

  • Rebuttals or explanations from the firms named are not in the original.
  • Portfolio performance data or statistics on failures are not in the original.
  • A detailed examination of alternative funding instruments is not in the original.
  • Venture investment conditions outside the United States are not in the original.
  • Specific policy proposals or regulatory design are not in the original.

What this piece takes from the essay is one structure: that management fees and carried interest create different incentives. That structure survives not as a claim but as written clauses, and it can be read in Articles 27 and 28 of the Korean model agreement as well. The political conclusions belong to the author and are not carried across.

What could not be confirmed

  • The actual rates for management fees and carry - the model agreement leaves the year and percentage fields blank and attaches a note that, absent separate regulation, they are set by agreement among the partners. The distribution of real rates inside individual partnership agreements is not published and could not be confirmed. An explanation that 「the law sets carry at 20 percent」 is therefore not supported by this document.
  • The full text of the registration and management rules for venture investment associations - the national law information centre page renders through script and the clause text could not be opened. What is established is that the rules sit with the Ministry of SMEs and Startups as Notice 2026-59, effective 1 July 2026, and that Article 12 treats the management fee as a partnership operating expense.
  • Rate examples for individual investment associations - practitioner guidance shows cases of 2 to 5 percent a year, but those concern individual investment associations, a different vehicle from venture investment associations, and cannot be transferred across. They were left out of the body for that reason.
  • The share of investment done in preferred stock - a figure putting a substantial proportion of new investment in preferred form appears in secondary sources, but the underlying data could not be opened and it was left out.
  • Revenue-based financing inside Korea - the 1.2 to 1.5 times repayment cap is a range described in overseas providers own materials. Availability and contract terms within Korea could not be confirmed.
  • The original tabulation behind the political giving figures - the compiling body and counting basis for the 115.3 million dollar and 20 percent figures could not be confirmed beyond what the essay itself states.
  • Average fund size and the trend toward larger funds in Korea - statistics exist on the venture investment portal, but they were not opened on this occasion.
  • How supervision actually operates - how the deployment ratio and the self-dealing restrictions are checked and enforced in practice could not be confirmed.
