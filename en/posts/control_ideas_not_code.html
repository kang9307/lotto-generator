Control the Intent, Not the Code

Where human control moved when AI writes the code - holding intent outside the code with decision records, contracts, invariants and tests

Aug 16, 2026 Dev & Infra

Opening: three 3,000-line pull requests before lunch

The first pull request you open in the morning is 3,000 lines long. It is the feature you handed to an agent yesterday, the tests are green, and the description reads well. Going through it line by line would take more than two hours. There are two more like it waiting.

What teams actually do in that situation is one of two things. They skim and approve, or they defer the review and become the bottleneck. The first is the appearance of control; the second trades control for delay and gets nothing back. The problem is not laziness, it is arithmetic. Generation throughput climbed far faster than one person's reading speed, while the approval process still rests on the assumption that a human read all of it.

antirez, the creator of Redis, went straight at this in a post published in August 2026. If you control the ideas in your software, he argues, looking at the code itself is «suboptimal and often pointless», and while he does check AI-generated contributions to the project he maintains, he would rather spend that time on QA and optimization. The Korean developer community picked the piece up through GeekNews, where the discussion continued.

There is a fork in how you read that. Take it as «you no longer need to look at code» and in six months you own a system nobody can explain. Take it as «the point of control moved» and you get a work list instead. This article takes the second reading. It maps where control went, what shape intent has to take once it lives outside the code, and above all what has to be true before skipping the read is safe.

1. Control moved from how it is written to what is being built and why

1.1 The assumptions that made reading work

Code review functioned as a control mechanism because of two assumptions. The first was that human-written code carries intent with it. The author sat nearby, so you could ask why. The second was that volume fit inside human throughput. When a few hundred lines land per day, controlling by reading is a coherent strategy.

Both assumptions are shaky now. Generated code does not carry an author's intent. It carries the defaults the model picked, and those defaults are usually plausible yet subtly different from what the team actually wanted. The volume side needs no argument. Only the reading speed stayed constant.

1.2 What review was really doing

Break down what a reviewer actually did and three activities fall out: checking syntax and style, checking that the implementation matches the requirement, and judging whether this direction is right at all. Tools and models have largely absorbed the first two. What remains is the third, and that was never a thing you did by reading code. Reading merely felt like the vehicle for it.

So the shift in the point of control is less a new burden than something previously hidden becoming visible. What to build and why was always the human's job. Reading code impersonated that judgement convincingly, and the impersonation stopped working.

1.3 Where the industry currently stands

In DORA's 2025 report, based on responses from close to 5,000 technology professionals worldwide, 90% said they use AI at work and more than 80% said it made them more productive. At the same time, roughly 30% reported little or no trust in AI-generated code. That combination is an accurate summary of the present moment: nearly everyone is using it, nearly everyone feels faster, and a large minority does not believe it. There is exactly one way to sustain using something you do not trust, and it is to source the trust from structure rather than from a human's reading.

2. Four containers for intent that live outside the code

Write down your design intent has been standard advice for twenty years, and it has mostly failed. The reason is not mysterious: documents have no teeth. Nothing happens when a document and the code disagree, so documents rot quietly. Any inventory of places to keep intent therefore has to be sorted by whether the thing is enforceable.

The point of the table is that only rows two through four are control mechanisms. Break a contract and the request is rejected; break an invariant and the insert fails; break a test and the pipeline goes red. They work whether or not anyone reads them. Row one is not control, it is the court record. That does not make it optional. Every control mechanism eventually becomes an obstacle, and when that day comes somebody needs grounds to decide whether it can be lifted.

Why this distinction matters in practice: most teams that resolve to capture intent produce a pile of row one and none of rows two through four. Nothing ends up enforced and the only durable result is more reading. Piling up policy documents does not control an agent, because what governs behaviour is enforcement, not the volume of rules.

3. Decision records - write down only what is expensive to reverse

3.1 What an ADR is

An Architecture Decision Record captures a single decision and its rationale on one page. The practice spread from Michael Nygard's 2011 post and is framed today around understanding a chosen decision along with its trade-offs and consequences. Shorter is better. Context, decision, rejected alternatives, consequences you accept. Four paragraphs is plenty.

3.2 What goes in and what does not

The most common way ADR adoption dies is recording everything. Once picking a library earns a document, you have an unread directory within two months. There is one test: record only decisions that are expensive to reverse.

  • The data model and identifier scheme — changing it drags migrations and external contracts along
  • Service boundaries and ownership — drawn wrong, the org chart eventually follows
  • The consistency model — wherever strong consistency was traded away, recovery is close to impossible later
  • Where authentication and authorization are decided — the client, the gateway, or the service
  • Introducing an external dependency, especially one that accumulates your data
  • How irreversible side effects are handled — payments, outbound mail, notifications to other systems

What does not need a record: how functions are decomposed, folder layout, code style, whether a particular helper exists. Those are cheap to change at any time, and attaching records to cheap changes turns the record set itself into overhead.

3.3 A new audience that reads these documents

Decision records gained a reader. The only audience used to be a colleague six months out; now an agent reads them before it starts work. That changes what the document has to be. Prose is fine for humans, but a document an agent will consult is only useful if each constraint is isolated into its own sentence. Consider performance produces no constraint whatsoever. List queries must be satisfied by a single query, per-row lookups are forbidden is a constraint.

4. Contracts and invariants - if a machine cannot read it, it is not control

4.1 Contracts sit on boundaries

An interface contract fixes the shape of data crossing a module, service or storage boundary. For an HTTP API that is a schema definition; for an internal module, a type signature; for messages, a serialization schema. The format matters far less than whether a machine can tell that it was violated.

Contracts became disproportionately valuable in a generated-code world for a specific reason. Models are strong at a single function and weak at the shape of a whole system. Yet incidents rarely originate inside a function; they originate between functions. A field name is subtly different, nullability disagrees, a timestamp is UTC on one side and local on the other. A contract closes exactly that seam.

4.2 Invariants sit on the data

An invariant makes the system enforce this is always true on its own behalf. The highest-value place to put them is the database, because application code is regenerated constantly while constraints attach to the data and outlive the code.

  • NOT NULL and uniqueness — two lines here replace thirty defensive lines in application code
  • Foreign keys — enforcing referential integrity in the application usually loses to concurrency
  • Check constraints — an amount cannot be negative, an end time cannot precede a start time
  • State transition tables — fix which state can move to which, and reject everything outside the table
  • Assertions — preconditions on entry, postconditions before return, failing loudly instead of continuing

Writing invariants down has a side benefit. When you hand work to an agent, the list is half the prompt. Telling a model what must not break is a narrower and more accurate instruction than telling it what to build.

4.3 Tests are the executable form of a requirement

Managing tests by coverage percentage is close to useless in this context, because generated code cheerfully generates the tests that pass it. What carries weight is tests derived from requirements.

  • Negative tests — is an unauthorized request rejected, is invalid input refused
  • Boundary tests — empty list, exactly one, the maximum, duplicates, out-of-order arrival
  • Property-based tests — state a property that must always hold and let the machine hammer it with random input
  • Golden tests — freeze the agreed output shape whole and require human approval when it changes
  • Contract tests — verify from both sides that provider and consumer are looking at the same schema

5. The conditions under which not reading is safe

This is the core of the argument. Can I skip reading the code is not a yes-or-no question, it is a conditional. When the following five are true you are on the safe side of the line; when even one is false, you read the change regardless of its size.

  1. The blast radius is fenced in. The maximum reach of the change is bounded by permissions and dependency direction, not by a list of files.
  2. Reverting is cheap. A rollback is one deploy, and no irreversible data change happens in between.
  3. Failure is loud. If it misbehaves, an alert fires before a human notices.
  4. Violations are caught before execution. Types, schemas, static analysis and permission scope checks block it pre-deploy.
  5. The asset in play is not destructive. The operation is read-only, or running it twice produces the same result.
Nature of the changeSafe to skip readingWhy
Copy and layout tweaksUsually yesCheap to revert, failure is immediately visible
Internal refactor with contracts unchangedUsually yesBoundaries are fixed, so the blast radius is fenced
Added testsYes, but read the assertionsA test that always passes turns the safety net into theatre

Authentication and authorization pathsAlways readFailure is silent and damage is retroactive
Money calculation and settlementAlways readOne rounding digit becomes an accounting entry
Personal data handling and loggingAlways readA log line, once written, is not recalled
Concurrency and cache invalidationAlways readGreen tests say nothing about behaviour under load

Dependency and build configuration changesAlways readThis is the door supply-chain risk walks through

Two words repeat down that table: irreversible and silent. Where they overlap is where a human must look, and everything else is safer delegated to machinery on the whole. Spread review attention evenly across the diff and there is nothing left for the places that matter.

6. The opposite risk - what breaks when you read intent and never the implementation

The danger in this direction is not that bad code slips in. What actually slips in is code that satisfies every stated requirement and is unusable in production. Three families of failure honour the contract and pass the tests and still collapse in operation.

6.1 Performance - prose does not forbid a query per row

Show the order list does not say fetch the list in one round trip. So an implementation that looks up user details once per row violates nothing. With three rows of test data it passes. At thirty thousand rows in production it falls over.

The fix is to move performance out of prose and into checks. Put a query-count ceiling on the hot paths and let the test fail when it is exceeded. Do the same with a latency budget. Those two alone catch most of this family before release.

6.2 Security - the plausible default is the dangerous one

Security defects are rarely dramatic. Code that trusts a user identifier sent by the client. An admin check performed only in the front end. An exception message that ships an internal path to the caller. All of them work flawlessly on the happy path, which is exactly why a review that follows the happy path never finds them.

Treat negative tests as part of the requirement instead. If an authorization path has no test asserting that an unauthorized request is rejected, approval should be impossible. On top of that, pull secrets, permission scopes and new dependencies out of code review and into dedicated gates. Anything left to human attentiveness eventually gets through.

6.3 Edge cases - deleted defences barely show in a diff

Added lines catch the eye; removed lines do not. A validation line, a permission check, a null guard quietly disappearing during a refactor is common, and the tests usually still pass without them, because the incident that put the line there in the first place has been forgotten.

Two responses work. One is to pair every defence earned through an incident with a regression test, so the test speaks up when the line goes missing. The other is making it a habit to read deletions first when opening a diff. The total volume you read does not change, but the class of problem you catch does.

6.4 And the comprehension gap

The slowest-arriving risk is neither performance nor security. It is that in six months nobody can explain the system. The cost of writing code fell, while the cost of understanding it did not, and arguably rose. Decision records and invariant lists are the only savings account against that gap. They look like pure overhead until the day something breaks, which makes them structurally similar to backups.

7. When review shifts from code to diffs and decisions

7.1 Approval means something different

Approval used to mean I read it and it looks fine. It now means every gate is green and I agree with the decisions recorded here. That is not wordplay; it relocates accountability. A claim to have read something cannot be verified, whereas a passing gate and a recorded agreement both leave evidence.

7.2 Reviewers get a fixed reading order

  1. Did a contract change — start with the schema diff. If compatibility broke, nothing else matters yet
  2. Did an invariant or constraint disappear — deleted constraints, relaxed nullability, removed assertions
  3. Did permissions or dependencies grow — new environment variables, new packages, widened scopes
  4. Is anything irreversible mixed in — migrations, deletions, outbound notifications
  5. Everything else — this is the part you can leave unread when time runs out

7.3 Splitting a change becomes human work

Agents produce in large units. A 3,000-line change is dangerous not because it is unreviewable but because it mixes units of meaning. When a contract change, an internal refactor and a migration arrive as one lump, there is no way to decide where the attention should go. Slicing generated output into meaningful units therefore becomes a new human task, and it is hard to automate: only the side that knows the intent can say what belongs together.

7.4 Comments change character

Rename this variable style comments lost most of their value, since the next generation pass will produce something different anyway. What survives are questions about constraints. Why did this validation go away, what justifies making this field nullable, was this decision recorded anywhere. The view that review exists for tomorrow's understandability rather than today's correctness gets sharper, not weaker, in a generated-code environment. In teams that already lean on branch protection, required checks and code owners, this mostly means redrawing those rules around risk instead of directory ownership.

7.5 How large automated changes are actually controlled

A 2025 paper describing how Google applied models to internal code migrations reports a test framework migration that touched 5,359 files and roughly 149,000 lines over three months. Controlling that by reading it line by line was never on the table. What made it tractable was narrowing the nature of the change, automating verification, and letting machines filter the failures. It is a demonstration at scale of where the point of control has to move.

8. Making it stick in a team - templates, gates, checklists

8.1 A gate design table

Gates last only when they sit at points that require no human attentiveness. Below is a minimum set worth wiring up.

Contract changeAPI schema diffBackward compatibility brokenVersion it or get owner approvalLow
MigrationPresence of DDLNo rollback procedureAttach a rollback scriptLow
Permissions and secretsNew env vars or widened scopeSecurity review not signed offNamed reviewer approvesMedium

The false-positive column deserves attention. The performance budget gate misfires often and expensively. Set the threshold slightly too tight and unrelated changes keep getting blocked, at which point the team stops turning the gate off and starts granting exceptions instead. Start with two or three critical endpoints and widen from there. The decision record gate is the opposite: a false positive costs one extra page of writing, so it is safe to enable early.

8.2 A change request template

Templates only get filled in when they are short. Past seven lines they are copied and left blank.

  • What is changing — one sentence
  • Why now — one sentence
  • What was decided — name at least one alternative and why it lost
  • Constraints added or removed
  • How to revert — if it does not fit on one line, that is itself a signal
  • Files a human must read — the author points at them
  • What could not be verified

The last two lines are the whole point. When the author directs the reviewer's attention, 3,000 lines become 300. And once there is a box for what could not be verified, leaving it unverified becomes an explicit choice rather than an omission.

8.3 Attach checklists to paths, not to people

A list titled fifteen things to check on every pull request dies in two weeks. Use per-path checklists instead. Touch the authentication directory and five auth-specific lines appear; touch the migrations directory and four migration-specific lines appear. Redraw ownership around risk rather than around the org chart while you are at it.

8.4 Adoption order

  1. The change request template first — it costs almost nothing and produces raw material for the next steps
  2. Detection of irreversible changes — start with migrations and deletions only
  3. Contract diffing — this presupposes that schemas live in the repository
  4. Permission and dependency gates — incidents stop being possible around here
  5. Mandatory negative tests and performance budgets — after the team is used to gates
  6. The decision record gate last, or it gets filled in as a formality

Run the order backwards, starting with more written rules, and it usually fails. Rules nobody enforces are ignored in proportion to their length, leaving only the cost of reading them. That is part of why committing a specification instead of code is interesting: it promotes the spec from documentation to something that gets verified.

9. What could not be verified

  • The original post gives no figures on the size, duration or defect counts of the projects antirez cites. Only his argument is quoted here; no effect has been translated into numbers.
  • Figures from the DORA 2025 report are taken from the announcement and public summaries. The finer breakdown of the trust distribution comes via reporting on the summary and was not checked against the tables in the full report.
  • Claims of the form adding gates reduces defects by N percent were left out, because no source worth citing turned up.
  • No credible comparative study of decision record adoption or review practice across Korean, Japanese, Chinese and Western teams was found. Statements about practice in this article are judgement, not statistics.

Conclusion: what makes code safe to skip is everything around it

Stop reading the code is half right. Stated precisely, it is build the state in which reading is unnecessary, and only then stop reading. That state is produced by the machinery around the code rather than by the code. Contracts on boundaries, invariants nailed into the data, tests derived from requirements, deployments that can be reverted, observability that makes noise. With those standing, most changes need no reading; without them, no amount of reading yields control.

My own read is that the shift in the point of control did not reduce the work, it changed its kind. Time saved on reading goes into designing constraints, isolating the places that cannot be undone, and recording decisions. That work is harder, more interesting, and above all the kind a model has trouble taking over, because what to build and why is still a question that needs someone to ask it.

If you change one thing tomorrow, add two lines to your change request template: files a human must read and what could not be verified. Those two lines are the cheapest available way to move review from code to decisions.
