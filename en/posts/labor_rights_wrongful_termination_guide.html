Introduction: The Day You're Told You're Fired - What Now?

One day, out of nowhere, you hear "don't bother coming in tomorrow." Or a messenger ping says "today is your last day." A job is not merely a place to work; it is the foundation of your livelihood and a space tied to your daily life and self-worth. Being pushed out of such a place without notice and without a convincing reason is not a mere inconvenience but a life-shaking shock. Yet many people give up their rights, thinking, "If the company says go, you have to go, right?"

Korea's Labor Standards Act imposes several limits so that an employer cannot dismiss a worker at will. A dismissal must have just cause, must follow the prescribed process, and must be preceded by 30 days' notice. A dismissal that breaks these rules is a wrongful termination that can be contested, and there is a path to reinstatement or monetary compensation through the Labor Relations Commission. This article gathers in one place what wrongful termination is, the types and requirements of dismissal, and the order in which to respond when you are wrongfully dismissed. It centers on Korea's Labor Standards Act, while repeatedly noting that all standards and figures can change by time and circumstance.

What Is Wrongful Termination?

Dismissal is the employer's unilateral act of ending the employment contract (the employment relationship). The Labor Standards Act provides that "an employer shall not, without just cause, dismiss, lay off, suspend, transfer, reduce wages, or otherwise punish a worker." In other words, a dismissal lacking just cause, or one that fails to follow the process set by statute or rules of employment, is a wrongful termination.

Typical situations you can contest as wrongful termination include: a dismissal without just cause; a dismissal for managerial reasons that fails to meet the restrictions on layoffs; a dismissal during a period in which the law prohibits dismissal (leave for treating an occupational injury or illness and the 30 days after it, or maternity leave before and after childbirth and the 30 days after it — Labor Standards Act Article 23(2)); a disciplinary dismissal that is excessive relative to the wrongdoing; and a dismissal that violates the disciplinary or dismissal process set by a collective agreement or rules of employment. The core is the two axes of "just cause" and "due process," and a breach of either can make a dismissal wrongful.

One line to remember: wrongful termination = a dismissal that lacks "just cause" or fails to follow "due process." Even if the grounds are valid, a flawed process leaves room to contest it.

Types of Dismissal - Ordinary, Disciplinary, and Managerial

Dismissals fall into three broad types by cause, and each has different requirements for being recognized as justified.

Ordinary Dismissal

This is dismissal for personal grounds — a lack of job capacity, inability to work due to illness or injury, loss of a required qualification, and the like — that make it hard to maintain the contract. Even here, mere "low performance" is not enough; the ground must be serious enough that, by social norms, the relationship cannot reasonably continue, for the dismissal to be considered justified.

Disciplinary Dismissal

This is dismissal as a sanction for the worker's misconduct (embezzlement, unauthorized absence, serious insubordination, and so on). For it to be justified, (1) the disciplinary ground must actually exist and be grave enough to warrant dismissal (proportionality of the sanction), and (2) the disciplinary process set by the rules of employment or collective agreement (convening a disciplinary committee, giving a chance to explain, and so on) must be followed. Crucially, a dismissal can still be wrongful if the process is skipped even where grounds exist.

Managerial Dismissal (Layoff)

This is a headcount reduction driven by the company's managerial needs rather than the worker's fault. Because the worker is not at fault, the law demands the strictest requirements. The detailed requirements are covered separately in the section on the strict requirements for managerial dismissal.

Dismissal Notice and Notice Pay

Even when dismissing, the employer must give the worker time to prepare for sudden job loss. Article 26 of the Labor Standards Act therefore imposes a notice duty.

30 Days' Notice or 30 Days' Ordinary Wages

An employer must give at least 30 days' advance notice to dismiss a worker, and if it fails to do so, it must pay at least 30 days of ordinary wages (dismissal notice pay). This also applies to dismissals for managerial reasons.

Notice Pay and Wrongful Termination Are Separate

Here is the most common misunderstanding to clear up. Receiving notice pay does not make the dismissal justified. Notice pay is merely compensation for not giving 30 days' advance warning; whether the dismissal itself is justified (whether there was just cause and due process) is an entirely separate matter. Case law also holds that even if a dismissal is void as wrongful, the worker is not without a legal basis to receive notice pay. In short, you can contest a wrongful dismissal even after receiving notice pay.

Exceptions Allowing Dismissal Without Notice

That said, 30 days' notice is not required in every case. Where the worker has been continuously employed for less than three months, where it is impossible to continue the business due to a natural disaster, calamity, or other unavoidable cause, or where the worker has intentionally caused serious disruption or financial damage to the business in a way that falls under the grounds set by an Ordinance of the Ministry of Employment and Labor, dismissal without notice is permitted (proviso to Article 26 of the Labor Standards Act). Note that the test is that continuing the business is "impossible," not merely "difficult," and that the third exception applies only to the grounds listed in the Ministry ordinance. Even then, only the "notice duty" is waived, not the "justification of the dismissal" itself — keep that in mind.

Don't be lulled by the company telling you to take notice pay. Receiving notice pay is not an acceptance of the dismissal. If you believe the dismissal was wrongful, consider a remedy application to the Labor Relations Commission separately from notice pay.

The Strict Requirements for Managerial Dismissal (Layoff)

Because a layoff stems from company circumstances rather than the worker's fault, Article 24 of the Labor Standards Act treats it as justified only when all four requirements are met.

  • Urgent managerial necessity: It need not be on the brink of bankruptcy; a reasonable headcount reduction to address a future crisis can also count, provided the necessity is objectively recognized. The law treats a transfer, acquisition, or merger of the business carried out to prevent deteriorating management as an urgent managerial necessity (Article 24(1)).
  • Efforts to avoid dismissal: The employer must do its utmost to avoid dismissal — halting new hiring, soliciting voluntary retirement, temporary leave, reassignment, and the like.
  • Reasonable and fair selection: The criteria for choosing who is dismissed must be reasonable and fair, with no discrimination on grounds such as sex.
  • Sincere consultation with worker representatives: The employer must notify the worker representative (a union organized by a majority of workers, or, if there is none, a person representing a majority of workers) of the methods for avoiding dismissal and the dismissal criteria at least 50 days before the intended dismissal date, and consult in good faith (Article 24(3)).

All four must be satisfied; missing even one can make the layoff a wrongful termination. An employer that intends to dismiss a number of workers at or above the scale set by Presidential Decree must also report to the Minister of Employment and Labor (Article 24(4)). Conversely, a dismissal that meets all the requirements of paragraphs (1) to (3) is deemed a dismissal with just cause (Article 24(5)). If you have been laid off, the starting point is to examine whether the company actually met these requirements.

Remedy Procedure (1) - Applying to the Labor Relations Commission

If you judge the dismissal to be wrongful, you can contest it through two broad routes. One is a remedy application to the Labor Relations Commission (administrative relief); the other is a lawsuit to confirm the dismissal is void (judicial relief). The Commission's relatively fast and low-cost procedure is widely used.

Apply Within Three Months - The Most Important Deadline

A wrongful termination remedy application must be filed within three months of the date the wrongful termination occurred, to the Regional Labor Relations Commission with jurisdiction over the workplace (Labor Standards Act Article 28(2)). The start date is generally the "dismissal date" stated on the dismissal notice. Because this three months is a strict deadline after which relief through the Commission becomes difficult, not missing it matters above all if you intend to contest the dismissal.

Overview of the Review Process

Once the application is received, the Regional Commission decides whether the dismissal was wrongful through investigation and a hearing. Both parties submit briefs and contest their claims and evidence at the hearing. If dissatisfied with the initial decision, you can apply for reexamination by the National Labor Relations Commission within 10 days of being notified of the remedy order or dismissal decision, and if still dissatisfied with the reexamination decision, you can file an administrative lawsuit within 15 days of being served with it. If no reexamination or lawsuit is filed within these periods, the remedy order, dismissal decision, or reexamination decision becomes final (Labor Standards Act Article 31). The employer can challenge within the same periods.

The key deadline: to contest a wrongful dismissal, never let "three months from the dismissal date" pass. This single line is both the start of relief and the most common point of failure. The next deadlines are 10 days for reexamination and 15 days for an administrative lawsuit.

Remedy Procedure (2) - Reinstatement and Compensation

If the Commission finds a wrongful termination, it issues a remedy order to the employer. The relief a worker can receive comes in two broad forms.

Reinstatement Plus Back Pay

The default remedy is reinstatement — returning to the job you held before the dismissal. Along with it, payment of the wages you would have earned (back pay) during the dismissal period is ordered. In other words, you can also recover wages for the period you were wrongfully pushed out.

Monetary Compensation Order - When You Don't Want to Return

Often you do not want to return to a company where trust has already broken down. In that case, you can request monetary compensation instead of reinstatement. Rather than ordering reinstatement, the Commission can order payment of an amount at least equal to the wages for the dismissal period (Labor Standards Act Article 30(3)). If you do not want reinstatement, it is common to request a monetary compensation order at the application stage.

Even when reinstatement has become impossible because the employment contract term expired or the worker reached retirement age, the Commission must still issue a decision, and if it finds the dismissal wrongful, it can order payment of an amount equal to the wages the worker would have earned during the dismissal period (Labor Standards Act Article 30(4), added in 2021). In other words, the end of the contract term does not by itself end the remedy procedure.

Enforcement Penalty - When the Order Is Ignored

If the employer fails to comply with the Commission's remedy order by the set deadline, an enforcement penalty (charge) may be imposed. This is a device to ensure the order's effectiveness. The framework in Article 33 of the Labor Standards Act is as follows. Each charge is up to KRW 30 million, and the employer must be notified in writing at least 30 days before it is imposed. It can be imposed repeatedly until the order is complied with, up to twice a year counted from the date of the first remedy order, but not for more than two years. Once the employer complies, no new charge is imposed, but charges already imposed are still collected. The worker can report non-compliance to the Commission within 15 days after the compliance deadline passes.

Proof and Evidence - What to Gather and How

A fight over dismissal is ultimately a fight over evidence. Calmly organizing your material from right after the dismissal decides the outcome.

Material Showing the Employment Relationship and Rules

  • Employment contract: Confirms the existence of the relationship, the job, wages, and contract type (regular, fixed-term, and so on).
  • Rules of employment / collective agreement: Confirm how dismissal and disciplinary grounds and procedures are set. This is the core basis for contesting a process violation.
  • Payslips / work-hour records: Used to calculate ordinary wages and prove length of service.

Material Showing the Fact, Grounds, and Timing of Dismissal

  • Dismissal notice: The most direct evidence confirming the dismissal date and grounds in writing. Because a dismissal takes effect only when notified in writing (Labor Standards Act Article 27), a purely verbal dismissal with no written notice can itself be grounds to contest it.
  • Texts, emails, messenger chats: A message like "don't come in tomorrow" is powerful material showing the fact and timing of dismissal. Capture and back it up.
  • Recordings / colleague statements: For a verbal dismissal, record the conversation or secure statements from colleagues who know the circumstances.
Evidence fades over time. Before your access to company mail and messengers is cut off, back up dismissal-related conversations and documents to your own device. But avoid collecting evidence by unlawful means.

The Trap of Recommended Resignation and Voluntary Quitting

A common tactic companies use to dodge a wrongful termination dispute is to make a "resignation" out of what is really a "dismissal." This is where you must be most careful.

Recommended Resignation - Stop Before You Sign

A recommended resignation is where the employer suggests you resign and you agree, ending the relationship. Because it formally takes the shape of a "resignation by agreement," once you sign the resignation letter it becomes very hard to later argue "it was actually a dismissal" and contest it as wrongful. When a company says "we'll process it as a recommended resignation, so write a resignation letter," if it is in substance a dismissal, your signature can amount to waiving your rights.

Being Pushed Into Voluntary Quitting

Some employers coerce resignation or threaten disadvantage if you don't sign, dressing an involuntary separation up as a voluntary one. Such "coerced resignation" may in substance be evaluated as a dismissal, but it is hard to prove. So if you receive a resignation suggestion you cannot accept, avoid answering or signing on the spot, record how it happened, and consult a professional.

One line to remember: signing a resignation letter is a "hard-to-reverse decision." If there is any room at all to contest a wrongful dismissal, consult a labor attorney or lawyer before you sign.

Linking to Unemployment Benefits - The Reason for Leaving Is the Fork

For your livelihood after a dismissal or departure, unemployment benefits (job-seeking benefits) are indispensable. But because benefits are, in principle, paid for an "involuntary" separation, how the reason for leaving is recorded is decisive.

Dismissal, Recommended Resignation, and Benefits

An involuntary separation without serious fault of your own — such as an employer-initiated dismissal or a recommended resignation — is often recognized as qualifying for job-seeking benefits. By contrast, a voluntary departure for purely personal reasons is, in principle, hard to qualify. However, even a voluntary departure may qualify if there is a statutory just cause such as unpaid wages, workplace harassment, or a difficult commute.

Wrongful Termination Remedies and Benefits

Where the dismissed state is maintained — as when you receive a monetary compensation order while contesting a wrongful dismissal — benefits are understood to be available, although this update (2026-09-29) could not re-confirm that handling against a primary source. However, cases such as reinstatement with back pay may be handled differently. Eligibility, duration, and amount depend on personal factors such as employment insurance history, so confirm specifics with your employment center for accuracy.

Responses by Situation

Even the same dismissal has different priorities depending on your position. Here are common situations. This is general guidance only; the actual judgment must be made with a professional, tailored to your case.

If You Were Suddenly Dismissed Verbally

First, immediately back up the notice messages and conversations, and if possible ask for the dismissal grounds and date in writing. Remember that a Commission remedy application is possible within three months of the dismissal date, and it is safer to consult a labor attorney or lawyer before then.

If You're Being Pressured to Sign a Resignation Letter

Do not sign immediately; record how the suggestion arose. Decide after comparing the recommended-resignation terms (severance, benefit eligibility, and so on) against the practical merit of contesting a wrongful dismissal. Always keep in mind that signing is hard to reverse.

If You've Been Selected for Layoff

Check whether the company actually met the four requirements: urgent managerial necessity, efforts to avoid dismissal, fair selection, and consultation 50 days in advance. If the requirements are deficient, there is room to contest it as wrongful.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q. Can I be dismissed freely during a probation period?

Even during probation, dismissal requires just cause. That said, because probation serves to assess suitability, the justification for dismissal is said to be recognized somewhat more broadly than for ordinary workers. Even so, an arbitrary dismissal with no reasonable basis can be contested.

Q. Can workplaces with fewer than five employees be contested for wrongful termination?

If your workplace regularly employs four or fewer workers, you cannot file a wrongful-termination remedy application with the Labor Relations Commission. The Labor Standards Act applies to businesses regularly employing five or more workers, and only the provisions listed by Presidential Decree (Enforcement Decree Article 7, Annex 1) apply to those with four or fewer (Article 11). For dismissal, only the restriction on dismissal timing (Article 23(2)) and dismissal notice (Article 26) apply; the ban on dismissal without just cause (Article 23(1)), managerial dismissal (Articles 24 and 25), written notice (Article 27), and the remedy application (Articles 28 to 33) do not (Easy Law, as of 2026-08-31). You can still claim 30 days' notice or notice pay. The government has said it will pursue a phased extension of the Act to workplaces with fewer than five workers, but the current wording of Article 11 (version in force from 2026-08-20), checked on 2026-09-29, is unchanged, and no amendment extending the dismissal rules to such workplaces was found to be in force. Because the headcount is calculated by the method set by Presidential Decree (Article 11(3)), check with the Commission or a professional if you are near the threshold.

Q. The company won't give me a dismissal notice. What do I do?

Article 27 of the Labor Standards Act requires an employer to give the grounds and timing of a dismissal in writing, and a dismissal takes effect only when notified in writing. If the employer gave the dismissal notice in writing stating the grounds and timing, that counts as this notification. If there is no written notice, that itself leaves room to contest the process, so record the circumstances of the notice and consult a professional. Note that this article does not apply to workplaces with four or fewer regular workers.

Conclusion - Rights Work "Within the Deadline, With Evidence"

The Labor Standards Act is a shield protecting workers from wrongful dismissal. But that shield is not handed automatically to someone who stays passive. Examining whether the grounds and process of the dismissal were justified, considering a Commission remedy application within three months of the dismissal date, and calmly gathering evidence beforehand — these fundamentals are the surest way to stand against wrongful termination.

Finally, three points again. First, you can contest a wrongful dismissal even after receiving notice pay. Second, the three-month remedy deadline must never be missed, and signing a recommended resignation calls for caution. Third, all standards — dismissal requirements, remedy procedures, benefit conditions — change by time and circumstance. Once more: this article is informational and not legal advice. For specific cases, always consult a licensed professional such as a certified labor attorney, a lawyer, or the Ministry of Employment and Labor (1350).

# Checklist When You Are Told You Are Dismissed - Deadlines, Scope, Before You Sign

## 1. Deadlines at a glance

| What | Deadline | Counted from | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dismissal notice | 30 days in advance (otherwise at least 30 days of ordinary wages) | Intended dismissal date | Labor Standards Act Art. 26 |
| Notice for consultation on managerial dismissal | At least 50 days before | Intended dismissal date | Art. 24(3) |
| Wrongful-dismissal remedy application | Within 3 months | Date of the wrongful dismissal | Art. 28(2) |
| Reexamination by the National Labor Relations Commission | Within 10 days | Date you are notified of the remedy order or dismissal decision | Art. 31(1) |
| Administrative lawsuit | Within 15 days | Date you are served with the reexamination decision | Art. 31(2) |
| Reporting non-compliance (worker) | Within 15 days | When the compliance deadline passes | Art. 33(8) |
| Enforcement charge | Up to KRW 30 million each, up to twice a year, two-year limit | Date of the first remedy order | Art. 33 |

If you do not challenge within the reexamination or lawsuit period, the decision becomes final (Art. 31(3)).

## 3. Checklist right after the notice

- [ ] I wrote down the dismissal date and marked the date three months later
- [ ] I received a **written** notice stating the grounds and timing (if not, I kept a record of my request)
- [ ] I backed up the notice messages from texts, email, and messengers to my own device
- [ ] I secured my employment contract, rules of employment (or collective agreement), and payslips
- [ ] For a disciplinary dismissal, I noted whether a disciplinary committee met and I had a chance to explain
- [ ] If there was no 30 days' notice, I checked whether notice pay applies (you can contest the dismissal even after receiving it)
- [ ] I checked whether a notice exception applies, such as less than three months of continuous service
- [ ] I booked a consultation with the Ministry of Employment and Labor (1350), a labor attorney, or a lawyer

## 5. Four-requirement check if you are selected for layoff

- [ ] Is urgent managerial necessity objectively recognized?
- [ ] Were there efforts to avoid dismissal, such as a hiring freeze, voluntary retirement, or reassignment?
- [ ] Were the selection criteria reasonable and fair, without discrimination on grounds such as sex?
- [ ] Was the worker representative notified at least 50 days before, with good-faith consultation?
