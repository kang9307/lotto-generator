How a Korean Car Lease Actually Works - Residual Value, Early Termination and Return Settlement

Where leases, long-term rentals and installment loans diverge, and which costs never appear in the monthly payment (Korean practice, as of August 2026)

Aug 19, 2026 Finance
This article is general information about how a product and its governing rules are structured, not financial advice. Actual terms and costs are set by your contract and by the finance company review. It describes car leasing practice in South Korea, which differs materially from lease markets elsewhere. The provisions and figures reflect public sources checked as of August 2026; product-level rates, residual percentages and per-unit charges must be confirmed in the contract itself.

When a quote lands in front of you, one line pulls the eye: the monthly payment. Yet the same car over the same term can come back from different providers at noticeably different monthly figures, and nothing on the page tells you whether the cheaper one is actually cheaper.

The reason is straightforward. A lease payment is not the price of the car divided by the number of months. The contract fixes in advance how much of the car will be left at maturity, subtracts that, adds the financing cost, and spreads the remainder. So the amount set aside for the end of the term, the residual value, can push the monthly payment as low as anyone likes. Nothing has been removed. It has been moved to the back.

This piece opens that structure. Where a lease parts company with an installment loan and a long-term rental, what the operating-versus-finance distinction actually changes, how residual value simultaneously drives your monthly payment, your choices at maturity and your early-termination exposure, and why return settlements produce arguments at the same few points every time. The tax arithmetic and the deductibility comparison across buying, leasing and long-term rental sit in Car Buying vs Leasing vs Long-Term Rental, and the annual vehicle tax itself is covered in Korea Vehicle Tax. Here we stay inside the lease contract.

1. What a lease actually lends you

A car lease in Korea is not a premium rental. It is a financing transaction. The Specialized Credit Finance Business Act divides specialized credit finance into credit card business, facility leasing, installment financing and new technology venture financing, and leasing belongs to facility leasing. The finance company buys the car you chose, hands over the right to use it, and you pay for that over a fixed term.

The Fair Trade Commission standard car lease terms state the relationship plainly. Title to the leased vehicle rests with the finance company, and the customer holds only the right of use and profit. There is an exception people routinely miss. The same terms allow registration in the customer name where the finance company consents, in which case the finance company must be recorded as first-ranking mortgagee.

  • So the flat claim that a leased car is always registered to the lessor is not accurate. That is the default, and registration can differ by product
  • Where title sits is not a formality. It decides the legal taxpayer and the shape of the insurance arrangement at the same time
  • Registration in the customer name still leaves a mortgage on the car, so it cannot be disposed of freely
  • The nature of the payment differs too. The standard terms define finance lease payments as principal installments plus interest, and operating lease payments as an amount paid to the finance company as a usage fee

Why does this first step matter so much? Because nearly everything that follows — who pays acquisition tax, whose name the insurance sits in, who gets the car at maturity, who absorbs the loss after an accident — derives from where title sits.

2. Lease, long-term rental and installment loan

The three are usually compared as a set, yet they rest on different statutes. An installment loan buys you the car while spreading the price. A lease is facility leasing under the Specialized Credit Finance Business Act. A long-term rental is a car rental business under the Passenger Transport Service Act. Different foundations, different terms bolted on top.

Insurance is where the split is sharpest. The standard car lease terms treat customer-managed insurance as the base case, with the customer selecting the insurer and handling coverage limits, enrollment, renewal and claims, and require own-damage cover written with the finance company as pledgee. There is also a route where, with the customer consent, the finance company becomes the policyholder and the customer the insured. Under the standard car rental terms, by contrast, the company rents out a vehicle it has already insured for liability and comprehensive cover, and the customer becomes an approved insured.

Read the table across and the character of each becomes visible. An installment loan is owning while repaying, a lease is using with financing attached, and a long-term rental is closer to buying a service. Compare them on the monthly payment alone and you will misread all three. The loan leaves an asset behind, the lease opens choices at maturity, and the rental bundles tax and insurance so that the payment covers a different set of items entirely.

One caution on the tax rows: they record who is legally liable, not who ultimately bears the cost. That distinction follows us through the rest of this article.

3. Operating lease versus finance lease

The pair appears in every product description and is rarely explained in terms of what it changes. The standard terms define it briefly. A finance lease is one where substantially all the risks and rewards of ownership of the leased asset transfer to the customer, and an operating lease is anything else.

Abstract as that sounds, every practical difference falls out of that one line.

  • Who carries residual risk. In a finance lease the risks and rewards have moved to the customer, so a car worth less than assumed at maturity is the customer problem. In an operating lease the finance company keeps a residual on its books and takes the car back
  • Mileage overage charges exist only in operating leases. The standard terms confine that item to operating leases, so a contracted mileage clause is itself a clue to the product type
  • Early-termination damages sit in different provisions. Terminating by purchasing the vehicle and terminating an operating lease by returning it are governed separately, with different caps
  • The payment has a different legal nature. Finance lease payments are principal and interest; operating lease payments are usage fees. Expense recognition and accounting treatment diverge from there

The accounting side changed once, decisively. Since K-IFRS 1116 took effect in 2019, a lessee no longer distinguishes operating from finance leases and recognizes a right-of-use asset and a lease liability for substantially all leases. The old off-balance-sheet appeal of operating leases no longer holds for entities applying K-IFRS. Choosing an operating lease to protect a debt ratio lost its basis at that point.

That is an accounting-standard matter, though, and lessor-side classification and tax treatment run on separate tracks. For an individual lessee, the far more direct questions are who carries residual risk and whether a mileage cap is attached.

4. Residual value, or why the monthly payment looks low

The most powerful variable in a lease quote is not the interest rate. It is the residual. The arithmetic explains why. What the payments recover is not the whole price but the price minus the residual value assumed at maturity, plus the financing cost on it. Raise the residual and there is less to recover; less to recover means a lower monthly payment.

The point to hold onto is that residual value enters in three places at once.

  1. The monthly payment — a higher residual lowers it
  2. The buyout price at maturity — keeping the car means paying that residual. Even for a re-lease, the standard terms set the new contract amount at the residual value of the vehicle at the end of the lease term, subject to mutual discussion where used-car market prices differ markedly
  3. The ceiling on early-termination exposure — where the customer terminates by purchasing the vehicle, the standard terms require that stipulated damages plus unrecovered principal stay within the sum of remaining lease payments and residual value

So a quote built on a high residual is cheap monthly and narrow at maturity. Buying the car needs a large lump sum; returning it leaves little to show for what you paid. A low residual reverses both: heavier monthly, lower barrier to keeping the car.

One decision rule makes quote comparison much easier. Settle first whether you intend to still be driving this car after maturity, then look at the residual.

  • If you intend to keep it, a lower residual works in your favour. The buyout is cheaper and the total moves closer to an installment loan
  • If you change cars on a cycle, a higher residual helps cash flow. In exchange, scrutinize the mileage cap and the return settlement terms much harder
  • If you have not decided, a high residual is risky. You can land in a zone where both buying and returning feel like a loss
  • If the actual used-car price at maturity exceeds the contracted residual, buying wins; if it falls short, returning wins. The structural catch is that you only learn that price once maturity arrives

Residual percentages vary by product, model, term and mileage cap, and no published standard value exists. The number on your quote is the number for your contract, so record the residual as both an amount and a percentage before signing.

5. Three paths at maturity - buy, return, re-lease

Start with something easy to overlook. Where the lease term ends normally, the standard terms provide that disposition of the vehicle follows the agreement between the customer and the finance company. In other words the standard terms do not hand you the three options; the individual contract does. Some products open all three, some open one.

Buying the car

You pay the contracted residual and take title. Because the car was registered to the finance company, the tax and registration costs of acquisition arise at this moment and were never part of the monthly payment. After transfer, annual vehicle tax is assessed on the new owner.

Returning it

You hand the car back and close out. In exchange comes appraisal and settlement: a depreciation amount based on condition, plus a mileage overage charge if the product is an operating lease. The next section deals with this separately.

Re-leasing

You sign again on the same car. The standard terms set the re-lease amount at the residual value of the vehicle at the end of the lease term, with mutual discussion where used-car prices differ markedly. Where the residual was set above the market, that clause acts as a cushion.

  • The contract must say which of the three is available. A verbal assurance is not a term
  • Check whether fees apply on top of the residual when buying
  • Obtain the depreciation schedule and per-kilometer overage rate at signing if you may return
  • If re-leasing is available, read how the residual will be determined for the new contract

6. Costs the monthly payment does not show

Two quotes with the same monthly figure can carry very different burdens, because of money paid at either end and items left outside the lease payment.

Deposits and prepayments

The names sound alike but they work differently. Under the standard terms a lease deposit works by having the finance company discount the corresponding amount from the monthly payment, with application ordered as costs, then interest, then principal. A prepayment can be handled in several ways.

  • Deducted from the purchase price of the car before the payment is calculated
  • Used to recalculate the lease payment, which may attract a fee
  • Divided across the remaining payment cycles and applied to the monthly payment
  • Applied against lease payments as they fall due

Paying the same amount up front, the method changes the total. The recalculation route in particular can carry a fee that never surfaces on the quote. If there is a prepayment on your quote, find where the handling method is written down.

What the lease payment leaves out

The standard terms provide that among the costs the customer bears in connection with purchase, delivery, maintenance, use, deregistration and scrapping, anything not included in the lease payment, together with public charges, falls on the customer. Fines and administrative penalties are named explicitly. Where legislation or an administrative measure changes those public charges, the company settles the difference and either charges or refunds it.

  • Tax — the legal taxpayer may be the owner, but if it is not inside the payment it arrives as a separate bill. Confirm inclusion on the quote
  • Insurance premiums — inside the payment or outside it, depending on who holds the policy. Coverage itself is a separate question, covered in the car insurance guide
  • Maintenance and consumables — some products bundle them, some do not. This is the single most common mismatch when comparing quotes
  • Registration and delivery costs — check for one-off items charged at the start
  • Mid-term change fees — whether adjusting the mileage cap or transferring the contract attracts a charge

7. Why early termination is expensive

This is where most disputes originate, and the structure explains the price.

Procedure first. The standard terms require a customer who wants to terminate to notify the finance company 10 days before the intended termination date. The finance company has its own grounds for termination, listed as failure to take delivery of the vehicle, breach of the prohibited acts in the terms, failure to perform obligations, and failure to insure or two or more missed insurance premium payments. That insurance sits among the termination grounds is worth remembering.

Now the money. Where the customer terminates by purchasing the vehicle, the stipulated damages are calculated by multiplying the unrecovered principal as of the termination date by the agreed rate, and damages plus unrecovered principal must remain within the total of remaining lease payments and residual value. Where an operating lease is terminated by returning the vehicle, the damages follow the same structure and are capped at the total of remaining lease payments.

The pivotal term is unrecovered principal. Early in the contract, little principal has been recovered. At the same rate, an early termination multiplies a much bigger number. Early termination is expensive because of timing, not only because of the rate.

The rates themselves vary widely. In results published on 19 December 2023, the Korea Consumer Agency reviewed the contract terms of 15 providers of car operating lease products and found penalty rates spread from 40 percent to 90 percent, more than a twofold gap, with 9 of them setting a top rate of 80 percent or higher. The agency also noted products where terminating early in the term produced little difference between the penalty and the total remaining lease payments.

  • Caps exist, but the rate decides the real burden. Read the rate and the formula together in the contract
  • Taking a long term and exiting midway rarely works out. Early exit is designed to be the costliest point
  • If you are unsure how long you will keep the car, shortening the term beats planning around termination
  • Check whether transfer to another party is available as an alternative. Transfers can carry their own review and fee
  • Because missed insurance premiums are a termination ground, settle at the outset who manages the policy

8. Where return settlements go wrong

If early termination is a problem of timing, return is a problem of appraisal, and appraisal resists precise measurement, which is why the same points recur.

The procedure in the standard terms runs as follows. An appraiser assesses the condition and performance of the returned vehicle and issues a vehicle appraisal report. Where grounds for depreciation exist, the amount is computed by applying the sum of the depreciation rates to the expected value at the time of return, explained to the customer, and charged only after consent is obtained. Operating leases carry a separate mileage overage charge, computed as excess distance multiplied by a per-kilometer rate.

Return can also be refused. The listed grounds are damage or unauthorized modification preventing normal operation; deliberate concealment or manipulation of defects, repair history or mileage; flooding, rollover, fire or cutting that makes repair impossible; failure to perform the obligations set out in the terms; and inability to register at the time of return.

The same Korea Consumer Agency review flagged structural problems on the return side. Among the providers surveyed, 8 applied depreciation rates that rise as the lease term progresses, depreciation could arise even where the customer was not at fault, such as in a third-party accident, and where the insurance payout fell short of the depreciation amount the difference was sought from the consumer. The agency said it would recommend that providers bring the damages calculation framework and the scope of consumer liability for appraisal and depreciation on return to a reasonable level.

That gives a usable rule for choosing a return-based product. Assume that part of a low monthly payment is being paid for through the return settlement terms. If you cannot obtain the depreciation schedule at signing, you are buying a product whose end-of-term cost is unknowable.

9. Business users and individuals - only where they diverge

The same product produces entirely different arithmetic for a business and for an individual, for one reason: a business may treat lease payments as a deductible expense and an individual may not.

On the business side the answer depends far more on tax requirements than on the lease terms. Expenses related to company passenger vehicles carry limits and conditions, and the recognized amount depends on whether those conditions were met. Deduction limits, the calculation method and the comparison of buying, leasing and long-term rental for tax purposes are covered in Car Buying vs Leasing vs Long-Term Rental, so this article does not repeat them. What suits a particular business is for a qualified tax professional to determine.

For an individual who is not running a business the arithmetic is different. There is no tax benefit, so if there is a reason to lease it has to be something other than tax.

  • Cash flow — a smaller sum up front, spread monthly. The total is usually larger
  • Replacement cycle — if you change cars on a schedule, returning at maturity replaces the work of selling
  • Outsourced upkeep — maintenance-inclusive products shift the burden, at a price built into the payment
  • It does not show — a leased car is registered as an ordinary passenger car, unlike a long-term rental registered as a commercial rental vehicle

The reasons an individual might avoid a lease are equally clear. Early-termination exposure and return-settlement risk land entirely on the individual. A business offsets part of it through deductions; an individual has no such cushion. If you intend to keep the car for a long time and can raise the lump sum, the range in which a lease wins on total cost is not wide. For a sense of used-car pricing and how depreciation runs, the used car buying guide is a useful companion.

Read the table down and one thing stands out. Most of it lives in annexes and detailed clauses. The quote sheet carries a monthly figure and a term; the items that determine what you actually pay sit at the back of the contract. Asking for the annexes before signing is the single most effective precaution with this product.

11. What could not be confirmed

Gathered here are the points that could not be pinned down for this article.

  • The Fair Trade Commission reference number and latest amendment history of the standard car lease terms - the provisions were read in a published full text, but the standard-terms number and the most recent amendment date could not be fixed, which is why article numbers are not cited in the body
  • Current lease classification for entities applying non-K-IFRS accounting standards - the K-IFRS 1116 change was confirmed; treatment under other frameworks was not
  • The exact plate-marking rules for rental-business vehicles - the difference in registration category was confirmed, but the underlying provision governing plate characters was not, so no characters are stated
  • Product-level residual percentages, per-kilometer rates and depreciation schedules - no published standard values exist. These have to come from the contract and its annexes
  • How lease usage is reflected in individual credit scoring - accounts differ by product type and no published credit-bureau criteria were confirmed
  • How acquisition tax is actually built into lease pricing by product - the payer under the terms was confirmed; how it enters the payment calculation varies and could not be established from public sources
  • Outcomes following the 2023 Korea Consumer Agency recommendation - the announcement that recommendations would be issued was confirmed; how provider terms changed afterwards was not

Leaving a figure blank is better than filling it with something plausible. The items above are best confirmed directly with the finance company you are contracting with.

12. Frequently asked questions

If I crash a leased car, what happens to my insurance record?

It depends on how the policy is structured. The standard car lease terms provide both for the customer selecting the insurer and managing the policy, and for the finance company becoming the policyholder with the customer as the insured where the customer consents. The outcome differs accordingly, so read the insurance clauses first and confirm the handling of any individual claim with the insurer.

Can I sell the car during the lease term?

Not freely, because title rests with the finance company. Even where the car is registered in the customer name, the finance company is recorded as first-ranking mortgagee, so the position is the same. Ending the arrangement means going through early termination, or a transfer where the contract allows one.

If I drive less than the contracted mileage, do I get money back?

The standard terms carry a charge for exceeding the cap but no confirmed provision for refunding under-use. Whether shortfalls are settled is left to the individual contract, so check it there.

Which costs less overall, a lease or an installment loan?

It does not generalize. Price, term, residual, rates and incidental costs all differ by contract, and for a business the deductibility question reshuffles the comparison. Structurally, though, if you are confident you will keep the car after maturity, the range where an installment loan wins on total cost is wide. Decide by putting two real quotes side by side on a total-cost basis.

If I have no time to read the whole contract, what is the minimum?

Four things: the residual value, the early-termination damages rate, the contracted mileage with its per-kilometer rate, and the itemized list of what the lease payment includes. Those four largely determine what you pay at maturity and on an early exit.
