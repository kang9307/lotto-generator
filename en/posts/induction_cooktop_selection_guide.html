Complete Guide to Choosing an Induction Cooktop - Types, Burners, Power, Installation

From how induction differs from radiant heat to burner count, power capacity, dedicated wiring, and magnetic cookware - a practical selection checklist

Jul 2, 2026 Life & Practical

Intro: "Electric Cooktop" Is Not One Thing

Once you start shopping for something to replace a gas range, you quickly discover that the term "electric cooktop" actually covers two different technologies. One is induction (IH, induction heating), and the other is the radiant (electric coil) type, often sold as a ceramic or halogen cooktop. Both look similar at a glance - a smooth glass surface - but the way they generate heat, and the trade-offs, installation requirements, and cookware compatibility that follow from it, are quite different.

Based on generally available information as of July 2026, this guide walks through the difference in heating principle between induction and radiant cooktops, induction's pros and cons, selection criteria such as burner count, power capacity, and dedicated wiring, the difference between built-in and freestanding units, how to check for magnetic cookware, running-cost concepts, and maintenance notes. Power specifications, wiring requirements, and electricity rates vary by country, product, and each home's existing electrical setup. This article summarizes general practical information; always confirm the product's spec sheet and consult a licensed electrician before any installation or wiring work.

1. Induction vs Radiant - Different Heating Principles

1.1 How a Radiant (Ceramic) Cooktop Works

A radiant cooktop runs current through a heating element beneath the glass surface, heating the element itself. That heat then passes through the glass - by radiation and conduction - into the pan sitting on top. Like a gas burner, the surface (specifically the glass directly above the element) gets hot first, and heat transfers from there to the cookware, which makes it the closest electric cousin to gas in principle. Its major advantage is that almost any cookware material works, as long as the base makes reasonably good contact with the glass.

1.2 How Induction (IH) Works

An induction cooktop passes high-frequency current through a coil beneath the surface to generate a magnetic field. That field induces eddy currents in the magnetic (ferrous) base of a pan placed on top, and those currents generate resistive heat directly within the pan's base itself. In other words, the pan itself becomes the heating element, not the glass surface. Because of this principle, induction only works with magnetic metal cookware, and the glass surface itself is not directly heated except by heat conducted back from the pan.

1.3 What the Difference in Principle Means in Practice

This fundamental difference translates into real, felt differences in use. Because heat is generated directly in the cookware, induction is generally reported to respond and heat faster - for example, bringing water to a boil - and residual heat on the glass surface tends to be lower, which is described as reducing burn risk right after cooking. A radiant cooktop, by contrast, heats the surface itself, so it differs from induction in response speed and residual heat, but it offers the advantage of working with virtually any cookware. Neither is simply "better" - the right choice depends on your priorities: speed and safety versus cookware flexibility and installation simplicity.

2. Induction's Advantages and Disadvantages

2.1 Advantages

Commonly cited advantages of induction include the following. First, fast heating response: because heat is generated directly in the cookware, response time - for tasks like boiling water - is generally reported to be quick. Second, safety: since the surface itself is not directly heated (aside from conducted heat from the pan), the burn risk from residual heat after a burner is turned off is described as lower than on a radiant cooktop, and because the unit will not activate without compatible cookware on it, an empty surface is less likely to become hot. Third, easier cleaning: since the surface tends to stay cooler, spills are less likely to bake on and harden compared with a radiant cooktop.

2.2 Disadvantages

Commonly cited drawbacks also exist. First, cookware restriction: only pots and pans with a magnetic base work, so existing aluminum, copper, or glass cookware often cannot be reused. Second, noise: some units and power settings are reported to produce a faint hum or vibration from the internal cooling fan or the magnetic field itself. Third, electromagnetic field concerns: since induction relies on a low-frequency magnetic field, consumers sensitive to this factor often weigh it in their decision - though it is also widely noted that products certified to domestic and international safety standards are managed within regulated limits. How much any of these trade-offs matter in practice can vary by product and household.

3. Selection Criterion 1 - Burner Count

Burner count is usually the first thing to decide. A single person or someone who cooks simply is often reported to manage fine with two burners, while a household preparing multiple dishes at once tends to find three or more burners more convenient. However, more burners generally mean a larger cooktop footprint and higher total power draw when used simultaneously, so weigh this against your counter space and the power-capacity considerations below. Some models offer a "zone bridge" feature that combines two burners into one larger cooking zone for oversized cookware, but whether this feature exists varies by product.

4. Selection Criterion 2 - Power Capacity (kW) and Dedicated Wiring

4.1 Checking Rated Power Consumption

An induction cooktop's power is specified as the maximum output per burner and the maximum total power draw when all burners run simultaneously (rated power consumption). This figure varies significantly between products: some lower-power, energy-saving models are designed to run comfortably within standard household outlet and wiring capacity, while other high-output models - often imported or premium lines - prioritize cooking power. Higher-output units can offer faster cooking and more headroom, but they also demand more current from your home's wiring.

4.2 When Dedicated Wiring May Be Required

If a unit's maximum power consumption exceeds what your existing kitchen wiring, outlet, and breaker are rated to handle, you may need a new dedicated circuit installed through licensed electrical work. Conversely, lower-power models that fall within existing wiring capacity can often be plugged in and used without any additional work. This threshold depends entirely on your country's standard voltage and wiring codes, the age of the building, the spare capacity on your electrical panel, and the appliance's rated power draw - so it cannot be generalized. Before purchase, check both the product's rated power consumption and the spare capacity your home's panel can support, and when in doubt, consult the retailer or a licensed electrician in advance.

4.3 Automatic Power Adjustment With Multiple Burners

Some products are reported to automatically reduce output on each burner if running several at maximum power simultaneously would exceed the unit's total allowed draw. Exactly how this works varies by manufacturer and model, so if you frequently run multiple burners at high power at once, it is worth confirming this behavior before buying.

5. Selection Criterion 3 - Built-In vs Freestanding

Installation format falls into two broad categories. A built-in unit is recessed into a cutout made in the countertop, giving a seamless look with the kitchen and often freeing space below for drawers or an oven - but it requires confirming precise cutout dimensions in advance, and installation difficulty and cost tend to be higher. A freestanding (countertop) unit sits on top of the counter and plugs into an outlet, requiring no cutout, which makes it simple to install and convenient for renters or a quick swap - but it looks less integrated with the counter and tends to come in smaller, lower-power configurations. New construction or renovation projects typically lean toward built-in, while rental housing or a fast, simple replacement typically leans toward freestanding.

6. Selection Criterion 4 - Checking for Magnetic Cookware

If you choose induction, you must confirm whether your existing cookware will actually work. The simplest method is to hold a magnet to the bottom of a pot or pan. If the magnet sticks firmly, it is induction-compatible; if it slides off or does not stick at all, it is not. Cast iron, enameled cast iron, and magnetic-grade stainless steel are generally usable, while plain aluminum, copper, glass, and non-magnetic stainless steel are not. If the base is stamped with a coiled-spring symbol, the manufacturer has officially marked it induction-compatible. A flat base also matters - a warped or concave bottom reduces contact area with the coil and can lower heating efficiency, so cookware with a significantly uneven base is best avoided.

7. Running Cost - Commonly Reported Trends

Induction is commonly described as having higher heating efficiency than radiant cooktops, since it heats cookware directly via a magnetic field with less energy loss than the surface radiation-and-conduction approach of a radiant unit. As a result, for the same cooking task, induction's electricity consumption is often compared as lower than a radiant cooktop's. That said, actual electricity cost depends on cooking habits, usage time, burner power settings, and each household's rate plan and contract terms, so it is difficult to generalize an absolute savings figure. For a realistic comparison, match the appliance's rated power-consumption figures against your own cooking patterns.

8. Brands and Price Range - General Concepts

The induction and electric-cooktop market includes major domestic appliance brands, premium imported built-in brands, and smaller budget-oriented brands. In general, price tends to rise with more burners, higher rated output, and more added features such as touch sensitivity, zone bridging, or residual-heat indicators. Conversely, budget models with two burners and basic functionality tend to be more affordable. Specific brand features and pricing change constantly with the market, so this guide does not recommend particular brands or models and instead summarizes the general factors that drive price. When actually purchasing, compare current products and reviews at the time.

9. Maintenance and Precautions

Because both induction and radiant units share a glass surface, some care points apply to either. First, residual heat on the surface or in the cookware itself right after cooking can cause burns, so if there is a residual-heat indicator, wait until it turns off. Second, food with high sugar content can bake onto the surface and harden, potentially causing scratches, so it is generally recommended to let the surface cool before removing residue with a proper cleaner or scraper. Third, dropping a heavy object on the surface or dragging cookware forcefully can crack the glass, so handle with care. Fourth, on induction units, leaving a damp cloth or a metal object on a burner can cause malfunction or unwanted heating, so avoid placing anything unrelated to cooking on the surface. Always defer to the product's own manual for specific care instructions, as this is the safest guidance.

10. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. What is the difference between induction and a radiant (ceramic) cooktop?

A radiant cooktop heats an element beneath the glass, and that heat passes through the glass to the pan. Induction uses a magnetic field to turn the pan's own base into the heat source. Induction is generally reported to heat faster with less residual surface heat, but it requires magnetic, induction-compatible cookware.

Q2. Do I always need dedicated wiring for an induction cooktop?

It depends on the unit's maximum power draw and your existing wiring capacity. High-output units may require a dedicated circuit, while lower-power units often work on existing wiring. Check the spec sheet and your panel's spare capacity, and consult a licensed electrician when in doubt.

Q3. How do I know if my existing cookware works on induction?

Hold a magnet to the base. If it sticks firmly, it works; if it slides off, it does not. Cast iron, enameled cast iron, and magnetic stainless steel generally work; plain aluminum, copper, glass, and non-magnetic stainless steel do not. Also confirm the base is flat.

Q4. Should I choose built-in or freestanding?

Built-in is usually preferred for new construction or renovation where a countertop cutout is feasible; freestanding is usually preferred for rentals or a quick, simple swap. Built-in costs more to install; freestanding units tend to be smaller and lower-powered.

Q5. Does induction really save on electricity compared to radiant?

Induction's higher heating efficiency is commonly cited as resulting in lower electricity consumption for the same cooking task compared with radiant. However, actual cost depends on cooking habits, usage time, and rate plans, so an absolute savings figure cannot be guaranteed.

Conclusion - Set Your Priorities First, Then Check the Spec Sheet

There is no single correct answer to whether induction or radiant is "better". If fast heating, safety, and easy cleaning matter most to you, induction is likely the better fit; if you want to keep using your existing cookware and prefer a simpler installation, radiant may suit you better. If you settle on induction, checking burner count, rated power consumption, whether dedicated wiring is needed, built-in versus freestanding format, and whether your cookware is magnetic - in that order - is a reliable way to avoid costly mistakes. Because power specifications, wiring requirements, and electricity rates all vary by country, product, and your home's electrical setup, always confirm the specific product's spec sheet and your home's wiring situation before making a final decision.
