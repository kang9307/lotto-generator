The Complete Air Purifier Buying Guide: CADR, Room Size, and Filter Life

From matching CADR to room size through HEPA grades, filter replacement costs, noise, electricity use, and pairing with ventilation - a practical guide to check before you buy

Jul 24, 2026 Life & Practical

Introduction: What to Check Before the Spec Sheet Talks You Into Anything

Air purifier listings are full of unfamiliar terms - CADR, standard coverage area, HEPA grade, ion generation. Most buyers end up deciding based on star ratings and brand recognition, and in the process miss the two things that matter most: whether the unit is actually sized for the room, and how much the replacement filters will really cost. An air purifier is a multi-year appliance that also comes with an ongoing consumable - the filter - so setting a clear standard before you buy pays off for years afterward.

This guide, current as of July 2026, walks through what to check in order: matching CADR to room size, what filter types and HEPA grades actually mean, filter replacement cycles and maintenance cost, how much the extra features are really worth, noise and electricity use, installation and usage tips, how an air purifier relates to ventilation and your AC's filter, and finally, selection criteria and a checklist by room and purpose. It does not recommend any specific brand or model - the goal is a set of standards you can apply to any product you're considering.

1. Matching CADR to Room Size

1.1 What CADR Actually Measures

CADR (Clean Air Delivery Rate) is the core performance metric: the volume of clean air a unit delivers per minute (cubic meters per minute). For the same size room, a lower-CADR unit takes longer to clean the air and has to run at high fan speed more of the time, which drives up both noise and power draw. CADR tells you how fast and how much air a unit actually moves - it says nothing about how finely that air is filtered, which is what the HEPA grade covers instead.

1.2 The Standard Coverage Area Formula and the 130% Rule

The common convention in Korea is to multiply CADR by 7.7 to get the "standard coverage area." This is the area over which, with one air change per hour, running the unit for 10 minutes can cut indoor particle concentration to 50% of its starting level, based on a 2.4-meter ceiling. The catch is that sizing a unit to exactly match your room's footprint using this number tends to feel underpowered in practice. Korea's Consumer Agency recommends choosing a unit whose standard coverage area is at least 130% of the room you'll actually use it in - for a 50-square-meter living room, that means looking for a unit rated for 65 square meters or more. Buying that extra headroom means the unit can maintain your target air quality without constantly running at maximum fan speed, which also works in your favor on noise and electricity use.

1.3 Converting Floor Area to Room Size

The more important point is that what you're sizing against is the actual room you'll run the unit in, not your entire home. If you'll only use it in the living room, size against the living room's floor area; if it's for a bedroom, use the bedroom's footprint. Matching CADR to the floor size of your whole apartment when you'll actually run the unit in a single room leads to buying a far bigger, noisier, and more expensive unit than you need.

2. Filter Types: Pre-Filter, HEPA, Activated Carbon, and Electrostatic

2.1 The Pre-Filter

The pre-filter is a mesh-like first-stage filter that catches hair and larger dust particles. It's washable and reusable more or less indefinitely, so there's no separate cost to keep it working - just rinse or vacuum it roughly every 2-4 weeks. If it's allowed to clog, though, airflow to the filters behind it drops and overall cleaning efficiency suffers, so don't skip this step.

2.2 HEPA Filters and What the Grade Actually Means (H11 vs H13)

The HEPA filter is the workhorse that physically traps fine particles. Grades follow the European EN 1822 standard, and a higher grade number means a higher removal rate for 0.3-micron particles. As a general rule, H11 removes roughly 95% of 0.3-micron particles, while H13 removes up to about 99.95%. In other words, the grade difference isn't about whether small particles get caught at all - it's about what percentage of them do.

There's a real trade-off worth knowing about. A higher grade means a denser filter material, which increases resistance to airflow; pushing the same volume of air through it requires a stronger motor, which usually means more noise and higher power draw. Most household air purifiers use grades in the E11-H13 range; anything above that (ULPA-class) is mostly reserved for semiconductor fabs and medical or research facilities that need far stricter cleanliness. For a typical home, H11-H12 is often plenty; stepping up to H13 or higher makes sense mainly if someone in the household has allergies or asthma and can live with the added noise and power draw.

2.3 Activated Carbon Filters and Electrostatic Precipitators

The activated carbon filter's job is to adsorb gas-phase pollutants - volatile organic compounds (VOCs), formaldehyde, and everyday odors. Think of the HEPA filter as handling particles and the activated carbon filter as handling smells and gases. Activated carbon can't be washed, and once its adsorption capacity fills up, its performance drops sharply, so it needs periodic replacement.

Electrostatic precipitators charge dust particles so they stick to an oppositely charged collection plate. This design is particularly good at capturing very fine particles like cigarette smoke and vehicle exhaust, and since the plate can be rinsed with water and reused more or less indefinitely, there's no ongoing filter cost. The trade-off is that if the plate isn't cleaned regularly, accumulated dust can be released back into the room, and some older designs produced ozone as a byproduct of the electrical charging process. Newer designs aim to suppress ozone generation, but it's worth checking for ozone-reduction certification or labeling before buying one.

3. Filter Replacement Cycles and Cost of Ownership

3.1 Recommended Replacement Intervals by Filter Type

As a general guideline, wash the pre-filter every 2-4 weeks, replace the activated carbon filter every 3-6 months, and replace the HEPA filter every 6-12 months. These figures assume average conditions - if you have pets, cook frequently, or live somewhere with heavy air pollution, shortening the interval is the safer call. If a filter starts producing a musty smell or the unit's replacement indicator lights up, replace it right away rather than waiting for the scheduled interval.

3.2 Estimating Your Real Annual Cost

The real cost of an air purifier doesn't end at the sticker price. HEPA and activated carbon filters (sometimes combined into a single composite filter) are consumables you'll buy once or twice a year, and the price of genuine replacement filters varies a lot between brands and models. A cheap unit paired with expensive proprietary filters can end up costing more in total cost of ownership over a few years than a pricier unit with cheaper filters. Before buying, check the manufacturer's site or product page for the genuine replacement filter price and the recommended replacement interval, and rough out your expected annual filter cost. A design that relies more heavily on washable pre-filters or an electrostatic plate will generally carry a lighter ongoing cost.

4. How Much Are the Extra Features Really Worth?

4.1 Air Quality Sensors and Auto Mode

Most units include a PM sensor that detects indoor dust concentration. The sensor itself doesn't clean anything - it measures and displays a concentration, and auto mode uses that reading to adjust fan speed on its own. That's genuinely convenient, since you don't have to keep adjusting the unit manually, but if dust builds up at the sensor's intake, it can read higher than the actual concentration, so cleaning the sensor area periodically helps keep the reading accurate.

4.2 Deodorizing and Harmful Gas Removal

Deodorizing and gas-removal claims mostly come down to the activated carbon filter's adsorption capacity. It has a real effect on everyday odors - pet smells, cooking smells, cigarette smoke - and on some VOCs. But since activated carbon's effectiveness drops sharply once it's saturated, how well you stick to the replacement interval discussed above is really what determines whether this feature delivers.

4.3 Ionizer and Sterilization Features: How Solid Is the Evidence?

Ion, plasma, and UV sterilization features get heavy marketing emphasis, but the strength of the evidence varies a lot by feature, and it's worth keeping that in perspective. Ionizing works by charging particles so they become heavier and stick to nearby surfaces like floors and walls - which can reduce the particle count floating in the air, but it doesn't actually remove particles from the room, and critics point out that whatever settles on surfaces stays there unless you clean regularly. Some ionizing and electrostatic designs can also generate ozone as a byproduct, raising concerns that they may be adding to indoor air quality problems rather than solving them. South Korean regulators have in fact taken enforcement action against exaggerated ionizer marketing claims. None of this means these features are worthless, but the evidence doesn't support ranking them above verified metrics like CADR, HEPA grade, and filter replacement cost.

5. Noise, Electricity, and Filterless Designs

5.1 Noise Levels for a Bedroom

Even small noises are noticeable while you're trying to sleep. As a general benchmark, around 30dB or below is quiet enough that most people won't notice it while sleeping, while noise above 35dB starts to bother a growing share of people trying to fall asleep. The quietest setting on typical air purifiers ranges roughly from 28dB to 45dB, with meaningful variation between models, so if you plan to run one overnight in a bedroom, check the noise spec at "sleep mode" or lowest fan speed specifically - not the number quoted for maximum output.

5.2 Electricity Cost

Air purifiers don't draw much power to begin with, so running one 24 hours a day typically adds somewhere in the range of roughly $4-8 (about 5,000-11,000 KRW) a month to the electricity bill - modest compared with larger appliances, so there's little reason to hesitate about leaving one running continuously on cost grounds alone. That said, running at maximum fan speed more often does draw more power, which is another reason to buy more CADR headroom than you strictly need, as covered above - it reduces how often the unit has to run flat out, helping both your electricity bill and the noise level.

5.3 A Caution About Filterless (Ion/Ozone) Designs

Some units skip the HEPA filter entirely and claim to clean the air using ions, ozone, or OH-radical technology alone. These designs avoid filter replacement costs, but as covered above, the evidence for their particle-removal effectiveness is comparatively weak, and there's a real possibility of ozone generation - both reasons for caution. If you're considering a filterless unit, check for relevant safety certification and any disclosed ozone output, and in a home with a pregnant person, infant, or someone with a respiratory condition, a proven HEPA-based design is the safer default.

6. Installation and Usage Tips

6.1 Placement

Don't push the unit flush against a wall - leave at least 10-30cm of clearance around the intake and outlet. If airflow is blocked by a wall or furniture, you'll get less real-world performance out of even a well-specced unit. A spot near the center of the room or close to where people actually spend time works better than a corner, and a dry room like a living room or bedroom is easier on the filters than somewhere humid, like near a bathroom.

6.2 Pairing With Ventilation

An air purifier recirculates indoor air, reducing particulates and some odors - it doesn't bring in fresh outdoor air or eliminate carbon dioxide. So no matter how good the unit is, it can't substitute for periodic ventilation. Since outdoor pollutants come in along with fresh air while a window is open, the efficient approach is to turn the purifier off for that brief window, then turn it back on once the window is closed.

6.3 Running It on High-Pollution Days

Even on days when outdoor air quality is poor, indoor CO2 and odors keep building up, so skipping ventilation entirely isn't the answer. Instead, pick a relatively better window during the day and open a window for about 3-5 minutes, then close it and turn the purifier back on. It's worth checking real-time air quality data from a public source to time this. On unusually bad days, running one fan-speed level above auto mode, or briefly switching to max speed right after you get home, can bring indoor concentration down faster.

7. Air Purifier vs. Ventilation vs. Your AC's Filter

These three play distinct roles. An air purifier continuously recirculates indoor air, filtering out particulates, some odors, and a portion of VOCs, but since it doesn't bring in new air, it can't lower CO2 concentration. Ventilation does the opposite - it brings in fresh outdoor air and lowers both CO2 and the concentration of pollutants that have built up indoors - but on a high-pollution day it risks bringing outdoor pollutants in along with it, which is why it needs to be kept brief. The filter built into an air conditioner is typically a coarse mesh meant to protect the cooling coil; it screens out larger debris but isn't certified against a CADR-style performance standard. An AC is built to lower temperature, not to filter air down to purifier-grade cleanliness, so having an AC filter doesn't remove the case for a dedicated air purifier. The best approach uses all three for what they're actually good at: brief ventilation to manage CO2, an air purifier running continuously to manage particulates and odor, and the AC handling temperature.

8. Selection Criteria by Room and Purpose, Plus a Checklist

8.1 Choosing by Space

For a bedroom you'll run overnight, prioritizing low sleep-mode noise over maximum CADR is the more sensible call. For an open space like a living room, you can either buy one high-capacity unit rated at 130% or more of the actual floor area, or split the load across two mid-size units depending on the room's layout. If you have pets or cook often and odor control matters, look for a generous activated carbon capacity; if a family member has allergies or a respiratory condition, a higher HEPA grade (H13+) is worth considering, with the trade-off of higher noise, power draw, and filter cost factored in.

8.2 Final Checklist Before You Buy

Working through these in order keeps you anchored to what matters instead of getting swayed by a spec sheet.

  • Have you calculated the actual floor area (in square meters) of the room you'll use it in?
  • Is the standard coverage area at least 130% of that room's floor area?
  • Have you confirmed the HEPA grade (H11 or higher) and whether it includes an activated carbon filter?
  • Have you checked the recommended replacement interval, genuine filter price, and estimated annual maintenance cost for each filter?
  • For bedroom use, have you checked the noise spec (dB) at sleep mode or lowest fan speed?
  • Have you checked power draw and the estimated monthly electricity cost (typically not a major burden)?
  • Are you treating ionizer or sterilization features as a minor bonus rather than a core deciding factor?
  • For a filterless design, have you checked for relevant certification and disclosed ozone output?
  • Have you planned for regular ventilation alongside it, since an air purifier doesn't replace that?

9. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. How do you calculate what room size an air purifier can handle?

Multiply CADR by 7.7 to get the standard coverage area. Korea's Consumer Agency recommends a unit rated at least 130% of the room's actual size - a unit rated for 65 square meters or more for a 50-square-meter living room.

Q2. Is HEPA H13 always better than H11?

H13 removes more (roughly 99.95% vs. about 95% for H11), but denser filters mean more noise and power draw. H11-H12 is often plenty for a typical home.

Q3. How often should filters be replaced?

Wash the pre-filter every 2-4 weeks; replace activated carbon every 3-6 months; replace HEPA every 6-12 months. Shorten this in a polluted environment.

Q4. Do ionizer or sterilization features actually work?

The evidence is thin for many claims - ionizing mostly relocates particles onto surfaces rather than removing them, and some designs can generate ozone. Treat it as a minor bonus.

Q5. If I have an air purifier, do I still need to ventilate?

Yes - it can't lower CO2. Even on bad-air days, ventilate briefly, turning the purifier off during that window and back on once it's closed.

Conclusion: The Combination Matters More Than Any Single Spec

An air purifier can't be judged on CADR alone, or HEPA grade alone. It becomes something you're happy with for years only once you've matched CADR to your actual room size, chosen a trustworthy HEPA-plus-activated-carbon combination, made peace with the ongoing filter cost, prioritized low noise if it's for a bedroom, and kept perspective on features whose evidence is thin. The surest way to apply all of that is to use the formulas and checklist in this guide to work through an actual product's spec sheet, item by item.

Certification details, filter prices, and electricity costs vary by timing, region, and your specific rate plan, so check the manufacturer's official materials and current public guidance before you buy and use one.
