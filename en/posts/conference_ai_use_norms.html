Tool or Stand-In - What Breaks When AI Answers the Questions for You

Ask what a given session exists to verify, and the line for AI use draws itself

Sep 2, 2026 Trends & Culture

The talk ended and a question came from the floor. The answer took a dozen seconds or more to arrive, and during that time the speaker was looking at a laptop screen rather than at the person who had asked. What came back was not an answer to that question but an answer about the topic in general. What to call that scene is the subject of this piece.

This is not an argument for not using AI. It is also not an argument that using it is fine. Neither of those is a rule you can apply to an actual situation. The rule you can apply sits one level down. Some uses help you prepare, and some uses substitute for the person who was supposed to be in the room. Where the line between those two falls, and where else that same line has already been drawn, is what this covers.

This article does not name a conference, a speaker or an institution. The account that prompted it did not name them either, and the interest here is not in whether individuals behaved badly but in what such use does to a procedure. Policies quoted below were checked against the documents each organisation was publishing as of September 2026.

The trigger - a question period at some conference

In late August 2026 an account of a conference visit went up on an English-language technical community and was picked up in Korean forums as well. The writer recorded two things.

The first was the audience. During the talks, most of the room was doing something else on a phone or a laptop. The second is the real subject. During the question period of a panel session, the panellists other than the writer were transcribing the questions into their laptops and reading out answers generated by AI. The person asking was a researcher who had worked in that field for a long time.

The writer called this laziness, and a discourtesy to the intelligence of the person asking. In the same breath they drew a line: using AI for repetitive work such as translation or editing a paper is one thing, and handing over the judgement and the answer wholesale is another. This article is an attempt to extend that line.

The discussion around the post was the more interesting half. The most-supported comment said, in effect, that once a language model starts speaking in place of a human response, the commenter wants out of the exchange. Someone else described faculty who penalise students for using AI while grading with it themselves. From the other side came the observation that audiences have been distracted since long before AI, and that AI did not invent the behaviour. That objection is correct. So this article sets the audience aside and looks only at the question period. The first is an old problem. The second has a different structure.

The line between a tool and a stand-in

Three questions are enough to separate a tool from a stand-in, and you need all three.

  • Could I have produced this from scratch? If it made something you can do faster, it is a tool. If it did something you cannot do, it is a stand-in
  • Can I review the output and point at what is wrong in it? If you cannot point at anything, you did not produce that artefact. You waved it through
  • Did this setting ask for an artefact, or for a person capable of producing that artefact? If it is the second, the quality of the result does not matter. The setting has been voided

The third one is what actually decides. The first two turn on individual conscientiousness, so the answers vary by person, but the third is a property of the setting, so it has exactly one answer. A journal does not ask who copy-edited the manuscript, because what the manuscript is asked for is a manuscript. A question period, by contrast, is not asking for an answer. It is asking for someone who can answer.

Laying the same activities out on a timeline makes it sharper. In the preparation window you can make something, look at it, throw it away and make it again. In the live window you cannot. Any use with no room for review to happen falls almost always on the stand-in side.

What is being doneWhat is replacedCan review get in?Which side

Note that the two right-hand columns move together. If review can get in, it is usually a tool. If it cannot, it is usually a stand-in. The exception is where the third question intervenes. Sometimes review is perfectly possible and the thing is still a stand-in. Peer review is the case in point. You can rewrite a review over several days, but what the process is buying is that reviewer's judgement, so no amount of polishing changes the classification. Time is not what decides. The purpose of the setting is.

Why the question period is different

A talk is a prepared artefact. Nobody generally asks who helped with the slides or how many times a sentence was rewritten. There was a reason that was acceptable. A question period comes after it.

Questions at an academic talk are not asked only to obtain answers. They check three things. Does the speaker know the limits of their own method? How do they handle a counterexample nobody planned for? Can this person carry the topic forward? None of the three can be established from a prepared document. The session exists precisely because these only show up in conditions you cannot prepare for.

So putting a stand-in in the question period also removes the ground under the talk itself. The reason nobody had to interrogate the provenance of the slides disappears. This is a matter of structure before it is a matter of honesty. Once you place a substitute for the thing being checked inside the check, the procedure verifies nothing whether it is passed or failed. When the invigilator sits the exam for you, the result is not cheating. The result is that there is no exam.

The person asking loses something too. A question is an attempt to get an answer, and also an attempt to open a relationship. Whether your question was a good one, whether the other side had already worried about that exact point, whether this is someone worth talking to further, can only be read off a human reaction. A generated answer carries none of that information. The speaker appears to have returned an answer, but in effect never received the question.

What actually breaks in the exchange

A seat with a stand-in in it usually shows. But the list below must not be used as a detection technique. Every one of these signals appears in honest people too. Some people slow down because they are nervous, some cannot hold eye contact, some answer in generalities because their preparation was thin. The moment you use this as a test, false positives injure people, and speakers who fear the false positive start behaving unnaturally to avoid it. Read the list instead as an inventory of what actually disappears when a stand-in is installed.

  • Lag. The gap between question and answer stretches. During it, the person who asked is not waiting for an answer so much as standing there unable to confirm that the question even landed
  • Gaze. The person answering looks at a screen rather than at the person who asked. The audience can no longer tell who is being answered, and what was a conversation becomes a reading
  • Collapse into generality. A generated answer gives the average of the topic instead of the specific conditions of the question. It is not wrong, but it is not an answer about this work
  • Failure on the follow-up. The second question arrives premised on the first answer. If you are not holding that first answer as your own, you cannot pick up the premise, and the exchange restarts from zero every time
  • No asking back. A large share of good answers begin with a check: is this what you were asking? A stand-in never asks back. The possibility that the question was misunderstood is not part of its arithmetic

How the same judgement works on the reading side is covered separately in Why Readers Close the Tab When Text Reads Like AI. In writing and in conversation alike, what people react to is not the fact that a machine made it but the sense that the other party is not present.

Norms that already exist in writing

None of this is entirely new. Academic publishing produced policy documents quickly from 2023 onwards. What the various organisations were publishing as of September 2026 comes out roughly as follows.

The wording differs, but three things overlap across all of them.

  • AI cannot be an author. And every document converges on the same reason. Not because it is not human, but because it cannot be responsible
  • Use must be disclosed. Except that grammar- and spelling-level use is generally carved out. Which is to say the norm is not aimed at AI use as such
  • Responsibility for the result stays with a person. Disclosure is not a device for transferring responsibility. It is a device for making its location unambiguous

Korea has its own documents. The publications list of the national research ethics portal run by the National Research Foundation of Korea carries a revised set of recommendations on the responsible use of generative AI tools, dated September 2025, and a research ethics guide to generative AI for university researchers, dated June 2026. What force documents of this kind actually carry was covered in Ethics Principles Are Not Law. Breaking them carries no penalty, but they operate to fix what the standard was once a dispute arises.

The striking thing about the table above, though, is not what it says but what is missing from it. Every one of these is a rule about manuscripts and reviews, which is to say about documents. There is no clause in any of them about what happens live in a lecture room. That is why a stand-in in the question period sits in a blind spot. A document has a moment called submission, and you can attach a disclosure step in front of it. A live exchange has no such moment.

Why AI cannot go on the author list

Author lists are usually read as a distribution of credit. But the reason these documents give is not about credit. ICMJE says a chatbot cannot be responsible for the accuracy, integrity and originality of the work. Cambridge University Press says AI does not meet its requirements for authorship and that accountability remains with the authors. The Elsevier disclosure template is built so that the author has to write, in their own words, that they take full responsibility for the content.

So the core of authorship is not contribution but answerability. There has to be somebody who will reply when a question comes later. An author list is a contact list before it is a credit table. Seen that way, the rule barring AI from the list is not a moral declaration but an administrative necessity. Put an entry on the list that nobody can address a question to, and the list stops functioning.

Carry that logic straight across to the question period and the answer appears.

  • The person answering has to be a person who can answer for that answer later. What is said in a conference room looks less binding than a sentence in a paper, but directions of research really do get set in those exchanges
  • A generated answer is unowned from the moment it is spoken. The speaker did not make it, and the thing that made it is not an accountable party. A gap in responsibility opens right there in the room
  • So the problem is not whether the answer was wrong. It is identical if the answer was right. Nobody can say whose credit it is when it is right, or whose fault when it is wrong

What disclosure fixes and what it cannot

There are problems that saying so out loud resolves, and problems it does not. The policies above handle the first kind well and barely touch the second.

What disclosure actually solves is calibration of scrutiny. If a reader knows which section was generated, they check its citations harder and re-verify its numbers. That is exactly why IEEE asks for the section and the level of use, not just a yes. Disclosure is not a request for trust. It is an instruction about where to be suspicious.

What disclosure does not solve is the case where the setting itself is asking for a person. If you announce before the question period that your answers will be generated and then read them out, the deception is gone. You have become honest. And the thing the session existed to verify remains unverified. Disclosure removes deception. It does not restore a procedure.

  • Polishing sentences in a manuscript — disclosure suffices, and several policies carve this out entirely
  • Generating figures and tables — disclosure plus a separate check against the source data. Rendering something plausible that was not there is the characteristic failure mode of this area
  • Writing a peer review — disclosure does not make it acceptable, because what review buys is that reviewer's judgement, and several policies forbid it outright
  • A live question period — disclosure does not make it acceptable either. But here there is almost no prohibition on the books, because the norms were built around documents

It helps to know that the rule has two layers: the layer where saying so is enough, and the layer where saying so changes nothing. The first is an information problem, so disclosure resolves it. The second is a procedural problem, so disclosure does not. How those same two layers separate in open-source contributions is covered in What a Project Must Decide When AI Enters Its Contributions.

Where using AI is entirely legitimate

Read only this far and the piece sounds like a warning. But most of the places AI actually gets used in academic work are fine, and some of them are closer to indispensable.

  • Drafting. The cost of filling a blank screen is real. A draft gets almost entirely rewritten anyway, so whose sentences populated that stage does not survive into the final text
  • Organising sources. Pulling conditions and results out of several papers into a comparison table is clerical, not judgemental. But citing something you have not read crosses the line in the same motion
  • Format conversion. Reference styles, rearranging to fit submission rules, trimming to a length. A large fraction of academic labour lives here
  • A rehearsal partner. Having it produce likely questions deepens preparation rather than shortcutting it. It is the opposite of having answers prepared for you. You take only the questions and write the answers yourself
  • Accessibility. For a speaker with a hearing, vision or motor impairment, live captioning, speech synthesis and input assistance are not stand-ins but the mechanism that makes participation possible. The judgement stays with the person and the AI builds the channel
  • Comprehension support. Receiving a question you could not catch as live text is a completely different act from receiving an answer

These items share one property. You can still explain the result after it exists. If you can say why the comparison table was grouped the way it was, or why you threw out a particular sentence from the draft, you used a tool.

What language support means for non-native speakers

The de facto common language of academic presentation is English. Two people doing identical research are not treated identically in the same room if one has English as a first language and the other does not. It is not that the content is missing. It is that the cognitive budget goes into building sentences, and the answer therefore looks shallower than it is. That cost is close to invisible from the side that has never paid it.

So language support should be seen as layers rather than as a single thing.

  • Polishing the manuscript. Plainly legitimate. The policies above acknowledge as much by carving out grammar and editing from disclosure, or softening it to a recommendation
  • Understanding a question through live captioning or translation. Legitimate. This is not producing the answer for you. It is getting access to the question. If you cannot hear it, there is no opportunity to answer at all
  • Rendering an answer you formed yourself. Close to the boundary but mostly on the tool side. If the content of the answer is yours, translation is a delivery mechanism. The time it takes costs you conversational rhythm
  • Feeding in the question and taking the answer. This is where it crosses. The distance between this and the previous three is technically small and procedurally decisive

Flatten those layers into a rule that says no AI at conferences, and the first people to lose are non-native speakers. A norm written broadly ends up working as a removal of accessibility. Which is why what should be forbidden is not AI use but the substitution of judgement. Converting that distinction into rule text is closer to the actual job in front of conference organisers.

The same line in other rooms

The conference is only the occasion. The identical line runs through every other professional domain. One question usually settles it. Does this setting want an artefact, or is it trying to verify a person capable of producing that artefact?

SettingWhat it is verifyingUse that stays a toolThe moment it becomes a stand-in
Job interviewWhether this person can do the work, and can be worked withPolishing the CV, rehearsing likely questionsGenerating answers live during the interview
Certification and practical examsThe candidate's own proficiencyWorked solutions and error explanations while studyingUse during the exam itself
Code reviewWhether the reviewer understood this change and approved itStatic analysis, change summaries, flagging missed pathsPosting generated comments on a diff you never read
Clinical consultationWhether a clinician assessed the patient directlyNote-taking, literature search, differential candidatesPassing on a generated impression without examining
Peer reviewThat reviewer's expert judgementVerifying citations, tidying phrasingGenerating the review itself
Counselling and mediationThe fact that the person spoke to a human beingOrganising records, explaining the procedureGenerating the response itself

Sit with that table and a second question follows. If a stand-in causes no problem at all, did the setting need to exist? That is not a jibe. Procedures that survive only as formality really do exist, and AI exposes them. A report nobody reads, a question period whose answers were settled in advance, a review whose default is approval: install a stand-in and nothing shows. The fact that nothing shows is itself a diagnosis of the setting. In that case the job is not to ban the use but to abolish the setting or revive it.

The grey zone where the line wobbles

The two ends are clear and the middle is not. Better to write that down honestly.

  • Reading a prepared answer. Keynotes read from a script, and prepared responses to questions submitted in advance, have existed for a very long time. There is exactly one difference. The prepared answer was made by that person
  • A colleague passing you a note. Common in joint presentations, and nobody objects. The difference is that the person passing the note is also an accountable party. A co-author can answer later
  • Search is fine but generation is not? Search returns candidates and the person does the choosing. Generation does the choosing too. Although in real tools that boundary keeps getting blurrier
  • The scope of having checked it. If you read a generated answer, judge it correct and say it, is it your answer? Yes if you have the competence to judge, no if you do not. And the person assessing whether that competence exists is, again, you

It is safest to assume nobody has a clean answer for this zone. But when the ends are clear, the middle can be settled case by case. That is what norms are for in the first place. Not to decide every case in advance, but to fix what the reference point will be once there is a dispute.

Deciding where no norm exists yet

Most settings, the conference question period included, have no written rule. There is a sequence that works there.

  • Write down in one sentence what this setting exists to verify. If it will not go down on paper, that is a signal about the setting rather than about the tool
  • Check whether the tool touches that object of verification. If it does not, it is a tool. Most uses are filtered out right here
  • Ask whether you could disclose it. If the other side would accept the disclosure, it is a tool. If saying it out loud would collapse the setting, it is a stand-in
  • Find someone to ask. Organiser or counterpart, if there is somebody to ask, ask. If the reason you are not asking is not that there is nobody but that asking feels like it would go badly, the answer is already in
  • Check that you can explain it afterwards. If you can field the question of how that answer came about, you used a tool

The third is the strongest in practice. If disclosing it would collapse the setting, it is a stand-in. That test runs without any written norm and mostly returns an answer within seconds. Summary

What happened at that conference is not a new species of wrongdoing. A new tool arrived on top of an old dividing line. The line reads like this. A tool makes what you can do faster. A stand-in substitutes for the fact that you are in the room.

The norms for documents are already settled. AI cannot be an author, use must be disclosed, and responsibility stays with a person. What matters is that the grounds for all three converge on one point. Can it be held responsible? Clauses about live exchanges are still mostly blank, but apply the same grounds and the answer is already determined.

There is one sentence this piece has declined to write: that we should stop using AI. Uses that carry someone over a language barrier, that make participation possible, that produce a draft, are not merely legitimate. They lower the threshold of academic work. What should be forbidden is not use but the substitution of judgement. A norm that flattens the two fails to stop what it should stop and stops what it should have left open.

What remains is a single question to put to the room you are standing in. What was this setting opened to verify? Answer that, and how far you can go with AI in it mostly settles itself.

What I could not verify

  • The facts of the account itself cannot be checked. Which conference it was, and what was on those screens, is the writer's observation. This article does not treat that observation as established fact, and deals only with the structure of the situation if it holds
  • Figures such as what percentage of the audience was doing something else are eyeball estimates from the room, so they are not reproduced here
  • The COPE position statement on AI and the Nature-family editorial policy page could not be opened directly — one refused access, the other redirected to a login screen. Neither is quoted as a result
  • The ACM authorship policy page would not open either. Its wording is reported only as far as it could be confirmed in the form quoted on a public policy page of an ACM-affiliated conference
  • For the Korean research foundation publications, only the titles and issue dates were confirmed from the list; the documents themselves could not be opened, so no content from them is reproduced
  • No conference rule explicitly permitting or forbidding AI use during a live question period was found. That means it was not confirmed, not that none exists
  • No survey data was found on how common a stand-in in the question period is, or what effect it has on audience assessment. The account here is about the structure of a procedure, not about frequency
