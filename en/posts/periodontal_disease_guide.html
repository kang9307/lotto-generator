Periodontal Disease (Gum Disease) Complete Guide: Symptoms, Causes, Treatment and Prevention

From the stages and causes of gingivitis and periodontitis to symptoms, systemic health links, scaling and root planing treatment, and proper brushing — a general information guide based on public health sources

2026-07-03 Health

Introduction: The Warning Signs Your Gums Are Sending

Periodontal disease, commonly known as gum disease, is one of the most common chronic oral conditions affecting adults worldwide. National survey data cited by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) show that a large share of adults over age 30 have some form of periodontal disease. It often begins subtly — a little blood in the sink after brushing — but left unaddressed, it is a progressive disease that can slowly destroy the bone and tissue supporting your teeth, eventually leading to tooth loss.

Just as important, periodontal disease does not stay confined to the mouth. A growing body of research points to bidirectional links between gum disease and systemic conditions such as diabetes and cardiovascular disease. Drawing on materials from the U.S. National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research (NIDCR), the American Dental Association (ADA), and the CDC, this guide covers the stages, causes, symptoms, systemic health links, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of periodontal disease as of July 2026. This article is general information only and does not replace medical or dental diagnosis. Please consult a licensed dentist for any symptoms you experience.

1. What Is Periodontal Disease — Gingivitis vs. Periodontitis

Periodontal disease is an umbrella term for inflammatory conditions affecting the tissues that surround and support the teeth — the gums, periodontal ligament, and alveolar bone. It is generally described in two broad stages.

1.1 Gingivitis: The Early, Reversible Stage

Gingivitis is the earliest stage of periodontal disease, occurring when bacterial plaque builds up along the gumline and triggers inflammation. Gums become red, swollen, and bleed easily during brushing or flossing. The defining feature of gingivitis is that no bone or ligament damage has yet occurred, which makes it reversible. With proper brushing, daily flossing, and a professional cleaning, gum health at this stage can be fully restored.

1.2 Periodontitis: The Irreversible Progressive Stage

If gingivitis is left untreated, inflammation can spread below the gumline, creating pathological gaps between the tooth and gum called periodontal pockets, and destroying the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone — a condition known as periodontitis. Periodontitis is defined by irreversible attachment loss: once bone and gum tissue have been lost, they cannot fully regenerate through cleaning alone. Clinically, periodontitis is often described as mild, moderate, or severe (advanced), with deeper pockets, greater bone loss, and higher risk of tooth mobility and loss as it progresses.

2. Causes — Inflammation Driven by Plaque, Tartar, and Bacteria

2.1 Plaque and Tartar

The root cause of periodontal disease is dental plaque, a sticky bacterial film that forms on tooth surfaces from food debris and bacteria. If plaque is not removed by brushing, it can harden within about 72 hours by combining with minerals in saliva into tartar (calculus) — a hardened deposit that a toothbrush can no longer remove. Tartar has a rough surface that makes it even easier for bacteria to attach, and the toxins and byproducts these bacteria produce trigger chronic inflammation in the gums.

2.2 Risk Factors

  • Smoking: Reduces blood flow to the gums and is the single most significant modifiable risk factor, substantially raising both the risk and severity of periodontal disease.
  • Diabetes: Poorly controlled blood sugar makes tissue more vulnerable to infection and inflammation, raising the risk of gum disease (see Section 4 for details).
  • Genetic predisposition: A family history of gum disease can make some people more susceptible even with similar oral hygiene habits.
  • Hormonal changes: Puberty, pregnancy, and menopause can make gums more reactive to plaque.
  • Misaligned teeth or ill-fitting dental work: Crowded teeth or loose crowns and fillings create hard-to-clean areas where plaque accumulates.
  • Certain medications: Some anticonvulsants, immunosuppressants, and calcium channel blockers can cause gum overgrowth.
  • Poor nutrition, chronic stress, and a weakened immune system can also increase risk.

3. Symptoms — Early Signs That Are Easy to Miss

  • Gums that bleed during brushing or flossing
  • Red, swollen gums that are tender to the touch
  • Persistent bad breath (halitosis) or a bad taste in the mouth
  • Gum recession, where gums pull back and tooth roots appear longer
  • Teeth that feel loose or gaps that appear between teeth
  • Discomfort while chewing or a change in how teeth fit together
  • Pus visible when gums are pressed — a sign of advanced periodontitis
Bleeding gums are more likely to be a sign of inflammation than a result of brushing too hard. If bleeding recurs, don't ignore it — see a dentist.

The tricky part is that gingivitis rarely causes pain, so it is easy to overlook. Even if symptoms feel mild, recurring bleeding or persistent bad breath warrants a professional evaluation.

4. Diagnosis — What Your Dentist Checks

Dentists and periodontists (gum specialists) use several methods to diagnose periodontal disease:

  • Periodontal probing: A thin, calibrated instrument measures the depth of the space between the gum and tooth. Healthy gums typically measure 1–3 mm; deeper measurements indicate more advanced disease.
  • Attachment level assessment: Evaluates how much of the supporting tissue has been lost.
  • Dental X-rays: Reveal bone loss that is not visible to the naked eye.
  • Tooth mobility and bleeding index assessment

These clinical assessments cannot be replaced by self-diagnosis. An accurate staging and treatment plan requires a professional evaluation.

5. Links to Systemic Health — Diabetes and Cardiovascular Disease

5.1 The Bidirectional Relationship With Diabetes

Periodontal disease and diabetes are described in the research literature as having a bidirectional relationship. Studies indicate that people with type 2 diabetes face roughly three times the risk of severe periodontal disease compared with those without diabetes. Conversely, some research has found that advanced periodontitis is associated with a roughly 20–30% higher risk of developing new-onset diabetes. This is thought to occur because chronic inflammation in periodontal tissue can contribute to systemic inflammation and insulin resistance. For people with diabetes, regular periodontal care alongside blood sugar management is particularly important.

5.2 Cardiovascular Disease and Other Systemic Effects

An association between periodontitis and cardiovascular conditions such as atherosclerosis and heart attack has also been reported. Proposed mechanisms include increased systemic inflammatory markers driven by chronic periodontal inflammation, bacteria entering the bloodstream (bacteremia), and impaired blood vessel lining function. It's important to note, however, that most current research demonstrates an association rather than proven causation — it has not been established that periodontal disease directly causes cardiovascular disease. Researchers are also actively studying links to pregnancy complications (preterm birth and low birth weight), rheumatoid arthritis, and chronic kidney disease. Gum health is increasingly viewed as one important piece of overall systemic health.

6. Treatment — From Scaling to Gum Surgery

6.1 Nonsurgical Treatment: Scaling and Root Planing

The first-line treatment for mild to moderate periodontal disease is scaling (removal of tartar) combined with root planing. Scaling uses ultrasonic or hand instruments to remove plaque and tartar from tooth surfaces and below the gumline, while root planing smooths the root surface to make it harder for bacteria to reattach and to help gum tissue reattach to the tooth. The American Dental Association's evidence-based clinical guideline recommends scaling and root planing as the first-line nonsurgical treatment for chronic periodontitis, and clinical studies report that it reduces pocket depth by 1–2 mm and improves attachment levels by up to 1 mm. Topical or systemic antibiotics may be used as an adjunct when needed.

6.2 Surgical Treatment: Gum Surgery

For severe periodontitis that does not respond adequately to nonsurgical treatment, gum surgery (flap surgery) may be considered. This involves lifting the gum tissue to directly access and remove deep tartar and inflamed tissue before suturing the gums back into place. In cases of significant bone loss, bone grafting or guided tissue regeneration may be attempted to help regenerate lost bone and tissue. The appropriate surgical approach is determined by a periodontist based on pocket depth, the pattern of bone loss, and overall health.

6.3 Maintenance After Treatment

Periodontal treatment is not a one-time event. Following treatment, regular supportive periodontal maintenance visits — typically every 3 to 6 months — are needed to monitor for recurrence. For periodontal disease, "control" and "maintenance" are more realistic goals than a permanent cure.

7. Prevention — Proper Brushing, Flossing, and Regular Cleanings

  • Proper brushing: Hold the toothbrush at roughly a 45-degree angle toward the gumline and brush gently in short, circular motions using a soft-bristled brush, at least twice a day.
  • Flossing or interdental brushes: Use daily to remove plaque from between teeth that a toothbrush cannot reach.
  • Regular professional cleanings: Every 6 to 12 months for most people without special risk factors, more often if your dentist recommends it based on your risk level.
  • Quitting smoking: Since smoking is the most significant modifiable risk factor, quitting benefits both prevention and treatment outcomes.
  • Managing systemic conditions: If you have diabetes, good blood sugar control also supports gum health.
  • A balanced diet and regular dental checkups round out an effective prevention routine.

8. Left Untreated — The Path to Tooth Loss

If periodontal disease is left untreated, plaque and bacteria continue to advance below the gumline, deepening periodontal pockets and gradually resorbing the alveolar bone that supports the teeth. Once enough supporting tissue is lost, teeth begin to loosen, and they may eventually fall out on their own or require extraction because they can no longer withstand normal chewing forces. In fact, periodontal disease is one of the leading causes of tooth loss in adults. Losing teeth reduces chewing function and affects appearance, places additional strain on remaining teeth, and can require complex restorative treatment such as dental implants. Catching and managing the disease at the gingivitis stage remains the most effective form of prevention.

9. Frequently Asked Questions

Q. What is the difference between gingivitis and periodontitis?

Gingivitis is inflammation confined to the gum tissue, without bone or ligament damage, and is fully reversible with proper care. Periodontitis involves destruction of the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone, an irreversible stage where damaged tissue does not regenerate on its own.

Q. Does bleeding gums always mean I have periodontal disease?

Bleeding is a common warning sign but does not confirm a diagnosis on its own. Repeated or persistent bleeding, however, likely signals ongoing inflammation and should prompt a professional dental evaluation.

Q. How often should I get a professional cleaning?

Generally every 6 to 12 months if you have no special risk factors. Smokers, people with diabetes, and those with a history of periodontal disease may need shorter intervals as advised by their dentist.

Summary as of July 2026

Drawing these public health sources together, the core message of periodontal disease management is early detection and consistent care. Caught at the gingivitis stage, the disease can be fully reversed. Once it progresses to periodontitis, even professional treatments like scaling and root planing aim for "control" rather than a permanent "cure." If you notice recurring bleeding gums or persistent bad breath, don't wait — schedule a dental visit.
