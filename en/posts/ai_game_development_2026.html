Fun Cannot Be Written as a Spec

Split game development into stages and the order in which AI arrives is not the order of model quality, it is the order in which verification was automated

2026-07-30 IT/Tech

Introduction: the sentence AI is changing game development carries almost no information

Game development is not one job. Concept art and textures, 3D models and rigging, sound, localisation, level design, client and server code, QA and bug reproduction, balancing and live operations are separate stages, each with its own output and its own pass criterion. Which is why the claim that AI is changing game development has an information content close to zero. Without saying which stage changed and on what evidence, the claim can be neither verified nor refuted.

This piece splits that claim by stage. And the moment you split it, a regularity appears. The stages AI settled into first are not the ones models are good at; they are the ones where a machine can render the verdict. It crossed over first wherever there was a compiler, an export specification, a string checker. Where the pass criterion lives only inside somebody's head, attaching a strong model does not move the schedule.

And that regularity has exactly one wall with no exception in principle. Fun cannot be written as a specification, and therefore cannot be verified. The thing this article most wants to do is mark the position of that wall precisely. Once you know where the line runs between outputs that can be verified and outputs that cannot, the question of which stages deserve budget answers itself.

1. The primary source - what was verified and what was not

The starting point first. A community item was followed through to the original article, which was opened and read. What could be verified is this. Quotations further down are English renderings of Korean sentences that appear in that article.

  • What kind of source it is — an interview published by a Korean games outlet on 29 July 2026. It is headlined as an interview about AI and games and carries the names of two reporters. It is a question-and-answer interview, not a conference write-up and not a research report.
  • So the sample size is one — what this article yields is not statistics but one person's stage-by-stage observations on one project. This piece does not extend it further than that.
  • It is not commercial production — the article records that the project was not begun with commercial intent. Schedule pressure, team coordination and ratings review, the ordinary constraints of commercial production, are therefore absent, so the conditions do not transfer directly to a studio.
  • The macro forecasts are not used as evidence here — the article also contains views on industry-wide change and on social policy. Those are personal opinions offered without supporting data, so none of them are used in the argument below. This is not a piece that evaluates any individual or company.
  • What the community summary states — the summary states that the project is built so that human players and AI agents connect over the same websocket protocol, leaving the server unable to tell them apart. That description comes from the summary, and the repository itself and the size of the codebase were not verified here.

Put together, the article is most valuable read as a casebook of stage-by-stage successes and failures. Every quotation below corresponds to an actual sentence in the article and never runs beyond one sentence. Nothing that could not be verified is quoted.

2. A map of the stages - lining them up by how their output gets checked

Before listing stages the test has to be fixed. This piece uses three questions. First, is the output a file. Second, can a machine decide whether it passes. Third, does a mistake surface immediately. Stages that answer yes three times have already crossed over; stages with a single no still have a person attached.

There is one conclusion to take from that table. Stages cross over in the order in which they acquired a verifier, not in the order of model quality. And the conclusion is more useful read backwards. If you want to hand a stage to AI, build the pass judge for that stage before you change the model. Without a judge no generator can close an improvement loop, and the bottleneck simply returns as a person inspecting every item by eye.

3. The line the engine vendor drew - what the official docs state and what they omit

There is one signal far more reliable than industry discourse: what the engine vendor actually shipped in the product. A capability that made it into a commercial engine is work the vendor already judged repeatable and checkable.

Unity's official manual describes in-editor AI in two branches. The manual states that the in-editor assistant provides in-editor guidance to write code, troubleshoot and automate tasks, and creates assets such as sprites, textures, sounds, animations and materials based on text prompts or reference inputs. Separately, for Sentis, it states that this enables you to integrate and run trained machine learning models on end-user devices or directly in the editor. Usage is surfaced through a cloud dashboard, described as a centralised view of AI usage, settings and point tracking.

What matters here is not the items on that list but the items missing from it. Within the manual as verified, the generated outputs the vendor names are sprites, textures, sounds, animations and materials. Level layout, quest logic, difficulty curves and economy balance are not on that list. Overlay it on the stage map above and the line matches almost exactly. Outputs whose verdict comes from a specification have been productised; outputs whose pass criterion is human judgement have not.

  • Metering is stated explicitly — a dashboard that tracks points means generation is a variable cost. Book it as a cost line, not as a free feature.
  • Inference is a separate branch — running a trained model on the device and generating assets in the editor are different problems. The first is bottlenecked by runtime performance and memory, the second by pipeline specifications.
  • What could not be verified here — the Unreal Engine documentation page rendered only a table of contents with no body text, so it would not open. Accordingly no Unreal capability is cited anywhere in this piece, and no cross-engine comparison is offered.

The axis where a tool drives the editor directly, meaning a command-line agent controlling the engine, was covered separately in driving Unity from a CLI agent. This piece does not repeat the tooling story and looks only at the stage verdict.

4. Where it lands (1) conversion rather than generation - the asset pipeline

When people discuss assets they usually picture generation. In practice, though, the first thing that automates safely is not generation but conversion. That distinction is the point of this section.

The interview records that a 3D tool was wired up over a protocol, and that resizing externally sourced assets to game-appropriate scale, exporting them into a usable format, and rendering a 128 by 128 image for icon use were all handled from instructions alone. It also records that the author had barely used that tool before. There is a sentence in the article to the effect that even sloppy instructions get understood as intended.

Why this work goes well is obvious. Rescaling, axis conversion, format export and thumbnail rendering are deterministic conversions whose correct answer is defined by a specification. Whether the size matches, whether an axis is flipped, whether the extension and compression settings meet spec: a script renders all of those verdicts. The stage already had a verifier.

CategoryRepresentative workPass criterionWhen failure surfaces
ConversionScale, axis alignment, export, atlasing, LODSpecification table (numbers)On import
Formulaic generationTiling textures, colour variants, normal mapsSpecification plus art directorWhen placed in the scene
Creative generationKey character art, signature backdropsPeople (taste, world coherence)At the review meeting
StagingCutscenes, camera, timingPeople (feel)During play
  • Turn pipeline specifications into check scripts, not documents — without a checker, human review becomes the bottleneck the instant volume rises. Throughput is set by checking speed, not generation speed.
  • Automate conversion first — bringing free or external assets up to spec is monotonous with an unambiguous failure verdict, which gives the fastest payback.
  • Keep creative generation to candidates — used as twenty options rather than a final answer, art direction authority stays with people.
  • Licensing of external assets does not resolve itself — a tool that fixes size and format does not check permitted use on your behalf. That problem is handled separately below.

5. Where it lands (2) code and review, and where the approval gate remains

Code is the stage with the best-equipped verifier. Whether it compiles, whether tests pass, whether static analysis stays quiet are all decided mechanically. Which is why it crossed over first.

The interview records that transition chronologically. Early in the project the generated code was read and reviewed line by line, and some months later even that review was handed to the model, reducing how often the code was read directly. The point for a practitioner is not that review was delegated but where the approval gate ended up.

The article answers that too. One of the entries left in the instruction file amounts to a rule that permission must be obtained before committing. In other words, generation and editing are delegated while human approval is reserved for actions that persist in the history. That arrangement is reasonable in itself. The problem is what comes next. The article records that the rule was repeatedly not followed, and explains why in terms of the model not learning, so that clearing the context and starting fresh wipes out everything from before.

That single explanation hands you the practical prescription. A policy written into a prompt is a rule that depends on session state. Reset the session and the rule disappears with it. So rules that must hold belong not in the prompt but in an enforcement mechanism outside the session.

  • Cut write permission at the tool level — for hard-to-reverse actions such as commit, push and deploy, withholding the permission entirely and having a person execute is stronger than any prompt rule.
  • Move it into branch protection and required checks — approval rules survive independently of sessions only when they live in repository settings.
  • Record the generated marker in commit metadata — when you later need to trace only the changes made under one model version, that marker is the sole clue you will have.
  • If review was delegated, add regression tests — reducing how often a person reads without raising the density of automated verification is a pure increase in risk.
  • Verbosity is a cost — the article includes remarks about the model attaching more error handling and defensive code than the situation warrants. The metric to watch is maintainable code volume, not generated volume.

6. Where it stalls (1) three dimensions - the overlapping staircase

The most concrete failure in the article is here. Building multi-storey structures with stairs to move between floors, the staircase from the first floor to the second and the staircase from the second to the third kept ending up placed on top of each other. The article records that a task normally finished within an hour or two took far longer, and that in the end it required reading all of the code and going down to why it was failing.

The account of the cause is short and unambiguous. The article contains sentences to the effect that the AI is quite short on spatial concepts, and that while it handles verbal work well its grasp of a three-dimensional world still lags considerably.

Read purely as a model limitation, this case yields no prescription. Read as a stage question it reads differently. The correct answer for staircase placement is not expressible in natural language; it is expressible as coordinate invariants. The intersection of the two stair volumes must be zero, the floor index at the entry point must increase monotonically, and a traversable path from the start position to the target floor must exist. All three can be checked automatically. Without a checker the verdict falls to a person watching the screen, and at that point the generation loop is bound to the speed of visual inspection.

So this section concludes in the same direction as the last. A large share of what looks like work AI cannot do is work whose verification we never automated. Spatial problems are not solved by writing longer text specifications; they are solved by pushing the requirement down into invariants and assertions.

  • Use geometric invariants as tests — volume intersection, floor index monotonicity and duplicate collider counts automate cleanly as scene checks.
  • Add reachability checks — whether start and goal are connected in the navigation graph can be decided at build time.
  • Screenshot regression is a secondary tool — it does not render a deterministic verdict, so use it to raise a flag, never as the pass criterion.
  • Withdraw delegation when failure drags on — once you reach the point in the article where all the code has to be read anyway, a person taking it over directly is shorter in total. Decide the withdrawal condition in advance.

7. Where it stalls (2) priorities - the weeks spent on waves

The article contains one more interesting self-observation. Producing waves rolling onto a beach and breaking, sand darkening as water pulls back, and the point where a river meets the sea, took several weeks. And of that work the article records a remark that it actually has little to do with gameplay, alongside a note that in a company somebody would have said not to spend time there.

This passage lands precisely on a common misreading of productivity tooling. When implementation cost falls, so does the reason not to build something. The judgement that a feature was simply too expensive used to be a natural priority filter. Weaken that filter and the filter has to be manufactured somewhere else.

  • The faster you get, the more you need a priority gate — work that implementation difficulty used to screen out must now be screened explicitly by design.
  • Attach a verdict metric to each task — the cheapest possible rule is that if you cannot state in one line which play-experience metric a task moves, you do not start it.
  • On a personal project this is a virtue — building for satisfaction is legitimate. Transplanting that condition into an argument about commercial productivity, however, is a misuse of the source.
  • Rendering quality can be judged on performance — looks are taste, but frame time, draw calls and shader instruction counts are numbers. Keeping taste verdicts and numeric verdicts unmixed is what keeps reviews short.

8. Where it stalls (3) fun - unspecifiable, therefore unverifiable

This is the centre of the article. Most of what stalled in the previous two sections dissolves once you build a verifier. But no verifier can be built for fun. There is no definition to verify against.

The usual detour is a proxy metric: retention, session length, time between returns, purchase conversion. With a metric the optimisation loop turns. The trouble is precisely that the loop optimises the metric. A metric is a partial shadow of fun, and handing a shadow to a system as its objective function grows the shadow. In games this side effect is unusually well known: the easiest way to lengthen sessions is not to add fun but to add waiting.

So the honest conclusion for this territory is this. Do not use AI as the judge; use it only as a generator. Judgement stays with people, kept short and frequent.

TargetCan it be specifiedVerification methodRole for AI
Rule consistency (no infinite resource loop)YesSimulation, static checksSearch, counterexample generation
Reachability (progress without blockage)YesNavigation, automated playPathfinding, regression detection
Difficulty curvePartlyWin rate, attempt-count distributionProposing parameter sweep candidates
Learning curve and comprehensibilityHardObserving new playersListing hypotheses about confusion points
Fun, immersion, satisfactionNoA person playingCandidate generation only
  • Bulk variant generation plus a short human verdict — build a structure that a twenty minute play session can filter and you gain speed without forcing an unverifiable thing into numbers.
  • Never hand a proxy metric to an objective function — keep metrics as after-the-fact observation and out of the optimisation target. Crossing that line is hard to undo.
  • Use automated play agents as a safety net — they are effective at catching dead ends, infinite loops and obviously destructive combinations. They are not used to judge fun.
  • Put the human verdict on the schedule — if judgement capacity is not planned, metrics end up rendering the verdict instead.

As an aside, the problem where calling a system intelligent slides the subject of the sentence from a person to a tool appears in the same shape outside games. The relationship between vocabulary and accountability was covered separately in how the word intelligence blurs accountability.

9. Sound and localisation - they yield exactly as far as the specification reaches

The two stages behave similarly. Formal checks automate completely, and judgement remains.

In sound, what gets decided automatically is loudness normalisation, clipping, silent regions, duration, continuity at loop points, sample rate and channel layout. What does not get decided is whether the sound belongs in this world, and whether it starts to grate on repeated playback. The latter is only knowable by listening repeatedly.

Localisation has one of the highest automatable verification rates of any stage. Placeholder preservation, tag pairing, UI width overflow, glossary conformance, leftover untranslated strings and line-break rules are all caught by checkers. Which is why this stage crossed over early. What remains is judgement about cultural fit, age-rating language and proper-noun policy. The working flow for this stage was already covered in a working record of a game localisation agent, so only the stage verdict is left here.

  • Build the checker before the translation — raise translation volume without one and people end up hunting regressions by eye.
  • The glossary is an output — with no glossary there is no criterion for consistency, and with no criterion there is no verification.
  • UI width differs by language — the same sentence varies greatly in length across languages, so overflow checks need per-language measurement.
  • Keep repeated-listening tests for sound — it is the clearest example of a stage where passing spec does not mean usable.

10. QA and bug reproduction - determinism comes first

QA runs in two directions: using AI for QA and doing QA on what AI produced. The former goes well; the latter breaks a premise.

Using AI for QA works because the verdict is mechanical. Fuzzing inputs to find crashes, clustering stack traces to merge duplicate reports, picking anomalous windows out of long logs, drafting reproduction steps in prose: in all of these, pass or fail is unambiguous. The crash reproduces or it does not.

The other direction is the problem. A traditional bug report works by leaning on the assumption that the same build and the same inputs give the same result. Generated content arriving at runtime breaks that assumption. If the same action in the same place yields different lines and opens a different branch, the concept of a reproduction procedure stops holding.

So it is safer to put a mode switch on every generation path. Below is that shape written out as a specification.

repro:
  build: 1.4.2-rc3
  platform: windows-x64
  input_log: runs/2026-07-30/seed-918273.inputlog
  rng_seed: 918273
  fixed_timestep: 0.0166667
  generated_content:
    npc_dialogue: replay-from-log    # <live | replay-from-log | disabled>
    procedural_level: seeded
    asset_variation: frozen
  assertions:
    - stair_volume_overlap == 0
    - stair_traversal.floor_reached == 2
    - nav.reachable(spawn, boss_room) == true
  verdict: reproducible
  • Build all three modes — live generation, replay from log, fully disabled. Without a replay mode you cannot write a QA report at all.
  • Make seed and fixed timestep mandatory report fields — if physics and randomness wobble, reproduction failures unrelated to generation get mixed in and causes cannot be separated.
  • The prompt bundle is a version too — edit a prompt and behaviour changes. If the prompt version is missing from build information, regression tracing breaks.
  • Treat a model swap as a major change — scenarios that passed can break again under a new model version, so the whole regression set has to run.
  • The game must stand up in disabled mode — if progress halts when the generation path dies, that is not a feature, it is a single point of failure.

11. The five bills for generated NPC dialogue

NPC dialogue demos best and causes operational trouble most often. The article has a real case. After release, two AI-driven characters, a merchant and a guard, stood in the game, and the article records that many connecting players tried to make them disregard their prior instructions. Yet it also records that the system instructions were not written with much care, with a sentence to the effect that the author had not laboured over the system prompt, followed by an account of the character fending off the demands anyway. The article reproduces one reply from those conversations: asked how the token bill would be covered, the character answered that it is a guard and the token bill is none of its concern.

Reading this only as a success story is risky. If the defence held because of the model's present capability rather than the design, then changing the model changes the defence with it. In a commercial service that is a regression. Here are the actual bills.

Of the five, the one discovered latest in practice is reproducibility. The first four get raised by design and operations early, but reproducibility only reveals its absence after a serious bug has already landed. Which is why per-utterance records have to be kept from day one.

  • Store a reference rather than the raw output — retention periods and deletion requests need to be handled separately from the record itself.
  • The world state hash is the key field — without it you cannot explain after the fact why the same utterance came out differently.
  • Record sampling parameters — a temperature change alone alters the feel. With no record, the list of candidate causes never narrows.
  • Log whether the cache was hit — the same field serves both cost analysis and reproduction analysis.
  • Aggregate blocks separately — a change in block rate is the fastest early warning of model or filter regression.

For reference, experiments where many agents interact and produce social behaviour have a different purpose from the commercial stages here. Those aim at observation, so reproducibility and unit-cost constraints are weak. That experimental axis was covered separately in agent society simulation experiments; this piece looks only at the stages of a game in operation.

12. Balancing and live operations - plenty of metrics, no target

Balancing looks like the best possible fit for AI. There are numbers, logs and win rates. And that is where the illusion starts. Having many metrics is a different thing from having a defined target.

Balancing with an explicit target really does automate. Bringing a fighter's win rate into a band, or confirming that no infinite multiplication loop exists in a resource cycle, has a clear objective function, so parameter search works. Commercial balance targets, by contrast, are usually not win rates. They are things like a felt sense of progression, preserved scarcity, or the perceived gap between new and veteran players, and none of those get written as specifications.

  • Where it lands — self-play simulation, parameter sweep candidates, anomaly detection in metrics, extracting candidate abuse patterns, ticket classification and reply drafts.
  • Where human approval is mandatory — disclosed probability values, prices, reward structures, and rating or age-related language. Regulation and trust are attached, so these are not automatic-apply territory.
  • Operational copy is verifiable — an announcement draft can be checked against a list of factual items, so generation pays off well. The numbers themselves still need a human cross-check.
  • Keep automated metric response narrow — detection automatic, action approved. Blur that boundary and the answer to who decided disappears when something goes wrong.
  • Experiment design does not go away — deciding what to compare is not replaced by generation.

13. Rights - the verified scope and the unverified scope

This section stays short and states only what was verified. The common error in this subject is writing policy detail from memory, and since store terms and engine licences are revised often, memory gets it wrong.

One thing was verified. Unity's official manual describes a cloud dashboard giving a centralised view of AI usage, settings and point tracking. Generation, in other words, is a metered variable cost. Beyond that, licence clauses on rights in the output or the scope of commercial use were not verified here.

What could not be verified is stated plainly too. The store level rules on disclosing AI content could not be opened, because the page rendered only its shell with no body text. So this piece contains no claim that any store requires anything. Provider statements about training data provenance were also outside what this round of research could confirm.

So instead of a policy summary, here are only the questions a team has to answer in its own documents. The answers have to be filled in by opening each provider's and each store's current terms directly.

15. What was left out because it could not be verified

Stating what is missing helps the reader more than hiding it.

  • Every Unreal Engine capability — the official documentation page rendered only a table of contents with no body, and after two attempts it was dropped. No cross-engine comparison was made either.
  • Store level rules on disclosing AI content — the page would not open, so no claim about what any store requires appears here.
  • Industry statistics and revenue figures — no authoritative source could be confirmed, so not one number for adoption rate, cost savings or market size is used.
  • The project repository itself — codebase size, licence and structure were not verified. The protocol-parity description is only as far as the community summary states.
  • The macro forecasts in the interview — views about the industry as a whole and about policy are personal opinion offered without data, so they are not used in the argument.
  • Current subscription tier conditions — the price tiers appearing in the article are personal experience as of publication and are not cited as current pricing information.
  • Comparisons between specific models — model names appear in the article, but it is not a comparative experiment, so nothing was carried over as a ranking of capability.

Conclusion: only stages with automated verification cross over

The argument in one sentence. Where AI actually lands in game development is not where models are strong but where the pass verdict is rendered by a machine. Code crossed over first not because code is easy but because there is a compiler and a test suite. Asset conversion crossed over because there is a specification table. Localisation crossed over because there is a checker.

That makes the decision simple. If you want AI in a stage, build that stage's verifier first. Stages where a verifier can be built usually cross over. Stages where building the verifier stalls at the question of what counts as passing will not cross over no matter which model you attach. Overlapping staircases are solved by invariants; whether that dungeon is any fun cannot be written as an invariant at all.

The best available move in front of that wall is to change the role. Do not use AI as the judge; use it as a candidate generator. Then put the human time needed for judgement explicitly on the schedule. Skip that and proxy metrics end up rendering the verdict, and from then on what gets optimised is not the game but the metric. That is the value of splitting a process into stages. It reveals not only what can be delegated but what must never be delegated all the way.
