One Metric to Steer By - What You Gain and What Quietly Rots

Decisions land faster. And whatever you stop measuring gets worse. Both effects priced in the same model

2026-08-01 Tech & IT

Opening: Twelve metrics on the screen and nobody knows which one comes first

Monday morning, twelve numbers on the meeting room screen. Signups, active users, conversion, churn, response time, incident count, deploy frequency, support tickets, repeat purchase, revenue, margin, satisfaction. Every one of them matters. And nowhere is it written down which one outranks which.

Then a decision comes up. Shipping a certain feature lifts signups but degrades response time. The room splits for forty minutes. One side holds up the signup number, the other holds up the latency graph. Both sides are arguing from metrics, and neither is wrong. Because nobody ever decided what to pick when the metrics point opposite ways, evidence cannot end the argument. In the end it is settled by whoever is loudest or most senior in the room.

The instinct to cut down to one number comes out of exactly this frustration. And the prescription genuinely works. What is discussed far less is what it costs. This article is an attempt to put a number on that cost.

The starting point is an engineering post published on 2026-07-28. Writing about how to wire up billing, its author put it this way: after all, Stripe just wants a number, it does not care how we get there. The context of that sentence is not management at all, it is billing system design. What the payment provider needs is a single final quantity, so the logic that computes it should not be scattered through product code but reconciled separately downstream.

But that sentence gets quoted far more often outside of code. Can an organization also run on a single number? That is the only question here. And the answer is neither yes nor no. It is closer to yes, provided you know exactly what you are paying.

What this article does not claim. I have no knowledge of how many metrics Stripe uses internally or how that company makes decisions, and this article makes no claim about any of that. The sentence quoted above was written by an outside developer on their own blog, and what it refers to is the shape of the input handed to a billing API. Nothing here is an inference about any company internal operations.

Every number in this article comes from an assumed model. No real organization was measured. Python 3.13.3, Windows 10 (win32), standard library only, fixed seed 20260801. Run the same code and you get the same numbers. If the model is wrong about your world, the numbers are wrong too. Assumptions are stated in each section and collected once more at the end.

Out of scope. The claim that changing what you measure changes what people do was covered in The Productivity Mirage and is not repeated here. The question here is the next one: knowing behaviour will shift, how many metrics should you keep? The general procedure for choosing what to measure at all is in How to Measure Adoption Outcomes, and the effect of decision quality and speed on an organization is in A Decision Problem, Not a Burn Problem.

1. Why shrink the metric set - conflicts leave the call unsettled

The problem with having many metrics needs to be stated precisely. The usual explanation is that attention gets scattered, but that is secondary. The real problem is that when metrics point in opposite directions, nothing determines which one wins.

I counted it in a model. Four thousand imaginary proposals, each with an effect on three axes (growth, quality, cost efficiency). Every proposal was built from an overall merit component and a trade-off component, so some proposals help every axis while others buy growth by giving up quality. Then I drew random pairs and asked, for each rule, whether the call gets settled.

The third line sets the direction of this whole article. Three metrics with weights written down go back to settling 100 percent of calls. What was blocking decisions was never the metric count. It was the absence of a priority rule.

  • One metric always settles the call. There is only one value to compare, so this is trivially true
  • Three metrics with no priority settle the call only when one side wins on all three axes. In the model that was 35.8 percent. The other 64.2 percent escalate or stretch the meeting
  • The growth pick and the quality pick disagreed 58.7 percent of the time. Conflict is not a rare exception, it is the default in more than half of all pairs
  • Attach weights of 0.5 / 0.3 / 0.2 to those same three metrics and the decided rate is 100 percent. You get settled calls without shrinking anything
  • If an unsettled call escalates and escalation costs three more rounds, mean effort rises from 1.00 to 2.93 rounds. Those three rounds are an assumption of mine, not a measurement

1.1 What cutting to one actually does

Read the table again and the prescription becomes clear. Cutting to one metric is one way of stating a priority, and it is the bluntest one. It is identical to assigning weights of 1.0 / 0.0 / 0.0.

ApproachDecided ratePriority expressedInformation discarded
12 metrics, no ruleLowNoneNone. But no conclusion either
3 metrics, no rule35.8%NoneNone. 64.2% left open
3 metrics, weights stated100%Continuous (0.5/0.3/0.2)The cost of arguing over weights
1 metric100%Extreme (1.0/0/0)Both other axes, entirely

That last cell is what the rest of this article is about. In the model, the growth-only rule and the weighted rule chose differently in 24.3 percent of pairs. One call in four goes the other way once you cut to one. That does not make those calls wrong. It makes them different, and the discarded axes are what sits inside the difference.

2. Where the power of a single metric comes from

People usually explain the single-metric effect with the word focus, but the model above gives a sharper answer. The power comes from the decision rule becoming complete. Complete means that whatever two options you bring, the rule returns an answer.

And this effect has a property that amplifies inside an organization. When a rule is incomplete, decisions travel upward, and decisions that travel upward get judged without their context. When a rule is complete, the decision stays where the work is, because anyone looking at the same metric reaches the same answer whether they sit above or below.

What you gainEvidence in the modelWhat comes attached
Calls always get settledDecided rate 35.8% to 100%Unmeasured axes never enter the call
Decisions take less timeMean 2.93 rounds to 1.00 (assumed model)Wrong calls land just as fast
Decisions can be delegatedA complete rule can be handed downThe room to game it moves down too
Explanations get shorterOne axis of justificationThe language for objecting disappears
Cross-team alignment is easierA single shared axisOne metric per team backfires (section 7)

The right-hand column has to be read alongside the left. The upside and the downside of a single metric are not separate properties, they are two faces of the same property. It is fast because the rule is complete, and complete means it never looks at the axes outside the rule. Asking to be fast while still covering the other axes is asking to make the rule incomplete again. How to strike that bargain is section 6, on guardrails.

3. What disappears - how an unmeasured axis collapses

Now the cost side. Everyone says unmeasured things get worse, but how much and how soon is rarely written down. So I calculated it.

The assumptions. Each period there is one unit of effort to split across three axes. Every axis decays on its own if left alone (quality -2.0, cost efficiency -1.4). Quality also carries a term where debt breeds more debt: once quality falls below baseline it loses extra in proportion to that debt (coefficient 8.0), mimicking rework crowding out improvement time. And quality debt reduces the growth axis gain three periods later (coefficient 5.0). The lag is there because in real life this feedback is not visible immediately.

Read it in order. Look only at the first eight periods and concentration wins overwhelmingly — 155.1 against 125.4. Hand in a scorecard at that point and the conclusion is that focus was right. And at that point, the conclusion is true.

  • The quality axis never falls sharply. It drops a little each period. So no single period ever triggers an alarm
  • By period 16 quality reads 66.6 against 125.6, a gap of 59.0 points. Both started at 100, so one lost a third and the other gained a quarter
  • The target axis, growth, peaks at 164.9 in period 12 and comes back down to 159.7 by period 16. The neglected axis eventually pulled the target axis down
  • The balanced regime reads 157.5 at period 16 and is still climbing. The concentrated one is falling. The two curves are about to cross
  • The model horizon is 16 periods, so the crossover does not happen inside it. Extend the period count in the code and you can see when it flips

3.1 Why none of this is visible

What this model reproduces is not a magnitude but a shape. Three properties overlap to keep the collapse out of sight.

  • It is slow. A drop of about one percent per period never looks significant in any single report
  • It is delayed. Quality debt takes three periods to show up in growth. Cause and effect land in different quarters and never get connected
  • The early signal points the wrong way. For the first several periods the target metric really does climb well, and that success reinforces the policy

So reports saying we cut to one metric and it worked are mostly true. They just tend to be written somewhere between period 6 and period 10. The danger of a single metric is not that it produces a wrong result, it is that the right result arrives first and the bill arrives later.

4. Goodhart law - two different failures

The widely quoted sentence is this. When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. It is worth pinning down where it comes from. It is usually attributed to the economist Goodhart, but the sentence in that exact form appears on page 308 of Marilyn Strathern 1997 paper in European Review, and that paper states that it was Hoskin who named it Goodhart law, pointing back to Goodhart observation about instruments of monetary control.

This distinction earns its keep in practice, because two different failures live under that one name, and they call for different responses.

4.1 You lose value even when nobody cheats

The first failure is the less familiar one. If the proxy carries noise, simply taking the top of the proxy costs you true value, with no gaming whatsoever. I generated twenty thousand items and took the top two hundred.

At fidelity 0.80 you give up 21 percent. And 0.80 is a fairly good proxy. Taking the top still costs you, for a simple reason. The items at the top are a mix of genuinely good ones and ones whose noise happened to land high. The more extreme the cut, the larger the second group.

  • This loss appears regardless of how many metrics you keep. It is present anywhere a proxy is used
  • What a single metric does is concentrate the loss on one axis. With several metrics the independent noise partly cancels. With one, that one noise is all there is
  • The fix is not to select fewer, it is to pick a metric closer to the outcome. Raising fidelity from 0.80 to 0.90 halves the loss, from 21.0 to 10.7 percent
  • Which is exactly why closeness to the outcome carries the largest weight in the candidate scoring of section 5

4.2 Under pressure the metric detaches from the outcome

The second failure is what people usually mean by Goodhart. The crucial piece of this model is that gaming ability is drawn independently of real skill. That single line decides the result. If gaming ability scales with skill, the metric and the outcome stay glued together. If it is independent, they come apart. I assumed the share of effort spent gaming rises with pressure.

Put the two middle columns side by side. The mean proxy goes from 1.00 to 2.48, two and a half times, while the mean true outcome falls from 1.00 to 0.29. The dashboard keeps improving while reality keeps degrading. That is what Goodhart law looks like from inside a company.

  • Correlation holds at 0.73 up to pressure 0.4. It breaks to 0.23 at 0.6 and goes negative at 0.8. Not a gentle slide, a snap in a narrow band
  • The last column stings most. It is the true value of the top decile by proxy. It starts at 1.58 with no pressure and ends at 0.14 at pressure 1.0, below the overall mean of 0.29
  • Which means the people the metric rewards become the group with worse outcomes. Promotions and bonuses run exactly backwards
  • Concluding that people are bad would be a mistake. The actors in this model responded optimally to the rule they were given. What is broken is the rule, not the people

5. What makes a metric a candidate

Everything above sets the selection criteria. A good single metric has to satisfy three things at once. It has to be close to the outcome, hard to game, and something the team can actually move. I added a fourth, how quickly it confirms, because slow feedback magnifies the lag problem from section 3.

I scored ten candidates from 1 to 5 and applied weights. The sub-scores are my judgment, not measurements. Your organization will produce different numbers. What to take from this is not the ranking itself but which properties push a score up and which pull it down.

A few rules fall out of the table.

  • The metric closest to the outcome is not automatically the best. Monthly recurring revenue scores a perfect 5 on closeness yet lands at 3.55 overall, because teams cannot move it directly. Hand people a metric they cannot move and they will produce narratives about the metric instead of movement in it
  • A metric that is easy to move is a warning sign in itself. Deploy count scores full marks on control and speed and still ranks near the bottom. Split your deploys and it rises immediately, and that rise means nothing
  • Deadline hit rate is especially bad. Padding estimates raises it. Any metric whose cheapest improvement path is to corrupt your estimates should be struck from the list
  • Time to first success ranks first because it has no weak axis. It sits close to the outcome causally, shortening it requires actually fixing something, the team can move it directly, and it confirms within days
  • With section 4.1 in mind, the heavy weight on outcome closeness makes sense: lifting fidelity from 0.80 to 0.90 halves the selection loss

6. Guardrails - how many constraints can you carry

The standard remedy for the collapse in section 3 is to attach constraints to the single objective: raise conversion, but p95 response time must not exceed 400 milliseconds. This works. But keep adding constraints and at some point you are multi-metric again. I calculated that point.

The assumptions. Each guardrail actually binds on 18 percent of decisions, and guardrails are correlated with each other at 0.30, which encodes the tendency for hard decisions to trip several constraints at once.

------------------------------------------------------------------------
   k   objective alone decides   a guardrail decides   live axes

   1                   82.0%                18.0%        1.18
   2                   69.6%                30.4%        1.36
   3                   60.2%                39.8%        1.55
   4                   53.3%                46.7%        1.73
   5                   48.0%                52.0%        1.89
   6                   43.2%                56.8%        2.09
   7                   38.8%                61.2%        2.28
   8                   36.2%                63.8%        2.44
------------------------------------------------------------------------
  at k=5 the single objective decides fewer than half the calls.
  at k=6 the mean live axis count passes 2.0. that is multi-metric.
  • At five guardrails the single objective decides only 48.0 percent of calls on its own. More than half of all decisions are overturned by a constraint
  • At six, the mean number of live decision axes hits 2.09. The name says single metric while the practice is two or more
  • The practical ceiling is around three. There the objective still decides 60.2 percent by itself, which preserves the pattern of mostly the objective decides, occasionally a constraint blocks
  • A guardrail must be a line, not a target. It says do not cross, never raise this. The moment you start raising it, it is a second objective
  • Log the decisions a guardrail blocked. If that rate keeps climbing, it is a signal that the objective itself is wrong

One common misreading deserves correction. Sliding back to multi-metric is not itself a bad thing. As section 1 showed, three metrics with stated weights also settle 100 percent of calls. The problem is calling it a single metric while actually judging on six axes. That destroys the delegability that was the point, while the organization keeps believing delegation still works.

7. Organization size - what happens when every team gets its own metric

Everything so far was about one team. With several teams a new problem appears. Giving every team its own single metric starts to pull the whole out of alignment as the count grows.

The model. When team i pushes its own metric, its own team gains and a small cost spills onto every other team: contention over shared resources, integration cost, waiting on each other releases. I set that per-team cost very small (mean 0.020). Teams only see their own metric, so they always push to the maximum. Under a shared metric, teams move only when the total improves.

E. One metric per team   assumed spillover model
------------------------------------------------------------------------
  teams   active (local)  active (shared)   total (local)  total (shared)  loss
------------------------------------------------------------------------
      2               2                2            1.76            1.76    0.0%
      4               4                4            2.98            2.98    0.0%
      8               8                8            6.98            6.98    0.0%
     16              16               16           12.96           12.96    0.0%
     32              32               22           11.75           15.96   26.4%
------------------------------------------------------------------------
  nobody misbehaves. every team maximizes the metric it was handed.

The shape of this table is the point. Up to 16 teams the loss is zero. Then at 32 teams it jumps to 26.4 percent. It does not degrade gradually, it snaps once a line is crossed.

  • The reason is that spillover accumulates in proportion to team count. The cost per pair stays the same while the number of other teams grows. What is negligible at 8 teams exceeds a whole team contribution at 32
  • Under the shared metric, the optimum at 32 teams was 22 teams pushing and 10 standing down. Under per-team metrics no signal to stand down exists anywhere. Every team own number says keep pushing
  • The crucial part is that nobody did anything wrong. Every team faithfully maximized the number it was handed, and the whole lost 4.21 as a result
  • In practice this shows up as every team dashboard is green but the overall result is not there. No amount of aggregating per-team reports reveals the cause, because the cause lives between the teams, not inside any of them
  • The same table explains why single metrics work so well in small organizations. With few teams the spillover is small, so the local optimum coincides with the global one. The success experience expires as you grow

So the workable shape for a multi-team organization is one metric for the whole, and per-team terms that decompose it. Do not invent separate team metrics, decompose the shared one. If you find a team metric that cannot be derived from the whole, that team is probably disconnected from the overall goal.

8. Rollout procedure and checklist

Translating the calculations into a procedure gives the following. Order matters. The distinguishing feature is that you write the retirement criteria before you pick the candidate.

StepDo thisOutputWhat happens if you skip it
1Write down five decisions currently stuck on conflictList of real casesYou pick a metric by taste
2Score candidates on the four axes (section 5)Candidate tableAn easily moved metric wins
3Write down the cheapest way to raise itList of gaming pathsYou find that path three months later
4Set at most 3 guardrails against those pathsLines not to crossUnmeasured axes collapse (section 3)
5Put the review date on the calendarA dateLater becomes never
6Write the retirement criteria firstConditions to drop itYou can never drop it (below)
7Build team metrics only by decomposing the whole (section 7)Decomposition tableMisalignment as you scale

There is a reason step 6 comes early. A metric is extremely hard to drop once it is in place. People have been evaluated on it, teams have succeeded on it, investments were made to make it look good. So the retirement conditions have to be written while nobody yet has a stake in that metric.

8.1 The review cadence earns its keep

I put a number on the claim that a review cadence is not a ceremony. The assumptions: with a metric left frozen, the gamed share creeps up by 0.06 per period and stops at 0.80. Replacing the metric resets the gamed share to zero but costs 0.5 periods of output as a transition penalty. Cumulative output over 24 periods:

------------------------------------------------------------------------
   review every   cumulative true output   vs never   resets
------------------------------------------------------------------------
   never                       10.54           -        0

  • Never reviewing yields a cumulative 10.54. Reviewing every four periods yields 19.34, 83 percent higher. A single cadence is worth that much
  • Even one review lifts it 48 percent — the 12-period row. Putting one in beats hunting for the perfect interval
  • More often is not better. Every two periods drops back to 17.78 as the transition cost eats the gain. The optimum is interior, a U-shape
  • Note that the step is a review, not a replacement. Keeping the metric is a valid outcome, and when it is, writing down why you kept it makes the next review easier
  • The right interval depends on how fast gaming accumulates. The easier a metric is to game, the shorter the interval must be. Those are the metrics that scored low on gameability in section 5

8.2 Checklist

  • Have you written down the cheapest way to raise this metric, and is that the behaviour you want
  • Can the team that owns it actually move that number
  • Are there three or fewer guardrails. Past four you are sliding back to multi-metric
  • Are all guardrails phrased as do not cross. A raise this in the mix is a second objective
  • Is the review date on the calendar, an actual date rather than the word quarterly
  • Were the retirement conditions written before the metric shipped
  • Does the metric confirm within days. Quarterly confirmation magnifies the lag from section 3
  • If team metrics exist, are they a decomposition of the whole or separately invented
  • Are you counting the decisions a guardrail blocked. A rising rate means the objective is wrong
  • When picking top performers by this metric, have you checked the last column of section 4.2
  • On replacement, have you decided what to do about people evaluated under the old metric
  • Does the dashboard visually separate objective from guardrails. Same size, side by side, and you are already multi-metric

9. What this article did not verify

A model is a model. Writing unverified things as if they were verified misleads whoever reads this next.

  • Every number came from assumptions. No real organization was measured. Different assumptions give different magnitudes. I do not rule out a reversal of direction either
  • The gaming response curve is a pure assumption. I hold no empirical measurement of how far people shift toward gaming as pressure rises. The sharp break in section 4.2 is a product of that assumption
  • The coefficients in section 3 are assumptions too. How fast quality debt builds, and how many periods before it hits growth, differ by organization. The peak landed at period 12 because I set the coefficients that way
  • The spillover cost in section 7 is likewise assumed. The break between 16 and 32 teams follows from setting the mean cost at 0.020. Change it and the break moves. What matters is not the number 32 but the shape: it snaps at some line
  • I did not survey outcomes at organizations that actually adopted single metrics. This article makes no claim that either approach produced better real results
  • No company internal operations are the subject here. The sentence quoted in the opening comes from an outside developer post about billing API design
  • Research on the cognitive limits of tracking many metrics at once was not consulted. The folk claim that many metrics scatter attention is not tested here, which is why it is not used as evidence

Conclusion: not a question of count, a question of priority

Compressed into one line, the calculation says this. Cutting to one metric is not a measurement problem, it is a priority problem.

The third line of section 1 is the evidence. Even with three metrics, writing the weights down returned the decided rate to 100 percent. What blocked decisions was never the count. It was that nobody had written down what outranks what. Cutting to one is the bluntest available way to state a priority, and it is fast because it is blunt.

That bluntness carries a price tag. What you stop measuring necessarily gets worse. In section 3 quality reached 66.6 by period 16, and that debt eventually pulled the target metric down from its period-12 peak. This is not an accident, it is contained in the design. It went fast because the rule was complete, and complete means it never looks outside itself.

So a single metric cannot travel alone. It has to come with guardrails and a review cadence. Three guardrails is the ceiling. Beyond that you have a multi-metric system wearing a single-metric label, which loses the delegability that was the whole point and keeps only the illusion of simplicity. And the review cadence is not ceremony: in the model, never reviewing against reviewing every four periods was 10.54 against 19.34 in cumulative output.

With multiple teams the arithmetic changes again. In section 7 there was zero loss up to 16 teams and 26.4 percent at 32. The experience of a single metric working well in a small organization expires as you scale. Rather than inventing a separate metric per team, decompose one shared metric down to team level.

There is one thing you can do right now. Write down three of the cheapest ways to raise the metric you use today. Then ask whether those are the behaviours the organization wants. If they are not, there is a good chance those paths are already in use. That is ultimately what section 4.2 showed. Apply pressure to a metric without guardrails and a review cadence, and Goodhart law is not a question of whether, only of when.

# Designing a single metric - candidate scoring, guardrails, review cadence

> Source: braindetox.kr — One Metric to Steer By
> Every number below comes from an **assumed model**. No real organization was measured.

## 1. One-line summary

- Cutting to one metric is **a priority problem, not a measurement problem**
- Cut to one and decisions land faster. And **whatever you stop measuring gets worse**
- So a single metric only works **together with guardrails and a review cadence**

## 2. Candidate scoring (1-5, weighted sum)

| Candidate | Outcome .35 | Hard to game .25 | Team control .25 | Speed .15 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time to first success | 4 | 4 | 5 | 4 | **4.25** |
| Paid conversion rate | 5 | 3 | 4 | 3 | 3.95 |
| Repeat purchase rate (30d) | 5 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 3.80 |
| Monthly recurring revenue | 5 | 4 | 2 | 2 | 3.55 |
| Support tickets per 100 users | 3 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 3.40 |
| Code review turnaround | 2 | 2 | 5 | 5 | 3.20 |
| Weekly active users | 3 | 2 | 4 | 4 | 3.15 |
| Deploy count | 1 | 1 | 5 | 5 | 2.60 |
| Deadline hit rate | 2 | 1 | 4 | 3 | 2.40 |
| Net promoter score | 3 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2.35 |

**Sub-scores are judgment, not measurement.** Re-score with your own numbers.

- The metric closest to the outcome is not automatically best → if teams cannot move it, they produce narratives
- **A metric that is easy to move is a warning sign** (deploy count, deadline hit rate)
- If the cheapest way to raise it is **padding estimates**, strike it from the list

## 3. Guardrail design

| Guardrails | Objective decides alone | Mean live axes | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 100.0% | 1.00 | Pure single metric |
| 1 | 82.0% | 1.18 | Safe |
| 2 | 69.6% | 1.36 | Safe |
| **3** | **60.2%** | **1.55** | **Practical ceiling** |
| 4 | 53.3% | 1.73 | Warning |
| 5 | 48.0% | 1.89 | Constraints decide the majority |
| 6 | 43.2% | 2.09 | **Multi-metric in practice** |

Rules:

- Guardrails are **do not cross this line** only. A raise this in the mix is a second objective
- **At most 3.** From four onward you are sliding back to multi-metric
- **Visually separate** objective from guardrails on the dashboard
- **Count** the decisions guardrails blocked. A rising rate means the objective is wrong

## 4. Review cadence

| Review interval | Cumulative output over 24 periods | vs never |
|---|---|---|
| Never | 10.54 | - |
| 12 periods | 15.58 | +48% |
| 8 periods | 17.96 | +70% |
| 6 periods | 18.90 | +79% |
| **4 periods** | **19.34** | **+83%** |
| 3 periods | 19.06 | +81% |
| 2 periods | 17.78 | +69% |

- **Even one review is +48%.** Put one in before hunting for the perfect interval
- **More often is not better.** A U-shape, because the transition cost eats the gain
- It is a **review**, not a replacement. If you keep it, write down why

## 6. Rollout procedure

1. Write down **five decisions currently stuck on conflict**
2. Score candidates on the four axes (table in section 2)
3. Write down **the cheapest way to raise** the chosen metric
4. Set **at most 3 guardrails** against those paths
5. Put the **review date** on the calendar (a date, not the word quarterly)
6. Write the **retirement criteria first** — only possible while nobody has a stake
7. Build team metrics **only by decomposing the shared metric**

## 7. Checklist

- [ ] Is **the cheapest way to raise this metric** the behaviour we want
- [ ] Can the owning team **actually move that number**
- [ ] Are there **3 or fewer guardrails**
- [ ] Are all guardrails phrased as **do not cross**
- [ ] Is the review **date** on the calendar
- [ ] Were the **retirement conditions** written before rollout
- [ ] Does the metric confirm **within days**
- [ ] Are team metrics a **decomposition of the shared metric**
- [ ] Are we **counting** the decisions guardrails blocked
- [ ] Have we checked the **real outcomes of top performers**
- [ ] On replacement, what happens to **people evaluated under the old metric**
- [ ] Does the dashboard **separate objective from guardrails**

## 8. Do not

- ❌ Raise a guardrail **like a target** — that instant it becomes a second objective
- ❌ Invent **a different single metric per team** — it misaligns as you scale
- ❌ Roll out a metric **with no retirement criteria** — you will never drop it
- ❌ Conclude focus worked **at period 6 to 10** — the bill arrives later
- ❌ **Blame people** when gaming appears — the rule is what broke
- ❌ Use these numbers **as your own values** — they are all from an assumed model
