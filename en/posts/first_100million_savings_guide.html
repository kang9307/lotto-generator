Saving Your First 100 Million Won - The Arithmetic of Time and Conditions

Tables of months to the goal, built from deposits, returns, tax and inflation, and the order the arithmetic implies

2026-07-31 Finance

Where this started: the arithmetic was never done

Setting a savings goal is common. What is far less common is the step that should follow it. In most of the conversations where the goal comes up, nobody works out how many years it takes. There is a target and there is no shortage of method, but the single line of division that connects them is missing.

This article fills in that division. It does not discuss any product or strategy. Instead it computes how many months it takes to reach 100 million Korean won for each combination of monthly deposit and annual return, lays that out as a table, and then adds tax, inflation and mid-course withdrawals on top. Every calculation uses a published formula and is checked against itself.

The result stated up front is this. What rules the early stretch is the deposit, not the return. A return only starts to matter once enough principal has accumulated, and until then the balance is too small for a return to act on. So the question of where to put the money comes after two other questions: how much can be set aside each month, and can it be structured so that it does not get broken into.

One thing needs to be nailed down in advance. The savings rate is set by income and fixed costs, not by willpower. The same resolve produces different results when housing costs and loan repayments are structured differently. Section 9 confirms that with arithmetic as well.

1. The formula first, and what it assumes

Before the tables, here is how the numbers were produced. A table whose assumptions are unknown cannot be read.

Target T = 100,000,000 KRW
Monthly deposit P, annual return r, monthly rate i = r / 12
Assumes end-of-month deposits and monthly compounding

Future value  FV(n) = P × ((1 + i)^n − 1) / i
Months        n = ln(1 + T × i / P) / ln(1 + i)
When i = 0    n = T / P
Months rounded up

Check: feed n back into FV and confirm it returns the target
       5 deposit levels × 4 return levels = 20 combinations, all checked
       mismatches: 0

This is the standard future-value calculation for a regular savings plan. The same amount goes in at the end of each month, and the whole balance earns i each month. Actual products calculate interest in different ways, so this result is a baseline for comparison, not the expected return of any product.

Here is every assumption that went into it, together with how each one differs from reality.

  • The monthly deposit is fixed for the whole period. In reality income changes, bonuses arrive and spending spikes. So the table gives the time when that amount can be sustained on average
  • The return is fixed for the whole period. This is the biggest simplification. Real returns vary month to month, and with anything that can lose principal there are stretches where the figure is negative
  • The 0, 2, 4 and 6 percent figures are all assumed values. They are not the actual return of any product, and using them does not imply those returns are achievable. They are simply gradations chosen for comparison
  • The figures are before tax. Section 5 computes separately how interest income tax shifts the table
  • Inflation is not reflected. Section 6 shows how far a nominal target diverges from a real one
  • There are no mid-course withdrawals. Section 7 covers what happens when the plan is broken even once
  • There are no fees or costs. Real vehicles carry costs, and costs reduce the return by exactly that much

A long list of assumptions does not make the table useless. It means that because the assumptions are stated, it is clear which of them to adjust. Sections 5, 6 and 7 do exactly that, releasing one assumption at a time and showing which way and how far the table moves.

There are several ways to read it. Start with the leftmost column.

  • The 0 percent column is pure division. It is the target divided by the monthly deposit, and it contains no assumption at all. That column is the physical floor for that deposit level
  • The 300,000 KRW row shows the widest spread. The gap between 334 months at 0 percent and 197 months at an assumed 6 percent is 137 months, or eleven years and five months
  • The 1,500,000 KRW row collapses that spread. The range is 67 to 58 months, a difference of only nine months, because the period is too short for compounding to do much
  • In other words the power of a return is a function of time. It grows the longer the period runs and shrinks the faster it ends. Turned around, that means anyone who wants to finish quickly cannot lean on returns
  • No cell in the table is short. Even the fastest combination, 1,500,000 KRW a month at an assumed 6 percent, takes four years and ten months

This table is colder than the previous one. A three-year target demands 2,543,000 KRW a month even at an assumed 6 percent return. The gap against 0 percent is only 235,000 KRW. It shows again how little a return can do over a short horizon.

Stretch the period to fifteen years and the return earns a larger share. The gap between 556,000 KRW at 0 percent and 344,000 KRW at an assumed 6 percent is 212,000 KRW, or 38 percent. But that requires the return to hold for fifteen years, and there is no guarantee such an assumption holds.

The order in which to use this table. Do not pick a target period and read off the required monthly amount. Confirm the amount you can actually save first, then see which row it lands on. Doing it the other way around writes down a number that cannot be sustained, and that is how the withdrawals in section 7 begin.

3. An extra 100,000 won beats two points of return

This is the most commonly misread point. Most talk about building wealth concentrates on lifting the return, yet putting the two options side by side inside the same table reverses the priority.

Taking an assumed 2 percent as the baseline, the left side shows raising the return to an assumed 4 percent, a gain of two percentage points, and the right side shows leaving the return alone and adding 100,000 KRW a month.

Baseline monthly depositBaseline months (2% assumed)Return raised to 4%SavedAdd 100,000 KRWSaved
300,000 KRW266 months225 months41 months210 months56 months
500,000 KRW173 months154 months19 months148 months25 months
800,000 KRW114 months105 months9 months103 months11 months
1,000,000 KRW93 months87 months6 months85 months8 months

The right side wins in all four rows, and not by a hair. At 300,000 KRW a month it is 41 months against 56, a margin of fifteen months.

The same result can be expressed another way. What annual return is the extra 100,000 KRW equivalent to, solved backwards.

  • For someone saving 300,000 KRW a month, an extra 100,000 KRW equals an annual return of 5.02 percent, which is 3.02 percentage points above the baseline
  • At 500,000 KRW it equals 4.81 percent, 2.81 percentage points above the baseline
  • At 800,000 KRW it equals 4.68 percent, 2.68 percentage points above the baseline
  • At 1,000,000 KRW it equals 4.64 percent, 2.64 percentage points above the baseline
  • So the smaller the deposit, the greater the relative power of adding to it, because 100,000 on top of 300,000 is a 33 percent increase while the same amount on top of 1,000,000 is a 10 percent increase

What matters here is that the two options are different in kind. This article cannot offer a way to reliably earn two more percentage points, and in general that comes at the price of greater volatility or a longer lock-up. An extra 100,000 KRW, by contrast, is a computable quantity whose availability is unambiguous.

All the same, the conclusion is not simply to add 100,000 KRW. Where that money comes from is the real problem, and that is the subject of section 9.

4.1 The composition at the moment the goal is reached

Finally, what the finished total is actually made of was checked at the moment the goal is met, at 500,000 KRW a month.

  • At an assumed 2 percent: 173 months, cumulative principal 86,500,000 KRW, gain 13,664,534 KRW, so the principal share is 86.4 percent
  • At an assumed 4 percent: 154 months, cumulative principal 77,000,000 KRW, gain 23,413,855 KRW, so the principal share is 76.7 percent
  • At an assumed 6 percent: 139 months, cumulative principal 69,500,000 KRW, gain 30,524,219 KRW, so the principal share is 69.5 percent
  • Under all three assumptions, at least seven tenths of the total was money the saver deposited

None of this says compounding is pointless. The 24,086,063 KRW accumulated by year thirteen is not a small sum. But for that gain to appear, 78,000,000 KRW had to go in first. The order is the point of the table.

5. Tax, what interest income tax does to the table

Every table so far has been before tax. Now tax goes in, starting with the basis for the rate.

Basis and reference date for the rate. Article 129 of the Korean Income Tax Act sets withholding rates and specifies 14 hundredths on other interest income. The National Tax Service withholding guidance shows the same figure. This was confirmed as of 31 July 2026. A local income tax is levied on top, so the total withheld is larger than 14 percent. The table below reflects only the 14 percent income tax, which makes it a conservative estimate that understates the effect of tax. Rates and the method of taxation can be amended, and the method itself can change with the size of a person's financial income, so confirm your own situation with National Tax Service guidance and a tax professional.

Because interest income tax applies to the interest earned, the calculation treats it as reducing the effective return by that proportion, multiplying the nominal return by one minus 0.14.

  • A nominal 2 percent becomes 1.72 percent after tax
  • A nominal 4 percent becomes 3.44 percent after tax
  • A nominal 6 percent becomes 5.16 percent after tax

Two things stand out. One is that the direction of the tax effect is certain but its size is smaller than the deposit effect in section 3. At 500,000 KRW and an assumed 4 percent, tax pushes the finish back five months while an extra 100,000 KRW pulls it forward twenty-five.

The other is that tax bites harder the higher the return. It costs three months at an assumed 2 percent and six months at an assumed 6 percent, because more interest means more withheld. That is why comparing pre-tax returns overstates the real difference.

  • Tax is a factor that pushes the date back, not one that breaks the goal. The worst case here was six months
  • But tax optimisation cannot substitute for saving more. The two operate at different orders of magnitude
  • Vehicles differ in tax treatment, yet this article does not claim any vehicle is better. Section 8 sets out character only
  • The real after-tax outcome depends on when and how interest is actually paid. This calculation simplifies it to even monthly taxation
  • How deposit interest is structured, and how simple and compound interest differ, is covered separately in reading deposit and savings rates

6. Inflation, a nominal target is not a real one

If tax pushes the date back, inflation erodes the goal itself. And this effect is far larger.

Every inflation rate used here is an assumed value. The three figures of 2.0, 2.5 and 3.0 percent are not a forecast or projection but gradations chosen to show sensitivity. Actual inflation differs every year, and the inflation an individual experiences differs again with what they buy. The purpose of this section is not to predict a number but to show what happens when a fixed nominal figure is treated as the goal.

This produces the most uncomfortable calculation in the article. Assuming 2.5 percent inflation, a nominal 2 percent return never reaches the goal in real terms. The real return is negative, and the computation returns no solution.

  • 500,000 KRW a month at a nominal assumed 4 percent: the nominal goal takes 154 months, the real goal takes 180 months, twenty-six months longer
  • 1,000,000 KRW a month at a nominal assumed 4 percent: 87 months nominal against 95 months real, eight months longer
  • At a nominal assumed 2 percent, neither 500,000 nor 1,000,000 KRW a month reaches the real goal under 2.5 percent assumed inflation
  • The real return was approximated as (1 plus nominal) divided by (1 plus inflation) minus 1
  • The gap widens with time, which is why a goal has to be written together with a period

The practical conclusion is to rewrite the goal. Stating it as a target amount by a specific year rather than a target amount alone brings inflation into the statement. And if the resulting period comes out long, read it as a signal to raise the target amount or shorten the horizon.

7. What breaking the plan costs

Every table so far carried the assumption of never breaking into the account. In practice that is the assumption that fails most often, so the cost of breaking it was simulated.

The baseline is 500,000 KRW a month at an assumed 4 percent, reaching the goal in 154 months. Each month the balance earns the rate and the deposit is added, then in specified months a withdrawal is taken or deposits are paused.

SituationMonthsDelayTotal withdrawn
No interruption (baseline)154 monthsbaseline0 KRW
Pause deposits 6 months from month 24160 months+6 months0 KRW
Withdraw 5,000,000 in month 24163 months+9 months5,000,000 KRW
Pause deposits 12 months from month 24165 months+11 months0 KRW
Withdraw 5,000,000 in month 24 plus 3-month pause166 months+12 months5,000,000 KRW
Withdraw 3,000,000 every three years, three times169 months+15 months9,000,000 KRW
Withdraw 10,000,000 in month 36172 months+18 months10,000,000 KRW

The row worth staring at is the third. 5,000,000 KRW is ten months of deposits, and the delay is nine months. Almost one for one. In other words a withdrawal converts directly into time, and the return that money would have earned vanishes along with it.

  • Taking money out hurts more than stopping deposits. A six-month pause costs six months, while a 5,000,000 KRW withdrawal costs nine
  • Taking a little out repeatedly is not safe either. Three withdrawals of 3,000,000 KRW every three years, 9,000,000 KRW in total, cost fifteen months
  • Withdrawals and pauses usually arrive together. A 5,000,000 KRW withdrawal alone costs nine months, and adding a three-month pause makes it twelve. The events that demand a lump sum usually reduce capacity to deposit as well
  • Early termination can also mean the agreed rate no longer applies, so the real loss can exceed what this table shows
  • That is the arithmetic case for keeping money to be spent first in a separate place from the goal account. How to size that amount is covered in the emergency fund guide

In short, making the goal account hard to break is a variable of the same magnitude as the return. In section 3 two percentage points of return pulled the date forward nineteen months, and in this table a single 10,000,000 KRW withdrawal pushed it back eighteen. The same order of magnitude, except that the first is controllable and the second is not.

8. The character of savings vehicles, three axes only

This section does not say which vehicle is better, and names no product or institution. It sets out character along three axes only: liquidity, principal protection and withdrawal constraint. Which axis matters most depends on the target period and personal circumstances, and that judgement belongs to the reader.

Deposit insurance cap and reference date. According to a Financial Services Commission press release, the deposit protection cap was raised to 100 million won with effect from 1 September 2025. The protected amount is 100 million won per person per financial institution, including principal and interest. This was confirmed as of 31 July 2026, and the rules can change. Note also that not every financial product is covered. Whether a given product is protected should be checked before signing up.

Connecting the three axes to the target period gives a frame for deciding. But this too is a frame, not an answer.

  • The shorter the period, the more liquidity and principal protection weigh. As section 2 showed, a return can do little over a short horizon
  • The longer the period, the more room there is to absorb withdrawal constraints. Those constraints may nonetheless collide with the situations in section 7
  • The section 2 table cannot be applied to anything that can lose principal, because the fixed-return assumption does not hold there
  • Tax treatment differs by vehicle but also by a person's income situation. A generalised comparison invites misreading, so it is left out here
  • Separating the goal account physically from the salary account is a structure that applies whichever vehicle is chosen
  • When overseas assets enter the picture, tax rules and currency add further variables. That part is covered in overseas fund investing and taxation

9. Reality check, fixed costs set the savings rate

Section 3 concluded that adding 100,000 KRW a month is powerful. But without asking where that money comes from, the conclusion is either empty or cruel. This section checks that with arithmetic.

First, monthly take-home pay against savings rate. Before tax at an assumed 2 percent, with each cell showing months to the goal.

The table reads the same whichever way it is taken. Lower income means a longer period at the same savings rate, and shortening that period requires a higher rate. And a higher rate is harder at lower income.

So what sets the savings rate. Fixed costs. Holding take-home pay at 3,500,000 KRW and variable costs at 700,000 KRW, only the fixed costs were changed.

A 300,000 KRW difference in fixed costs shifts the amount available to save by 300,000 KRW, and that stretches the period from 73 months to 210 months. Same income, same variable costs, same resolve, and a result that differs by nearly three times.

And fixed costs do not change overnight. Housing has a contract term, loan repayments are already set, and cancelling insurance carries a price. These are not items that respond to a decision to spend less this month.

  • So reducing a low savings rate to a question of willpower is a mistake. The savings rate is the residual left after fixed costs and minimum living costs are taken out of income
  • Advice to spend less usually targets variable costs, yet in the table above the variable costs stayed at 700,000 KRW and the result still varied threefold. Even driving variable costs to zero cannot lift the savings rate above 31.4 percent under the 2,400,000 KRW fixed-cost row
  • The effective approach is listing fixed-cost items and writing down when each one comes up for renewal, because those are the moments when change is possible
  • Not letting fixed costs grow when income grows is the same point. Income that flows into fixed costs leaves the amount available to save unchanged
  • A period that comes out unmanageably long is a conclusion of the arithmetic, not a failure. The right reading is a signal to adjust the target amount or the horizon
  • If the exact monthly take-home figure is unclear, checking it first with the Korean salary net pay calculator is the better order

10. Applying this to your own numbers

Here is the order in which to actually use the calculations. The important part is that choosing a vehicle comes last.

10.1 Step one, work out what can be saved

  • Fix the monthly take-home figure from three months of actual deposits, not from an estimate
  • List fixed costs item by item: housing, loan repayment, insurance, telecom, subscriptions, transport
  • Take a three-month average of variable costs. A single month is distorted by outliers
  • Take-home minus fixed minus variable equals the amount available to save. That value is the vertical axis of the section 2 table
  • If that value differs greatly from what was actually left over in the last three months, the second figure is the right one. Use the record, not the plan

10.2 Step two, work backwards from the period

  • Read the monthly amount required for the desired period from the reverse table in section 2.1
  • Compare it with the amount from step one. It usually falls short. That is normal
  • If it falls short, move one of target amount, target period or amount available to save
  • The return is not on that list. Adjusting an uncontrollable value to make the arithmetic work turns a plan into a hope
  • Consult the inflation tables in section 6 and decide whether the goal is stated in nominal or real terms. Beyond ten years this distinction grows large

10.3 Step three, build something that will not break

  • Put an emergency reserve outside the goal account first. The table in section 7 is the reason
  • Set the automatic transfer date to just after payday. That inverts the order of saving whatever is left
  • Add at least one step of friction to taking money out of the goal account. What transfers instantly leaves instantly
  • Choose the vehicle using only the three axes from section 8, not by ranking returns
  • Set a review interval, six or twelve months, and recompute whenever income or fixed costs change
  • Write the period on a calendar. Not the amount alone, but the amount by a specific month and year

11. What the arithmetic said

With the calculations finished, what remains fits into a few lines.

First, the time to the goal is mostly set by the deposit. At 300,000 KRW a month, two percentage points of return cut 41 months while an extra 100,000 KRW cut 56. The deposit won in all four comparisons. Section 4 explains why: 98 percent of the balance in year one is principal, and even at the finish line at least seven tenths is principal.

Second, tax pushes the date back and inflation erodes the goal. Counting only the 14 percent income tax cost up to six months, and assuming 2.5 percent inflation, a nominal 2 percent return never reached the goal in real terms. So a goal has to be written with a period attached, not as an amount alone.

Third, a structure that does not break is a variable of the same magnitude as the return. A single 10,000,000 KRW withdrawal pushed the date back eighteen months, while two percentage points of return pulled it forward nineteen. The same size, except the first is controllable and the second is not.

Fourth, and most importantly, the savings rate is the residual of fixed costs. At the same income, a 300,000 KRW difference in fixed costs split the period into 73 months and 210 months. Reading a failure to save as a failure of will sends people to fix the thing that cannot be fixed. The place to look is the list of fixed costs and the renewal date of each item.

There is one thing that can be done immediately. Open the last three months of bank records and average what was actually left over. Find that number on the vertical axis of the section 2 table and the period appears, the one that was never calculated. If the number is unwelcome, the thing to adjust is not the return but one line in the list of fixed costs.

## 8. Character of savings vehicles (character, not ranking)

| Type | Liquidity | Principal protection | Withdrawal constraint | What to verify |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Instant-access account | Very high | Within deposit insurance cap | None | Interest is close to none |
| Deposit and installment savings | High | Within deposit insurance cap | Maturity | Early-termination rate |
| Pension-type account | Low | Depends on holdings | Eligibility and timing limits | Tax treatment of early withdrawal |
| ISA-type account | Medium | Depends on holdings | Mandatory holding period | Maturity and extension terms |
| Investment products | Varies by product | None, principal can be lost | Varies by product | Loss potential and costs |

Deposit insurance cap: from 1 September 2025, 100 million won per person per financial institution including principal and interest (checked 2026-07-31). Rules can change.

## 10. Applying it to your own situation

- [ ] Step 1. Fixed the **actual monthly take-home** from three months of bank records, not an estimate
- [ ] Step 2. Listed fixed costs line by line (housing, loan repayment, insurance, telecom, subscriptions, transport)
- [ ] Step 3. Averaged variable costs over three months
- [ ] Step 4. Computed take-home minus fixed minus variable to get the **amount available to save**
- [ ] Step 5. Read the required monthly amount for the target period from table 2
- [ ] Step 6. If the available amount is smaller, **adjusted one of target amount, target period, or available amount** (return is not an adjustment lever)
- [ ] Step 7. Decided from table 6 whether the goal is stated in nominal or real terms
- [ ] Step 8. Kept an **emergency reserve outside** the goal account
- [ ] Step 9. Chose vehicles using only the three axes in table 8 (liquidity, principal protection, withdrawal constraint)
- [ ] Step 10. Set the automatic transfer date to **just after payday**
- [ ] Step 11. Added **at least one step of friction** to taking money out of the goal account
- [ ] Step 12. Set a review interval (six or twelve months)
- [ ] Step 13. Agreed to recompute the table whenever income or fixed costs change
