The video that would not open, and what it left behind

Install Linux, open a video file you already had, and watch codec not found come back at you. That experience spans generations of users, and the fix was always shaped the same way. Add one more repository, install a bundle of packages with unfamiliar names, and only then does the file play. Sometimes a video that streamed fine in the browser refused to open in a local player. Sometimes the exact same file played on one distribution and not on another.

None of that was a technical limitation. The decoder code had been written years earlier, the source was public, and performance was never the issue. What stood in the way was patents — or more precisely, not the patents themselves but the liability carried by whoever ships that code to millions of people.

In July 2026 one piece of that structure closed by expiry. A patent that had held on for 25 years ended, and an older standard was reported to be free of patent encumbrance worldwide for the first time. That is welcome news, but it is also easy to over-read. What ended, and what did not, blur together fast. This article draws that line.

What it means for a patent to attach to a codec

Anyone who has worked with open source for a while trips over this once. If the source is public and the license is permissive, surely we are done? Patents sit on a different layer.

  • Copyright protects expression. It controls copying, modifying and distributing the particular code somebody wrote.
  • A patent protects a method. Write the code from scratch, and if you use the method, you are still inside the claim.
  • Which is why a free software license is not the same thing as a patent license. Clearing the copyright side does not settle the patent side along with it.
  • Some licenses carry an explicit patent grant from contributors, but that grant covers patents the contributors hold. An unrelated third party's patent falls outside it entirely.

Codecs are an especially bad case because of how standards are shaped. A video compression standard is not one invention but a composition of hundreds of techniques. Motion prediction, transforms, quantization, entropy coding, loop filtering — each stage has its own methods, and each method can carry a patent belonging to a different company.

That is where the character of a standard essential patent comes from. If you follow the standard, there is no way to avoid the technique, so there is no designing around it. Implementing the standard is, by construction, practicing the patent. That differs fundamentally from an ordinary patent you can engineer past, and it is the reason codec licensing grew its own institutions.

Patent pools and royalties - one counter instead of fifty

Picture hundreds of patents on a single standard, scattered across dozens of companies. If an implementer had to negotiate with each holder individually, the negotiation alone would outlive the product. The institution that grew up to solve this is the patent pool.

  • Holders place their patents with one administrator, who sells them bundled as a single license.
  • The implementer signs with one counterparty, and the administrator distributes collected royalties back to the holders.
  • Rates are usually set per unit shipped, or by service scale for streaming, or both at once.
  • Annual caps are common, so that a high volume vendor is not billed without limit.

A handful of administrator names recur in video. MPEG LA ran MPEG-family pools for years and that lineage carried into Via Licensing Alliance. On the HEVC side, Access Advance states that it runs the HEVC Advance pool, and a VVC Advance pool for the newer standard. Sisvel operates separate programs covering VP9 and AV1.

There is one point here that gets missed constantly in practice. Holding a pool license does not settle every right on that standard. Holders who never joined the pool always exist, and some standards have several competing pools at once. A pool reduces transaction cost; it is not a certificate of immunity.

Rates are not fixed either. According to Streaming Media, Via LA restructured its AVC streaming terms, replacing the single annual cap of 100,000 dollars set back in August 2010 with a tiered structure. The top tier was reported at 4.5 million dollars per year, with the next tiers at 3.375 million and 2.25 million, while small and nascent services stay at 100,000. Notably, the restructuring was described as applying to implementers newly seeking a license from 2026 onward, with companies holding an active license as of the end of 2025 keeping their original streaming terms.

What actually ended - the last member of one pool

Here is the substance of the news. What expired is a single patent, but the reason it mattered is that it was the last remaining right in the MPEG-4 Visual patent portfolio. In standards language that is MPEG-4 Part 2; in practical terms it is the format Xvid and DivX used.

The values above come from the Wikimedia community tracker for MPEG-4 Visual patent status and from reporting on this news. That tracker states plainly that it is not suitable as official legal advice, and it is worth carrying that caveat along whenever the numbers are quoted.

The final one, BR PI0109962-0, is recorded as covering a process for storing and processing image information from successive images over time. It was filed on April 9, 2001, granted on July 19, 2016, and expired on July 19, 2026. From filing to expiry: 25 years.

Why only Brazil ran the full 25 years

Same invention, yet it ended in Europe in 2021 and stayed alive in Brazil until 2026. The reason is that the two jurisdictions count patent term differently.

  • Most countries count twenty years from the filing date. A long examination eats into the time you can actually enforce.
  • The sole paragraph of Article 40 of Brazil's Industrial Property Law (Law 9,279/96) put a floor under that, guaranteeing an invention patent at least ten years from grant.
  • This patent was filed in 2001 and granted in 2016. Fifteen years of examination, the floor applied, and the expiry slid out to 2026.

That provision has since been struck down. In ADI 5529, a constitutional challenge filed in 2016, Brazil's Federal Supreme Court declared the sole paragraph unconstitutional on May 6, 2021. The reasoning ran through the constitutional guarantee that patents are temporary, and through the observation that terms varying with examination delay damage legal certainty and competition.

So why was this patent not cut short then? Because, as reported, the retroactive effect was confined to pharmaceutical and health related patents, and the Brazilian patent office reduced terms for 3,341 patents in that scope. A codec patent sat outside that boundary, kept the old arithmetic, and ran to July 2026.

This is the most practical lesson in the whole episode. Patent geography is not uniform. Expiry in the US and Europe does not mean expiry everywhere, and one procedural quirk in one jurisdiction can push the free-to-use date of an entire standard out by five years.

The real reason distributions held back - redistribution liability

This is where the user-facing symptom connects to the distributor's problem. The hinge is that using something and distributing it are different acts.

  • An individual playing a video on their own machine is not the target the royalty structure is built around.
  • A distribution, by contrast, is a party that builds software and ships it widely. Bundling a patented implementation makes the act of shipping itself the exposure.
  • The nature of the shipper matters too. An entity with a US presence and revenue is an attractive target; one without is a much lower practical risk.
  • The LWN coverage put this bluntly. Distributors facing little litigation risk carried on shipping the very same codecs.

So distributions each found a different compromise.

  • Push it into a separate repository. Keep it out of the distribution proper and let a distinct party host it, so users opt in themselves. That is exactly the extra-repository ritual users kept running into.
  • Let a third party pay the royalties on a binary. Cisco has covered the patent costs for a specific H.264 implementation called OpenH264 and distributed it at no charge — with the condition that it is the binary Cisco distributes.
  • Separate it by runtime. Application distribution runtimes carried codecs so that the base distribution and the runtime held different responsibilities.

An interesting exception surfaced alongside this news. Fedora was already shipping xvidcore in 2023 while the Brazilian patent was still in force. Individual distributions have long weighed jurisdiction and actual risk and landed in different places — and it also shows that policy does not auto-follow an expiry date.

The 2022 change that switched off hardware acceleration

The clearest expression of this structure came in 2022. That September, Fedora changed how it built the Mesa graphics library so that the hardware accelerated paths for H.264, H.265 and VC-1 were turned off. openSUSE moved the same direction shortly after.

The reasoning went like this.

  • Mesa was implementing the software portions of decode and encode as part of the video acceleration API.
  • Hardware on its own does not infringe, but the combination of hardware and software performs the work, so the implementation as delivered could constitute infringement.
  • Hardware vendors sold their parts without licensing them for end user software. The assumption was that customers would obtain a license separately.
  • Removing the code closed off the ground for a willful infringement claim.

To users this arrived as hardware decoding suddenly vanishing. Alternatives existed: a third party repository shipped rebuilt Mesa and FFmpeg with the codecs restored, and applications delivered through app runtimes kept their codec support through the runtime build.

What to take from this is not a verdict on any distribution but an understanding of the shape. Patent exposure attaches to a combination, not to one lump of code. Knowing your own tree contains no encoder is not enough on its own; what the software you shipped does once it meets particular hardware is inside the question too.

HEVC is the tangled one. Access Advance states that its HEVC Advance pool licenses more than 29,000 patents essential to HEVC, and that its VVC Advance pool licenses more than 4,500 essential to VVC. It also states that it acquired the administrator of the HEVC and VVC pool previously run by Via LA and continues it as a separate program. Counters are consolidating, but that does not mean an implementer now has only one place to check.

Where royalty free codecs came from, and why it is not quiet

The web needed codecs, and per-unit royalties fit the web's business model badly. Browsers ship hundreds of millions of copies for free, and video shows up everywhere. That gap produced the push toward royalty free codecs.

  • Google built on technology it had acquired and released VP8 and then VP9 as open formats.
  • In 2015 AOMedia formed, with Google, Netflix, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, Nvidia, Intel, ARM, Samsung, Cisco and Mozilla among the participants named.
  • The result was AV1, shipped alongside a royalty free patent license under which members open their own patents at no charge.

One distinction has to be read carefully here. Royalty free is a commitment by the people who built the standard, not a warranty that no third party patent exists. That difference gets flattened more often than any other point in this field.

  • Sisvel states that it has run licensing programs covering VP9 and AV1 separately since March 2019.
  • Public descriptions listed twelve patent owners in the VP9 program and fourteen in the AV1 program.
  • Standard rates were published as 0.24 euro per display device and 0.08 euro per non-display device for VP9, and 0.32 and 0.11 euro for AV1.
  • On March 24, 2026 Access Advance announced that Dolby, a licensor in one of its pools, had sued Snap in the United States and Brazil alleging infringement of patents relating to AV1 and HEVC.
  • That announcement described the filing as the first assertion against an AV1 implementation by one of its licensors and the first AV1 assertion against a streaming platform.

Whether that claim has merit is a matter for a court, and there is no reason to preempt it here. The takeaway for practitioners is single and narrow. Choosing a royalty free standard does not remove the licensing review. It changes the character of the review rather than eliminating its basis.

A small user-visible change can sound like an anticlimax, but the value of this episode lies elsewhere. It leaves a concrete number on the record: an application filed in 2001 could hold up free distribution of a standard until 2026. When you weigh which standards a system you are designing now will depend on, that sense of duration transfers directly.

Four axes to judge your own position

The short version is that what you should look at depends entirely on what you are doing, because the same codec exposes you in different places.

  • First, which standard. The fact that something sits in an MP4 container tells you nothing. Container and codec are different layers. Pin down whether the stream is H.264, HEVC or AV1 before anything else.
  • Second, encode or decode. Rate structures usually separate the two, and which one you sell as a product feature changes what has to be reviewed.
  • Third, which jurisdictions you distribute into. Exactly what this episode demonstrated. Cleared in one country is not cleared everywhere.
  • Fourth, where the capability comes from. Using a function provided by an already licensed operating system or component sits differently from shipping a decoder yourself.

Lay your own situation across those four and the picture usually resolves like this.

  • Individual users have effectively nothing to manage. If the distribution does not ship it by default, it stays a matter of adding a repository.
  • Internal tooling has narrow distribution and no sale, which keeps it simpler, though some organizations still require a check under internal policy.
  • Publicly distributed open source makes the shipping entity and the jurisdiction the live questions. Whether a codec implementation goes in the tree or stays an optional component becomes a genuine design decision.
  • Hardware products hit per-unit rates directly. The thing to verify is how far up the chain your component supplier's license actually reaches, and that lives in the contract.
  • Streaming services face service-scale rates and timing together. The AVC restructuring applying to newly licensed implementers is exactly that kind of timing effect.

The same caveat attaches to every one of these. What is written here is a structural description drawn from public sources, and conclusions about a particular product in a particular jurisdiction belong to legal counsel. If codec choice genuinely moves your product cost or your distribution shape, that is the point at which to bring a professional in.

What I could not confirm

Here are the items public sources did not settle. They are also the reason those values do not appear in the body.

  • The final expiry date for the H.264/AVC pool. No full schedule published by the administrator was available.
  • Expiry schedules for the HEVC and VVC pools. Same as above.
  • Whether counterpart patents existed in Korea and when they ended. Korean registration status for the MPEG-4 Visual family was not confirmed.
  • The claim scope of BR PI0113271-7. Beyond the expiry date, the covered subject matter was not confirmed.
  • Whether distributions changed policy after this expiry. No official project announcements were confirmed.
  • The course of the pending dispute. Only the announcement itself was confirmed; nothing about subsequent procedure.
  • Current Sisvel rates and participant counts. The figures cited were published early in those programs; current values were not confirmed.

Conclusion - three things to take away

First, what ended is one standard. The last right in the pool covering MPEG-4 Part 2 is reported cleared, while H.264, HEVC and VVC — the ones actually in use today — all sit on their own separate structures. Generalizing this single item to codecs at large is where the error enters.

Second, patent geography and patent time are not uniform. The same invention ended in Europe in 2021 and ran to 2026 in Brazil. One rule about counting term, and one boundary on the retroactive reach of a constitutional ruling, produced a five year gap. When you assess standard dependencies, ask where it expires as well as when.

Third, royalty free does not mean risk free. A no-charge commitment from the people who built a standard is a different statement from a guarantee that every patent touching an implementation has been cleared. That distinction became a public dispute in 2026.

Codec selection has long been treated as a question of compression efficiency and hardware support. If you add one more axis, make it whether that standard's licensing shape fits how you distribute. If a patent that held on for 25 years left anything behind, it is the evidence that this axis stays relevant far longer than you would expect.
