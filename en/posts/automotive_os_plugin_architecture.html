Introduction: the computer line on the quote

A new car quote now carries a line that did not exist ten years ago. A large display, a consolidated controller, a connected-services fee. Where horsepower and fuel economy used to sit, compute performance and a software update policy have moved in. And yet the body is something people keep for well over a decade, while the compute inside it ages at roughly the pace of a laptop.

Which is why the obvious question shows up: why not just replace that part? Swap the compute layer the way you swap a graphics card, every few years. A recent Korean discussion argued exactly that for a carmaker's vehicle software platform, saying it should be a plug-in module rather than a built-in fixture, and the counterarguments stayed in the thread alongside it.

This article does not rule on whether the idea is right. It does something else: it splits the idea by layer and shows how far it travels and where it stops. The short version is that the blocker is not technical impossibility but the cost of proof and the shape of liability. And that cost comes back to whoever keeps the car longest, just in a different form.

What this article does not do. It does not describe how to touch vehicle control systems, how to use diagnostic equipment, or how to work around manufacturer policy. On safety-critical systems even the explanation stays at a conceptual level. It is also not legal advice, and standards and regulations are referenced only at the level of published names and scope. Actual certification and approval judgments belong to the competent authorities and to specialists in the field.

1. Software in a car is not one block

The phrase vehicle OS creates the confusion. It calls up the phone picture, where one operating system presides over the whole device. Cars are not built that way. Several lineages made under different rules ride in the same body, and those lineages differ in purpose and, more importantly, in what happens when they fail.

1.1 Three broad lineages

  • Safety-critical control — braking, steering, drive torque, restraint systems, anything that puts force into the physical world. Response time is baked into the design conditions, and behaving predictably is itself a requirement.
  • Comfort and infotainment — display, navigation, voice, audio, apps. The heaviest compute and the fastest-moving fashion. This is mostly where a driver decides whether a car feels current.
  • Connectivity and gateway — external links, remote services, update delivery paths. It controls nothing on its own, but it is the only seam between inside and outside, so it turns up in the risk calculation for both other layers.

This split matters not because the performance tiers differ. It matters because the kind of rule attached to each layer differs. Which is how one sentence about modularising vehicle software turns into three separate projects once anyone tries to schedule it.

1.2 The rules attached to each layer

LayerWhat it doesConsequence of failureTiming demandProcedure to change it
Safety-critical control Braking, steering, drive, restraints Can turn into physical harm immediately Deterministic response is a design condition Functional safety argument revisited, type approval impact assessed
Comfort and infotainment Display, navigation, voice, apps Inconvenience, service outage Loose Comparatively light, until it touches cluster display or camera imagery

1.3 The dividing line is consequence, not performance

The same class of bug becomes a completely different kind of event depending on the layer it lands in. Navigation freezing is annoying; a braking control assumption going wrong hurts people. ISO 26262, the automotive functional safety standard, handles that difference explicitly. During hazard analysis and risk assessment it grades along three axes — severity, exposure and controllability — and the result is ASIL A through D plus a QM category where ordinary quality management is enough. D is the strict end.

One detail gets missed constantly. The level is not stamped onto a component; it attaches to a hazard. The safety goals that follow are then allocated across the whole system. That property is the root of the re-verification problem later in this article. Worth noting too: the second edition in 2018 widened the scope from passenger cars to road vehicles generally, excluding mopeds, and the series covers the full lifecycle from development through production, operation, service and decommissioning.

2. What the swap-it-out argument is really after

The modularity proposal is not sentimental. It presses on a real sore spot. It is after three things, and the fact that those three aim at different layers is what gets blurred most often.

2.1 Lifecycle mismatch

A body lasts ten to fifteen years. High-performance compute hardware turns over far faster. The Korean discussion this article starts from put the contrast at its centre: a body at 10 to 15 years against compute hardware at 3 to 6. Layer on the pace of model generations in AI, which move in months, and the gap widens further.

That discussion also offered an estimate that compute-related cost accounted for a figure in the range of two to three million won within a compact model's price increase. That number is the author's estimate, not a manufacturer's published cost breakdown. Cost structures are usually not disclosed, so this article treats it as directional evidence only and does not use the figure as a fact.

2.2 Adding features after the sale

  • Getting new features after purchase is no longer unfamiliar. The constraint is the compute ceiling. You can ship software, but the compute that software demands cannot exceed the silicon already installed.
  • So post-sale features split into two kinds: those that run inside existing hardware headroom, and those that require a hardware generation. The first already happens, the second does not.
  • The module replacement proposal is aimed at the second kind. Which means the reply that OTA already handles this is only half right.

2.3 Supply chain flexibility

  • If the compute module becomes a standardised slot, dependence on one semiconductor supplier drops. For anyone who has lived through a supply shock, this axis has real operational appeal.
  • The same platform becomes easier to place across vehicle classes and powertrains. The problem of justifying high-performance compute as you move down into cheaper segments eases here too.
  • On the other side, more suppliers means more interfaces and more responsibility boundaries to manage. Flexibility is not free, and its price shows up in the certification and liability items below.

3. The standard already exists - what AUTOSAR answered and what it left

The first rebuttal to any modularity proposal is that a standard for this already exists. It is half true, which is exactly why it needs splitting carefully.

The industry standard AUTOSAR comes in two branches. The Classic Platform targets deeply embedded electronic control units and uses a virtual functional bus so application software can be developed and deployed independently of hardware; this is where hard real-time demands live. The Adaptive Platform targets compute-heavy territory as a distributed computing and service-oriented architecture, following the concept that a system is a set of services which may call one another.

So decoupling software from hardware is not a new idea. It is the direction an industry standard has been travelling for years. What is left, then?

  • What the standard organised is software architecture and interfaces at development time. It is not a procedure for physically replacing compute hardware in a customer's hands after the sale.
  • Even with a standardised interface, there is no automatic guarantee that a new module honouring that interface inherits the existing safety argument. Logical compatibility and safety equivalence are separate questions.
  • Conforming to a standard is also not the same as conforming to regulation. Following a standard architecture does not exempt anything from type approval procedure.
  • So the rebuttal holds at the technical interface layer and does not yet answer at the post-sale replacement and recertification layer.

4. What already works - the current reach of OTA

Over-the-air updating is not experimental. If anything, regulation is already written on the assumption that it happens. UN Regulation R156 deals with software updates and their management system, treating software change across the vehicle lifetime, including over-the-air delivery, as something to be managed. It explicitly recognises OTA as a route for mitigating security risks identified after production.

Two things need separating here.

  • Institutionally open is not the same as free to send anything. A requirement to run a management system is, turned around, a requirement to judge and record which changes affect approval.
  • Sending software and changing compute hardware are entirely different acts. An OTA update cannot raise the ceiling of silicon that is already installed.

That second distinction is the axis of this whole article. The module replacement proposal targets exactly the territory OTA cannot reach: generational turnover of compute capability. And that territory is hard precisely because it demands physical access and re-verification together.

5. Change it and you have to prove it again

In functional safety what gets certified is not a part but an argument: the claim that this system reduces these hazards in these ways, bundled with its evidence. Replace a part and several premises of that argument move with it.

5.1 The premises that move

  • Timing assumptions. A different compute module means different worst-case response assumptions, and those assumptions are interlocked with the slack budgeted to other systems.
  • Failure modes. New hardware fails in new ways. Whether existing diagnostic coverage catches those new modes has to be looked at again.
  • Freedom from interference. When functions of different levels share a resource, you have to show the lower-level side cannot disturb the higher. Change the resource layout and that argument changes too.
  • The whole lifecycle. The standard covers production, operation, service and decommissioning as well as development. Post-sale replacement happens by definition in the operation and service phases, so the procedure has to be in the design from the start.

Which is why the desktop expansion model, where plugging something in means it gets recognised, does not carry over. Matching a slot specification is the front half of the problem. The back half is all proof cost.

5.2 The compromise that follows, and its weak point

Realistic compromises tend to converge on one shape: fix the safety-critical systems and the network backbone, and make only the high-compute layer riding on top replaceable. The Korean proposal cited above takes that form. The direction is reasonable on its face.

The weak point is that the boundary between those layers is not as clean as it looks. The moment an infotainment display also renders rear camera imagery and warning indicators, that display is no longer a part unrelated to safety. If a high-compute module handles perception for driver assistance, the case is even clearer. Drawing the boundary is not a line on a diagram but a decision about where functions live, and that decision runs straight into product appeal, because consolidating many functions onto one large screen is what sells.

6. What regulation already settled - R155 and R156

The practical baseline in vehicle cyber security was set by two UN regulations.

  • UN R155 — uniform provisions on cyber security and the cyber security management system. It requires defining organisational processes, responsibilities and governance to manage risks related to cyber threats to vehicles and to protect them from attack.
  • UN R156 — uniform provisions on software updates and the software update management system. It requires software updates to be managed so that regulatory compliance, cyber security and vehicle safety hold.
  • Categories in scope include passenger cars, goods vehicles and trailers, and application for new types began on 6 July 2022. Extension to existing types and to all vehicles is set separately by regulation and by jurisdiction.

One property here is decisive for plug-in structures. In both regulations the object of approval is the manufacturer's management system, not a product. Which means admitting an external supplier's module after the fact requires folding that supplier into the manufacturer's threat management and update management systems somehow. Opening up does not end with publishing an interface document; it becomes opening up governance alongside it.

7. Where responsibility blurs

In this debate liability jams before technology does. Saying modules are replaceable means the configuration of a single car changes over time, and then any incident starts with establishing whose configuration it was and when.

  • When the compute module maker, the software maker and the integrator who built the safety argument are all different, the location of the defect and the location of responsibility come apart.
  • Who performed the replacement adds another branch. Work done inside a manufacturer's service network differs in character from work done outside it.
  • What happens when a module past end of support keeps being used has to be settled in advance too. End of support is an event that always arrives; it is not an exception case.
  • Given that regulation pins the management system on the manufacturer, practice tends to start from a picture where the vehicle maker carries final responsibility.

From there the vehicle maker's arithmetic becomes clear. Opening up easily reads as a trade where responsibility stays and control shrinks. So the real line of resistance is not that it cannot be done technically but whether there is a reason to take that trade. Conversely, for opening up to hold, liability allocation has to be settled in contracts and in law together, and that is not an architecture decision. It is an industry agreement.

8. When software goes into something you keep for years

Everything so far was the maker's side. From the buyer's side this shift wears a different face. A car sits among the most expensive long-lived durable goods most people own. Put software deep inside something like that and the problems already familiar from consumer electronics replay on a far longer timeline.

8.1 Four problems

  • End of support. A car keeps driving after security updates stop. What remains is not a broken car but a car carrying a connected seam that is no longer maintained. With a phone you replace it; with a car that option costs far more.
  • Subscriptions. Gating features on already-installed hardware behind a monthly fee has drawn criticism in several markets. For a buyer the problem is less the price than the ambiguity about what was actually purchased.
  • Resale value. Features bound to an account, subscriptions that do not transfer, and a hardware generation nearing end of support all convert into money when the car changes hands.
  • Offline behaviour. What stops when the servers close? The wider the answer, the more tightly that car's lifespan is tied to the maker's service operation plans.

A replaceable module structure has genuine potential to improve the first and third of these. If there is a path to refresh the hardware generation, end of support slides later, and the used market gains room to price model year and performance separately. That path only opens when replacement cost is reasonable, though. If a swap module costs a large fraction of the car, nobody swaps anything.

8.2 What to ask when buying today

ItemWhat to askWhy it matters
Support period How many years of security updates are provided Once it falls short of real ownership, the character of connected features changes

These items are often absent from the contract. It is better to get them in writing during the sales conversation, and if you plan to keep the car a long time, the first and third weigh on total cost more than fuel economy does.

9. What this direction means in the Korean market

This debate carries particular weight in Korea because of industrial structure. In a system where vehicle makers and suppliers are tightly affiliated, publishing an interface is a decision to open the door to suppliers outside the group. It looks like a technical decision and functions as a procurement one.

  • For suppliers, opportunity and threat arrive together. An open specification lets them sell beyond one vehicle maker, and equally lets another supplier walk into their seat.
  • Defining the standard first is the advantageous move. But one company cannot do it alone; several vehicle makers have to accept the same specification. That is roughly where standards alliances in other industries have formed and fallen apart.
  • Korean owners do not keep cars briefly. End of support and resale transfer may be felt as early as elsewhere rather than later.
  • Software staffing becomes the bottleneck. Defining and maintaining module boundaries needs people who understand architecture and certification together, and that layer is thin in every country.

This section is an observation about industrial structure and not a basis for investment decisions. Nothing here comments on any company's outlook or share price, and it should not be read for that purpose. The advantages and disadvantages discussed are matters of procurement and engineering organisation.

10. What could not be confirmed

The scope of this article has clear edges. The items below were not settled here, and nothing in the body was written as if they had been.

  • The text of ISO 26262 clauses. It is a paid standard and the clause text was not read. What this article referenced is the standard's name, scope, ASIL level system and grading axes. Wording of detailed requirements was not covered.
  • Detailed provisions in the UN regulation originals. The UNECE document pages could not be reached, so the names, intent, vehicle categories and new-type application date rest on a national vehicle certification authority's published guidance. Clause-number detail was not covered.
  • Extension dates for existing types and all vehicles. Schedules split by regulation and jurisdiction and sources disagree on how to state them, so only the new-type date was kept.
  • How Korean certification maps to these UN regulations. This was not confirmed, and the article makes no claims about the Korean regime.
  • Any specific maker's actual OTA reach. Which company updates which systems remotely is not consistently confirmable from public material, so it was left out.
  • The basis for the cost estimate. The figure cited in section 2 is the discussion author's estimate and is not backed by published manufacturer cost data.

One more separation is worth making. The plug-in structure discussed here is at the proposal and debate stage. There is no evidence that customer-replaceable compute modules have become standard in production vehicles. By contrast, the two AUTOSAR platforms and the R155 and R156 framework are in actual application, and so is over-the-air updating. Read those two together and the whole discussion looks further along than it is.

Conclusion: the problem is not the slot

First, one sentence about modularising vehicle software becomes at least three projects in practice. Safety-critical control, comfort and infotainment, and connectivity and gateway sit under different rules and cost different amounts to change. Discussions that skip this split usually have people using the same word for different things.

Second, the blocker is proof cost and liability structure, not technical impossibility. Standardising interfaces is old work; the hard part is rebuilding the safety argument for a changed configuration, and folding external suppliers into the manufacturer's management system. The fact that regulation approves a management system rather than a product keeps working on this all the way through.

Third, the real beneficiary of this debate is whoever keeps the car longest. Lifecycle mismatch is an owner's asset problem before it is a manufacturer's cost problem. When does support end, what remains when the servers close, what follows the car when it is sold — those three questions are already live for anyone buying today, modular or not.

One last note. What will outlast this debate is not the name of any platform but the question of what inside a car should endure and what is allowed to change. Where that line falls explains most vehicle architecture decisions for the next decade. Slot specifications come after the line, not before.
