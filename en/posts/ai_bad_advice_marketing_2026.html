Another Set of Bad AI Advice - Plausible Generalities vs Reality 2026

Keeping Your Judgment in Front of a Confidently Wrong Machine

Jun 13, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: The Advice Is Smooth, but the Outcome Drifts

You ask, "Build a marketing strategy for my brand." In seconds, a tidy answer comes back. Define your target. Keep a consistent brand voice. Make data-driven decisions. Widen your touchpoints across channels. The sentences are clean, the logic is plausible, and above all nothing in it looks wrong. That is exactly the trap. If nothing is wrong, then nothing is specifically right for you either.

One of the recurring themes of 2026 is that the marketing and business advice generative AI produces can be surprisingly harmful. The danger is not when AI gives an obviously absurd answer - those are easy to catch. The real risk is the plausibly general answer: an average that fits everywhere and is decisive nowhere. This article works through why that average appears, how it turns into losses in marketing and business, and how human judgment can close the gap - keeping things balanced and fact-first.

The conclusion up front: AI is a powerful tool, but its great strength - rapidly synthesizing the average - is also its weakness. The average makes an excellent starting point and a dangerous destination. This is not an argument to abandon AI advice. It is a proposal to treat it as an object of verification, especially in fields like medicine, law, and finance where the cost of a mistake is high.

1. The First Reason It's Plausibly Wrong - Averaging

A large language model (LLM) is, at heart, a system that predicts the most plausible next word from an enormous pile of text. Here "plausible" is deeply tied to statistical frequency. What the model does best is to synthesize and smoothly recompose the most common pattern of answers that appear on the internet for a given question.

Ask for marketing advice, and the model synthesizes the most average recommendation from millions of marketing blogs, courses, and reports. So the answer almost always sounds reasonable - averages usually do. But real marketing is not won at the average. It is won on the peculiarities of a specific market, the unique strength of your product, the gap a competitor missed, the context that only your customers share - the non-average details. And that is precisely where the model is blindest.

AI is good at producing an answer that fits everyone. But in marketing, an answer that fits everyone is also an answer no one remembers.

Indeed, a shared worry in the 2026 marketing industry is the flood of "just good enough" content. Generative AI has dramatically lowered the barrier to producing content that is smooth enough to hold attention but not distinctive. Publishing frequency goes up while a brand's distinctiveness and memorability get diluted. Mass-produce the average, and the whole market converges to the average, with no one standing out within it.

2. The Second Reason - It Doesn't Know Your Context

The second gap is more fundamental. The AI does not know your specific situation. Whether your budget is five thousand dollars or five million, whether your customers are conservative buyers in their sixties or early adopters in their twenties, what regulations your industry faces - unless you tell it, the model answers blind to all of it.

So the model ends up answering for an "average company." The problem is that the average company does not exist in reality. Every actual business departs from the average in resources, constraints, risk tolerance, and customer mix. Advice stripped of context can only retreat into generality, and generality does not help at the decisive moment.

Ask about "channel strategy for a product launch," for instance, and the model usually says to "balance social, search, email, and influencers." Not wrong. But telling a cash-strapped solo founder to run four channels in balance at once is effectively a prescription to fail mediocrely on all of them. Knowing the constraint flips the answer entirely to "concentrate on one channel." Context reverses the direction of the advice.

3. The Third Reason - Confident Hallucination

The third gap is the most dangerous: hallucination, and confident hallucination at that. A hallucination is output in which the model fills a gap in its knowledge with a plausible falsehood instead of honestly admitting it does not know.

Why doesn't the model just say it doesn't know? According to OpenAI's analysis, standard training and evaluation procedures reward "guessing and getting it right" more than "admitting uncertainty" - much like an exam where filling in a guess beats leaving a blank. As a result, models are trained to fabricate a plausible answer rather than leave a blank, even in domains they do not know. The bigger problem is that the model does not clearly signal how confident it is: an answer it surely knows and one it fabricated come out in the same self-assured tone.

In a marketing and business context, this shows up concretely as:

  • Nonexistent statistics: citing a figure of murky provenance as fact, like "email marketing returns 42 dollars per dollar spent." The number sounds plausible but is often unverified.
  • Fake case studies: inventing unverifiable success stories such as "Brand A tripled revenue with this strategy."
  • Outdated tactics: because of the training data's cutoff, recommending tactics that have already lost effectiveness or become risky under current platform policy, as if they were current.

Fluency and accuracy are different dimensions. I explored that distinction more deeply in a separate piece, Ted Chiang - No, AI Is Not Conscious. The gist worth carrying over: a falsehood stated with confidence sounds more trustworthy than a truth said with hesitation. That is exactly what makes confident hallucination dangerous.

4. The Risk in Marketing - Convergence to the Average

Now let us see how these three gaps turn into real losses on the marketing front. The most common pattern is the convergence to the average noted above.

A brand hands AI its blog posts, ad copy, SEO keywords, and social posts. The output is fast and clean. But competitors feed similar prompts into the same tools and get back similar averages. After a few months, the whole industry's content grows eerily alike: the same tone, the same structure, the same "best practices." The brand's distinctive voice fades, and consumers remember no brand in particular.

One industry analysis summed it up by noting that the loudest criticism of AI-generated content is that it is too generic, too synthetic, too samey. More fundamentally, when content creation degenerates into a volume strategy, distinctiveness collapses; even as publishing frequency rises, a brand's authority and memorability erode over time.

Another risk lives on the customer-experience side. One market forecast predicts that in 2026 about a third of companies, pushed by cost-cutting pressure to deploy customer-facing generative AI prematurely, will instead erode brand and customer trust through frustrating self-service experiences. It is the classic loss that arises when AI is mistaken for a replacement rather than a tool.

5. The Risk in Business Decisions

Move beyond marketing into general business decisions, and the character of the risk shifts. Here the central problem is that average advice replaces the decision-maker's critical thinking.

Research points out that over-reliance on AI advice lowers cognitive engagement and degrades task performance. More troubling, the wrong choices of an advisee can affect far more people than the decision-maker alone. Decisions like business strategy, hiring criteria, and pricing policy ripple through the whole organization once they go wrong.

The concrete business risks line up like this:

  • Homogenized strategy: ask the same AI the same question your competitor does, and you get the same strategy. Building a differentiation strategy with an averaging tool is a contradiction in itself.
  • Missed risk signals: because AI gives the "on-average reasonable" answer, it fails to warn of risks specific to your business. Regulatory, contractual, and reputational risks often live outside the average.
  • Blurred accountability: "the AI analyzed it that way" muddies the responsibility of the person who actually decided. Accountability for a tool's output always rests with whoever adopted it.

6. Why People Over-Trust AI Advice

So why do we lean on AI advice so easily? This is not weakness of will but a natural tendency rooted in human cognition.

6.1 Automation Bias

Psychology has a concept called automation bias: the tendency to trust the output of an automated system more uncritically than one's own judgment. It has been observed repeatedly even in highly trained fields like aviation and medicine. The better a system performs on average, the lazier people grow about verification.

6.2 The Illusion of Authority

To this is added the illusion of authority. Because AI presents information in a confident, well-organized form, people grant its output undue credibility. A well-written sentence looks like expertise in itself. But the smoothness of the prose has nothing to do with the accuracy of the content.

6.3 The Cost of Convenience

The last factor is simple convenience. Getting an instant answer reduces the cognitive effort of finding and weighing sources yourself. The problem is that the effort you save is matched by a weakening of the critical-thinking muscle. One study suggests that whether AI harms critical thinking depends on when you use it. If you take the AI's answer before forming your own judgment, your thinking anchors to that answer.

7. Four Ways to Verify AI Advice

Here is the most practical part. You do not need to abandon AI advice. You need the habit of treating it as a draft, not a finished product. Four verification tools:

7.1 Check the Source

When AI offers a statistic, case, or citation, look up the source directly. University library guides commonly recommend this. Search for the source the AI named and see whether the document actually contains that content. If you cannot find it, treat it as a hallucination. AI is not built to cite truthfully, so it may name a document that does not in fact contain the claim.

7.2 Inject Context

The most powerful way to narrow an average answer is to state your context specifically. Spell out budget, market, customers, constraints, and risk tolerance, and the model moves off the average toward something closer to your situation. Good advice comes from good questions. A context-free question only returns a context-free answer.

7.3 Challenge It

Throw questions that shake the model's assertions. "When could this advice be wrong?" "What is the counter-case?" "Under what conditions does this strategy fail?" This is especially effective at breaking confident hallucination. Have it argue one position, then immediately argue the opposite, and you can gauge how robust its answer really is.

7.4 Cross-Check With Experts

The higher the impact or the harder to reverse, the more you should cross-check against independent sources or human experts. If the information is low-stakes and low-impact, a light check suffices; but for decisions with real-world consequences, an extra verification step is mandatory. "Lateral reading" - taking the AI output and comparing it against other trustworthy sites - is also useful.

Verification tool Core question Risk it blocks
Check the source Does this evidence actually exist? Hallucination, fake stats
Inject context Did I tell it my situation? Averaging, generality
Challenge it If this is wrong, why? Confident assertion
Cross-check Would a human reach the same conclusion? High-stakes misjudgment

8. Use It as a Tool, but Let Humans Judge

Compress all of this into one sentence: use AI as a tool, but let humans do the judging. What discussions of responsible AI consistently stress is not full automation but human-AI collaboration: keep human oversight at critical decision points, and use AI output only after it has been thoroughly verified.

There is an interesting paradox here. The person who carefully treats AI as an object of verification actually uses it better. Knowing a tool's limits lets you aim its strengths precisely. AI's real strengths lie in fast drafts, organizing patterns, expanding brainstorms, and converting formats. Aiming precisely at those, while keeping final judgment and responsibility with humans, is the safest and most productive arrangement.

One more thing. The difference between organizations that use AI well and badly is decided not by how much they use it but by how well they verify it. On the inflated expectations and organizational dysfunction surrounding AI, I went broader in the LLM collective delusion at our workplace. A culture of verification has to take root before the tool's adoption pays off.

9. Field-by-Field Caution - Medicine, Law, and Finance Especially

Finally, I want to stress that not all fields carry the same risk. Medicine, law, and finance are domains where wrong advice translates directly into harm to health, wealth, or legal rights. In search quality evaluation, this is called YMYL (Your Money or Your Life).

The risk of AI advice here is not merely abstract. In an MIT Media Lab study, participants could not effectively distinguish low-accuracy AI medical answers from doctors' answers, and showed a high tendency to follow that potentially harmful advice even when it was low-accuracy. Studies of automation bias in medical AI, meanwhile, warn that medical errors and operational failures compromise patient outcomes and impose substantial financial burdens on providers. In finance, a flawed AI model can lead to regulatory fines or market disruption; in law, wrong advice translates directly into the loss of rights.

In low-stakes domains, a wrong AI answer is inefficiency. In high-stakes domains, a wrong AI answer is harm. You must not treat the two with the same level of trust.

So the principle in these fields is clear. Use AI only as an information-organizing and drafting tool, and route final judgment through review by a qualified professional. Do not delegate diagnoses, legal decisions, or investment decisions to AI output. One medical-education study warned that over-reliance on AI risks eroding the critical thinking of current and future doctors while reinforcing existing bias. The convenience of a tool cannot replace the responsibility of expertise.

10. Conclusion - Average as a Start, Human as the Finish

Bad AI advice usually arrives not as a blatant lie but as a plausible generality. Averaging, missing context, and confident hallucination combine to produce an answer that fits everywhere and is decisive nowhere. In marketing it leads to the death of distinctiveness, in business to the delegation of judgment, and in high-stakes fields to real harm.

But this is not a call to keep AI at arm's length. It is a call to understand its strengths precisely and use it where they apply. The average AI synthesizes is an excellent starting point: superb for fast drafts, spotting missing angles, and tidying formats. The key is simply not to take that average as the destination - to keep the last step, verification and judgment and responsibility, in human hands.

Three things you can apply today:

  1. Take it as a draft, finish it yourself. Use the AI's answer as a start, but do not publish or decide on it as-is. A single pass of source-checking and context injection changes the quality.
  2. Make challenging a habit. Ask "if this is wrong, why?" every time. It is the simplest shield against being swayed by a confident tone.
  3. Verify in proportion to risk. Keep light things light, but route high-stakes decisions in medicine, law, and finance through a human expert's cross-review.
