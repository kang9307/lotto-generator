Why Unexpected Inflation Quietly Cuts Your Real Wage

The window where nominal pay rises and purchasing power falls, and what actually gets decided inside it

The number on this year's offer letter is bigger than last year's. The percentage was not out of line with what everyone else got. And yet the grocery run does not feel like it costs less of your income, and the card statement does not feel lighter. Something appears to be missing from the arithmetic, but there is no strange line item on the payslip.

This is not a misperception, and it is not a failure of negotiation. Pay is set once a year. Prices move every month. Purchasing power leaks out in the space between those two clocks. This article opens that structure in order: where pay actually gets decided, why employers do not simply match inflation, how the erosion shows up in official statistics, and how far an individual can realistically reach. Asset allocation and investment products are outside its scope.

Where nominal and real part ways

Two words need separating first. Nominal pay is the amount that lands in the account. Real pay is what that amount will actually buy. The arithmetic is simple: divide nominal pay by a price index and you have the real figure.

Official statistics work the same way. In Korea, the Ministry of Employment and Labor divides average monthly total pay from its establishment labor force survey by the consumer price index. Total pay has three parts, and the split matters later.

  • Regular pay - base salary, allowances, and paid-leave allowances
  • Overtime pay - premiums for extended, holiday and night work
  • Special pay - fixed bonuses plus variable, performance-linked bonuses

Here is the part that is easy to miss. Real pay does not need a pay cut in order to fall. If the raise is smaller than inflation, purchasing power shrinks while the nominal figure grows. A pay cut is visible and requires a signature. This produces no paperwork at all, which is exactly why it goes unnoticed.

One more distinction. Falling inflation is not falling prices. When the rate drops from 5 percent to 2 percent, the price level keeps climbing, just more slowly. A gap that opened once is only closed if pay outruns prices for several years afterwards.

Wage stickiness: pay that will not fall, raises set once a year

What economists call wage stickiness is really two things stacked together, and separating them makes the mechanism much clearer.

Downward rigidity: nominal pay rarely falls

Employers almost never respond to a bad quarter by cutting salaries 10 percent next month. Pay is locked into employment contracts and workplace rules. In Korea, changing workplace rules to workers' disadvantage requires the consent of a majority of employees under Article 94 of the Labor Standards Act, and a disadvantageous change made without that consent has no effect. There are reasons beyond law as well: cutting pay collapses morale, and the people with options leave first.

So far this is a property that protects workers. The problem is that it also runs in the other direction. Employers know a raise cannot be taken back. That makes a large raise in response to an inflation spike feel riskier than its actual cost, because if inflation subsides the higher payroll stays.

Adjustment frequency: the decision happens once a year

This is the real channel. Raise percentages are typically set by an annual negotiation or an annual internal decision. The consumer price index, meanwhile, is published monthly and moves monthly.

Expected inflation is not the problem. If both sides believe prices will rise 3 percent next year, that 3 percent goes onto the table in advance. What breaks the system is unexpected inflation. Once the raise is locked and prices then overshoot, the percentage already in motion does not change for the rest of the year. The window to recover the loss does not reopen until the next round, and that round rarely backfills what was lost.

One more layer sits on top. Employers generally apply a single headline raise to most of their workforce. They do not tune the percentage worker by worker to match individual price exposure, because managing the fairness question and explaining the differences is expensive. So one number, chosen once, sets the real-pay path for an entire organisation.

Following the erosion in numbers: an illustrative case

Words alone do not land, so here is the arithmetic. The salary figures below are invented for illustration. The inflation rates are real published Korean figures. Assume a starting salary of 3,000,000 won a month at the end of 2020 and a fixed 3 percent raise every year.

Three things are worth reading out of that table.

  • Five consecutive raises, and purchasing power is still below where it started. Nominal pay rose 15.9 percent while cumulative inflation over the same period was 16.6 percent
  • The lowest point is not the year inflation peaked, but the year after. Prices jumped 5.1 percent in 2022, yet the bottom lands in 2023, because inflation kept accumulating while the raise stayed at 3 percent
  • Recovery is slow. Even after inflation fell back into the 2 percent range, the gap narrows far more gently than it opened

Converted to money, multiplying each year's monthly shortfall by twelve and summing across the five years erases roughly 1,830,000 won in 2020 purchasing power. That amount appears nowhere on a payslip. The salary numbers are invented, and the point is not the figure but how a raise that trails inflation by a little compounds over time.

What the study actually showed

This mechanism was recently checked against large-scale payroll records. The University of Chicago Becker Friedman Institute released a working paper on 12 August 2026 titled Sticky Wage Norms and the Real Wage Cost of Unexpected Inflation, by Erik Hurst, Christina Patterson, Nela Richardson and Ye Liv Wang. It uses administrative payroll data from a large US payroll processor and covers 2021 through 2024.

The reported findings, in summary:

  • Firms apply a single modal annual raise to most of their workers, and those norms barely moved despite the inflation surprise
  • Among workers continuously employed at the same firm across 2021 to 2024, 43 percent saw a cumulative real wage decline, averaging 9 percent
  • Job changers saw nominal wage growth that tracked inflation nearly one for one. Even so, including them, the share of all workers with a real wage decline was 37 percent - the detour trimmed the figure by only 6 percentage points
  • Indexing raises to inflation would have closed roughly 40 percent of the gap, and losses were larger for older workers with lower mobility
  • The paper attributes the unusually long slump in consumer sentiment less to inflation itself than to the incompleteness of the indexation

That last point is the paper's most contested claim. Price increases alone do not explain why sentiment stayed depressed for so long, but adding the experience of pay failing to keep up gives the pattern a mechanism. This is a US result and cannot be transplanted wholesale into countries with different bargaining institutions. What follows shows that a similarly shaped episode appears in Korean statistics.

Why employers do not match inflation, and the job-change detour

It is not simply stinginess. Raise decisions have their own logic, and knowing it determines what is worth saying in a negotiation.

  • Raises cannot be undone. With no way to know whether an inflation spike is temporary, a large raise leaves permanently higher payroll after prices settle
  • Budgets are fixed in advance. Payroll sits inside an annual plan that usually locks at the end of the prior year
  • Fairness administration is expensive. Matching inflation for some employees leaves no story for the rest
  • Price pass-through lags. A firm that cannot raise its own prices immediately has no headroom to raise payroll first

So the choice employers actually make is often this: leave the raise norm for existing staff alone and lift pay only for new hires, at market rates. Adjusting individual offers is far quieter internally than rewriting the norm. The visible result is the new arrival who out-earns someone with several years of tenure.

That is exactly why changing jobs works as a detour. A move is the one moment pay gets repriced at market. And it is equally clear why it does not overturn the structure. Moves are infrequent, and from the following year the worker is inside the new employer's raise norm. Search and adjustment cost time and money, and where pay is built on tenure, moving means giving that up. Above all, the conditions that reduce mobility - age, location, caregiving obligations - are usually not chosen.

Recovery through mobility is therefore recovery available only to those who can move. The heaviest residual losses land on people with the least bargaining power and the least mobility, which is the same reason the paper finds larger losses among older workers.

Where pay gets decided: the Korean case

In Korea there are broadly three channels through which raises are set. All three run on an annual clock, and none of them index to prices automatically. The specific institutions are Korean, but the annual-clock pattern is what generalises.

Channel Coverage Cycle and timing How inflation enters
Collective wage bargaining (settlement rate) Workplaces with 100 or more regular employees Once a year, rate agreed in advance by both sides One input among several. Not a formula
Statutory minimum wage All workplaces, no sector differentiation Decided and published by 5 August each year under Article 8 of the Minimum Wage Act, effective the following 1 January under Article 10 One consideration in the commission's deliberation
Individual employer pay adjustment Most workplaces outside the above Typically once a year, per company rules Employer discretion

The minimum wage lag is worth looking at closely. The rate applying in 2026 was published on 5 August 2025. It is 10,320 won an hour, up 290 won or 2.9 percent from 2025, equivalent to 2,156,880 won a month on a 209-hour basis. The ministry noted it was the first figure agreed by both labour and management in 17 years.

Look at the structure. Price information available at the decision date has to cover a window running five to seventeen months later. If prices move unexpectedly inside that window, the published figure does not change. For anyone paid near the minimum, that lag is a lag in purchasing power.

What to read from it:

  • Real pay fell two years running, in 2022 and 2023. The 2022 decline was the first recorded since the survey expanded to cover all workplaces
  • In 2022 nominal pay rose a healthy 4.9 percent and real pay still went negative. Not because the raise was small, but because inflation was larger
  • 2023 is a different case. Inflation had fallen to 3.6 percent while total pay rose only 2.5 percent, against a 4.2 percent settlement rate. Total pay includes special pay, so when bonus payouts shrink, agreed rates and actual amounts pull apart
  • 2024 and 2025 are positive again. But a positive growth rate means the direction reversed, not that the lost level was recovered

Chaining the published growth rates into an index makes the difference visible. Setting 2020 at 100 gives 102.0 in 2021, 101.8 in 2022, 100.7 in 2023, 101.2 in 2024 and 102.1 in 2025. Across five years real pay rose about 2 percent, roughly 0.4 percent a year. Sample revisions splice the series in places, so treat this as an approximation.

Where the index and lived experience diverge

Even when the statistics report positive real pay, the feeling does not follow. The reasons sit in the nature of the indices themselves, and they apply well beyond Korea.

First, a consumer price index is the average household's basket, not yours. Korea's statistical agency also publishes a living cost index covering frequently purchased, high-share items. The two diverge regularly.

  • 2021 - consumer prices 2.5%, living costs 3.2%
  • 2022 - consumer prices 5.1%, living costs 6.0%
  • 2024 - consumer prices 2.3%, living costs 2.7%

Because real pay is computed by dividing by the consumer price index, a household whose spending concentrates in that faster-rising set loses more than the statistic shows. The index is not wrong; the average is simply not the individual.

Second, wage statistics are an average across all workers. If hiring in high-paying industries grows or low-paying jobs disappear in a given year, the average rises even when no individual's pay changed. So the headline real-pay change and your own real-pay change were never going to be the same number.

Third, people remember prices of specific items, not an index. If frequently purchased goods jump, the impression persists after the aggregate cools. Rather than saying statistics fail to capture reality, it is more accurate to say the index and the impression are measuring different things.

Automatic indexation as an alternative design, and its price

Some countries do link pay to prices automatically. Belgium is the standard example, and the paper above uses it as its comparison case.

The mechanism works like this. The reference is not the headline consumer price index but the health index, which strips out alcohol, tobacco and some fuels while retaining heating oil, gas and electricity. When a smoothed version of that index crosses a preset pivot, wages, pensions and social benefits rise automatically by 2 percent. In the private sector, sector-level collective agreements set the frequency and method, while the reference index stays common.

The advantage is unambiguous. When inflation surprises, the loss of purchasing power does not depend on individual bargaining power. People with low mobility receive the same protection as everyone else. That is precisely why the paper reaches for the comparison.

The costs belong in the same view, or the judgement is one-sided.

  • The price and wage feedback objection is the oldest one. Costs passed back into prices trigger the next round of indexation
  • Competitiveness disputes follow. If neighbouring labour costs are not indexed and yours are, the gap widens
  • Belgium has consequently amended the system by legislation several times. Legal practice sources report that a reform effective June 2026 introduced limits on indexation above a salary threshold

Korea has no comprehensive automatic indexation. Inflation enters minimum wage deliberations and collective bargaining as one consideration. This is a design choice rather than a ranking, and each choice carries its own bill.

What an individual can and cannot do

This is the section that has to stay honest. The share an individual can close through negotiation is limited. There is no method inside this structure for beating inflation.

Out of reach

  • The company-wide raise norm does not move for one employee. That is a budget and fairness question, not a matter of negotiating skill
  • Backfilling a year already lost is not available at most employers
  • Inflation itself is obviously not a controllable variable

Within reach

  • Frame the case in real terms. Rather than a number you want, place the published inflation rate and the published settlement rate on the table and ask where the current raise sits between them. Both are public, so both sides read the same screen
  • Learn the employer's decision date first. The pay window opens once a year, and a conversation started after it closes rolls into the next cycle
  • Check the composition of total pay. A large variable-bonus share swings year to year with company results, which is part of why 2023 total pay growth in Korea fell short of the agreed settlement rate
  • If you are paid near the statutory minimum, confirm that increases apply automatically. The Korean hourly minimum for 2026 is 10,320 won. Inclusion rules and weekly holiday allowance change the actual calculation, so for a specific workplace it is safer to confirm with a labour attorney or the competent labour office first
  • A job change is the largest lever, but not a repeatable one. The repricing effect is real, and so are search costs and the risk of a bad move

What I could not confirm

Items this article could not pin down:

  • The paper's sample size and detailed methodology. The working paper PDF would not open, so the sample size and the derivation of the average 9 percent decline could not be checked against the full text. Figures here rest on the institute's public abstract and summary of key findings
  • The 2025 collective settlement rate. The annual results table does not yet carry a 2025 value, so the cell is left blank
  • Splicing the 2025 wage levels. Published monthly averages do not compare directly with the prior year's release in places, so the table reports growth rates rather than amounts
  • The current share of workplaces on seniority-based pay scales. How much pay still rises with tenure bears directly on the job-change decision, but the underlying figure could not be verified
  • The text of Belgium's 2026 amendment. The threshold limit was confirmed only through legal practice summaries, not the official gazette

Conclusion: what changes once you see the structure

To summarise: real pay does not need a pay cut in order to fall. It only needs a small persistent gap between the raise and inflation. That gap opens because pay is set annually and because a raise, once given, cannot be withdrawn. And the route out of it, changing employers, is open only to people who can move.

Korean statistics show the same shape. Real pay fell in 2022 and again in 2023, and although growth turned positive in 2024 and 2025, that does not mean the lost level was recovered. Chained across five years, real pay rose about 0.4 percent a year.

What changes once you see this? Two things. First, the direction of self-blame shifts. A raise that trailed inflation is more likely a product of the cycle on which raises are decided than of poor negotiating. Second, you know what to say. Placing the published inflation rate and the published settlement rate next to your current raise is a far more tractable conversation than asking for a number. There is no guarantee it works. But separating what belongs to the individual from what belongs to the institution leaves you knowing where to look when it does not.
