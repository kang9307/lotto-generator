What a Model Has to Carry When It Plays Along With You

Continuing a piano performance with 125 million parameters - the symbolic choice, the size a latency budget allows, and the open question of training data

Aug 21, 2026 Tech & IT

Opening: after you lift your hands, what has to arrive and when

Say you play four bars and lift your hands. If you want the music to continue, when does that continuation have to arrive? A second later is too late. Half a second is too late. The window musicians grant each other in an ensemble is far shorter than that.

That single question separates music generation from every other kind. A text summary can take three seconds and an image ten, and the result is worth the same when it lands. A model that plays along is different. The value of the output is bound to its arrival time. A note that misses its moment is not a bad note; it is an unusable one.

In August 2026 a developer built a model that takes MIDI keyboard input and continues a piano performance, then published the process. The main model has 125 million parameters - a size the author himself calls GPT-2 for piano. The point of this article is not to introduce that product, but to look at the constraints a model takes on the moment it declares that it will play along, and at how those constraints go on to decide representation, size and evaluation in that order. I have not used the app, which is why there is no impression of the sound anywhere here.

What the model actually does

Here is what the published development notes establish.

  • Input and output are both MIDI events, not audio waveforms
  • A decoder-only transformer: RMSNorm, rotary positional embeddings, causal self-attention, SwiGLU-style MLP blocks. Three sizes of roughly 33M, 64M and 125M, with 125M as the main one
  • Pretrained on a few hundred thousand MIDI files amounting to about 300 million note events, then preference-tuned with DPO
  • Exported to Core ML with weights quantized to INT8. Throughput is about 108 notes per second on an iPhone 15
  • Context is trained up to 512 notes and rebuilt from the most recent 384 when needed
  • No published weights or training code were found — the author makes no statement either way

One premise the author states sets up most of what follows. The live input is not a clean MIDI file but a person playing, so notes land slightly early or late and velocities are uneven. The distribution of the training data and the distribution of real input are not the same.

Making symbols and making sound are different problems

Two jobs with entirely different characters hide inside the phrase music generation. Without separating them, everything downstream blurs.

AxisSymbolic (MIDI, score) generationAudio waveform generation

Size of one secondOn the order of tens of bytes176,400 bytes at CD-quality stereo
What it carriesPitch, timing, duration, velocity, pedalTimbre, room and noise as well

The size gap is arithmetic you can do by hand. Stereo at 44.1kHz and 16 bits is 176,400 bytes per second. Write one note as five categorical fields and, generously, that is 5 bytes; even at a brisk ten notes per second that is 50 bytes. Three orders of magnitude apart. This compares representations rather than compute, but it does show how differently dense the two problems are.

That is why the symbolic side can be built small. The model never has to learn timbre. What a piano sounds like is the job of the sampler at playback; the model only decides which note, when, and how hard. It is the same division of labour as a person reading a score and playing it.

The cost is equally clear. A symbolic model cannot produce timbre - not by choice, but because it is outside the representation. Pedal state does fit: the MAESTRO dataset records sustain, sostenuto and una corda pedal positions. And the result only becomes sound after one more stage. That is why asking whether it beats the audio tools that render a finished track means little. They are not in the same event.

The real-time constraint - a latency budget sets the size

Start with how quickly people react to misalignment. Two studies are worth citing, and both measured audio latency rather than model latency.

StudyConditionReported result
Schmid et al., Audio Mostly 2024 (37 participants, PEST method)Base latency 0msMean just noticeable difference 49ms
Base latency 64msMean just noticeable difference 27ms
Base latency 512msMean just noticeable difference 77ms
Washburn et al., Frontiers in Psychology 2021 (piano duet, networked performance)Transmission latency 10msTempo gradually accelerates
Transmission latency 20msTempo is maintained
Transmission latency 40msProgressive deceleration observed

Read these carefully. A just noticeable difference is the threshold for detecting a change in latency, not its presence. The threshold does not simply grow with the base either - the 64ms condition was the most sensitive of the three - and participants with more musical training caught smaller differences. Overlay the two studies and the direction is unmistakable. Performance behaviour already shifts in the tens of milliseconds.

So let us draw up the budget. Latency in a system that plays along is not one lump but four segments.

  • Input sensing - key scanning and MIDI transmission
  • Deciding - whether this is a moment to come in, or whether the player is still going
  • Generating - model compute up to the first note
  • Output - sampler rendering and the audio buffer

The model gets only part of this. The audio buffer alone already spends a non-trivial slice.

Here throughput and latency have to be separated. About 108 notes per second is throughput; inverted, that is roughly 9 milliseconds per note on average. This model divides a quarter note into 24 steps, so at 120 BPM one step is about 20.8 milliseconds and a sixteenth note is 125 milliseconds. Since making one note takes less time than a sixteenth note takes to pass, there is room to run ahead of the performance and fill in advance. But the author did not publish the delay to the first note. Having enough throughput and having the first note arrive on time are different claims.

That is how size gets decided. Multiply parameters by ten and per-note compute broadly follows, while the latency budget stays where it was. The ceiling for a model used mid-performance is set by the clock, not by quality. The usual server-side trick of enlarging the batch does not apply either. The next note is only knowable once the one before it is fixed, and human input does not arrive early.

There is one more constraint. The author writes that four-note prompts were the hardest, eight worked better, and the 16 to 32 note range was substantially more reliable. In real-time terms that ordering hurts, because the moment you need to come in is usually the moment with the least context. The model is strong once the performance is underway and weak at the start, while the place a person wants help is generally the opposite.

Why a few hundred million parameters is enough

Put it beside a language model and the reason sharpens.

  • The vocabulary is small. Pitch spans the 128 MIDI values, onset delta 0 to 48 steps, duration 1 to 96 steps, velocity from 4 to 124 in steps of 8. Each axis is tens to a bit over a hundred values, an order of magnitude away from a language model with tens or hundreds of thousands of tokens
  • The structure is regular. Meter is periodic and harmonic motion carries a strong statistical bias. Compared with the context dependence of natural language there is simply less uncertainty to predict
  • There is no world knowledge to store. A large share of a language model parameter budget goes to remembering facts, and continuing a piano line needs none of it
  • It emits one whole note per pass. Sampling attributes separately costs four or five passes per note. With a compound token bundling five fields, one pass advances the music by one complete note - which is a saving of latency budget

A small vocabulary is not free. The moment notes snap to a grid, whatever lived between the grid lines disappears. Rubato, the small stagger inside an arpeggio, the backbeat deliberately laid late. Dividing a quarter note into 24 steps is the classic way to hold both triplets and sixteenths in one grid, but a grid is a grid. Quantizing velocity in steps of 8 coarsens the staircase of expression.

What the author tried and abandoned is part of the same balance sheet. The traditional note-on and note-off representation drifted over time, and masking grammatically impossible tokens was accurate but slow. In real time, a slow correct answer is not a correct answer.

The data problem was selection, not scale

The most quotable passage in the development notes sits here. Scaling the dataset to roughly five times its size made the resulting models worse. Cleaning and selecting the data mattered more than adding more of it, the author writes.

The preprocessing list runs like this: keeping piano-centric material, handling parallel tracks, filtering on note density and range, fingerprint-based deduplication, and grouping the versions of a single piece. It reads less like collecting and more like throwing things out.

Why go that far becomes obvious once you think about where web-scraped MIDI comes from. One folder holds performances a person actually played, scores entered mechanically, automatic transcriptions of audio, and game arrangements. The same file extension covers timing of entirely different character. Mechanical entry has none of a human's wobble; automatic transcription carries both missing notes and misplaced ones. Pour them into one pot and the model learns two contradictory timing norms at once.

Two datasets make useful poles. MAESTRO gathers ten years of the International Piano-e-Competition on Yamaha Disklaviers, about 200 hours with audio and MIDI aligned to roughly 3 milliseconds. It carries key-strike velocities and pedal positions - homogeneous but small. Lakh MIDI is 176,581 files scraped from publicly available sources on the internet and de-duplicated by MD5 checksum. Large and mixed.

Choose scale and the noise comes with it; choose homogeneity and you lose volume. Aria, presented at ISMIR 2025, reported pretraining on roughly 60,000 hours of symbolic solo piano and then fine-tuning on a smaller high-quality subset. Big first, clean second - and that ordering is itself one answer to the dilemma.

There is no correct answer for a good performance

Automatic metrics count only what can be counted. This project used repeated pitch n-grams, pitch entropy, range, note density, rests and chord density. What those catch is outright breakdown: looping the same two bars forever, or staying trapped inside one octave. A result can also sit inside every normal range and be thoroughly dull. Metrics hold a floor; they do not raise a ceiling.

So people listen. Listening has problems of its own.

  • It is slow. Comparing one pair of 20-second clips takes 40 seconds. With hundreds of candidates that becomes days
  • It drifts with order. Whatever is heard first gains or loses an edge
  • It only inspects short spans. The Anticipatory Music Transformer also used 20-second clips in its human evaluation and reported that within that span the generated accompaniments showed musicality similar to that of human composers. Nothing guarantees that what holds for 20 seconds holds for five minutes
  • The answer depends on the question. Does it continue naturally and is it musically good are different questions, and one item collapses them together

The author routed around this bottleneck by handing judgement to a commercial LLM, and what came out of that says more about music evaluation than about tooling. Placing two candidates side by side and asking for a choice worked better than absolute scoring; the order was flipped and the question asked twice to reduce position bias; continuity and musical quality were scored separately. The familiar traps of human evaluation, met all over again.

The preference-tuning numbers the author reports are these.

DPO settingPreference rate over the base model
β = 0.0161.08%

The last setting, which trained only on pairs where both judgements agreed, came out best. Pairs where the judgement wavered were noise rather than signal. One condition attaches to these figures, though: the judge is a model, not a person, and how far its preferences overlap with human ones is not established in the published material. The author also notes the result is still far from perfect and occasionally loops.

Training data and copyright - what is settled and what is contested

Separate out the settled facts first.

  • The author used publicly available datasets and collections and focused mostly on older classical music in the public domain. Individual dataset names are not listed
  • No published weights or training code were found — the author states nothing either way
  • Lakh MIDI is distributed under CC-BY 4.0. Its own maintainer notes on the project page that the copyright meta-event in MIDI is used inconsistently, so attributing each file to a particular author is not feasible
  • MAESTRO carries CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, which adds non-commercial and share-alike conditions

This is where the contested part begins. Three layers overlap, and they are not the same kind of thing.

  • Copyright in the work itself - older classical repertoire is generally out of term
  • The status of a performance or transcription of it - which can turn on who made it and through what work
  • The license on the dataset collection - a distribution condition for the collection, not a certificate that rights in each item inside were cleared

Saying the pieces chosen were public domain does not cover all three layers. In a collection whose own maintainer says attribution is not feasible, there is no way to check which layer any individual file belongs to. Whether material carrying a non-commercial condition can feed the training of a commercial product also remains a matter of interpretation. None of this is unique to this project.

The output side sits the same way. The MIDI-GPT paper describes its model as avoiding consistent replication of training data while producing music stylistically similar to it. Avoiding replication and having no rights question about the output are different statements, and the distance between them is exactly where the argument is happening.

This section reports what published license documents and papers state; it is not a legal determination. Whether a particular use of data or output is lawful depends on jurisdiction, purpose and contract, so seek professional advice before relying on it.

What this is for a musician

There are places it reads as a tool and places to be wary. It does not resolve neatly to one side.

  • Accompaniment - handing the left hand or the rhythm part over while practising alone. It only works if it fits inside the latency budget
  • Opening ideas - play four bars and take back several possible continuations. The choosing stays human
  • Filling in - infilling with both sides fixed. Closest to real arranging work, and the problem the Anticipatory Music Transformer line addresses
  • A practice partner - something that repeats the same conditions as many times as you want

There are also places to be wary.

  • The more plausible the result, the less you ask why that note. Losing the hours spent pulling a progression apart by hand hurts beginners most
  • The distribution of the training data becomes the definition of unremarkable. Suggest statistically common motion often enough and taste drifts toward it
  • With the tool attached in real time, the practice of sitting with being stuck disappears - and being stuck is usually part of composing. On top of that, using output commercially while the copyright layers remain unsettled is a choice that carries risk today

Where the replacement argument holds and where it does not also splits. A large share of a musician's job is not choosing the next note but deciding how to place it, and a symbolic model addresses only the first. Conversely, in demand that needs volume and cares little about individual nuance - background music, say - the pressure is real, and it is already visible on the audio side rather than the symbolic one.

Compressed to a line: models like this make the supply of ideas cheap, and the cheaper ideas get, the more judgement is worth.

What being on device changes

For this subject the usual ordering of on-device benefits is rearranged.

  • The round trip disappears. Here that comes before privacy. One network hop alone can exceed the ranges in the table above
  • Performance data never leaves the device and no connection is needed. Practice rooms and stages were never places with good links
  • The cost is a ceiling on size, heat and battery, and first-launch delay. The author also notes that the first launch is annoyingly slow while Apple's runtime optimizes the model

The general case for on-device execution is covered separately in the piece on fitting a 27B-class model into a phone. There the protagonist is compression; here the clock comes before compression. Shrinking tens of gigabytes into a few is not the same problem as squeezing a hundred-million-parameter model inside a sixteenth note. The first is bottlenecked on memory, the second on a deadline.

What could not be verified

This article stays inside what the published notes and papers establish. These are the gaps.

  • There is no latency figure. The author published only throughput, 108 notes per second, and not the delay from input to first note. That is why nothing here claims the model responds within so many milliseconds
  • The conditions under which those 108 notes per second were measured are unknown - battery state, concurrent work, other settings
  • With weights and training code unreleased, third-party reproduction and verification are impossible. No figure for the effect of INT8 quantization on output quality was found either
  • The names and licenses of the individual datasets used are not specified. Lakh MIDI and MAESTRO appear here as poles for symbolic music data, not as confirmation that this model used them
  • There is no independent listening evaluation. What is published is the author's own listening plus LLM judgement. I have not used the app either, which is why no claim about responsiveness or satisfaction appears here
  • No source was found that compares symbolic and audio models on compute by a common measure. The byte arithmetic in this article compares representation density, not compute

Conclusion: generation with a deadline

The most portable thing in this project is not the model but the order of the constraints. Deciding to play along creates a deadline; the deadline sets a ceiling on size; the ceiling sets the representation; the representation sets the evaluation method and the data requirements.

So the case is not confined to music. Every model that interrupts human action in real time walks the same chain. Interpretation, input assistance, driver assistance, a voice reply that comes in mid-conversation. The question they share is not how clever it is but by when it must answer, and how much the answer is still worth past that moment.

The open questions are equally plain. The smallness of a symbolic model is paid for by giving up timbre; the better selected the data, the narrower its provenance; evaluation still cannot escape short clips. The three copyright layers remain unsettled. None of these is a problem that more parameters solve.
