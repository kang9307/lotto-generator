Gallstones: A Complete Patient Guide

Symptoms, Causes, Treatment & Prevention — Evidence-Based Overview (as of June 2026)

2026-07-01 Health & Wellness

Introduction: What Are Gallstones?

Gallstones (cholelithiasis) are hardened deposits that form inside the gallbladder — a small, pear-shaped organ tucked beneath the liver that stores bile, a digestive fluid produced by the liver. Gallstones affect roughly 10–15% of adults worldwide and are one of the most common digestive disorders. Their prevalence has risen steadily in East Asian populations as diets have shifted toward higher fat and refined-carbohydrate intake.

Despite how common they are, about 70–75% of people with gallstones never develop noticeable symptoms. For those who do, the experience can range from mild indigestion to excruciating abdominal attacks, and — if complications arise — to life-threatening infections. This guide, updated as of June 2026, covers the full picture: how gallstones form, who is at risk, what symptoms to watch for, when to seek emergency care, how they are diagnosed, and what treatment and prevention options are available.

1. How Gallstones Form — Bile Chemistry Imbalance

Bile is a complex fluid containing cholesterol, bilirubin, bile salts, and lecithin held in delicate balance. When that balance is disrupted, certain components crystallize and gradually solidify into stones. Two main types account for nearly all cases.

1.1 Cholesterol Gallstones (about 80% of cases)

When bile contains more cholesterol than bile salts can dissolve, the excess cholesterol forms microcrystals that fuse into stones over time. Obesity, high-fat and high-refined-carbohydrate diets, rapid weight loss, elevated estrogen (from pregnancy or oral contraceptives), and reduced gallbladder emptying all tilt the balance toward cholesterol supersaturation.

1.2 Pigment (Bilirubin) Stones

Excess bilirubin from conditions such as hemolytic anemia, liver cirrhosis, or biliary infections causes calcium bilirubinate crystals to precipitate. Pigment stones are more prevalent in East Asia and in patients with underlying liver disease or parasitic biliary infections.

1.3 Impaired Gallbladder Motility

If the gallbladder does not empty efficiently, bile stagnates and becomes highly concentrated, creating an ideal environment for crystal formation. Prolonged fasting, total parenteral nutrition, pregnancy, diabetes, spinal cord injury, and certain medications can all impair gallbladder contraction and increase risk.

2. Risk Factors — The 4Fs and Beyond

Medical tradition summarizes the classic gallstone risk factors as the 4Fs: Female, Fat, Forty, and Fertile. However, several additional factors matter equally in clinical practice.

3. Symptoms — From Silent Stones to Biliary Colic

3.1 Asymptomatic Gallstones

The majority of gallstones produce no symptoms. Studies consistently show that 70–75% of carriers remain symptom-free over long follow-up periods, with only 10–25% developing symptomatic disease within five years. Most silent gallstones are found incidentally on abdominal ultrasound performed for another reason.

3.2 Biliary Colic

The defining symptom of gallstone disease is biliary colic. A stone temporarily lodges in the cystic duct, the gallbladder contracts forcefully against the obstruction, and severe pain ensues. The classic pattern: pain begins 30 minutes to 2 hours after a fatty meal, builds to a steady (not truly "colicky") peak in the right upper quadrant or epigastrium, persists for 30 minutes to several hours, and then gradually subsides as the stone dislodges. Typical associated features include:

  • Right upper quadrant or epigastric pain radiating to the right shoulder or mid-back
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Abdominal bloating and indigestion
  • Sweating during attacks

3.3 Chronic Digestive Complaints

Some patients report chronic dyspepsia, fatty-food intolerance, belching, and right-sided flank discomfort separate from acute attacks. These symptoms are non-specific and can overlap with many other digestive conditions, so imaging confirmation is essential before attributing them to gallstones.

4. Complications — What Happens If Gallstones Are Ignored

4.1 Acute Cholecystitis

When a stone persistently obstructs the cystic duct, bile stasis and bacterial overgrowth trigger acute gallbladder inflammation. Typical presentation: fever (38–39°C), chills, right upper quadrant tenderness, and pain lasting more than 6 hours — distinguishing it from simple biliary colic. Without timely treatment (antibiotics and cholecystectomy), the gallbladder can progress to gangrene, empyema, or perforation. According to NIH StatPearls, possible complications of acute cholecystitis include perforations, pericholecystic abscess, and biliary peritonitis.

4.2 Choledocholithiasis and Cholangitis

When a stone migrates into the common bile duct (CBD), it causes obstructive jaundice. If bacteria seed the obstructed duct, acute cholangitis develops, presenting with Charcot's triad: fever, jaundice, and right upper quadrant pain. Cholangitis can rapidly progress to septic shock (Reynolds' pentad) and is a true medical emergency requiring urgent biliary drainage and antibiotics.

4.3 Gallstone Pancreatitis

A stone impacting the ampulla of Vater causes reflux of bile into the pancreatic duct, triggering acute pancreatitis. Patients experience severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, with markedly elevated serum amylase and lipase. Gallstone pancreatitis ranges from mild to life-threatening and requires hospitalization with supportive care and, ultimately, cholecystectomy to prevent recurrence.

Emergency Warning Signs — Go to the ER Immediately
  • Severe abdominal pain lasting longer than 6 hours
  • High fever (above 38.5°C / 101.3°F) with rigors
  • Jaundice — yellow skin or yellow eyes
  • Dark tea-colored urine with pale, clay-colored stools
  • Sudden severe worsening of upper abdominal pain with vomiting

5. Diagnosis — Ultrasound First

Abdominal ultrasound is the primary and most accurate diagnostic tool for gallstones, with a sensitivity exceeding 95%. Classic findings include an echogenic focus with posterior acoustic shadowing. Blood tests (liver enzymes, bilirubin, amylase, lipase) help assess for complications. When CBD stones or pancreatic involvement are suspected, MRCP (magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography) provides detailed imaging without radiation. CT scan is useful for detecting complications; endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) excels at identifying small stones and gallbladder polyps.

6. Treatment — Surgery, Medication, and Monitoring

6.1 Watchful Waiting for Silent Stones

Because the annual risk of symptom onset is low (about 2–3% per year) and cholecystectomy carries its own operative risks, current guidelines recommend expectant management for asymptomatic gallstones. Exceptions — where prophylactic surgery may be preferred — include gallstones larger than 3 cm, concurrent gallbladder polyps, calcified ("porcelain") gallbladder wall, immunocompromised patients, and patients about to travel to medically remote regions.

6.2 Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy (Gold Standard)

For symptomatic gallstones, laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the definitive treatment. Three or four small incisions allow keyhole instruments to remove the entire gallbladder. Most patients are discharged within 1–2 days and return to normal activities within 1–2 weeks. Bile continues to flow from the liver directly into the small intestine — digestion is not significantly impaired. According to the Tokyo Guidelines and WSES guidelines, early laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the treatment of choice for acute cholecystitis.

6.3 Ursodeoxycholic Acid (UDCA) Therapy

UDCA (ursodiol) reduces cholesterol saturation of bile and can dissolve small cholesterol stones over 6–24 months. It is an option for patients unfit for surgery or those who decline it, with approximately 50% response rates. UDCA is also prescribed prophylactically during rapid weight loss phases after bariatric surgery to prevent new stone formation. Stones tend to recur after stopping medication, so surgery remains the more durable solution for symptomatic disease.

7. Dietary Prevention — What to Eat and Avoid

7.1 Foods to Reduce

  • Saturated and trans fats: fatty meats, butter, cream, fried foods, fast food
  • High-cholesterol foods in excess: organ meats, squid, shrimp
  • Refined carbohydrates and added sugar: white bread, sugary drinks, pastries
  • Alcohol: disrupts bile metabolism and increases liver cholesterol output

7.2 Foods to Increase

  • Dietary fiber: whole grains, oats, broccoli, carrots, apples, berries — enhances bile acid recycling and reduces cholesterol absorption
  • Omega-3 fatty acids: salmon, mackerel, sardines, walnuts — anti-inflammatory and may reduce gallstone formation
  • Plant proteins: tofu, legumes — healthy substitutes for animal fats
  • Water: at least 1.5–2 liters daily — prevents bile concentration

7.3 Lifestyle Principles

  • Eat regular meals — prolonged fasting concentrates bile and promotes stone formation
  • Lose weight gradually — no more than 0.5–1 kg per week; consult a doctor about UDCA if rapid loss is needed
  • Avoid large high-fat meals in one sitting, which trigger gallbladder over-contraction
  • Exercise regularly — moderate aerobic activity (150 min/week) improves gallbladder motility and supports weight management

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Can the gallbladder grow back after removal?

No. Once removed, the gallbladder does not regenerate. However, bile continues to reach the small intestine via the bile duct, and the vast majority of patients eat a normal diet after recovery from surgery. A small percentage may experience loose stools temporarily, as bile now drips continuously rather than being released in controlled bursts.

Can gallstones dissolve on their own?

Occasionally, small stones pass spontaneously through the bile duct — but this passage itself can trigger biliary colic or, in worse cases, duct obstruction. Folk remedies such as olive oil and lemon juice "liver flushes" have no proven medical evidence and may precipitate acute attacks. Medical dissolution with UDCA is the only pharmacologically validated approach, and it only works for small cholesterol stones in a functioning gallbladder.

Does everyone with gallstones need surgery?

No. Asymptomatic gallstones generally warrant watchful waiting, not surgery. The decision to operate is made when symptoms are recurrent or severe, when complications have occurred, or when specific high-risk features are present (large stones, polyps, immunosuppression). Discuss your individual situation with a gastroenterologist or general surgeon.
