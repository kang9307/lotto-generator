Renault's Rare-Earth-Free Electric Motor - Why It Dropped Permanent Magnets

Replacing the Magnet With Electricity - How EESM Motors Work and Why They Matter

Jun 14, 2026 Tech

Introduction: The "China Variable" Inside an EV Motor

When we talk about electric cars, our attention almost always goes to the battery. Yet the part that actually turns the wheels - the electric motor - hides a supply-chain pressure point of its own: the rare-earth permanent magnet. The vast majority of EV traction motors sold today are permanent magnet synchronous motors (PMSM), with neodymium magnets embedded in the rotor. The raw materials and processing for those magnets are concentrated, to a remarkable degree, in a single country.

One automaker has refused that structure head-on: Renault. Renault leaves the permanent magnet out entirely and instead winds coils onto the rotor, creating the magnetic field with electricity (an Externally Excited Synchronous Motor, or EESM). That choice, in production since the 2012 ZOE, suddenly looks prescient in 2025-2026, just as the geopolitical, environmental and price risks of rare-earth dependence all came into focus at once. This article unpacks why Renault dropped the magnet, how the alternative works, what it gains and gives up, and what the choice means for the EV industry - through a technical, environmental and industrial lens.

The one-line version: Renault's motor is not a "weak motor that lacks magnets." It is a motor that replaces the magnet with electricity, trading a sliver of peak efficiency to remove the rare-earth pressure point entirely.

1. Why an Electric Motor Needs a Magnetic Field

Every rotating electric motor works on the same principle: two magnetic fields push and pull against each other to produce torque. One field comes from the stationary stator on the outside; the other from the rotor spinning inside. The stator field is usually made by running alternating current through coils. The interesting question is how you make the rotor's field - and that is exactly where motor types diverge.

  • Make it with a magnet - Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motor (PMSM): strong permanent magnets in the rotor carry a field at all times, with no extra power needed.
  • Make it with electricity - Externally Excited Synchronous Motor (EESM, wound rotor): coils are wound onto the rotor and fed current to act as electromagnets.
  • Make it by induction - Induction motor: no electricity is fed directly to the rotor; the stator field "induces" current in it.

Over the past decade the PMSM became the mainstream choice for EVs. Because the rotor always carries a field, there is almost no extra power loss to maintain it, so the motor is small, light and efficient. Estimates put PMSM at roughly 80-85% of EV traction motors. And the source of that strong magnet is the rare-earth magnet.

2. What Exactly Is a Rare-Earth Permanent Magnet?

The magnet inside an EV motor is typically a neodymium magnet (NdFeB, neodymium-iron-boron). Small amounts of heavy rare earths such as dysprosium or terbium are sometimes added to improve high-temperature performance. Neodymium and praseodymium (NdPr) are the core elements.

Rare-earth magnets are ideal for motors for two reasons:

  • High field density: they pack a much stronger field into the same volume, so the motor can be smaller and lighter - raising power density (output per kilogram).
  • Heat tolerance: a motor gets hot as it runs, and a properly formulated rare-earth magnet keeps its magnetism even at high temperature.

A single EV motor is reported to contain roughly 1-2 kg of neodymium and praseodymium. That may not sound like much, but multiply it by global EV sales and the demand becomes enormous. The trouble starts when that demand is tied to a supply concentrated in one place.

3. The Rare-Earth Problem - Common Yet Not Common

Despite the name "rare earth," these elements are fairly common in the crust. What is genuinely scarce is not the ore but the capacity to mine and refine it into usable form. Rare earths are chemically very similar, making separation difficult, and the process generates environmentally heavy waste. For cost and regulatory reasons many countries exited the refining business, and China filled the gap.

The result is a supply chain tilted heavily in one direction (estimates):

Stage China's share (est.)
Rare-earth mining~60%
Refining / processing~85-90%
High-performance magnet manufacturingover 90% (NdFeB magnets ~96%)

That concentration radiates into three risks:

  • Geopolitical risk: when refining and magnet making sit in one country, export controls become a pressure point on the entire industry. In 2025, tighter restrictions on rare earths and magnets were used as leverage in trade negotiations.
  • Price risk: a concentrated supply means volatile prices. In 2025, neodymium-praseodymium oxide prices were reported to spike nearly 40% in a single month.
  • Environmental risk: mining and refining generate hazardous waste, including radioactive residue. There is an irony in a "clean" EV carrying an environmentally heavy material in its motor.

As all three risks surfaced together in 2025-2026, the "motor that uses no magnet at all" was re-evaluated - not as a green slogan but as a supply-chain security strategy.

4. Renault's Answer - How the EESM Works

Renault's path is clear: hand the job of making the rotor field to electricity instead of a magnet. The design is called an Externally Excited Synchronous Motor (EESM) or Wound-Rotor Synchronous Motor (WRSM). "Excitation" is the electrical-engineering term for generating a magnetic field.

Step by step:

  1. Instead of embedding permanent magnets, the rotor is densely wound with coils.
  2. Direct current is fed into those rotor coils.
  3. The energized coils become electromagnets, producing a field similar to a permanent magnet's.
  4. That field synchronizes with the stator's rotating field to create torque.

The key is that the field strength can be freely controlled with current. A permanent magnet's strength is fixed once installed; an excited field can be raised, lowered, or switched off entirely by adjusting the current. Renault has been a pioneer of this design since the 2012 ZOE and is developing a next-generation motor, the E7A, with Valeo. According to Renault, the E7A targets around 200 kW (about 270 hp) and 400 Nm, is roughly 30% smaller than the previous generation, and raises system voltage to 800 V to cut charging time (mass production is reported around 2027).

How Do You Get Electricity Onto a Spinning Rotor?

This raises one technical headache. The rotor spins - so how do you supply current to coils on it? Traditionally this used slip rings and brushes, contact parts that transfer power. But brushes wear and need maintenance. So recent designs transfer power to the rotor contactlessly through the shaft. ZF, for example, introduced "I2SM," which transmits the field energy inductively within the rotor shaft. This contactless excitation is a key advance that lifts the durability and efficiency of wound-rotor motors.

5. Wound Rotor vs Permanent Magnet - A Precise Comparison

So what do you gain and lose by swapping the magnet for electricity? This is not a matter of better or worse but a trade-off. The essentials in one table:

Aspect Permanent Magnet (PMSM) Externally Excited (EESM, wound rotor)
Rotor fieldPermanent magnet (always on)Coils + current (electromagnet)
Rare earthsRequired (neodymium magnet)Not required
Peak efficiency / power densityHigh (field is "free")Lower (rotor current losses)
Size / weightSmall and lightSomewhat larger and heavier
High-speed / field weakeningMust counter the magnet field (extra loss)Just reduce or switch off the field
Efficiency over wide speed rangeOptimized for a bandEven across a wide range
Structural complexitySimpleComplex (rotor power feed)
Supply-chain riskHigh (rare-earth dependent)Low (copper and iron)

Where the Permanent Magnet Wins

A permanent magnet provides the rotor field "for free." Because no power is spent maintaining the field, rotor-side losses (mainly copper losses) are minimal, giving higher peak efficiency and power density. For the same output, the motor can be smaller and lighter. That is why the PMSM came to dominate.

Where the Wound Rotor Wins

The wound rotor's strengths are control flexibility and high-speed efficiency. The faster a motor spins, the more a permanent magnet motor must inject "field-weakening" current to suppress the back-EMF the magnet generates - and that costs losses. A wound rotor simply reduces its rotor current, so it is more flexible and efficient at high speed, holding efficiency evenly from low-speed city driving to highway cruising. With no magnet, there is also no worry about demagnetization or magnet damage under hot, high-speed conditions.

Crucially, wound-rotor efficiency is by no means poor. Production examples from Renault and BMW show a well-designed wound-rotor motor reaching around 95% efficiency with ample power density. Compare only the very peak under identical conditions, though, and the permanent magnet still tends to hold a slight edge.

6. Why Renault's Choice Is Getting Attention Now

Renault did not adopt the wound rotor yesterday. It has done so since the ZOE, more than a decade ago. So why the renewed interest now? Because the outside environment changed.

As EVs moved from niche to mainstream, demand for rare-earth magnets surged - and at the same time, the concentration of their supply was laid bare by the 2025-2026 export controls and price spikes. In that environment, a "motor that never needed a magnet in the first place" becomes more than a cost saving or a green talking point: it is a risk hedge for the whole industry. That is why Renault's long-standing choice suddenly looks like a strategic asset.

The pattern echoes the energy supply-chain story we covered elsewhere. Just as the contest moved to infrastructure like storage and grids once generation costs converged - as seen in the milestone where wind and solar first beat gas - EV motors are moving past the "0.1% efficiency" fight toward "where and how do you secure the raw materials."

7. What Other Automakers Are Doing

Renault is not walking this road alone. Through 2025-2026, major automakers and suppliers have moved in unison to cut rare-earth dependence.

  • BMW: already mass-produces an electrically excited motor in its fifth-generation eDrive. The iX3 motor is reported to reach roughly 4-5 kW/kg and ~95% efficiency, rivaling permanent magnet motors - a flagship example of high-performance, high-volume wound-rotor production.
  • ZF: developed "I2SM," transferring field power inductively within the rotor shaft, easing the slip-ring/brush weakness without any magnet.
  • Tesla: currently NdFeB-based, but has announced its next-generation drive unit will eliminate rare earths (still moving toward volume).
  • GM: co-developing rare-earth-free magnets (such as iron-nitride based) with the startup Niron. Unlike Renault's "remove the magnet" approach, this is the other branch: "make a new magnet without rare earths."

So the industry's de-rare-earth strategy splits into two branches: one, like Renault and BMW, replaces the magnet with electricity (wound rotor); the other, like GM and Niron, builds a new magnet without rare earths. The two approaches compete and advance together.

Reality check: even so, an estimated 95% of EV traction motors sold in 2025 still used rare-earth magnets. The transition has begun, but it is still a transition in motion rather than a revolution.

8. Industrial and Supply-Chain Implications

Put Renault's choice and the industry trend together, and the implications for the EV industry are clear.

  • A first step toward diversification: copper and iron have broader, easier supply than rare earths. Replacing the magnet with coils shifts the critical input from "hard to get" to "easy to get."
  • Weighing efficiency against security: the wound rotor loses a little peak efficiency. Automakers must choose between "a few percent of efficiency" and "supply-chain stability" - and the higher the geopolitical risk, the more the latter is worth.
  • Design and control as differentiators: the wound rotor needs more sophisticated engineering - rotor power feed, field-weakening control. That is both a barrier to entry and an edge for those who do it well.
  • "Fully rare-earth-free" is hard: even with a magnet-free motor, other parts (sensors, speakers, some electronics) and wider industries like wind turbines still use rare-earth magnets. De-rare-earthing the motor is one piece of a much bigger picture.

In the end, the axis of EV-motor competition is shifting subtly - from "who builds the most efficient magnet motor" to "who manages raw-material risk best while still delivering enough performance."

9. Cost, Recycling, and a Realistic Timeline

It helps to separate three numbers that often get blurred together: the cost of the magnet, the cost of the motor, and the cost of the risk. The rare-earth magnet is a small fraction of an EV's bill of materials, so swapping it out rarely makes the car dramatically cheaper on a calm day. The economic case for the wound rotor is not really about shaving a few dollars off a magnet - it is about removing exposure to a supply that can spike 40% in a month or be restricted by export policy overnight. In other words, the wound rotor buys price stability and optionality, which is a different kind of value than a lower sticker price.

There is also a parallel track that is easy to overlook: recycling. Even automakers committed to permanent magnets are investing in recovering neodymium and dysprosium from end-of-life motors, factory scrap, and even hard-disk drives. A mature magnet-recycling loop would soften the supply problem without forcing a switch in motor architecture. So the realistic picture is not "wound rotor wins, magnets lose," but a portfolio: some models go magnet-free, some keep magnets but diversify and recycle their sources, and some adopt rare-earth-free magnets like iron-nitride as they mature. Each lever attacks the same underlying risk from a different angle.

On timing, it is worth being sober. Building independent refining and magnet capacity outside the dominant supplier is widely estimated to take many years and large capital - figures of a decade or more, and hundreds of millions of dollars per facility, are commonly cited. That long lead time is exactly why a design that sidesteps the bottleneck entirely, the way Renault's does, carries strategic weight today rather than at the end of some distant transition. The motor you can build now without a constrained input is worth more than the perfect magnet you cannot yet source freely.

10. Reading It From a Korean Vantage Point

This trend is directly relevant to Korean industry.

  • Automaker and supplier strategy: Korean automakers and suppliers should seriously evaluate motor options that cut rare-earth dependence. If giving up permanent-magnet efficiency is hard, they at least need a parallel strategy of supply diversification and rare-earth recycling.
  • Materials and magnets: rare-earth-free magnets and the precision winding/excitation tech for wound rotors are fresh materials-and-components opportunities. R&D that reduces magnet-import dependence is both security and industrial upside.
  • Power electronics and drive control: with the wound rotor, the sophistication of the inverter and control algorithms governs performance. Strengthening power semiconductors and control software pays off in the wound-rotor era.

11. Conclusion + 3 Things to Remember

Renault dropping the permanent magnet is not a performance-sacrificing retreat but a strategic move that removes a supply-chain pressure point wholesale. The EESM design - shifting field generation from "magnet" to "electricity" - gives up a little peak efficiency in exchange for freedom from the geopolitical, environmental and price risks of rare-earth dependence. That is why Renault's old choice suddenly looks prescient amid the supply-chain upheaval of 2025-2026.

Three things to remember:

  1. Electricity instead of a magnet. Renault's motor winds coils on the rotor and makes the field with current, using no rare-earth magnet at all. It is not a "weak motor" but a "different kind of motor."
  2. A trade-off, not a ranking. The permanent magnet leads in peak efficiency and power density; the wound rotor leads in control flexibility, high-speed efficiency and supply-chain stability. Done well, the wound rotor reaches about 95% efficiency.
  3. The battleground has moved. Motor competition has widened from a "decimal-point efficiency" fight to "where do you secure the materials." With BMW, ZF, Tesla and GM all heading de-rare-earth in their own ways, Renault's choice is a milestone on that road.
