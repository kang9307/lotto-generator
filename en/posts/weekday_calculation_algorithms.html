Why Finding the Day of the Week Needs a Formula

Where 7 and 365 refuse to line up, what Zeller and Sakamoto compute in their place

Aug 28, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: How Does a Computer Know Today Is Friday

28 August 2026 is a Friday. What has to happen for a computer to produce that sentence? It does not leaf through a calendar. There is no table of weekdays sitting somewhere to be looked up. It calculates. And the calculation is messier than you would expect.

It is messy because the calendar is regular and irregular at the same time. The weekday comes back around exactly every 7 days. A year, meanwhile, is either 365 or 366 days, and which one it is gets decided by a three-tier condition of 4, 100 and 400 years. Month lengths are a jumble of 28, 29, 30 and 31 with no arithmetic reason behind the ordering. A perfectly regular cycle has to be laid on top of an irregular system that people patched by hand over a thousand years.

In August 2026 GeekNews carried a piece on making day-of-week calculation faster. It was published by Ben Joffe on the 17th of the same month, and it proposes cutting the final step — turning a day count measured from an epoch into a weekday — down to one multiplication and a shift. The idea rests on 7 being a Mersenne number, one less than a power of two, which lets a remainder modulo 7 be moved into a remainder modulo 8.

What is worth looking at here is not the trick itself but the ground it stands on. Why was a formula needed at all, what did the formulas precompute, and why does anyone go after the last remaining division? At the end comes one honest question. Does this actually make anybody program faster?

1. Seven Does Not Divide 365

Every part of the problem starts from a single division. Divide 365 by 7 and the quotient is 52 with a remainder of 1. So after a common year the same date slips one weekday later. In a leap year it is 366, the remainder becomes 2, and the slip is two days.

If that slip were the same value every year it would not be a problem. One multiplication would settle it. The problem is that slips of 1 and slips of 2 are mixed together in a regular way, and what decides the mixing is the leap year rule.

1.1 The Three-Tier Leap Year Rule — 4, 100, 400

The Gregorian leap year test is three conditions stacked on one another.

  • If the year divides by 4 it is a leap year
  • Except that if it divides by 100 it is a common year
  • Except that if it divides by 400 it is a leap year again

That is why 1900 was a common year and 2000 was a leap year. Put into code it takes three divisions, or three remainder operations. As we will see later, those three divisions account for a large share of the cost of a weekday calculation.

1.2 How the Gregorian Calendar Ended Up This Shape

The rule was not chosen arbitrarily. The real length of one turn of the seasons is a little longer than 365 days. The Julian calendar, which adds a day every four years, valued that little bit at a quarter of a day, which was slightly generous compared with reality. That tiny excess accumulated over centuries, the equinox drifted earlier on the calendar, and the basis for Easter shifted with it.

The reform of 1582 did two things. It cut ten days out of the calendar to clear away the accumulated error, and it added the 100 and 400 rules so the error would not build again. As a result, across 400 years there are not 100 leap years but 97, and the total number of days in 400 years is 146,097.

Here something elegant happens. Divide 146,097 by 7 and you get exactly 20,871. In other words the Gregorian arrangement of weekdays repeats perfectly every 400 years. The weekday layout of 2026 is the same as that of 2426, and the same as that of 1626. This property is the floor that every formula below leans on.

2. The Simplest Method — Counting From an Epoch

Before building a formula there is the method anyone thinks of first. Pick one reference day, count how many days have passed since then, and look at the remainder modulo 7.

The reference day is conventionally 1 January 1970, and that day was a Thursday. Writing the day count from the epoch as rd, the weekday is (rd + 4) % 7. With 0 standing for Sunday, Thursday is 4, so 4 gets added.

Let us actually count the days up to 28 August 2026. From 1970 through 2025 is 56 years, and among them 14 are leap years, from 1972 through 2024. The year 2000 falls under the 400 rule and so is a leap year, meaning it is not dropped. So up to 1 January 2026 there are 56×365+14 days, that is 20,454. The 28th of August is the 240th day of that year, so adding 239 gives rd = 20,693. Add 4 and you get 20,697, whose remainder modulo 7 is 5. With 0 as Sunday, 5 is Friday. Correct.

This method has three problems.

  • Producing the day count already demands a formula — moving what the paragraph above did by hand into code needs the three leap conditions and the cumulative month lengths, exactly as before. The division has not disappeared; it has moved to an earlier stage
  • Counting in a loop slows down in proportion to the input — an implementation that walks year by year deciding leap status takes longer the further you go from 1970. You end up with a program that produces the weekday of the year 3000 more slowly than that of 2026
  • Everything before the epoch falls apart — the % operator in C-family languages gives the remainder the sign of the dividend

The third is especially quiet and especially dangerous. 1 March 1900 was a Thursday, and the rd of that day is −25,508. (rd + 4) % 7 becomes the remainder of −25,504 divided by 7, and in C that value is −3. Use it as an index into an array of weekday names and you read outside the array. To get the correct answer of 4 you have to fold once more, as in ((rd % 7) + 7 + 4) % 7. Since −25,508 divides exactly by 7 the remainder is 0, and (0 + 7 + 4) % 7 = 4, which gives Thursday. That is two divisions used.

3. Turning It Into a Formula — Zeller Congruence and the Sakamoto Method

Getting rid of the loop calls for a closed form that goes straight from year, month and day to the weekday. There are two such forms, and they absorb the same irregularity in different ways.

3.1 Zeller Congruence — Straightening Out the Month Lengths

The Gregorian form of the Zeller congruence looks like this. q is the day, m the month, K the last two digits of the year and J the first two.

h = (q + ⌊13(m+1)/5⌋ + K + ⌊K/4⌋ + ⌊J/4⌋ + 5J) mod 7
    (m: 3=March … 12=December, 13=January, 14=February. For January and February, decrease the year by 1)
    (h: 0=Sat, 1=Sun, 2=Mon, 3=Tue, 4=Wed, 5=Thu, 6=Fri)

The strangest-looking parts of this expression are putting March first and 13(m+1)/5. The two are one thing.

Starting the year in March sends February, the month whose length varies, to the very end. The leap day then shifts no month after it, so the remainders modulo 7 of the month lengths running from March form the fixed pattern 3, 2, 3, 2, 3, 3, 2, 3, 2, 3, 3. And ⌊13(m+1)/5⌋, as m runs from 3 to 14, gives 10, 13, 15, 18, 20, 23, 26, 28, 31, 33, 36, 39. The differences of that sequence are exactly the same pattern. The staircase made by month lengths has been drawn with a single division, and the remaining K and J terms are the three-tier leap rule transcribed.

Let us check it against real dates.

  • 2026-08-28 — q=28, m=8, K=26, J=20. ⌊13×9/5⌋=23, ⌊26/4⌋=6, ⌊20/4⌋=5, 5×20=100. The sum is 28+23+26+6+5+100=188 and 188 mod 7 = 6. h=6 is Friday. Correct
  • 2000-02-29 — February, so m=14 and the year drops to 1999. q=29, K=99, J=19. ⌊13×15/5⌋=39, ⌊99/4⌋=24, ⌊19/4⌋=4, 5×19=95. The sum is 29+39+99+24+4+95=290 and 290 mod 7 = 3. h=3 is Tuesday. 29 February 2000 was indeed a Tuesday
  • 2026-01-01 — January, so m=13 and the year is 2025. q=1, K=25, J=20. ⌊13×14/5⌋=36, ⌊25/4⌋=6, ⌊20/4⌋=5, 5×20=100. The sum is 1+36+25+6+5+100=173 and 173 mod 7 = 5. h=5 is Thursday. Correct

3.2 The Sakamoto Method — The Same Idea in Twelve Slots

Where Zeller drew the staircase of the months with a division, the Sakamoto method simply writes that staircase into a twelve-slot table.

t = [0, 3, 2, 5, 0, 3, 5, 1, 4, 6, 2, 4]
if m < 3: y = y − 1
w = (y + ⌊y/4⌋ − ⌊y/100⌋ + ⌊y/400⌋ + t[m−1] + d) mod 7
   (w: 0=Sun, 1=Mon, … 6=Sat)

Let us confirm it with the same dates. For 28 August 2026, m is at least 3 so y stays 2026, and t[7] is 1. ⌊2026/4⌋=506, ⌊2026/100⌋=20, ⌊2026/400⌋=5. The sum is 2026+506−20+5+1+28 = 2,546 and 2,546 mod 7 = 5. w=5 is Friday. For 29 February 2000, y drops to 1999 and t[1]=3 is used. 1999+499−19+4+3+29 = 2,515 and 2,515 mod 7 = 2, that is Tuesday. For 1 March 1900, 1900+475−19+4+2+1 = 2,363 with remainder 4, a Thursday. All three dates agree with Zeller and with counting from the epoch.

Where the twelve numbers in the table come from is the heart of this method. The values from March onward are the days accumulated before that month, taken modulo 7, with exactly 1 subtracted. The days accumulated before August are 212 and 212 mod 7 = 2, yet the table value is 1. Before October it is 273 days with remainder 0, yet the table says 6, that is −1. That −1 does one job. When m is at least 3 the year is not decreased, so the ⌊y/4⌋ term counts 29 February of that very year even though it has not arrived yet, and the −1 in the table undoes that one slot. January and February decrease y and so do not have the problem, which is why t[0]=0 and t[1]=3 match the accumulated days exactly.

Put the two methods and epoch counting side by side and the differences become plain.

From this a criterion emerges. If you already hold a day count, Zeller or Sakamoto is a loss. Instead of starting from a day count the two formulas pay three or four divisions. Conversely, if all you have is year, month and day, the two formulas are shorter than building a day count and then taking a remainder. Which side wins is settled not by the algorithm but by what form your code is holding the date in.

4. Why a Twelve-Slot Table Replaces a Division

Sakamoto table looks small but carries an idea. Month lengths are not a value you can derive by calculation. There is no arithmetic reason behind the arrangement of 30-day and 31-day months. Trying to compute something that cannot be computed requires a detour like ⌊13(m+1)/5⌋, and that detour is precisely a division.

The table turns that division into a single memory read. Twelve small integers come to 12 bytes as a byte array, or 48 bytes even as 32-bit integers. A cache line is typically 64 bytes, so the whole table fits inside one cache line.

  • While the table stays resident in cache, a lookup is overwhelmingly cheaper than a division
  • If the table grows and is evicted from cache, one lookup becomes a main memory access, and that cost exceeds several divisions
  • A table requires the index to be inside the valid range — a wrong calculation produces a memory fault rather than a wrong answer
  • Table values have to be checkable — a table of constants whose origin nobody can explain can be neither fixed nor ported

4.1 Branches and Lookup Tables — The Table Does Not Always Win

There is one more reason to use a table. It removes branches. Line up an if per month, or write a switch, and the CPU has to predict which way it goes every time. A correct prediction costs almost nothing, but a wrong one throws away the instructions already in flight and refills the pipeline. That penalty comfortably exceeds several multiplications.

So the closer the input is to random, the more a lookup table wins. The month of a random date cannot be predicted, and an unpredictable branch keeps being wrong. Conversely, in code where dates from the same month arrive back to back, prediction is right nearly every time and the branch version can be faster.

There is something easy to miss as well. A large table flips the board. Turn all 400 years of weekdays into a table and the divisions vanish, but the table becomes 146,000 entries. A table that does not fit in cache makes every lookup a memory wait, and during that wait the CPU does nothing. This is exactly the place where what was called an optimisation produces the opposite result.

5. Why Division Is Expensive

Every method so far leaves a mod 7 at the end. Ahead of it sit three or four more divisions inherited from the leap year rule. The reason attempts to reduce them keep appearing is that integer division is among the most expensive of the everyday arithmetic operations.

The reason lies in the nature of the circuit. Multiplication can produce all its partial products at once and add them down in a tree. Because the stages do not wait on one another it pipelines deeply, and a multiplier can accept a new multiplication every cycle while emitting results in order a few cycles later. Division is the opposite. It is an iterative process fixing the digits of the quotient one at a time from the top, so the next step cannot start until the previous result is out.

  • Latency is long — it takes several times longer than a multiplication for the result to appear
  • Throughput is bad too — in many implementations the divider is not fully pipelined, so throwing divisions in sequence makes each wait for the one before it to finish
  • Timing varies with the operands — some implementations take a different number of cycles depending on the values, so code with division in it has an execution time that depends on the input
  • The numbers differ per microarchitecture — latency and throughput vary a great deal across CPU generations, so no single number can state them. If you need the value for a particular chip, look at that chip instruction tables

5.1 What a Compiler Does With Constant Division

Fortunately every division in a weekday calculation has a constant divisor. 7, 4, 100 and 400 are all known at compile time. In that case the compiler does not emit a division instruction; it rewrites the operation as a multiplication by a reciprocal followed by a shift.

The principle is simple. To divide x by 7 you multiply by something close to 1/7, and since there are no fractions in the world of integers you scale by a power of two beforehand and shift back afterwards. Dividing 232 by 7 and rounding up gives 613,566,757, which is 0x24924925 in hexadecimal. Multiply x by that and take only the upper 32 bits and you generally get ⌊x/7⌋.

Check it with a small example. 216=65,536 divided by 7 and rounded up is 9,363. With x=100, 100×9,363=936,300 and the quotient after dividing by 65,536 is 14, and ⌊100/7⌋=14, so it matches. With x=21, 21×9,363=196,623 and the quotient is 3, and ⌊21/7⌋=3, again matching. One multiplication and one shift stood in for a division. But this conversion is not free. It has a valid range, and beyond that range it goes wrong in silence.

6. Seven Is a Mersenne Number — The Point the Source Article Seized On

That is the background. What the source article touched is the mod 7 left at the end.

6.1 Moving a Seven-Day Week Into an Eight-Day Week

Seven is 23−1. Numbers of that shape are called Mersenne numbers, and one property follows from it. The remainder of x modulo 7 is the same as the remainder modulo 8 of x plus ⌊x/7⌋.

x mod 7  =  (x + ⌊x/7⌋) mod 8

The proof is one line. Write x as 7q+r and x+q becomes 8q+r; divide by 8 and the 8q vanishes leaving only r. Since r is less than 7 it fits straight into the remainder slot modulo 8.

Try it for real. Checking with the 20,697 found earlier, ⌊20697/7⌋=2,956 and 20,697+2,956=23,653. The remainder of 23,653 modulo 8 is 5. And 20,697 mod 7 is also 5. It holds for small numbers too. With x=13, ⌊13/7⌋=1, 13+1=14, 14 mod 8 = 6, and 13 mod 7 = 6.

Why is that a gain? Because a remainder modulo 8 is not a division. One mask keeping the low 3 bits is all it takes. And the remaining ⌊x/7⌋ can be turned into a multiplication and a shift, as the previous section showed. One division becomes a short chain of multiply, shift, add and mask. The source article calls this transformation Mul-Add-Shift, and gives the name Nundinal Map to the mapping that moves the seven-day cycle into an eight-day space.

6.2 The Valid Range of a Magic Constant — Where It Starts Going Wrong

The problem deferred earlier comes back here. A reciprocal built by rounding up is very slightly larger than the true 1/7. That tiny excess grows as it is multiplied by x, and from some point it inflates the quotient by 1.

You can verify this directly with the 16-bit example. The constant 9,363 is exact for x up to 13,109. 13,109×9,363=122,739,567 and the quotient after dividing by 65,536 is 1,872, and the true ⌊13109/7⌋ is also 1,872. But it first breaks at 13,110. 13,110×9,363=122,748,930 and the quotient is 1,873, yet 7×1,873=13,111, so the true quotient is 1,872. The answer is 1 too large. No exception, no error. Just a wrong weekday.

In 32 bits the structure is the same and only the boundary sits further out. The source article writes that the rounded-up constant it uses is exact for only about 80% of 32-bit inputs, and attaches a Range Compression step that pushes inputs into the valid interval. It works by adding a fixed offset to inputs on the negative side, moving the distribution into the valid interval. Half the technique goes not to speed but to securing correctness.

  • A magic constant is not an algorithm but a contract that comes with a range — leave the range unwritten and nobody will be able to fix it later
  • Failure outside the range is quiet — it shows up not as a crash but as a weekday off by one day
  • Signed inputs are particularly dangerous — in the negative interval the difference between flooring and truncation becomes a wrong answer directly
  • Port it to a different bit width and both the constant and the range must be recomputed — a constant that is right in 32 bits is not optimal in 64

7. But Is This Ever an Actual Bottleneck

The honest answer is that in most programs it is not.

In code that prints a single date on a screen, the weekday calculation is an invisible sliver of the total time. Around it sit string parsing, timezone rule lookup, and choosing a weekday name to suit the locale. If one web request takes a few milliseconds, saving a hundred weekday calculations will not move even a few decimal places of the response time.

The places where it does matter have clear conditions. The same calculation repeats a very large number of times, and the surrounding cost has already been stripped away.

  • Bulk log parsing — a pipeline that pulls date fields out of hundreds of millions of timestamps and sorts them into weekday or day buckets. A nanosecond per line becomes a real fraction of the total processing time
  • Time-series indexing and partitioning — the path that converts a date into a partition key at every write. It sits in the hot loop of the write path
  • Bulk recomputation of calendar rules — batches that expand recurring schedules thousands of times over, or recompute business-day maturities in bulk
  • The implementation of a date library itself — the place where being slow makes every piece of code above it slow as well
  • Small, slow hardware — in environments where the divider is weak or absent, the relative weight of one division is nothing like it is on a desktop

A common thread shows through. Every one of them is a loop with little to do besides the weekday calculation. If no such loop exists in your code, this technique is something to know about rather than something to apply.

8. Correctness Comes Before Performance

If you have decided to implement this yourself instead of using the standard library, what you gain and what you might lose belong on the same scale. What you gain is a few divisions. The list of what you can lose is longer.

  • Out-of-range inputs — feed in a 13th month, a 30 February or a day zero and the formula raises no error but hands back a plausible weekday. Validation becomes the caller job
  • Negative years and dates before the common era — the ⌊⌋ in the formulas is mathematical flooring, while integer division in most languages truncates toward zero. The moment the year goes negative the two diverge
  • The Julian transition — push a Gregorian formula back before 1582 and it disagrees with the dates in actual historical records. The moment of transition differed by country, so which calendar a date was written in becomes a property of the data
  • Overflow — if the sum of intermediate terms exceeds the integer range the result is meaningless. Signed integer overflow is undefined behaviour in some languages
  • Mismatched weekday numbering conventions — Zeller has 0 as Saturday, Sakamoto has 0 as Sunday, and ISO 8601 has 1 as Monday. Mix values from different conventions and things land days apart

The order goes like this. Make it right first, make it provably right next, and make it fast after that. Weekday calculation has a property that makes keeping to that order easy. Because it repeats every 400 years, all 146,097 days can be checked exhaustively. A few hundred thousand cases is nothing at all as a test. Compare an existing implementation against a new one slot by slot across the entire 400-year span and you learn whether the optimisation broke correctness as a fact rather than a guess.

9. You Do Not Know Until You Measure

The source article attached benchmarks. How to read those numbers is the last topic here. The conditions come first. What follows are relative times the original author measured in their own environment, with the method Neri published in 2024 set at 1.00×. Smaller is faster.

The measurement method is stated in the original too. Random dates are loaded into memory in bulk and run through, and in latency mode the result is fed by bitwise XOR into the next calculation to prevent parallel execution. Each trial is repeated 5 times, the median is taken, and the baseline of the loop itself is subtracted. The input range is the whole of signed 32-bit.

Strip those conditions away and carrying over only the phrase four times faster makes that sentence false. Within the same table the same method wanders between 0.25× and 0.55×. A different CPU and a different compiler mean a different result, and that is not a flaw in some particular implementation but simply how this kind of measurement behaves.

The places where microbenchmarks fool people are largely fixed.

  • Input distribution — random dates and sorted dates produce completely different branch prediction outcomes. If your real data is sorted and you are measuring at random, those numbers are not yours
  • Dead code elimination — use the result nowhere and the compiler deletes the calculation itself. If a time near zero came out, you measured a removal rather than an optimisation
  • Inlining — a function inlined in a microbenchmark may fail to cross a call boundary in real code. The reverse happens too
  • Confusing latency with throughput — code whose next calculation waits on the previous result and code that pours work in independently have different performance characteristics. That is why the original measured two separate modes
  • Warm-up and frequency — the first run mixes in cache filling, and under sustained load the clock comes down. That is why repetitions and a median are needed
  • An environment with everything else stripped away — a loop that pulls integers from an array and computes only the weekday, and real code that parses a string and builds an object, have their bottleneck in different places

So the conclusion is simple. Somebody else benchmark is evidence that the technique exists, not evidence that your code gets faster. If this calculation really is your bottleneck, a profiler will tell you that first. If the profiler said nothing, there is no reason to change anything.

What Could Not Be Confirmed

  • The benchmark table in the original lists throughput and latency separately. Which of the two the figures above belong to could not be pinned down
  • The original table also holds platform rows beyond those carried over here, along with absolute values in nanoseconds, but only the rows that could be read consistently were included
  • The original paper for the method Neri published in 2024 could not be checked. This article treats the Neri method only as the comparison baseline the source article chose
  • What instruction sequences the major compilers actually emit for constant division varies by version and target architecture, and the output of any specific combination could not be confirmed
  • Measured integer division latency and throughput figures for CPUs in wide use today are not included in this article

Wrapping Up

Weekday calculation is hard not because of the algorithm but because of the calendar. A perfectly regular seven-day cycle has to be laid on a system where 365 and 366 are mixed by the 4, 100 and 400 rules. That mismatch shows up inside code as division.

The formulas are different ways of reducing that division. Zeller drew the staircase of month lengths with the single ⌊13(m+1)/5⌋, while Sakamoto wrote the same staircase into a twelve-slot table and undid the one slot of the leap day by subtracting 1 from the table values. The source article went further back and turned the final mod 7 into a mask by 8, using the property that 7 is a Mersenne number. All three stages do the same thing. Compute in advance what can be known in advance, and at run time do only what is left.

And what remains at the end is a judgement. If this calculation is not your bottleneck, the standard library is the right answer. If it is, then before changing anything, fix correctness by comparing all 400 years exhaustively, record the valid range of the constant as a test rather than a comment, and measure with your own input distribution. Reverse the order and what you gain is a few cycles while what you lose is a date off by a day. That trade rarely comes out ahead.
