Recommended Resignation in Korea - What It Legally Is

The space between dismissal and quitting: what to verify before you sign, and where unemployment benefits are actually decided. Korean law, as of August 2026.

Aug 11, 2026 Law & Legal

Read this first - scope, date, and limits

This article describes Korean law as of August 2026 and is not legal advice. The statutes and mechanics below are a general explanation. Whether a specific separation counts as a dismissal or a mutual termination, whether unemployment benefits will be granted, and whether an agreement holds up all depend on the employment contract and how the termination actually came about. Those determinations are made by the competent employment center, regional labor office, Labor Relations Commission and courts — not by this page. For your own case, contact the Ministry of Employment and Labor counselling centre (1350, a Korea-only number) or a certified labor attorney or lawyer. Statutes and administrative interpretations change, so check the current text at the Korean Law Information Center (law.go.kr). Readers outside Korea should note that the rules below are Korean and have no application elsewhere.

Korean law has no such term

A meeting is scheduled, the company suggests you resign, and a document usually appears on the table in the same conversation. Yet if you search the Labor Standards Act for the Korean phrase behind recommended resignation, you will not find it. What the statute regulates is dismissal and the limits on it, and on the other side sits resignation, the employee ending the contract on their own. The recommended resignation is a name that practice invented for the space between the two.

Having no name does not mean having no legal character. In substance a recommended resignation is usually a termination of the employment contract by mutual agreement. The employer proposes, the employee declares an intention to resign, the employer accepts. What ends the contract is not one side acting alone but the two intentions meeting. That single difference is what separates it from a dismissal.

And that difference changes everything downstream. Korean law layers three requirements onto a dismissal; none of them attach to a termination reached by agreement. From the employer side that makes the recommended resignation the path of least friction. From the employee side it is the path on which the statutory protections do not switch on automatically. That is not a moral judgement, just the structure.

One important qualifier: the title on the document does not fix the legal character. Submitting a resignation letter does not always mean the departure was voluntary. If the employee had no realistic alternative and the outcome was in practice determined by the employer alone, there is room to treat the substance as a dismissal. That analysis rests not on the file name but on facts — how the meeting ran, how the message was delivered, what was signalled as the consequence of refusing.

What the Labor Standards Act demands of a dismissal

To see what a recommended resignation bypasses, look at what it bypasses. Three layers sit on a dismissal.

The first is cause. Article 23(1) reads:

An employer shall not, without justifiable cause, dismiss, lay off, suspend, transfer a worker, reduce their wages, or take other punitive measures against a worker.

The same article restricts timing in paragraph 2. An employer may not dismiss a worker during a period of leave for medical treatment of an occupational injury or disease and for 30 days thereafter, nor during a woman's pre- and post-natal leave and for 30 days thereafter. Exceptions apply where a lump-sum compensation under Article 84 has been paid or the business can no longer continue.

The second is notice. Article 26 requires notice at least 30 days in advance, and where no such notice is given, payment of 30 days or more of ordinary wages. Three situations remove the notice duty:

  • the worker's continuous service is shorter than three months
  • natural disaster, incident or other unavoidable cause makes it impossible to continue the business
  • the worker intentionally caused serious disruption to the business or property damage, in the cases specified by Ministry of Employment and Labor ordinance

The third is form. Article 27 requires the employer to give written notice of the reason for and timing of the dismissal, and paragraph 2 states that a dismissal takes effect only if so notified in writing. That is a validity requirement, not a procedural nicety. Where written advance notice already specifies the reason and timing, it counts as this notice (paragraph 3).

How a recommended resignation routes around all of it

The picture is now clear. Those three layers attach to a dismissal. When the ending is reached by agreement, there is nothing for them to attach to.

  • The just-cause question never opens. Nobody has to argue whether underperformance or a reorganisation is a legitimate ground for dismissal
  • The written-notice problem disappears. The absence of a dismissal letter is not a procedural defect, because there was no dismissal
  • Pay in lieu of notice does not arise by default. Thirty days of ordinary wages attaches to a dismissal without notice, not to an agreed exit
  • The threshold for a remedy rises. To contest it you must first establish that the substance was a dismissal

So proposing a recommended resignation is not, in itself, unlawful. A contracting party is proposing to end a contract by agreement, and the employee has the option of declining. What creates a problem is when that option is hollowed out — being told that refusal will be met with adverse personnel action, that a dismissal is coming regardless, or being asked to sign on the spot with no time to respond. Enough facts of that kind, and the argument that the substance was a dismissal becomes available.

Running the other way, there are real reasons an employee may prefer this route. A settlement payment can be attached, the handover period and separation date become negotiable, the process runs without disciplinary proceedings, and no disciplinary record follows the employee out. Receiving the offer means a negotiation has opened, not that a conclusion has been reached. The rest of this article is therefore written around what to verify and what to ask.

The separation confirmation form is where benefits are decided

In practice the document carrying the most weight is not the settlement agreement but the separation confirmation form — the employment insurance form an employer files about a departing insured worker, prescribed as Form 75-4 of the Enforcement Rules of the Employment Insurance Act. The separation reason code entered there is where the employment center begins its eligibility review.

Filing is triggered by a request, not automatic. Under the official guidance, when the departing worker or the employment center asks for it, the employer must issue or submit the form within 10 days. Missing that window carries an administrative fine, and entering content that differs from the facts carries a heavier one.

The last two rows are the point. The identical label appears in code 23 and in code 26. What separates them is why the recommendation was made — management necessity or a downturn puts it at 23, a cause attributable to the worker puts it at 26. Ministry guidance on code 26 groups together the case of a disciplinary dismissal for serious work-related fault, the case where disciplinary dismissal was warranted but the employer instead recommended resignation, and the case where the conduct fell short of disciplinary dismissal but the employer recommended resignation over performance or capability issues.

The code scheme has been revised before. For the wording actually in force, check the completion instructions to Form 75-4 of the Enforcement Rules of the Employment Insurance Act and the guidance of your local employment center.

Code 23 is not automatic - Article 58 of the Employment Insurance Act

Here is the common misreading. The claim that a recommended resignation means benefits is only half right. The code is where the analysis starts, not where it ends. The governing provision is Article 58 of the Employment Insurance Act, titled restriction of eligibility according to the reason for separation, and it splits the restrictions in two.

The first branch is dismissal for a serious cause attributable to the worker: conviction of imprisonment or heavier for violating the Criminal Act or a statute relating to the job, conduct causing enormous disruption to the business or property damage, and prolonged absence without leave in breach of the employment contract or work rules without justifiable cause.

The second branch is separation for the worker's own reasons, and this is the sentence to read closely. Alongside leaving to change jobs or to start a business, the provision lists a person who had a serious attributable cause and who left on the employer recommendation instead of being dismissed.

Read that structure and code 26 explains itself. Where grounds for disciplinary dismissal exist and the employer instead recommends resignation, the exit is a recommended resignation in form but potentially within Article 58 in substance. Which gives four practical takeaways.

  • The code is a filing, not a decision. Eligibility is determined by the employment center on the facts
  • Code 26 does not automatically disqualify anyone either. Insured unit period, capacity and willingness to work are assessed alongside it
  • Code 23 can still be revisited if the underlying facts turn out otherwise; a mismatch between the filing and reality is itself a problem
  • If the two sides remember the reason differently, align it in advance. Conflicting accounts stretch the review out

The most useful thing anyone can do is state the reason accurately and put it in writing. If the agreement or resignation letter records that the exit followed the employer's proposal and why, there is far less room for the accounts to diverge later. Eligibility rules and the application process as a whole are covered in Korea Unemployment Benefit Complete Guide 2026.

Voluntary departures that are still eligible

Sometimes the negotiation collapses and the employee leaves on their own. That does not close every door. Article 58 does not restrict eligibility where the departure falls within justifiable reasons prescribed by ordinance, and that list is Table 2 of the Enforcement Rules of the Employment Insurance Act. Grouped by type, the recurring ones are:

  • Deterioration of working conditions below what was presented at hiring or below the previous terms, continuing for a set period
  • Unpaid wages, pay below the minimum wage, breach of the limits on extended working hours
  • Business suspension under which the wages received fall below a set proportion of average wages
  • Discrimination — unreasonable differential treatment on grounds such as religion, sex, physical disability or union activity
  • Sexual harassment, sexual violence and other sexual mistreatment
  • Workplace harassment
  • Insolvency or closure appearing certain, or mass workforce reduction scheduled
  • Transfer, acquisition, merger, change of business line, organisational restructuring or introduction of new technology
  • Commuting made difficult by relocation of the workplace, a transfer or similar
  • Needing to personally care for a parent or cohabiting relative with illness or injury for a set minimum period
  • Pregnancy, childbirth or care of a child aged 8 or under where continued work is difficult and leave was not permitted
  • Compulsory military service, reaching retirement age or expiry of a fixed term
  • Exposure to injury risk at a workplace where a serious accident occurred, or physical or mental impairment making the work unmanageable
  • Any other circumstance objectively recognised as leaving no choice but to separate
Each entry carries detailed conditions such as minimum periods or proportions. The list above shows the types; whether your situation meets the conditions has to be checked against the current text of Table 2 and confirmed with the employment center. If unpaid wages are the trigger, pair this with the procedure in Unpaid Wages in Korea: Complete Guide.

Five things the agreement actually settles

A separation agreement does not only settle whether you leave. Money and dates are settled with it. In order:

1. The separation date

The last day worked and the employment insurance loss date are not necessarily the same day. Move the separation date by a day or two and that month's wages, social insurance contributions, whether annual leave accrues, and the continuous service calculation all move with it. Because leave entitlements hinge on completing a year of service, a separation date sitting on that boundary is not a small difference.

2. The settlement payment

A settlement payment is not a statutory entitlement. No provision of the Labor Standards Act sets its size; it is agreed. So the thing to check is not only the amount but its character. Whether it is a pure settlement, whether it absorbs an amount equivalent to pay in lieu of notice, whether it absorbs unused annual leave allowance — each changes what actually reaches you. The payment date, method, and who handles tax should be on the page too.

3. Unused annual leave allowance

Annual paid leave left unused at separation raises an allowance question. If the agreement only says the settlement payment includes it, there is no way to verify how much of the figure is leave. Asking for the days and the daily rate to be itemised separately is the cleaner path.

4. Statutory severance pay

Severance pay is not a negotiable item but a statutory entitlement. Article 8(1) of the Employee Retirement Benefit Security Act requires an employer to establish a system capable of paying 30 days or more of average wages for each year of continuous service. Average wages means the total wages paid over the three months before the calculation event, divided by the total days in that period; where that figure is lower than ordinary wages, ordinary wages are used instead. Payment is due within 14 days of separation, extendable by agreement where special circumstances exist. Calculation and pension receipt are covered in Complete Guide to Severance Pay and Retirement Pension.

5. The ancillary clauses

The clauses at the back of the agreement are the ones that last longest: confidentiality, non-disparagement, waiver of claims, non-competition, repayment of company loans or training costs, treatment of stock options and unvested bonuses, and issuance of employment and service certificates. A waiver drafted broadly can be read to bar even a wage claim you discover afterwards. This is the part to read sentence by sentence, because it defines what you are giving up.

After signing - when can it still be undone

Case law has mapped this area fairly clearly. The reference point is the Supreme Court decision of 10 April 1992, case 91Da43138. It treats the submission of a resignation as an offer to terminate the employment relationship, and holds that the employee may withdraw that offer before the employer's acceptance reaches them. Withdrawal is not permitted where special circumstances make it contrary to good faith, such as causing the employer unforeseeable damage.

The reverse rule is settled too. Where employer and employee have agreed to end the relationship, the meeting of intentions occurred at that moment, and neither side may withdraw at will. Early retirement schemes are explained the same way: an application is merely an offer to terminate by agreement, so the employee may withdraw it before the employer accepts.

Translated into practice, everything turns on one question: when did the employer accept.

  • Resignation submitted but no acceptance communicated yet — the window for withdrawal is open
  • Acceptance or approval has reached the employee — withdrawal becomes difficult
  • An agreement signed by both sides — treated as concluded, so unilateral withdrawal is difficult
  • Communicate any withdrawal in a form that leaves a record, which helps if it is later examined

Case law applies differently on different facts, though. When internal approval was completed and when the communication arrived can change the answer. If withdrawal is on the table, get it checked before spending time on it.

If you decide to contest - three months comes first

If you are heading toward a dispute rather than an agreement, the first thing to check is not evidence but the calendar. Article 28(2) of the Labor Standards Act provides:

An application for remedy under paragraph 1 shall be filed within three months from the day on which the unfair dismissal or other measure occurred.

Those three months form an exclusion period. Unlike a statute of limitations it is not interrupted, so once it lapses a decision on the merits becomes hard to obtain. It is easily confused with the three-year limit on wage claims, but they are different clocks and this one runs out far sooner.

  • Start date — for a dismissal, calculated from the dismissal date stated on the notice. Where the stated date and the receipt date differ, confirm which governs
  • Venue — the Regional Labor Relations Commission with jurisdiction over the workplace
  • If it ended as a recommended resignation — you are arguing the substance was a dismissal, so the same window applies. Time spent negotiating is time off that clock
  • Workplaces with four or fewer employees — Articles 28 through 33 are described as not applying, so this route is largely unavailable

Procedure, drafting the application, the content of remedial orders, and evidence are covered in Wrongful Termination and Workers' Rights - A Complete Guide. The one thing to carry from here is that the clock runs during the negotiation too.

If the workplace has fewer than five employees

A workplace with four or fewer regular employees operates under a different set of provisions. The basis is Article 11 of the Labor Standards Act with Article 7 and Table 1 of the Enforcement Decree, and on dismissal it splits like this:

  • Applied — the timing restriction on dismissal (Art. 23(2)) and advance notice (Art. 26)
  • Not applied — the bar on dismissal without justifiable cause (Art. 23(1)), dismissal for managerial reasons (Arts. 24 and 25), written notice (Art. 27), and the remedy for unfair dismissal (Arts. 28 through 33)

In that structure a recommended resignation carries a slightly different weight. With no procedure available to contest an unfair dismissal, the terms of the agreement are effectively the whole game. Conversely, advance notice applies regardless of size, so an immediate termination with no notice is a separate point worth checking.

Counting regular employees is not straightforward, however. Whether the proprietor is included and how part-time and daily workers are counted can push a workplace across the five-employee line. For a borderline workplace, start with the counting method. What can be verified from the contract itself is set out in Employment Contract Checklist.

Benefit fraud is a different matter entirely

Searches about recommended resignations tend to trail into how should the reason be worded so benefits come through. There is one answer: state what actually happened. Filing a reason contrary to the facts to obtain benefits is fraud, and the consequence does not stop at returning the money.

Article 62 of the Employment Insurance Act allows an order to return all or part of job-seeking benefits obtained by false or otherwise improper means, and provides for additional collection on top. The specific rates sit in the Enforcement Rules and rise with the number of restrictions imposed over the preceding decade.

  • Return order — all or part of the job-seeking benefits paid (Art. 62(1))
  • Additional collection — assessed on the improperly received amount, with the rate rising on repetition
  • Where the employer colluded — the rate rises sharply and the employer is jointly liable
  • Criminal liability — obtaining benefits by false or improper means is a criminal offence
  • Voluntary disclosure — there is a provision exempting additional collection where the person reports before an investigation begins

The specific danger point is employer and employee aligning on a reason that is not true. Asking for, or agreeing to, a false entry on the separation form is hazardous for both. The employer faces the fine for false entry plus joint liability; the worker faces return and additional collection. Asking for a favourable code that is not accurate and asking for an inaccurate code to be corrected to the truth are entirely different acts. The second is a legitimate request, and if it is disputed you can explain the facts to the employment center and have them assessed.

If you believe the code does not match reality, ask the employment center before arguing with the employer. Eligibility is decided there in any event, so finding out what you need to substantiate and preparing that material is the faster route.

Where this differs from systems you may know

If you work for a foreign-invested company or a head-office policy applies alongside Korean law, concepts from other jurisdictions get mixed in. Here is the character of each, without the numbers.

  • United States — the baseline is at-will employment, so the starting point differs from Korea's just-cause review under Article 23. Instead, terminations commonly run through a severance agreement containing a release of claims, and waivers of age-discrimination claims are separately regulated under the OWBPA, which requires consideration and revocation periods. The number of days involved was not verified for this article
  • Japan — draws a similar line between kaiko (dismissal) and taishoku kansho (resignation solicitation). Its employment insurance uses a category for people who separated for employer-side reasons, treating them differently from voluntary leavers
  • China — provides for termination by mutual consultation as a distinct category, and statutory economic compensation attaches to it. That differs in character from Korea, where a settlement payment is purely a matter of agreement

In all three, periods and amounts are set by each jurisdiction's own law and are amended over time. This article is written to Korean law, so if foreign rules also bear on your situation, verify them against materials for that jurisdiction.

Frequently asked questions

What happens if I do not sign a resignation letter

The situation remains one where no agreement was reached. If the employer still wants the relationship to end, the next instrument is dismissal, and a dismissal brings Articles 23, 26 and 27 back into play. How things actually unfold depends on the workplace and the underlying reason, so no particular outcome can be assumed.

The recommendation was only verbal - do I need a record

The person who later has to explain the reason is usually the employee. Writing down the date, who attended and what was said, on the same day, keeps your account consistent. Sending a confirming email and keeping the reply is another route.

Does a settlement payment reduce unemployment benefits

A settlement payment is not a statutory entitlement but an agreed figure, while job-seeking benefits are calculated on wage items defined by the Employment Insurance Act. How the payment is characterised in the filing can affect handling, so record the composition of the amount clearly in the document and confirm it with the employment center.

Are severance pay and a settlement payment separate

They are. Severance pay is a statutory entitlement under the Employee Retirement Benefit Security Act; a settlement payment is agreed. If the agreement contains language to the effect that severance pay is included in the settlement, that sentence has to be clarified before signing.

Do I need code 23 to receive benefits

The code is where the analysis starts, not the conclusion. Requesting a code that does not match the facts can lead to benefit fraud. The proper route is an accurate filing followed by an eligibility determination, and if you believe the stated reason is wrong, explain the facts to the employment center and have them assessed.

Conclusion - it is an agreement, not a notification

The biggest misconception about a recommended resignation is that it is a meeting where a decision is delivered to you. The legal structure is the opposite. It cannot take effect without the employee's acceptance, and without that acceptance the employer has to use a different instrument. If that instrument is dismissal, the cause, notice and writing requirements all reappear.

So the task in that room is neither to dig in nor to sign quickly. It is to establish what is being settled: how the reason will be recorded, when the separation date falls, what the settlement payment absorbs, how leave and severance are computed, and which reason will be filed on the separation form. Answer those five and the rest becomes arithmetic.

The timeline is worth carrying too. The separation form runs on 10 days from the request, severance pay on 14 days from separation, and the Commission remedy on three months. While a negotiation drags on, the shortest of those expires first. And whichever way it ends, recording the reason accurately is ultimately the safest choice available.

Once more: this article is not legal advice. Two separations bearing the same name can end differently depending on the contract, the size of the workplace and how the termination came about, and those determinations are made by the employment center, regional labor office, Labor Relations Commission and courts. Before signing, take the document to a certified labor attorney or a lawyer; for programme questions use the Ministry of Employment and Labor counselling centre (1350, reachable within Korea).
