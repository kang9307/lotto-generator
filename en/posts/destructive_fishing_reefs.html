Why Reef-Wrecking Fishing Persists

Recovery takes decades. Rent is due today.

Aug 27, 2026 Trends & Culture

This story can start at a bag of frozen shrimp

Turn the bag over in the seafood aisle and the country of origin is printed there. How it was caught is not. Net, trap or line, and what else vanished from that patch of sea in the process, are simply not part of what a label covers. That small blank space already explains about half of the problem this article is about.

In August 2026 a study put a number on the frequency of destructive fishing in one tropical archipelago for the first time. The number is not the body of this piece, though. That such methods are harmful is not news, and they do not persist because no law forbids them.

The real question is this. Wrecking a reef eventually erases your own fishing ground, so why does the choice keep getting made? Answer it with morality and nothing gets explained. The answer sits in incentives, and incentives are a thing you can change.

One note up front — this is a general explanation drawn from published research and institutional sources. It is not an assessment of any particular country, region or group of people, and it does not describe how any fishing method is carried out.

What was established - and the limits of it

The study behind this is by Ben Williams and colleagues, published in Remote Sensing in Ecology and Conservation on 12 August 2026. It is a Zoological Society of London journal, and the author list includes researchers from Hasanuddin University in Indonesia alongside British and Australian institutions. The site is the Spermonde Archipelago off South Sulawesi.

  • Method - a hydrophone recorded ambient sound for 16 months, and a machine learning detector picked out blast signatures from it
  • Material classified - roughly 3,699 hours of audio
  • Events verified - 3,567, averaging 23.14 a day, or about one every 62 minutes, extrapolating to some 8,446 a year
  • Detection range - at least about 16.95 kilometres, meaning the figures describe what falls inside that radius
  • Estimated damage - up to about 16.72 hectares of reef a year within that detection range
  • Background - satellite records show live coral cover in the archipelago fell roughly 76 per cent over the past three decades

Three qualifications are worth stating plainly. These figures estimate what one hydrophone can reach, not a national total. The decline in coral cover has multiple overlapping drivers, and this practice is identified as a major one among them. And such methods have been reported across many tropical waters. That the numbers came from here is a different statement from saying here is unusual.

What the study genuinely added is frequency. That this kind of fishing exists has been known for a long time, but measuring how often it happens was the hard part. Each event is brief and the evidence is underwater.

What a reef actually does, and what happens when it goes

Treat a reef as scenery and the weight of the problem never lands. A reef does three jobs at once, and all three connect directly to how the people nearby make a living.

One - it is the structure fish live inside

The value of a reef lies less in the coral itself than in the structural complexity the coral builds. Crevices, overhangs and shade are what let juveniles escape predation long enough to grow into something worth catching. Flatten that structure and species counts drop and individual fish get smaller. Removing fish and removing the house the fish live in are different acts. The first draws down inventory. The second dismantles the plant.

Two - it is a wall that shaves down waves

A 2014 meta-analysis by Ferrario and colleagues in Nature Communications pooled more than 255 studies on the buffering effect of reefs. Reefs dissipate wave energy by 97 per cent on average, with the reef crest alone accounting for 86 per cent. Wave height falls by about 84 per cent. The same work put the population connected to those risk reduction benefits at more than 100 million, and compared a median cost of 19,791 US dollars per metre for building artificial breakwaters with about 1,290 dollars per metre for reef restoration.

That contrast is not just a number. A coast that has lost its wave buffer has to buy the function back with money. And the invoice goes to everyone living on that coast.

Three - it is the base of the catch

Tallies from the International Coral Reef Initiative and related bodies connect reef fisheries to food and income for roughly 500 million people, and the figure widens once tourism and coastal protection are included. The annual value of reef fisheries runs to about 6.8 billion US dollars, of which Southeast Asia accounts for something like 2.4 billion. Small-scale fishers in developing countries number around 6 million.

The key point is that it comes back around. Today's catch may rise. But from the following season onward, more leaves than was gained. The structure is one of cutting into your own fishing ground to pull today's income forward.

Why the choice still gets made - the commons problem

Turn toward morality here and the explanation breaks. No group knows a reef is shrinking more intimately than the people who live off it. The obstacle is not knowledge. It is arithmetic.

The gains from restraint are shared, the cost is carried alone

A reef is not divisible property and access is hard to exclude. In a resource like that, restraint scores as a personal loss. If I hold back today, the fish are still there, but nothing guarantees the spare goes to me. The benefit of restraint is spread across everyone while the cost falls on me alone. That is not people being foolish. It is what the rules produce.

Discount rates on future income are necessarily high

Recovery takes decades and rent is due today. The thinner the savings and the less stable the income, the more heavily future income gets discounted - which is a consequence of constraints, not of temperament. Someone whose next month is secure and someone whose next week of groceries is uncertain will give different answers to identical information.

Switching costs capital

Moving to a different method is not a matter of resolve. New gear costs money up front, a new way of working takes skill and time, and different catch means rebuilding a route to market. The harder capital is to reach, the more tightly a fisher is bound to whatever pays back immediately. It is worth noting this is not only a problem of the very poorest. Those who can absorb the up-front cost are the ones best placed to scale.

Risk sits at the front, margin sits at the back

Seafood supply chains generally run fisher, middleman, processor, exporter. Enforcement risk and physical risk are carried at the front, while most of the value added appears at the back. The fact that the place where the method is chosen and the place that captures the gain are different places matters a great deal when deciding where to intervene.

DimensionThe individual calculationThe collective outcome

One thing to take from the table. Raising penalties alone does not change the left-hand column. When the chance of detection is low, the expected value of a penalty stays low, and enforcement pressure by itself pushes hardest on those with no alternative, which builds resistance. To move the arithmetic you have to touch the entries in the left column themselves.

How long a broken reef takes to come back

Recovery time is the axis that makes this problem asymmetric. According to NOAA Ocean Service material, coral growth rates run as follows.

  • Massive corals - 0.3 to 2 centimetres a year
  • Branching corals - up to 10 centimetres a year
  • Time for a single reef to form - up to 10,000 years
  • Barrier reefs and atolls - 100,000 to 30 million years

Physical destruction adds a problem of its own. A coral that dies and leaves its skeleton standing is not the same as a skeleton broken into mobile rubble. When rubble rolls in the swell, settling larvae get swept off before they can hold. As long as the substrate is unstable, the recovery clock never starts. That is why damaged patches left alone often look identical decades later.

That does not mean recovery is impossible. A 2024 Current Biology study by Lange and colleagues reported that at restoration sites around Pulau Bontosua in South Sulawesi, coral cover, colony size and carbonate production tripled after transplantation, and after four years the sites were indistinguishable from nearby healthy reefs.

How not to overstate that result — the survey covered shallow water of 2 to 3 metres under the conditions of a specific restoration programme. Above all, restoration only works where the damage has stopped. Installing structures in a place that keeps getting broken is not recovery, it is consumption.

Why watching an ocean is so hard

The prohibitions generally exist already. What is missing is enforcement, and the reasons are largely physical.

  • Area versus manpower - a patrol boat is in one place at a time, and surveillance cost scales almost linearly with the area of water
  • Events are brief - by the time anyone arrives, what remains is the outcome, not the event. When detection odds are low, raising penalties barely raises expected cost
  • Satellites lag - satellite imagery shows changes in cover, but that is an outcome months or years later, and it cannot pin down when something happened
  • Reporting has limits - in a small coastal community, informing on a neighbour carries a social cost

This is where the study drew attention. Sound captures the moment an event occurs, which satellites cannot do. Narrowing down location, though, needs time-synchronised sensors in an array, and sensors bring power, communications and retrieval plans with them. And detection is not enforcement. Knowing where something happened still leaves the question of who is going to get there.

Compressed into a sentence. None of these is sufficient alone, and none transplants without conditions. Marine reserves in particular get cited for their successes, but those figures describe full protection. Half-open arrangements were frequently indistinguishable from no protection at all. It is the strength and the enforcement, not the name of the instrument, that produces the result.

What is visible from the consumer end

Back to the bag of frozen shrimp. What a shopper can see is where it came from. What is hard to learn is how it was caught. Several things compound that asymmetry.

  • Origin labelling is designed to convey provenance, and gear type is not the centre of what is declared
  • Processing blurs the identity of the raw product, and blending sources blurs it further
  • Method is fixed only at the catching stage, and several businesses sit between there and the shelf

Certification schemes try to fill that gap. The Marine Stewardship Council explains that every business from a certified fishery through to the consumer must hold chain of custody certification before the label can be applied, and states that species labelling errors on labelled products run below 1 per cent. The global average mislabelling rate the same material cites is around 30 per cent. It reads more accurately with the caveat that these are the certifier's own figures.

The limits are equally clear. Certification chiefly assesses stock status and management adequacy rather than being built to screen for specific gear types. And because it demands documentation and audit costs, the observation that small-scale fisheries face a high barrier to entry has been made for years. Given that the waters this article concerns are largely the domain of small-scale fishing, choosing a labelled product does not aim directly at the problem there.

So consumer choice is a signal rather than a solution. What actually shifts conditions is which clauses the large-volume buyers put into their contracts. Individual choices add pressure in the direction of those clauses being written.

What changes when climate stacks on top

Bleaching is what happens when heat stress drives corals to expel their symbiotic algae and weaken or die; physical destruction removes the skeletal structure itself. The trouble comes when both land on the same patch.

The fourth global coral bleaching event, confirmed by NOAA and the International Coral Reef Initiative, was formally announced on 15 April 2024. According to the tally, between 1 January 2023 and 30 September 2025 roughly 84.4 per cent of the world's coral reef area was exposed to bleaching-level heat stress, with mass bleaching documented in more than 83 countries and territories. Set beside earlier events, the change is stark.

  • First (1998) - about 21 per cent of reefs exposed to bleaching-level heat stress
  • Second (2010) and third (2014–2017) - about 37 per cent and about 68 per cent respectively
  • Fourth (2023 onward) - about 84.4 per cent, the most extensive on record

The combination is not simple addition. Recovery begins when surviving colonies supply larvae, so when bleaching thins that source it also thins the candidates available to fill a physically cleared patch. A rubble bed is hard for larvae to settle on. And as heat stress events grow more frequent, the next one arrives before the recovery curve has been climbed.

A common objection follows. If the climate signal is that large, are local efforts not pointless? The conclusion runs the other way. Heat stress cannot be controlled locally, but physical destruction and rubble condition can be. When colonies survive a bleaching event, whether their patch is still standing determines the starting line for the next recovery. Reducing local pressures is not a competing option to climate action but closer to a precondition for it.

Where this touches Korea

There are no tropical reefs on the Korean coast, which makes this easy to read as someone else's business. There are three points of contact.

Imported seafood

Korea consumes more seafood than domestic production alone can supply. According to figures released by the Ministry of Oceans and Fisheries in February 2026, total domestic fisheries production for 2025 came to 3.93 million tonnes worth 10.2366 trillion won, up 8.7 and 1.4 per cent year on year. A substantial share is still sourced through imports, and Southeast Asia is one of the main routes. However, no public statistic showing what portion of imports originates from reef fisheries could be confirmed. That absence is itself the information asymmetry described above.

The labelling regime

Korea's origin labelling regime sorts seafood into domestic, distant-water and foreign and requires the production area to be shown. It is a regime about where; how is not its centre. Most labelling regimes share that structure. Carrying method information down to the retail shelf requires catch records and distribution history to be joined up, and no single labelling rule does that.

The structure itself

This is the most substantive point of contact. Seagrass beds, tidal flats and spawning grounds are not coral reefs, but they share exactly the same properties: they are common resources, they take time to recover, and surveillance cost scales with area. How are use rights allocated? Does the person who holds back capture the gain? Who carries the cost of switching? The questions take the same shape. Read as a case study it is a foreign story; read as a structure it is the same problem.

What could not be established

The following were left unasserted, either for lack of data or because public sources did not narrow the range.

  • Frequency in other waters - the observations estimate what lies within hydrophone range. Conditions where no equivalent measurement exists are unknown
  • Variation in damage per event - annual estimates exist, but the spread by terrain, depth and coral type could not be confirmed
  • Reef-fishery share of Korean seafood imports - no public statistic showing this directly was found
  • Exceptions in Korean labelling rules - product-by-product notices were not checked exhaustively, so whether any category requires gear disclosure is left open
  • Mislabelling rates on certified products - the figures of under 1 per cent and 30 per cent come from the certifier and were not cross-checked against independent verification

In short

Wrecking a reef is not taking the fish; it is removing the house the fish live in, and the loss returns along three paths - catch volume, coastal buffering and local income. That the choice persists anyway comes not from ignorance or malice but from an incentive structure in which the gains from restraint are shared, the cost is carried alone, recovery takes decades and rent is due today. Which is why every approach with documented results was not a prohibition but a device for changing the entries in that calculation.

Detection technology getting better is a real advance. Something long discussed as an estimate has become a number. But a number appearing does not resolve anything. Once the information gap is filled, what remains is a question of resources and authority, and in front of that sits the work of reading correctly why the choice made sense in the first place. Misread the arithmetic and the prescription misses too.
