Introduction: depression is not a character flaw or a failure of will

Few conditions are simultaneously as common and as misunderstood as depression. Nobody tells a person with a chest infection to simply cough less. Yet people living with depression still hear that it is all in their attitude, or that they just need more willpower. That misunderstanding is not merely rude. It is one of the most powerful barriers to starting treatment, delaying care by months and sometimes years.

In its depressive disorder fact sheet updated in August 2025, the World Health Organization estimates that roughly 5.7% of adults worldwide live with depression — 4.6% of men and 6.9% of women — amounting to about 332 million people. The same fact sheet notes that even in high-income countries only about one third of people with depression receive mental health treatment, with inadequate investment, workforce shortages and stigma cited as the main barriers.

Korea offers a useful case study of the same gap. According to the National Mental Health Information Portal, the lifetime prevalence of major depressive disorder in Korea is 5.0% (3.0% in men, 6.9% in women) and the twelve-month prevalence is 1.5% (1.1% and 2.0%). Meanwhile the number of people actually treated keeps climbing: parliamentary figures released in September 2025 from national health insurance records put the 2024 count at 1,106,603 patients, up 32.9% from 832,483 in 2020, with women accounting for 67.2%. That is less likely to be an explosion in prevalence than a sign that more people are finally crossing the threshold into care.

This guide does not promise that any particular step will cure depression. Instead it lays out, in order, the things that actually help: how to distinguish ordinary low mood from a depressive disorder, what diagnosis is actually based on, what really happens at a first appointment, what treatment options exist and roughly how long each takes, which misconceptions repeatedly derail recovery, and what people close to someone with depression should and should not say.

1. How ordinary low mood differs from depression

Saying you feel down usually does not describe an illness. Sadness after loss, failure or conflict is a normal response and is not in itself something to treat. The problem is that failing to distinguish this normal response from clinical depression produces errors in both directions. Some people pathologise a few bad days and become needlessly frightened; others dismiss months of genuine depression as simply being a sensitive person, and lose the window for early treatment.

Three practical dividing lines are worth holding onto: duration (the two-week mark), breadth (does it drag sleep, appetite, energy and concentration down with it, or only mood?), and functional impairment (has work, school, housework or keeping up relationships actually become difficult?).

Dimension Ordinary low mood Pattern suggesting a depressive disorder
Duration Hours to a few days; usually fades within two weeks Two weeks or more, most of the day, nearly every day
Trigger Usually tied to an identifiable event — loss, failure, conflict No clear trigger, or a reaction far larger and longer than the event
Capacity for pleasure Things you enjoy still lift the mood, at least briefly Nothing brings interest or pleasure back (anhedonia)
Physical changes Sleep and appetite largely intact Insomnia or hypersomnia, appetite and weight change, persistent fatigue
Functioning Work, study and relationships broadly maintained Getting to work, studying, washing or holding a conversation becomes hard
Self-perception Framed as going through a difficult stretch Expands into global self-condemnation — believing you are worthless as a person

This table is a map, not a medical certificate. In practice, bereavement reactions, adjustment disorder, physical conditions such as thyroid dysfunction or anaemia, side effects of certain medications, and the depressive phase of bipolar disorder can all look similar. That differential cannot be worked out by self-assessment.

1.1 Depression looks different at different ages

Korea's National Health Information Portal, run by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency, notes that depressive symptoms present differently across age groups. Not knowing this makes it easy to mistake a loved one's depression for a personality issue or simply for ageing.

  • Children and adolescents: irritability and reactivity often dominate rather than visible sadness. Falling grades, school refusal or frequent physical complaints may be the first signs.
  • Adults: alongside classic low mood and loss of interest, cognitive symptoms stand out — an inability to concentrate at work, or paralysis over ordinary decisions.
  • Older adults: many report physical symptoms such as indigestion, pain or insomnia rather than naming sadness. Marked memory complaints sometimes require careful differentiation from dementia.
  • Pregnancy and postpartum: WHO reports that more than 10% of pregnant and postpartum women experience depression. Postnatal depression is not a question of a mother's mindset; it needs its own assessment and support.

2. Symptoms — how depression shows up in mood, thinking and the body

Depression is not confined to mood. The content and speed of thought change, and so do the body's rhythms. That is why the person affected often concludes that they have simply become lazy, while those around them only register that something is different.

2.1 Mood and emotion

  • Sadness, emptiness, or a numbness in which nothing registers at all
  • Loss of interest or pleasure in activities that used to matter (anhedonia)
  • Tears that are hard to explain — or, conversely, an inability to cry at all
  • Irritability and flashes of anger, especially common in adolescents and in men
  • Anxiety and agitation frequently ride alongside the low mood

2.2 Thinking and cognition

  • Impaired concentration and memory, often described as brain fog
  • Difficulty deciding — even trivial choices get postponed indefinitely
  • Excessive guilt and worthlessness, and unusually harsh self-judgment
  • Hopelessness about the future, and the loss of any felt sense that things could improve
  • Recurrent thoughts of death or self-harm — if this is present, do not weigh up other criteria; seek help immediately

2.3 Body and behaviour

  • Insomnia, classically early-morning waking without being able to fall back asleep, or hypersomnia
  • Loss of appetite and weight, or the opposite pattern of overeating and weight gain
  • Persistent fatigue and real difficulty getting the body upright in the morning
  • Psychomotor slowing of speech and movement, or restless agitation
  • Headaches, indigestion and muscle pain that investigations fail to explain
  • Basic maintenance — washing, housework, replying to messages — quietly stops
  • Increased use of alcohol or other substances, which blunts feeling briefly but worsens depression
The inertia of depression is less a matter of not wanting to act than of the energy required to begin simply not arriving. Understanding that distinction alone substantially reduces both self-blame and the misreading of others.

3. How diagnosis works — two weeks and nine symptoms

Diagnosing major depressive disorder is not an impression formed on the spot; it rests on internationally agreed criteria. The American Psychiatric Association's DSM-5-TR and WHO's ICD-11 (single episode depressive disorder 6A70, recurrent depressive disorder 6A71) differ in wording but share almost the same skeleton.

3.1 The structure of the criteria

The core structure runs as follows. Five or more of the nine symptoms below must be present nearly every day for at least two weeks; at least one of them must be either depressed mood or loss of interest and pleasure; and there must be a change from previous functioning together with clinically significant impairment in social, occupational or other important areas.

# Domain Description
1Core symptom ADepressed mood most of the day
2Core symptom BMarkedly diminished interest or pleasure in almost all activities
3Appetite and weightUnintended weight loss or gain, or a marked change in appetite
4SleepInsomnia or hypersomnia
5PsychomotorAgitation or retardation observable by others
6EnergyFatigue or loss of energy
7Self-perceptionFeelings of worthlessness or excessive, inappropriate guilt
8CognitionDiminished ability to think or concentrate; indecisiveness
9Thoughts of deathRecurrent thoughts of death or suicidal ideation

The crucial point is that the criteria stop meaning anything the moment you tick them off alone and draw your own conclusion. Clinicians do not simply count symptoms. They also establish whether there has ever been a manic or hypomanic episode — if so, the whole approach shifts toward bipolar disorder — whether a substance or another medical condition explains the picture, and how the presentation differs from a bereavement reaction.

3.2 The PHQ-9 and CES-D screen; they do not diagnose

The easiest thing to find online is a self-report questionnaire, most often the nine-item PHQ-9 or the CES-D. It is essential to understand exactly what these instruments are for.

  • Purpose: screening — flagging people who warrant further assessment. They are not instruments for assigning a diagnosis.
  • Korean guidance: the National Health Information Portal suggests professional consultation at a PHQ-9 total of 9 or above or a CES-D total of 16 or above.
  • International literature: a PHQ-9 cut-off of 10 is widely used; a meta-analysis reported pooled sensitivity of roughly 0.78 and specificity of roughly 0.87 at that threshold. In other words, both missed cases and false positives exist.
  • How to read a score: a high score is not a diagnosis, and a low one is not reassurance. If the item on thoughts of death applies to you, seek help immediately regardless of the total.
  • Where they genuinely shine: tracking progress during treatment. Repeating the same instrument at four-week intervals makes improvement visible instead of guessed at.

4. Signs that mean you should not postpone an appointment

Wondering whether things are bad enough to justify a visit is among the most common questions clinicians hear. The threshold is simpler than people assume: if daily life is coming apart, that is reason enough. If any of the following applies, there is no case for waiting.

  • Depressed mood or loss of interest has continued nearly every day for two weeks or more
  • Basic functioning — work, school, housework, personal hygiene — is genuinely no longer being maintained
  • Waking in the early hours unable to fall back asleep has repeated for weeks
  • Weight has changed noticeably without any intention to change it
  • Mistakes at work and lapses in concentration have increased visibly
  • Attempts to suppress the feeling with alcohol, substances or overeating are increasing
  • People close to you keep saying that you do not seem like yourself
  • Low mood after childbirth has lasted beyond two weeks, or caring for the baby feels overwhelming
  • Thoughts of death or self-harm are present — in that case, do not weigh duration or severity; seek help now
If you feel at risk right now: in the United States, call or text 988. Anywhere else, findahelpline.com and the IASP crisis centre directory list verified services by country. In South Korea, the domestic lines are 109 and 1577-0199, both 24 hours. If life is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number, and stay with someone you trust if you can.

5. What actually happens at a first appointment

A large share of the reluctance to book comes from not knowing what will happen. Knowing in advance lowers the barrier. A first psychiatric appointment generally follows this shape.

5.1 The flow of the visit

  1. Registration and pre-visit questionnaires: you will often complete a symptom measure such as the PHQ-9 while waiting. Answering honestly is the most efficient use of a short consultation.
  2. The interview: when it started, in what circumstances, and which symptoms are present. Sleep, appetite, energy and concentration, past medical history, current medications, alcohol and caffeine, family history and recent stressors are all standard topics.
  3. Ruling things out: blood tests may be suggested if thyroid disease, anaemia or another physical cause is plausible. Any history of manic or hypomanic episodes is checked carefully.
  4. Psychological testing: if indicated, a fuller battery is usually scheduled separately rather than done on the first day.
  5. Explanation and plan: you should leave with an account of your current state, the treatment options available, and a follow-up appointment.
  6. Prescription, where relevant: if medication is indicated, you should also be told when to expect an effect, which side effects are common, and what to watch for before the next visit.

5.2 Preparation that makes the first visit easier

  • Bring notes on your symptoms: when they began, which parts of the day are worst, and any changes in sleep, eating or weight. This raises the density of a short interview dramatically.
  • Photograph the medications you take: including over-the-counter products and supplements, so interactions can be checked.
  • Write down three questions in advance: for instance, how the clinician reads your current state, which treatments are available to you, and what to do between now and the next appointment.
  • Ask someone to come with you if going alone feels impossible: a companion can fill in changes you have not noticed yourself.
  • If cost or records worry you, ask about that too: asking questions is a normal part of the consultation, not an imposition.

5.3 Two worries that come up constantly

Will a record follow me around? Medical records are protected; in Korea, for example, medical law bars third parties from accessing them without the patient's consent. Disclosure obligations when applying for insurance are a separate matter, and what must be declared varies by insurer and product — so it is far more accurate to ask the insurer directly than to trust categorical claims found online. What is certain is that the losses from leaving depression untreated are far larger than the paperwork worries that delay care.

Will they just put me on tablets? Not every case of depression begins with medication. WHO presents psychological treatments as first-line care, and Korea's National Mental Health Information Portal likewise notes that medication alone is uncommon: it is often combined with psychotherapy, and some people are treated with psychotherapy or cognitive behavioural therapy without medication at all. The direction of treatment is decided with the clinician according to severity and individual circumstances.

6. Treatment options — psychotherapy, medication and beyond

There is no single correct treatment for depression. It is a matter of combining options according to severity, previous response, coexisting conditions and personal preference. The table below summarises the character of each option and the time frames generally described for it. Actual timelines vary widely between individuals.

Option What it involves Typical time frame described When it is usually considered
Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) A structured therapy identifying, testing and changing the negative thought patterns and avoidance that maintain depression Weekly sessions over a period of months is typical Mild to moderate depression; where relapse prevention is also a goal
Interpersonal therapy (IPT) Focuses on the relational context — loss, role transitions, interpersonal disputes Often delivered in a structured short-term format Where relationship change or loss is closely tied to onset
Behavioural activation Breaks the cycle of reduced activity feeding worsening mood through planned activity Some people notice change relatively early in treatment Where inertia and withdrawal dominate the picture
Medication Antidepressant classes such as SSRIs, SNRIs and NaSSAs, selected to fit the presentation NIMH: usually 4 to 8 weeks to take effect Moderate or severe episodes, marked impairment, recurrent illness
Combined treatment Psychotherapy and medication delivered together The two schedules run in parallel Where a single modality has not produced sufficient response
Brain stimulation (ECT, rTMS) Electroconvulsive therapy, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation and related approaches Delivered as a defined course of sessions Treatment-resistant or severe illness; where rapid improvement is needed

Korea's National Mental Health Information Portal puts the treatment response rate for major depressive disorder at around 50 to 70%, and describes treatment in stages: an acute phase of 2 to 3 months, a continuation phase of 4 to 6 months and a maintenance phase of 6 to 24 months. That means this is not a matter of a few weeks — and equally, it does not mean a life sentence.

6.1 Options when depression proves treatment-resistant

Depression that fails to respond adequately after two or more adequately dosed and adequately long treatment attempts is commonly described as treatment-resistant. The first step is to revisit the diagnosis: checking for bipolarity, physical conditions such as thyroid disease, and coexisting anxiety or substance use. Switching or combining medications, intensifying psychotherapy and brain stimulation approaches are then considered. NIMH notes that esketamine, used in treatment-resistant depression, can act within a couple of hours, unlike conventional antidepressants — but this is administered in a controlled clinical setting under strict indications.

One thing must be stated plainly here: this article does not recommend any specific drug, clinic or clinician. Drug classes are named only to explain that options exist. What is actually prescribed can only be decided through consultation.

6.2 Lifestyle measures are adjuncts, not treatment

Exercise, sleep, daylight and social contact are frequently cited as helpful in managing depression, and WHO lists physical inactivity and harmful alcohol use among risk factors. But these do not replace treatment. In moderate or severe depression, telling someone that exercise will fix it is not encouragement; it hands a person who lacks the energy to start exercising one more experience of failure. The realistic order is the reverse: increase activity once treatment has restored a little energy.

7. What you must know about antidepressant treatment

Misconceptions about antidepressants are the single most common reason treatment is abandoned. If you are prescribed one, these four points are essential.

7.1 The effect is not immediate

NIMH states that antidepressants usually take 4 to 8 weeks to work, and that sleep and appetite often improve before mood does. Not knowing that sequence is how people conclude after two weeks that the medication is useless because they are sleeping better yet still feel low — and stop on their own. Judging efficacy takes time, and that judgment is made together with the clinician.

7.2 The warning about heightened risk early in treatment

The US Food and Drug Administration warns that people under 25 may experience an increase in suicidal thoughts or behaviour on antidepressants, particularly during the first few weeks. This is not a reason to avoid medication; it is a reason for close monitoring and frequent appointments early on. If an adolescent or young adult is starting an antidepressant, family members should watch for changes alongside them and contact the clinical team immediately — without waiting for the scheduled appointment — if things worsen.

7.3 Stopping on your own is dangerous — discontinuation syndrome

Stopping medication because you feel better is common and risky. The Korean literature on antidepressant discontinuation syndrome, including a review in the Journal of Korean Neuropsychiatric Association, describes a range of physical and psychological reactions when a long-standing antidepressant is reduced or stopped abruptly. Commonly reported features include the following.

  • Dizziness and disturbances of balance
  • Flu-like aching and fatigue
  • Nausea and other gastrointestinal discomfort
  • Sleep disturbance, including insomnia and vivid dreams
  • Unusual sensory sensations, sometimes described as brief electrical shocks
  • Emotional symptoms such as anxiety and irritability

These typically begin within days of stopping and settle over a relatively short period, though they can last longer in some people. Prevention rests on gradual, clinician-planned tapering and adequate advance explanation. A further complication is that discontinuation symptoms can be mistaken for relapse. Reading symptoms that followed a self-directed stop as proof that recovery is impossible corrodes trust in treatment as a whole. Any reduction or discontinuation should be planned in discussion with the prescribing clinician.

7.4 Side effects are to be managed, not endured in silence

  • Early nausea, headache and drowsiness often diminish with time — but report them at your appointment rather than quietly enduring them.
  • Sexual side effects, weight change and sedation vary by drug class and individual, so there is room for adjustment.
  • Interactions with other medicines and supplements are possible; disclose everything you take.
  • Alcohol can reduce treatment effectiveness and increase side-effect risk, so discuss it with your clinician.
  • New severe agitation, impulsivity or an increase in suicidal thoughts must be reported immediately.

8. Common misconceptions during recovery

What usually slows recovery is not the symptoms themselves but the way they are interpreted. The following misconceptions are corrected repeatedly in clinics and in public health information.

  • You should be able to beat it with willpower. Depression affects mood, sleep, appetite and cognition together. The moment it is framed as a matter of will, the single most effective option — treatment — is erased from the list. This framing does not help.
  • Once you start medication you can never stop. Treatment moves through acute, continuation and maintenance phases, and tapering is planned with a clinician once the maintenance period is complete. The problem is not planned tapering but unplanned, unilateral stopping.
  • I feel better, so I can stop now. Symptom remission is not a finish line. Korea's National Mental Health Information Portal notes that 6 to 9 months of maintenance treatment after improvement matters for preventing relapse.
  • Recovery means feeling a little better every day. Recovery is not linear. Waves of improvement and setback are ordinary, and one bad day does not mean relapse.
  • You have to find the cause before you can get better. Episodes frequently arise without any clear trigger. Delaying treatment while hunting for a cause is the greater loss.
  • Psychiatric medication makes you numb. Emotional blunting is a reported side effect, but there is room to respond by adjusting class or dose. It should be raised at an appointment rather than tolerated.
  • Counselling is enough; I do not need a doctor. Counselling and medical assessment serve different roles. Excluding physical illness, checking for bipolarity and judging whether medication is needed happen in a medical setting.
  • Being admitted to hospital is the worst-case scenario. Inpatient care is one treatment option chosen when safety or intensive treatment requires it. It is not evidence of failure.

9. What to say — and what not to say — to someone with depression

The reactions of people close by have a real influence on whether treatment begins. NIMH describes the role of family and friends as encouraging the person to seek professional help, offering support, understanding, patience and encouragement, keeping them socially connected, and helping with treatment adherence and getting to appointments. The table below translates those principles into actual conversation.

Situation Helpful words and actions What to avoid
When they first tell you Thank you for telling me. What is hardest right now? — listening without judging Interrogating the cause, or reacting with alarm and changing the subject
When they seem unable to move Shall we walk for just ten minutes together today? — a small, concrete offer You need to get moving, or exercise will sort this out
When they blame themselves That may be the illness talking rather than the truth about you — separating symptom from person It is all in how you think about it, or it depends on your attitude
When they hesitate about seeing a doctor Shall we look for an appointment together? I will come with you the first time — lowering the threshold Unverified warnings that seeing a psychiatrist will leave a damaging permanent record
When comparisons come up What you are carrying is genuinely hard — acknowledging their experience Other people have it worse, or I went through worse than this
When they mention wanting to die Take it seriously, stay with them, and help them contact a crisis line together Shutting the conversation down, or promising to keep it secret
When recovery is slow There is no rush. I am still here — being a predictable presence How long is this going to go on? or you should be over this by now

9.1 Carers burn out too

  • Do not take on the therapist's role. The job of someone close by is connection and company, not treatment.
  • Do not carry it alone. Where possible, share the load across several family members and friends.
  • Set boundaries. Remaining on call around the clock is how the supporter collapses first.
  • Accepting help is also a strategy. Community mental health services in many countries offer counselling and education for families.
  • Safety concerns are the exception. When there is risk to life, safety outweighs confidentiality and professional services must be involved.

10. Adjustments at work and school, and where to find support

What matters as much as treatment is having the conditions to receive it. Sustaining a weekly appointment while working twelve-hour days is not realistic. Requesting adjustments is not asking for a favour; it is a practical measure that raises the odds of recovery.

10.1 Adjustments worth requesting at work

  • Protected time for appointments: shifting hours or taking half-days around a regular clinic slot
  • Workload and deadline adjustment: temporary changes early in recovery. Requests with a defined time limit are accepted far more readily than open-ended ones
  • Night and shift work adjustment: recovery slows badly once the sleep cycle is broken
  • Leave and sick leave: entitlements vary enormously by country and employer. In Korea, for instance, labour law contains no statutory paid sick leave for general illness, so availability depends on company rules and collective agreements — check the staff handbook first
  • Income support: some jurisdictions provide sickness benefit for time off due to non-occupational illness. In Korea this exists only as a regional pilot programme, not a nationwide entitlement, and its eligibility rules and amounts change — verify current terms with the responsible agency
  • You decide how much to disclose: there is no obligation to reveal a diagnosis. Framing the conversation around the adjustments you need is a legitimate approach

10.2 Adjustments at school and university

  • Speak first to a homeroom teacher, school counsellor or university student counselling service and ask what the adjustment process is
  • Deadline extensions and alternative examination arrangements vary in scope according to institutional policy
  • Rules on medical leave of absence and tuition treatment differ by institution, so check the academic regulations
  • In Korea, young people can reach counselling and local referrals through the youth helpline 1388

11. Preventing relapse and managing for the long term

Relapse in depression is not the exception. It is therefore more realistic to define recovery not as the absence of symptoms but as having a working system for responding to relapse.

11.1 Complete the maintenance phase

As noted above, treatment follows an acute phase of 2 to 3 months, a continuation phase of 4 to 6 months and a maintenance phase of 6 to 24 months, with 6 to 9 months of maintenance after improvement described as important for preventing relapse. The most common route back into an episode is feeling better and quietly stopping. The end point must be decided together with your clinician.

11.2 Write down your own early warning signs

Relapse rarely arrives without warning. Most people have a recurring prodrome. Writing it down while well means that when judgment is clouded, the document can judge for you.

  • Three personal early signs, for example: early waking three nights running, starting to dodge messages, finding it hard to wash
  • What to do when they appear: bring the appointment forward, tell a specific person, prioritise restoring sleep
  • Two people to contact, with their numbers, plus your local crisis line
  • What to avoid: drinking more, staying up all night, making major decisions during this window
  • Share the document with one family member or friend

11.3 Managing daily rhythm — as an adjunct

  • Fix the wake time: anchoring the time you get up is easier to execute than fixing bedtime
  • Set a minimum unit of activity: not an hour of exercise but five minutes outside the front door — a size that is hard to fail at
  • Limit alcohol: WHO lists harmful alcohol use among risk factors. Alcohol dulls feeling briefly and worsens depression
  • Keep a minimum of social contact: it need not be a deep conversation. Preserve one recurring point of contact
  • Postpone big decisions: resigning, moving or ending relationships are safer left until the picture is stable
  • Keep simple records: a daily mood score and sleep duration make useful material for appointments

12. Frequently asked questions

Q. How do I tell ordinary sadness from depression?

Look at duration, breadth and functional impairment. Sadness usually fades within two weeks, still allows some pleasure in things you enjoy, and leaves daily life intact. Major depressive disorder involves depressed mood or loss of interest persisting for two weeks or more, most of the day and nearly every day, dragging sleep, appetite, energy and concentration down with it and clearly impairing function. This comparison is a guide only; the actual determination comes from a clinical interview with a qualified professional.

Q. Can a self-test such as the PHQ-9 diagnose me?

No. The PHQ-9 and CES-D are screening instruments, not diagnostic ones. Korea's National Health Information Portal suggests professional consultation at a PHQ-9 total of 9 or above or a CES-D total of 16 or above; international literature commonly uses a PHQ-9 cut-off of 10, where a meta-analysis reported sensitivity of about 0.78 and specificity of about 0.87. A high score is not a diagnosis and a low one is not reassurance. If the item on thoughts of death applies to you, seek help immediately regardless of the total.

Q. When do antidepressants work, and can I stop them myself?

NIMH states they usually take 4 to 8 weeks, with sleep and appetite often improving before mood. Stopping on your own is dangerous. Abrupt discontinuation can cause discontinuation syndrome — dizziness, flu-like symptoms, gastrointestinal upset, sleep disturbance and unusual sensory sensations. The FDA also warns of increased suicidal thoughts or behaviour in people under 25 during the first few weeks, so close monitoring matters early. Starting, changing and stopping should all be discussed with the prescribing clinician.

Q. What should I avoid saying to someone with depression?

Avoid anything that reframes the illness as a failure of will — telling them it is all in their attitude, that they lack resolve, or that others have it worse. Such remarks do not help and delay treatment. What helps is listening without judgment, acknowledging their state, offering concrete assistance such as booking an appointment or going along, and staying in touch. If they mention thoughts of death, take it seriously rather than changing the subject, do not leave them alone, and help them reach a crisis line — 988 in the US, 109 in Korea, or a verified service via findahelpline.com.

Q. Does depression relapse, and how do I reduce the risk?

Relapse is common. Korea's National Mental Health Information Portal describes an acute phase of 2 to 3 months, a continuation phase of 4 to 6 months and a maintenance phase of 6 to 24 months, noting that 6 to 9 months of maintenance treatment after improvement is important for prevention. Stopping medication or appointments once symptoms lift is a frequent route back. Regular sleep and activity rhythms, limiting alcohol, a written plan of personal early warning signs, and rebooking promptly when those signs appear all help.

Conclusion: treatment is a procedure, not a test of character

The most persistent bad frame around depression is the language of overcoming it. That framing converts treatment into a moral task and makes asking for help feel like defeat. What is actually required is not a contest but a procedure: knowing the two-week threshold, watching for functional impairment, booking an appointment, completing the phases of treatment, and writing down relapse signals in advance.

Two facts recur throughout this guide. First, depression is extremely common — WHO puts it at 5.7% of adults worldwide, roughly 332 million people, and Korea's treated population alone has passed a million. Second, the proportion reaching treatment remains low; WHO notes that even in high-income countries only about one third receive care. Much of that gap comes not from the severity of the illness but from stigma and missing information.

One final point. If you are having thoughts of suicide right now, do not try to test that thought alone. It may be a product of the state you are in, and that state can change with treatment. Crisis lines are open around the clock — 988 in the United States, 109 in South Korea, and a verified service in your own country via findahelpline.com.
