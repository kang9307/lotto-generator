Why the Pelican Test Worked and Why It Is Done - LLM Evaluation Moves to Long-Horizon Work

How to grade an artifact that pours out 5,500 lines over two hours, plus eight design principles for building an evaluation that fits your own work

Aug 3, 2026 IT/Tech

Introduction: What do you make a new model do first?

When a new model drops, almost nobody opens a leaderboard first. Most people throw their one familiar prompt at it and read the vibe. The most famous of those one-liners became draw me an SVG of a pelican riding a bicycle.

This test is strange in several ways. There is no paper behind it, no documented rubric, and the person who created it never claimed it was a serious benchmark. And yet, from late 2024 through the first half of 2026, that single line told you more about the gap between model generations — faster and more honestly — than formal evaluation suites with dozens of papers attached. That was not luck. There was a structure to it.

That structure is now collapsing. The usual explanation is that the test got memorized, but someone actually measured that suspicion and the data does not support the popular story. The real reason lies elsewhere, and that reason determines what evaluation looks like next.

There are four things to take from this piece.

  • Five structural reasons a meme outlasted formal benchmarks — this is a design you can copy
  • What happened when the contamination suspicion was actually measured — no significant boost showed up, and the test is still finished
  • The four things long-horizon agent evaluation demands — automated grading, partial credit, stopping rules, cost
  • Eight design principles for building your own evaluation — twenty items is enough to start

1. Why one pelican beat the formal benchmarks

It starts with Simon Willison's October 2024 blog post. He gave two reasons for the choice. One was that he likes pelicans. The other was that SVG files of a pelican riding a bicycle were not floating around the web yet, so they were unlikely to have been sucked into training data. The first is a joke. The second is the design decision that held the whole thing up.

1.1 It avoided contamination by design rather than by detection

Formal benchmarks fight contamination after the fact. You release a problem set, then statistically estimate whether those problems ended up in training data, strip the contaminated items, and ship a new version. That loop is always one beat late.

The pelican test went the other way. It picked a combination for which no correct file exists in the world. With no answer to retrieve, the model has to synthesize on the spot. The vector format helped too. SVG is declarative markup, not pixels, so the model has to write coordinates and curves as text, and any copying is visible at the source level.

1.2 It needed no grader

In most evaluations the expensive part is not the items, it is the grading. Humans are slow and costly; model judges introduce systematic error such as position bias, which one study examined head-on across 15 judges, 22 tasks, and roughly 150,000 evaluation instances.

The pelican test has none of that cost. Render it in a browser and a human decides in three seconds. Three beaks is a fail. A wheel passing through the body is a fail. A test that needs no grading infrastructure can be run by anyone, and a test anyone can run accumulates results fast.

1.3 Four capabilities stacked into one line

It looks like drawing. What it actually demands is layered.

  • Spatial reasoning — placing beak, neck, wings, wheels, and frame in correct relative positions on a coordinate plane
  • Code generation — turning that judgment into syntactically valid markup. One wrong character and the render breaks
  • Composing a physically impossible scene — a pelican's anatomy and a bicycle's controls do not mesh. The model has to reconcile a combination absent from training data
  • Internally simulating the rendered result — the model cannot see its own SVG. It writes text while predicting the resulting image in its head

That last one matters most. The real reason this test was brutal is that the model had no feedback loop. And the direction evaluation is moving right now is precisely toward giving that loop back.

Five reasons it worked: (1) no answer file, so contamination is avoided by construction (2) zero grading cost (3) four capabilities demanded simultaneously (4) visual output, so results spread easily (5) a single prompt you can throw at any model unchanged. What knowledge benchmarks and behavior benchmarks each miss is covered separately in the blind spots of LLM evaluation.

2. But it is memorized now, right? Someone measured it

Once a test gets famous, the follow-up is predictable: the labs must have tuned for this prompt. That suspicion was actually measured.

The method is simple and clever. Looking at pelicans alone cannot separate skill from favoritism, so the study built 48 combinations by crossing 8 animals with 6 vehicles, ran them across seven frontier models, and gathered 1,008 images. It then controlled for the intrinsic difficulty of each combination using fixed effects and regressed to see whether any model spiked specifically on pelicans, or specifically on the pelican-bicycle cell.

  • No lab showed a statistically significant boost on pelicans or on the pelican-bicycle cell
  • The largest observed effect for any model failed to clear the significance threshold
  • Pelicans ranked 6th out of 8 animals. Models draw them worse than cats, whales, and raccoons
  • Bicycles ranked second worst out of 6 vehicles
  • The pelican-bicycle combination ranked 42nd out of 48, still on the hard end

One oddity survives. All 21 pelican-bicycle images faced right — the only cell in the grid with unanimous orientation. But rightward facing was already the majority across the whole dataset, so it is thin as evidence. The author's own conclusion was that there is little evidence of pelicanmaxxing.

Read that result in two directions. One is reassuring: at least for this meme, the familiar narrative of leaderboard-driven optimization is not backed by the data. The other is uncomfortable. If contamination is not the cause, the test is losing power for some other reason. The general mechanism by which a metric decays once it becomes a target is covered in why companies build their own coding benchmarks, so it is not repeated here.

3. The real reason is resolution, not contamination

A good test spreads candidates across score bands. A test where everyone scores 90 is not badly graded. It is over.

Top models now generally produce a recognizable pelican and a recognizable bicycle. Past that point, the remaining differences are line smoothness, color harmony, naturalness of posture — the domain of taste. Three things happen at once.

  1. Rankings flip with the grader. Two people order the same pair in opposite directions. The problem of splitting subjective quality across multiple lenses hit speech synthesis first, and the local TTS benchmark is that case
  2. Scores remain, information leaves. When the difficulty band sits below the ability band, numbers keep coming out but you cannot decide anything with them
  3. The measured thing drifts away from the work. What matters in production today is not whether one call produces something pretty, but whether a system can use tools, observe its own failures, revise its plan, and hold coherence for hours

The third is decisive. A one-shot artifact test cannot touch that by construction. Inside a format of one prompt and one response, there is no room for plan revision, no room for self-verification, no room for recovery from a mid-run failure.

4. What the new shape looks like: 1M tokens, two hours, 5,500 lines

A post by Andrej Karpathy in August 2026 made the shift visible. The gist is that we are starting to leave the territory where you test an LLM by asking for an SVG of a pelican on a bicycle, and the alternative he floated looks like this.

  • Input: the first paragraph of The Lord of the Rings
  • Ask: render it with three.js
  • Budget: 1 million tokens, roughly ten dollars
  • Result: about two hours of generation producing 5,500 lines of code that procedurally renders the story

His own verdict was that it is kind of janky but fun. What he flagged as remarkable is the right thing to flag: the model had to place polygon assets at (x, y, z) coordinates and orchestrate the code that animates all of it. Producing bespoke content that would be impractical for a human to hand-build, for about ten dollars, is the point of the demonstration.

The limits surfaced at the same time. Per the post, the model could not run and iterate on what it had generated. Weak video recognition pushed it toward screenshots, which left errors and unfinished polish. The missing feedback loop that made the pelican test hard in section 1.3 is still there — just scaled up a thousandfold.

What an eval designer should take from this demo. The interesting part is not the output. It is that this task is close to ungradeable. There is no reference frame, no success criterion, and no agreed answer to who looks at the resulting 5,500 lines and by what standard. That is the subject of everything below.

5. One-shot artifacts versus long-horizon work: what changes for evaluation

The two formats do not merely differ in difficulty. The entire measuring apparatus is different.

AxisOne-shot artifact (pelican type)Long-horizon work (agent type)
Unit of execution1 callHundreds to thousands of steps, tool calls included
ArtifactA single fileCodebase plus execution trajectory plus intermediate state
GradingThree seconds of a human eyeAutomation required. Humans sample only
Score shapeEffectively a ranking or impressionWithout partial credit the signal dies
Failure visibilityOnly the result is visibleYou can see which step broke and why
ReproducibilityFix the temperature and you are doneTools, network, clock, and retries all need pinning
Cost per runNear zeroDollars per run, escalating fast with repetition
Contamination exposureShort prompt, leaks easilyToo large to memorize whole. But environment leakage is a risk
Discriminating rangeUp to mid tier. Collapses at the topVariance stays wide even at the top

The contamination row is the one people misread. Long tasks are hard to memorize wholesale, but a different hole opens: the agent reaching the answer file or the test code itself. Design the environment badly and the model reads the answer key instead of solving the problem.

6. Four things you need to grade long-horizon work

6.1 Automated grading, because the volume is not human-readable

A single run leaves thousands of lines and hundreds of thousands of tokens. Twenty tasks run three times each leave sixty trajectories. Reading all of it was never on the table.

So the starting point is committing executable pass criteria as code alongside the task. FreshBrew pushes this approach in the code migration space. It targets 228 repositories, asks for migration to JDK 17, and takes the verdict from builds and tests as machine judgments rather than human impressions. Leaving no room for taste is the whole of that design.

6.2 Partial credit, or how to split the space between 0 and 1

If a run that completed nine of ten steps and a run that stalled at step one both score zero, the improvement signal disappears entirely. But the moment you introduce partial credit, a new problem opens: what counts as a step?

  • Checkpoints — split the task in advance and count gates passed. Easy to build, but the model can aim at gates instead of the goal
  • Distance-based — measure the gap between goal state and current state. For code, that has to be structural, like syntax-tree-level differencing, not surface strings
  • Rubrics — write an itemized scoring sheet and have a model judge apply it. Flexible, but judge bias comes in with it

These are not exclusive. The realistic combination is to carve off everything machine-decidable with machines and hand only the residual taste to a rubric. The harder question of turning senior-level engineering judgment into a score is covered in evaluating senior-grade agents.

6.3 Stopping rules, or when do you cut it off

This is the item most often omitted in long-horizon evaluation. Without a budget ceiling and termination conditions, the same task scores differently every run and comparison stops being meaningful.

You need more than a ceiling. You need to detect the state where the model is already broken but still spinning. Vending-Bench observed exactly that failure class. In this evaluation, which has models run a vending machine business over a long stretch, single runs exceeded 20 million tokens, and models misread delivery schedules, forgot orders they had placed, and fell into meltdown loops they almost never recovered from.

One observation there matters a great deal: there was no clear correlation between when failures occurred and when the context window filled. This is not a problem you fix by extending context. The point that long-context performance and long-horizon coherence are separate also shows up in RULER, which argues that simple retrieval tests reveal only a superficial form of long-context understanding.

6.4 Cost, the number that must sit next to the score

One-shot tests let you skip cost because a run was worth pennies. Long-horizon evaluation is different. A score without tokens, time, and retry counts cannot be compared to any other score. That is why the one million tokens and roughly ten dollars in the demo above are part of the result, not trivia around it.

In practical terms, a long-horizon evaluation with no fixed budget ceiling becomes a contest that whoever spent more money wins. Pin at least one of the three axes: token ceiling, wall-clock ceiling, or attempt ceiling.

7. The walls found by the people who got there first

Long-horizon evaluation is not new ground. Several years of trial and error are already stacked up. Three results side by side show the terrain.

EffortWhat it tried to measureWall it hit
Time horizon measurement (METR)How many human-minutes long a task is that a model completes at a 50% success rateThe authors themselves flag external validity. Do the test tasks represent real work?
Vending-BenchLong-term coherence at a 20-million-token scaleMeltdown loops, occurring independently of context window saturation
Agentic benchmark validity checks (ABC)Whether the benchmark itself measures what it claims10 task validity criteria, 20 outcome validity criteria. Real defects found in existing benchmarks

First, the time horizon work proposed a unit called the 50% task-completion time horizon: express capability as how many minutes a human would need for a task the model completes half the time. Its central claim is that this horizon has roughly doubled every seven months since 2019, with reliability, mistake adaptation, logical reasoning, and tool use cited as the drivers. But the authors themselves record external validity as a limitation. How well the task suite represents real work is a separate question.

Second, the meltdown loops from the previous section. The observation that longer runs get harder for reasons unrelated to memory capacity has to feed straight back into eval design. Do not use context window size as a predictor of performance.

Third, and this is the chilling one. The agentic benchmark checklist catalogs what benchmark builders get wrong. It splits into 10 task validity criteria (is success really possible only through the target capability?) and 20 outcome validity criteria (does a success verdict really equal success?). It then found defects in benchmarks in wide use: cases where test coverage was insufficient so passing did not equal correctness, and cases where empty responses were counted as successes. Such defects, it notes, can over- or underestimate performance by up to 100% in relative terms.

The message is plain. A long-horizon evaluation is likely to be wrong the moment you build it. Why after-the-fact re-verification is a mandatory step, not an optional one, is shown concretely in the medical tool reproducibility case.

8. Why worlds and games are especially hard

A task like render one paragraph of The Lord of the Rings in 3D looks like a coding task, but from a grading standpoint it is a different species. There is no reference frame.

The game content generation field has wrestled with this for a long time. The Procedural Content Generation Benchmark bundles 12 game-related problems, from generating levels of various kinds to generating rule sets for simple arcade games, each with its own content representation, control parameters, and evaluation metrics. It scores on three axes.

  • Quality — does the artifact hold up functionally? Is it playable? Are there unreachable regions?
  • Diversity — do repeated runs produce genuinely different results? Making one good thing is not procedural generation
  • Controllability — can you steer the output toward the conditions you asked for?

These three pull against each other. Push quality alone and diversity dies; push diversity alone and quality collapses. That is exactly why an evaluation of LLM world-building must not resolve to a single does-it-look-plausible verdict. One impressive result can appear with controllability at zero.

There is also the verification problem. In the demo, the model could not play what it built and leaned on screenshots — which, in evaluation terms, means the ability to generate and the ability to verify what was generated are still separate. If you design an evaluation, measure those two apart. Measure them together and you will never learn which one is the bottleneck.

9. Building an evaluation for your own work: eight design principles

From here the subject is not leaderboards but evaluations your team uses to decide things. Public benchmarks do not represent your codebase, your domain documents, or your users anyway.

  1. Start with tasks that can be judged automatically. Work with a taste-free signal — build passes, tests green, schema validation clears — comes first. Taste-dependent tasks get bolted on later
  2. Take items from work you actually repeat. Invented puzzles are fun and irrelevant. Last month's ticket queue is the best item bank you have
  3. Twenty items is enough to start. Stalling the whole effort while trying to reach a hundred is far worse
  4. Rotate a few items every quarter. A frozen item bank always goes stale. Internal evaluations are no exception
  5. Store trajectories, not artifacts. Keep only the score and you will never learn why a run failed. Save tool call logs and intermediate state whole
  6. Always fix a budget ceiling. Tokens, time, or attempts — at least one. With no ceiling it is a spending leaderboard, not a score
  7. If you use a model judge, install bias correction first. Randomize presentation order and ask twice with the order swapped to see whether the verdict flips. A win rate produced without that procedure is not worth reading
  8. Do not delete a saturated test. Demote it. Pull items that lost discriminating power off the ranking and keep them as regression checks. They still tell you when something that used to work broke

The eighth is the one teams miss most. A test that lost discriminating power is not the same as a useless test. An item every top model passes cannot rank anything, but the day a model fails it, that is the signal.

The narrower your domain, the more your own evaluation is worth. Why tasks bound to a specific language and format, like Korean public documents, need a dedicated benchmark is covered in the KOLongDoc case.

10. Where the pelican still earns its keep

Saying it is done does not mean discard it. It means change its job.

  • As a regression check. For confirming in five seconds whether a new release broke something that used to work, it is still the best there is
  • For comparing small models. Saturation happened at the top. Down in the lightweight tier the test still discriminates
  • As a design lesson. The idea of picking a combination with no answer file transfers directly to your own domain. Listing the tasks in your work for which the web holds no answer is worth far more than reading public benchmark scores
  • As a communication tool. When explaining model differences to a non-technical decision maker, two pictures beat ten tables

What not to do is equally clear. Do not pick a model based on this result. What it measures is spatial reasoning and code generation within a single call, not reliability inside your pipeline. How the unit a headline number is divided by completely changes the impression it leaves is covered in the piece taking apart the Chrome security bug announcement.

11. What could not be confirmed

Everything this piece could not pin down, in one place. The following are not settled within the range of public material.

  • The demo's execution log. Two hours, 5,500 lines, one million tokens, roughly ten dollars — these are the poster's own statements. No third-party reproduction from a public repository of the code and trajectory was confirmed
  • The basis of the ten dollar figure. Which price list it reflects, and whether cache hits and retries are included, is not distinguished
  • Each lab's internal private evaluations. There is no external way to see what they run and against what criteria. The contamination conclusion here rests on a single study of public outputs and is not a statement about internal training data
  • Grading procedure details in the pelican study. That a model judge was used is public, but how far judge bias correction was applied does not go beyond what that write-up describes
  • The latest value of the time horizon. The doubling estimate rests on data as of publication. Whether the same slope holds in later generations needs separate confirmation

Conclusion: where evaluation has moved

The pelican test working was not luck. It picked a task with no answer, drove grading cost to zero, and stacked several capabilities into one line. While formal benchmarks went through committees and papers, one line written by one person worked more honestly. That is why.

Why it is ending is equally clear, and it is not contamination. The ability band of top models has risen past the difficulty band of this test, and the industry's attention has moved from a single artifact to a two-hour trajectory.

The new shape is much harder. No reference frame, no settled way to split partial credit, no obvious stopping rule, and real money per run. It is already documented that even the people building benchmarks actually get task validity and outcome validity wrong.

So the work right now is not reading someone else's leaderboard more carefully. It is picking twenty machine-decidable tasks from your own work, fixing a budget ceiling, and starting to store trajectories. The idea behind the pelican — choose a task the world has no answer for — carries over intact. The only thing that changes is that the artifact is no longer one picture. It is a two-hour log.
