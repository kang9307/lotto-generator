Introduction: Retirement Planning and Tax Saving in One Account

Everyone wants to claw back a little of the tax that vanishes from each paycheck. But what if that refund were not a one-off perk, but also a way to build the retirement fund your future self will live on thirty years from now? Pension savings accounts and the IRP (Individual Retirement Pension) bundle exactly those two things - tax saving today and a pension tomorrow - inside a single account. Every filing season the refrain returns: "put money in a pension account and the tax authority gives some back." Yet surprisingly few people understand the mechanism well enough to use it fully.

This article is an informational guide that lays out pension savings and the IRP in one read. It explains what the two accounts actually are, how the deduction limit and rate work, how pension savings and the IRP differ, how you invest inside them with ETFs and funds and what the fees look like, and - most importantly - the tax you pay when you finally draw a pension and the penalty you face if you break the account early. The framing centers on the Korean system, but the idea of a tax-advantaged retirement account is common to many countries, so general readers can still grasp the big picture.

1. What Are Pension Savings and the IRP?

Start with the names. What people loosely call a "pension account" comes in two main forms: the pension savings account and the IRP (Individual Retirement Pension). Both share a common purpose - they are long-term accounts the government wraps in tax benefits to encourage retirement saving. They cut your tax when you pay in (the deduction), and in exchange the money is meant to be drawn as a pension in old age.

1.1 Pension Savings - Open to Anyone, Flexible

A pension savings account can generally be opened by anyone, regardless of income. A homemaker or a student with no income can hold one, and there are relatively few limits on what you may invest in. A pension savings fund account opened through a brokerage can freely hold funds and ETFs, which suits people who want to manage their own assets. Early withdrawal is also more flexible than with an IRP.

1.2 IRP - Extra Room for Those With Income

The IRP is mainly opened by people with employment or business income. It is used both to roll over a retirement payout and to contribute extra for a larger deduction. The IRP, however, carries a safety-oriented restriction: you cannot put the entire balance into risky assets such as equity funds, and a portion must sit in safer, principal-protected products. Materials from the Financial Services Commission's August 2018 amendment of the Retirement Pension Supervisory Regulations show risky-asset investment capped at 70% of the balance, with target-date funds (TDFs) meeting criteria set by the Governor of the Financial Supervisory Service allowed up to 100% in DC and IRP accounts. In return, it provides additional deduction room on top of the pension savings account.

One-line comparison: pension savings means "free investing and withdrawal," while the IRP means "restrictions in exchange for extra room." The two are best seen not as rivals but as complements you use together.

2. The Tax Deduction - The Biggest Draw

The main reason these accounts are loved is simple: you get tax back just for paying in. More precisely, a fixed percentage of what you contribute is subtracted directly from the tax you owe that year - a tax credit (deduction). Unlike an income deduction (which lowers your taxable base), a tax credit comes straight off the calculated tax, so the effect feels large.

2.1 Contribution Limit vs Deduction Limit

One thing to watch: the "amount you can contribute" and the "amount that earns a deduction" are different. Article 40-2 of the Enforcement Decree of the Income Tax Act (version in force from 1 July 2026) sets the contribution limit for all pension accounts combined at 18 million won a year, and lets amounts such as a matured ISA transfer be paid in on top of that. Only a capped portion earns a credit. According to the National Tax Service, contributions eligible for the credit are capped at 6 million won a year for pension savings and 9 million won a year when retirement pension accounts such as an IRP are included. If an ISA contract ends and you move its balance into a pension account, the credit limit rises by 10% of the transferred amount, up to 3 million won, and this extra room applies only in the year of the transfer.

2.2 A Rate That Depends on Income

The rate has two tiers by income. Per National Tax Service guidance, those with total salary of 55 million won or less (comprehensive income of 45 million won or less) receive 15%, and those above that receive 12%. The often-quoted 16.5% and 13.2% add local income tax on top. Article 103-13 of the Local Tax Act requires 10% of withheld income tax to be collected as personal local income tax at the same time, so when year-end settlement lowers your income tax, the local income tax falls by 10% of that amount as well. In other words, the lower your income, the more you get back on the same contribution.

A "planned" change is not an "enacted" one. The 2026 tax reform plan, announced by the Ministry of Finance and Economy on 3 August 2026 and finalized as the government bill on 1 September, includes a proposal to apply an additional 15% credit rate to IRP contributions by young people aged 15 to 34. It is a government proposal that takes effect only if the National Assembly passes it, and as of 29 September 2026 it is not in force. Its content or timing may change during parliamentary review.

To anchor the idea, here is a simple illustration. The numbers below merely show "how it is calculated"; actual application varies by point in time and individual situation.

3. Pension Savings vs IRP - The Key Differences

Laying the differences side by side makes the choice easier. The crux is eligibility, freedom of investment, early withdrawal, fees, and the deduction limit.

ItemPension savings (fund)IRP

Assets allowedFew limits (free equity weighting)Risky assets up to 70% of the balance (qualifying TDFs up to 100%)
Early withdrawalRelatively flexibleRestricted (strict conditions)

The credit limits were checked against National Tax Service guidance on 29 September 2026 and the risky-asset cap against 2018 Financial Services Commission materials; both change if the rules are amended. In short, pension savings is the natural pick if you want to invest freely and keep flexibility for emergencies, while the IRP is the way to fill the extra deduction room.

4. What You Invest in Inside the Account - Holdings and Fees

A pension account is less a "deposit passbook" and more a "container in which you grow assets." Put in the same 9 million won, and what you hold inside and how you grow it can change the result dramatically thirty years later.

4.1 What You Can Hold - ETFs, Funds, Deposits

Both pension savings fund accounts and IRPs can hold a broad range of assets - from principal-protected deposits to bond funds, equity funds, and exchange-traded funds (ETFs). Low-cost index ETFs in particular are often praised as a good fit for long-term, regular contribution investing. Remember, though, that the IRP caps the share of risky assets, so you cannot fill 100% of it with equity products other than a qualifying TDF.

4.2 Fees - Small-Looking, but They Compound

In long-term investing, fees are never trivial. Even a difference of a fraction of a percent per year, compounded over decades, meaningfully changes the outcome. Brokerage pension savings fund accounts often carry no separate account fee, while an IRP may carry account-management or asset-management fees. Before opening one, make a habit of checking both the account fee and the product's own expense ratio.

Judging the investments themselves (which ETF to buy, and so on) depends heavily on your risk tolerance and goals and is beyond this article. Make investment decisions on your own responsibility, and consult a professional if needed.

5. Tax When You Receive the Pension

This is the part many people miss. If you took a deduction when paying in, it is not a free lunch. Think of it this way: the tax shaved off at contribution is partly settled at withdrawal. But because the rate at withdrawal is designed to be lower than the rate you got back, a net tax saving generally remains.

5.1 Pension-Income Tax - A Low Rate at Withdrawal

To draw it as a pension you must meet the conditions in Article 40-2 of the Income Tax Act Enforcement Decree: apply to start pension payments after age 55, and withdraw only after five years have passed since the account was opened. When credited contributions and their gains are drawn in pension form this way, pension-income tax is withheld. Per National Tax Service guidance, the private pension withholding rates are as follows.

Condition at the time of receiptIncome tax rateFigure often shown with local income tax
Under 705%5.5%
70 to under 804%4.4%
80 or older3%3.3%
Lifetime contract (paid until death, cannot be cancelled)3%3.3%

In other words, the older you are at the time of receipt, the lower the rate. Deferred retirement income - a severance payout moved into an IRP and drawn as a pension - is not taxed by this table but by a separate method that charges 70% of the retirement income tax that would otherwise have been due (60% or 50% depending on the number of years drawn).

5.2 Separate vs Comprehensive Taxation

According to the National Tax Service, if private pension income - excluding deferred retirement income and amounts withdrawn for unavoidable reasons such as medical purposes - totals 15 million won or less a year, you may choose separate taxation that ends with withholding. If it exceeds 15 million won, for payments received on or after 1 January 2023 you may choose at filing between comprehensive taxation and 15% separate taxation. Separate taxation can favor those with large other income, while comprehensive taxation can favor those with little income besides the pension - so there is no single right answer. Thresholds and rates can be revised, so it is wise to consult a professional when planning how to draw.

6. The Penalty for Breaking It Early

The biggest trap of a pension account is that breaking it when you are short on cash is costly. The deduction was, after all, granted on the promise that you would keep the account until retirement.

6.1 "Other Income" Tax - Giving Back the Benefit

If you withdraw or close the account as a lump sum rather than as a pension, an "other income" tax is applied to the deducted contributions and their gains. Per National Tax Service guidance the rate is 15% (16.5% with local income tax), and it is always taxed separately rather than combined with your other income. In effect you can end up handing back the amount you received at filing season. So putting money you will need in the short term into a pension account can be a loss rather than a saving.

6.2 So Use Only Spare Money

For this reason, the rule of thumb is to put only long-term spare money you will not soon need into a pension account. Keep emergency cash and lump sums you will use within a few years elsewhere, and fill the pension account with "money you will not touch until retirement." That fits both the purpose of the system and your interest. Note that some unavoidable circumstances - natural disaster, death, emigration, and the like - may qualify for exceptions, and per National Tax Service guidance amounts withdrawn for unavoidable reasons such as medical purposes are treated as pension income eligible for separate taxation, so check before closing if something happens.

7. Opening and Contribution Strategy

Once you understand the system, it is time to act. The following are commonly mentioned general strategies; adjust them to your own income and cash flow.

7.1 A Combination That Fills the Combined Limit

A combination often used by people trying to make the most of the deduction limit is to "fill 6 million won in pension savings first, then add 3 million won in an IRP to reach the combined 9 million won." Freedom of investment and flexibility of withdrawal are the reasons frequently cited for filling pension savings first. Because limits can change over time, confirm the current figures.

7.2 Steady Amounts You Can Sustain

More important than maxing the limit is not breaking the account midway. Because the early-exit penalty is steep, it is better to sustain an affordable amount for a long time than to overstretch toward the limit and then break it when you need quick cash. Setting up a monthly auto-transfer also gives you a time-diversification effect.

7.3 Year-End Lump Sum vs Spreading Over the Year

Because the deduction is based on the year's contributions, you can still claim it even if you fill the account all at once at year-end. From an investing angle, however, many argue that spreading contributions through the year diversifies your entry points. Either way, the principle is the same: within the limit, in an amount you will not break.

8. Correcting Common Misconceptions

8.1 "I can pull my money out anytime" - Partly Wrong

Pension savings is more flexible to withdraw than an IRP, but pulling out the deducted portion as anything other than a pension brings the "other income" tax. "Withdraw freely" and "withdraw without penalty" are not the same thing.

8.2 "The bigger the deduction, the better, always" - Caution

A deduction only matters if you owe tax that year. Someone who owes little tax may not get the full amount back even by maxing the limit. It is sensible to weigh the size of your assessed tax as well.

8.3 "A pension is tax-free when you receive it" - Misconception

It is not entirely tax-free at withdrawal. It is simply that the rate at withdrawal is designed to be lower than the rate you got back, so a net benefit remains overall. Read it as "low-rate taxation," not "no tax."

9. Scenarios by Situation

The same system carries different priorities depending on your circumstances. Here are three commonly cited situations. These are general examples only; make your actual decisions based on your own income, taxes, and cash flow.

9.1 Early-Career - Start Small, Stay In

If you have just started earning, it is more natural to begin with pension savings in an affordable amount than to overstretch toward the limit from day one. This is the stage with the longest runway to retirement, so even a small amount started early and compounded carries a large effect. What matters is not "how much you pay in" but "whether you can sustain it without breaking the account." Once you have grown comfortable contributing a small monthly amount by auto-transfer, widening the limit with an IRP as your income rises is a staged approach that eases the burden.

9.2 High Earners - Fill the Combined Limit, but Watch the Tax Cliff

If you owe plenty of tax, you can capture the full benefit of the deduction, so filling the combined pension savings and IRP limit is often recommended. Keep in mind, though, that the deduction rate drops once income crosses the threshold, and that when you later draw a pension, exceeding a certain annual amount can change how it is taxed. In other words, it is sensible to set your contribution size by looking not only at "the deduction when paying in" but also at "the taxation when drawing out."

9.3 Near Retirement - Withdrawal Planning Is the Point

For those close to retirement, "how to draw" matters more than "paying in more." Pension-income tax tends to lighten the later and longer you spread the payout, so how you plan the timing and duration of withdrawal changes your real take-home amount. Receiving it all in one year can push you into a higher tax bracket, so it is wise to study spreading it across several years with a professional. Choices at the withdrawal stage are hard to reverse, which makes advance planning especially important.

10. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q. Can I open one even if I am not an employee?

A pension savings account can generally be opened even without income. But a deduction only helps if you owe tax, so with no income at all the benefit may be hard to feel. An IRP has more restricted eligibility, so check the current requirements.

Q. Can I hold both a pension savings account and an IRP?

Yes, and the deduction is calculated on a combined limit. Using both accounts together to fill the combined limit is a common approach.

Q. What happens to my IRP if I change jobs?

Rolling a retirement payout into an IRP to defer taxation and keep it invested is a common move. The process and tax treatment vary by situation, however, so confirm with your financial institution or a professional before transferring.

Conclusion - The Deduction Is Just the Start; Staying In Is the Point

The appeal of pension savings and the IRP is clear. The moment you pay in, the deduction hands back a meaningful sum, and that money rolls forward as retirement capital. But the real value comes not from "one refund" but from "staying in for the long haul." Because breaking it early forces you to give the benefit back, the key is to fill a pension account from the start with spare money you will not touch until retirement.

Three points to repeat. First, limits and rates depend on income and the point in time. Figures like 9 million won and 15% (16.5% with local income tax) in this article were checked as of 29 September 2026, and they shift with tax-law revisions. Second, look at both the withdrawal tax and the early-exit penalty before you sign up. Third, always confirm current rules with the tax authority, your financial institution, or a professional. ## 2. Before you pay in

- [ ] Do you owe income tax this year? If you owe little, you will not get the full credit back
- [ ] Have you checked whether your total salary is at or below 55 million won (comprehensive income 45 million won)?
- [ ] Is this spare money you will not touch until after age 55?
- [ ] Have you set aside emergency cash and money you will need within a few years?
- [ ] Have you decided how far to fill the 6-million-won and 9-million-won limits?
- [ ] If an ISA matures this year, have you confirmed you will use the extra limit (10%, up to 3 million won) in the same year?
- [ ] For an IRP, have you decided what to hold within the 70% risky-asset cap?
- [ ] Have you checked account fees and product fees in the provider's disclosures?

## 4. Before you draw

- [ ] Are you past 55, and have five years passed since the account was opened?
- [ ] Have you checked your age band at receipt (under 70 / 70-79 / 80+) and its rate?
- [ ] Have you separated deferred retirement income (a severance payout moved into an IRP), which uses a different method (70% of retirement income tax, 60% or 50% depending on years drawn)?
- [ ] Have you calculated whether your annual private pension income exceeds 15 million won?
- [ ] If it does, have you compared comprehensive taxation and 15% separate taxation together with your other income?
- [ ] Have you reviewed with a professional how to spread payments over several years instead of one?

- [ ] Do you know that a non-pension withdrawal puts 15% other-income tax (16.5% with local income tax) on credited contributions and gains?
- [ ] Have you checked with your provider and the National Tax Service whether an unavoidable reason such as medical purposes applies?
- [ ] Is there another way to cover the need without closing the account?

## The one line

The tax credit is **a discount you get when you pay in**, and the price is **not breaking the account until you draw it as a pension after 55**.
