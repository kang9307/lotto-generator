.self TLD — A New Top-Level Domain Designed for Self-Hosting

The concept, differences from .local and mDNS, ICANN application status, and the real limits on TLS and DNS for homelab users

2026-07-01 Dev & Infra

Introduction: Why a Name Matters

Anyone who has run a homelab has hit this wall. You want to give your NAS, your self-hosted Git server, or your Nextcloud instance a proper name like nas.home or git.local — but the moment you try, devices disagree, browsers throw security warnings, and smartphones refuse to connect altogether. This friction has been the recurring complaint of the self-hosting community for years.

In June 2026, the Human-Centered Computing Foundation (HCCF), a US-based 501(c)(3) nonprofit, made its proposal public: applying to ICANN's 2026 new gTLD application window for the top-level domain .self. "A new top-level domain designed to support self-hosting" is the pitch. The framing is simple, but the implications — technical, institutional, cultural — are worth unpacking carefully.

One clarification upfront: as of June 2026, .self is a proposal, not a functioning TLD. It is not in the IANA root zone. The gap between "an application has been filed" and "you can use this today" is enormous, and much of the discussion around .self blurs that line. This analysis keeps it sharp.

1. The Rise of the Self-Hosting Ecosystem — Context

Understanding why .self emerged requires understanding where the self-hosting ecosystem stands in 2026.

1.1 Homelab Culture Has Grown Up

Between 2024 and 2026, the self-hosting community matured significantly. Several forces converged: cloud service price hikes and the spread of subscription fatigue, privacy concerns following repeated data incidents, and the commoditization of capable hardware — Raspberry Pis, cheap mini PCs, second-hand servers. The 2026 homelab stack has effectively standardized: Jellyfin for media, Nextcloud for files, Vaultwarden for passwords, Gitea for code, AdGuard Home for DNS blocking, Tailscale or WireGuard for VPN access, and increasingly Ollama for local LLMs.

As the ecosystem matures, DNS and name resolution become the visible friction point. When you run one service, an IP address is fine. When you run a dozen, and your family uses them too, name-based access becomes essential. The .self proposal grew from this need.

1.2 Data Sovereignty as a Driver

HCCF frames .self not merely as a technical tool but as an expression of digital self-determination. The structural pressures driving this framing are real: GDPR Article 44+ on cross-border data transfers, the US CLOUD Act's reach over US-provider data regardless of geography, and repeated high-profile platform shutdowns and policy reversals. "Running your own server" is increasingly the answer to these pressures, not just a hobbyist activity. That said, a domain name does not protect data. The actual protection comes from server location, encryption, and operational practice. The name is symbolic — and there is value in symbols, but we should be precise about what each layer does.

2. What .self Proposes — Concept and Structure

2.1 One Subdomain Per Person

HCCF's core proposal is a public-benefit TLD: one free subdomain per person (e.g., alice.self or mynas.self), bundled with shared infrastructure — VPN tunnels, mail servers, and open-source client software. The model explicitly references ISRG and Let's Encrypt as the public-good precedent, with sponsorships covering operational costs.

The vision is coherent. The financing is not yet demonstrated. ICANN gTLD compliance costs hundreds of thousands of dollars annually. A free-domain model without registration revenue has no proven path to sustainability at that scale. Community discussions consistently raise the .tk cautionary tale: a free TLD so thoroughly colonized by spammers and malicious actors that Facebook, antivirus vendors, and browser vendors blocked it outright. Free access attracts both legitimate users and adversarial automation — and adversarial automation scales faster.

2.2 Two Implementation Paths

For .self to actually work, one of two paths must be followed.

The first is public gTLD delegation — ICANN approves .self and it enters the IANA root. This makes it a globally reachable TLD like any other, opening the door to public CA certificate issuance in principle. The timeline: ICANN reviews typically take years, with no guarantee of approval.

The second is Special-Use-Domain designation — modeled on .onion (RFC 7686, 2015), where IETF standardization reserves the string without placing it in the public root. This is faster but structurally limits global reachability: browsers and resolvers only honor the domain if explicitly configured to do so. No public root entry means no standard CA issuance.

My read: the Special-Use-Domain path is more realistic in the near term, but if .self takes that route, its practical advantages over .internal (which IANA reserved for private use in 2024) are modest. You get a more evocative name — "self" rather than "internal" — but the technical situation is almost identical.

3. .local and mDNS — What Is Actually Different

3.1 How mDNS .local Works

.local is a special-use domain defined in RFC 6762 (Multicast DNS). Its mechanism is fundamentally different from unicast DNS. When a device looks up mynas.local, it sends a multicast query to the local network — effectively shouting "does anyone own this name?" — and the device with that name responds directly. Apple's Bonjour service uses this to make printers, AirPlay targets, and smart TVs appear automatically. No DNS server required.

The advantages: zero configuration within a local segment. The limitations: it is strictly broadcast-domain-scoped. It does not work across subnets or over VPN. It has reliability problems at scale. And it does not play well with formal DNS infrastructure that production services expect.

3.2 What .self Would Add

.self is designed around unicast DNS. A local DNS server (Pi-hole, AdGuard Home, Technitium, Unbound) manages the .self zone and serves authoritative answers to querying devices. The implications: consistent resolution across subnets, resolution over VPN when clients use the same DNS server, and no reliance on multicast broadcast. Services registered in .self are accessible to any device that uses the home's internal DNS resolver.

But here is the honest assessment: homelab operators already do exactly this with .lan, .home.arpa, or .internal. The technical capability exists. What .self would add is a standardized, officially recognized name for this namespace — and, if gTLD delegation follows, the possibility of public CA certificate issuance. The second point is where the real friction lies.

3.3 The .internal Precedent

IANA's reservation of .internal for private use in 2024 is the closest recent precedent. It prevents .internal from being registered as a public domain (eliminating collision risk), without placing it in the public root. This is effectively the Special-Use-Domain approach applied to an intuitive name. .self could follow the same path, and some community members argue this would be sufficient — cleaner semantics, collision-free, deployable with any local DNS server today.

4. TLS Certificates — The Hardest Layer

4.1 Why Certificates Are the Real Problem

Name resolution is solvable with a local DNS server. TLS certificates are not. Browsers validate two things on an HTTPS connection: that the server name matches the certificate, and that the certificate is signed by a trusted CA. Public CAs like Let's Encrypt do not issue certificates for TLDs absent from the IANA root. So mynas.self cannot get a Let's Encrypt certificate today.

The two practical workarounds are: (1) build a local CA with mkcert or Step CA and manually install its root certificate on every device; or (2) buy a real public domain, use the DNS-01 ACME challenge to obtain a wildcard certificate (e.g., *.home.example.com), and run split-horizon DNS. Option 2 is more work upfront but delivers browser-trusted HTTPS on every device without per-device configuration — including iPhones and iPads.

4.2 What Changes If .self Becomes a gTLD

Official gTLD delegation opens the door to CA/Browser Forum discussions about permitting certificate issuance for .self domains. But this door-opening is not automatic certificate availability. The CA/B Forum would need to amend policy. Each CA would need to implement the change. Browsers would need to update. Domain ownership verification infrastructure would need to exist. Each step takes time — collectively, years. TLS relief from .self is a long-horizon possibility, not a near-term solution.

5. Apple Devices and the Non-Standard TLD Problem

5.1 Apple's Networking Stack Is Strict

Apple devices apply multiple security layers that actively interfere with non-standard TLD resolution. Safari treats unrecognized TLDs as search queries rather than domain names. iCloud Private Relay intercepts DNS queries from Safari and routes them through Apple relay servers, bypassing any local resolver configuration. Encrypted DNS profiles (DoH/DoT) further override local resolvers. Self-signed certificate chains are rejected by default. The result: a .self DNS entry on your home server may resolve perfectly on Linux but silently fail on iOS.

mDNS .local is Apple-native (Bonjour), so it has no equivalent problem. .self has no Apple support and cannot have it until .self is in the IANA root. The workarounds — disabling iCloud Private Relay, explicitly specifying local resolvers, installing local CA roots — are available but fragile and disrupted by Apple policy updates.

5.2 The Structural Dilemma

This is not a .self-specific problem. It applies equally to .lan, .home.arpa, .internal, and any non-IANA TLD. Operating systems and browsers extend full trust only to IANA-root TLDs. Crossing that barrier requires per-device manual configuration. The only structural fix is .self becoming an official gTLD — or browser vendors adding explicit support for a Special-Use-Domain reservation. Neither is fast.

6. Alternatives That Work Today

6.1 Real Public Domain + Split-Horizon DNS

The community's most robust current pattern: buy a cheap domain (e.g., example.com, ~$10/year), create internal service subdomains in your local DNS server, and obtain a wildcard Let's Encrypt certificate via DNS-01 challenge (e.g., *.home.example.com). All devices — including iPhones, iPads, and Android phones — receive browser-trusted HTTPS with zero per-device configuration. The domain is public, so no browser treats it suspiciously. This is the pattern I recommend for anyone who wants a clean, maintainable setup today.

6.2 .internal / .home.arpa

For pure-internal services where browser certificate trust is not required (monitoring dashboards, admin panels accessible only from the LAN), IANA-reserved .internal or IETF-recommended .home.arpa work fine. No collision risk, no external dependency. Accept that browsers will show certificate warnings unless you install a local CA root on each device.

6.3 Tailscale MagicDNS / Headscale

Tailscale's MagicDNS integrates automatic DNS resolution into its mesh VPN fabric. Devices on the Tailscale network reach each other by name without any manual DNS configuration. Tailscale handles TLS through its own CA. Self-hosted alternative: Headscale gives you full control of the control server. This solution is rapidly becoming the community standard for frictionless remote access to self-hosted services — it works on every OS, requires no per-device DNS setup, and does not depend on any ICANN decision.

7. My Assessment

7.1 What .self Gets Right

The self-hosting ecosystem is maturing. As it expands from hobbyists to families to small businesses, the DNS and TLS friction becomes a real adoption barrier — not just an inconvenience. The .self proposal correctly identifies this friction, names it publicly, and brings it into an institutional space (ICANN) where structural solutions can emerge. The process itself has value: the discussion it generated around Special-Use-Domain paths, financing models, and abuse prevention will benefit adjacent communities even if .self never gets delegated. .onion gave the Tor community an official name; .self could do the same for self-hosting.

7.2 The Honest Limits

ICANN review takes years and is not guaranteed. Free-domain financing is unproven at ICANN compliance scale. The Special-Use-Domain path is faster but leaves .self technically equivalent to .internal. TLS relief, even after gTLD delegation, requires additional years of CA/B Forum and browser policy work. And the data sovereignty framing, while resonant, is symbolic: the name does not protect data. The server, its location, and its encryption do.

The German analysis from drweb.de put it concisely: .self makes data sovereignty "linguistically addressable" rather than technically solving it. That is a real contribution, and a limited one.

7.3 Practical Recommendation (As of June 2026)

If you need clean self-hosted name resolution and TLS today, do not wait for .self. Use a real public domain with split-horizon DNS and a Let's Encrypt wildcard certificate, or adopt Tailscale MagicDNS. Watch the .self application — the ICANN window closes August 12, 2026; track review progress after that. If .self reaches Special-Use-Domain standardization quickly, a single local DNS configuration line is all you need to adopt it.

8. Conclusion — What Comes Before the Name

The .self TLD proposal is a well-aimed attempt to address a real problem in the self-hosting ecosystem. The direction is right. But a TLD does not simultaneously solve name resolution, TLS trust, browser support, and sustainable financing. Each layer requires its own solution.

What the self-hosting ecosystem actually needs — before a new TLD name — is HTTPS without local CA installation, automatic service discovery when a new device joins the home network, and seamless remote access without a VPN setup. Some of these become addressable if .self is formalized. Others require router firmware, operating systems, and browser policies to move together. When that convergence arrives is uncertain. But the fact that this conversation is now happening at the ICANN level — that the self-hosting community has grown large enough to file a TLD application — is itself a signal worth recording in June 2026.
