Learning the Deadlift - What Actually Matters and What Is Just Gym Folklore

The hip hinge, the setup sequence, what 「keep your back straight」 really means, and how to judge variations, grips and gear

Sep 1, 2026 Health & Wellness

No lift in the gym attracts as much talk as the deadlift. The warning that it will wreck your back and the pitch that you are missing out if you skip it circulate in the same room. Neither usually arrives with evidence attached, which is why someone starting out learns not what to pay attention to but what to be afraid of.

The scope here is narrow. One lift. How many sets, how often in a week, how it fits with everything else belongs to programming, and that lives in the complete beginner strength training guide. What follows is what this movement demands, and which of the widely repeated claims about it actually have literature behind them.

This article is general information, not a diagnosis or prescription for any individual. If you have pain, a diagnosed condition, or a history of surgery, consult a medical or exercise professional before starting. Nothing below replaces clinical care, and no specific load, repetition count or frequency is recommended anywhere in it.

What the movement actually is - the hip hinge

The deadlift is neither a back exercise nor a leg exercise. It is the act of standing a load up off the ground by folding and then extending at the hip, and the name for that pattern is the hip hinge. The body folds at the hip the way a door opens at its hinge.

It gets confused with the squat because both look like sitting down and standing up. What differs is where the fold happens. In a squat the knee bends a great deal and the torso stays relatively upright. In a deadlift the knee bends less, the hip folds deeply, and the torso tips forward. If the knees travel forward while the body drops, you have slid over toward a squat.

This is not a matter of feel but of where the load sits. In a squat the weight rests over the axis of the trunk; in a deadlift it sits in front of the body and starts lower than the feet. In the 2025 conventional-versus-sumo comparison in Frontiers in Bioengineering and Biotechnology, the conventional pull showed greater hip flexion and ankle dorsiflexion along with a larger hip extension moment. You stand up by extending the hip, not by straightening the legs.

  • The simplest way to see whether a hinge is happening is video shot from the side. A mirror shows you the front, and from the front this difference is invisible
  • The shape matches the everyday act of picking something off the floor. How much of it actually transfers is a separate question
  • Add weight before the hinge exists and the body finds another route. Usually that means rounding the back or driving the knees forward

Read this first - signs that mean stop and get seen

This section sits near the front for a simple reason. Technique can be fixed later. This cannot.

Training or not, if any of the following applies, stop where you are and get medical attention.

  • Pain radiating into the leg, tingling, or altered sensation occurring together
  • Weakness in the leg, such as difficulty lifting the foot
  • Bladder dysfunction, including trouble passing urine
  • Pain that is severe, or that does not improve with rest and analgesics
  • Back pain that began after an obvious injury such as a fall or trauma
  • Fever, or unexplained weight loss, occurring alongside the pain

One more belongs here. A sudden loss of bowel or bladder control, numbness in the area that would touch a saddle, or weakness in both legs at once is the combination that raises suspicion of cauda equina syndrome, and that is treated as an emergency.

That list is drawn from the low back pain entry of the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency national health portal, which sets out when a physician should examine you. Whether it started during a deadlift is beside the point.

The list should not be read backwards, though. A 2014 analysis in Archives of Trauma Research reported that these red flags have high specificity and low sensitivity. Their presence should be taken seriously; their absence is not evidence that nothing is wrong.

There is also a group that should get advice before starting at all. The exercise preparticipation screening process revised by the American College of Sports Medicine in 2015 turns on four things: current physical activity level, the presence of diagnosed cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, the presence of signs and symptoms, and the intensity of exercise intended.

  • A history of spinal surgery, disc disease, or spinal stenosis - whether to include this lift at all is an individual judgment. Background sits in the herniated disc guide and the low back pain relief guide
  • Diagnosed cardiovascular disease or uncontrolled hypertension - heavy resistance work swings blood pressure sharply in the moment
  • Symptoms with no known cause - that is something to establish in a clinic, not through training

Why lifting off the floor is hard - the moment arm

The arithmetic is plain. Torque is force times distance, and the distance here is the perpendicular distance from the load to the axis of rotation, the moment arm. The further the bar sits from the hip, the more torque the same weight generates. A large share of the work of resisting that torque falls to the spine and the muscles around it. This is where the instruction to keep the bar against your shins comes from.

Folklore runs a step ahead of the evidence at this point, though. A 2018 study in Sports compared a maximal isometric setup with the bar over the navicular bone against one with the bar over the third metatarsophalangeal joint. Even in the farther condition there was no statistically significant difference in L5-S1 compression or shear force (estimates of 13,163 N versus 13,406 N). What did change were joint angles and the distribution of muscle activity. With the bar close, the shin was more vertical (8.56 versus 19.54 degrees), the knee less bent (51.29 versus 68.63 degrees), and the biceps femoris and upper lumbar erector spinae did more of the work.

Read precisely, that says a few centimetres of difference at the setup did not translate into a large difference in lumbar load. The real reason for keeping the bar close lies elsewhere. If the bar drifts forward during the pull, the moment arm keeps growing from that instant, and most failures happen there. The start position is the condition that prevents the drift.

  • The moment arm is a trajectory, not a still photograph. Contact at the start is worthless if the bar swings away past the knees
  • The compression figures above are model estimates. They are not grounds for transferring an absolute number onto your own body
  • Shorter arms or a longer torso means the chest sits lower under the same load. That is why copying someone else's position rarely works
  • Holding the bar against the body with the lats is the device that protects the trajectory. It is less about pulling with the arms than about pinning the bar to you

The setup sequence - what each step removes

The setup is not a ritual. Each step removes one specific problem. The reason for running the same order every time is that when the order varies, you cannot tell what went wrong.

  1. Foot position - roughly hip width. The stance you land in after a light jump on the spot is usually your width. What this step sets is the size of the base you can push against
  2. Bar to foot - seen from above, the bar sits over mid-foot; seen from the side, near the laces. Starting directly over the centre of mass is what stops the bar dragging you forward or back
  3. Shins to the bar - not by pushing the knees forward, but by folding at the hip first and then bending the knees slightly until the shins meet the bar. The condition is that the bar does not move while you do it
  4. Grip - where the arms hang vertically, usually just outside the shins. A wider grip lowers the bar and adds to the distance you have to move it. Arms are cables here, not muscles
  5. Chest up, bar pinned - the feeling of drawing the bar toward the armpits. The upper back locks in place, and the torso is less likely to collapse when the first load arrives
  6. Breath and bracing - draw air in, expand the circumference of the abdomen, then tighten in that state. It is not sucking in; it is expanding in every direction and setting
  7. Take out the slack - there is always play between bar and plates, and between bar and arms. Apply force to remove it before you push. Skip this and the load arrives suddenly, before the body is ready
  • The point of the setup is not a good position but a repeatable one
  • A long setup lets the brace leak away. Keep the order fixed, but run the last two steps together

What 「keep your back straight」 actually means

Neutral spine means roughly preserving the natural curve of the lumbar spine. It does not mean arching backwards, and it does not mean making a ruler-straight line. The trouble is the sentence that usually follows. The claim that any rounding at all will injure you circulates as fact, and the literature does not support it that strongly.

A 2021 study in PLOS ONE compared manual workers with and without a history of low back pain across a 100-lift task. The result ran opposite to expectation. The group with pain history lifted more slowly, with more knee bend and less lumbar flexion. Of 21 biomechanical variables, exactly one showed an association with change in pain intensity. The authors wrote that the usual assumption linking low back pain to greater lumbar flexion or greater forces was not observed, and that this raises questions about the current safe-lifting paradigm.

That does not license the conclusion that flexion is meaningless. A 2025 study in Frontiers in Sports and Active Living measured 13 lifters deadlifting at 60, 70, 80 and 90 percent of one-rep max, and found that as the load rose, flexion angles in the lower thoracic and upper lumbar spine increased and posterior pelvic tilt arrived earlier in the pull.

Put the two together and one usable sentence falls out. Increasing flexion reads more accurately as a load gauge than as an injury alarm. A back that has started to round is not a signal that damage has occurred; it is a signal that this weight is at the top of what you currently control.

  • Do not extract from this the conclusion that you cannot get hurt. What has been shown is that the link between flexion and back pain does not appear as strongly as the belief suggests
  • Nor does it follow that flexion can simply be left alone. Flexion growing with load is an observation that control is being lost
  • When judging position, watch the change rather than the absolute angle. If the first and last repetition differ, that difference is the information
  • Continuing while in pain and simply fixing the position is a different matter. Pain is something to have investigated, not something to correct with technique

The four variations

Variations are not difficulty tiers but different distributions of demand. What differs is which missing capacity stops you.

Conventional

The standard version, feet about hip width, hands outside the shins. In the 2025 comparison cited above it showed a large hip extension moment with relatively high activity in the biceps femoris and erector spinae. It asks the most of the hip hinge, which makes it the most honest way to learn one. The cost is that limited hip and hamstring range shows up as a rounded back before the bar has even moved.

Sumo

Feet wide, hands gripping inside the legs. The torso sits more upright and the bar travels a shorter distance. In the same study, sumo produced greater frontal and transverse plane range and moments at the hip and knee, with higher vastus lateralis activity. What it asks for is hip mobility and force in the abduction direction.

Romanian

It does not start on the floor. From standing, the knee angle stays roughly fixed while the hip folds and extends. The hamstrings and glutes work under length through the lowering phase. This is the best way to isolate and practise the hinge on its own.

Trap bar (hexagonal bar)

You stand inside the frame and grip handles at your sides, which brings the load far closer to the trunk axis. In a 2011 comparison of 19 powerlifters in the Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, the hexagonal bar produced lower peak moments at the lumbar spine, hip and ankle, and a higher peak moment at the knee. The same lifters handled a heavier maximum (265 versus 245 kg) and produced greater peak power (4,872 versus 4,388 W).

ConventionalHip extension, trunk enduranceLearning the hinge pattern itselfLimited hamstring and hip range
SumoHip abduction and rotation range, leg driveWhen the hips open wellIf they do not, the start position never forms

  • Variations are not ranked against each other. They ask for different capacities
  • The claim that body proportions determine which variation suits you is widespread, but no study was found supporting the practice of assigning variations by arm or torso length
  • There is no need to try them all. Picking one and staying with it until the setup becomes repeatable leaves you with more
  • Sumo looking easier on the back is plausible from the torso angle alone, but the study above also shows load growing in other directions in sumo. The demand is not removed; it moves

What the trap bar offers a beginner

The largest single thing is that there is structurally less room for the bar to drift forward. With a straight bar the load sits in front of the body, and most beginner failures come from it moving away. With a trap bar the handles are at your sides, which makes that particular error hard to commit. The accuracy of the setup matters less to the outcome.

The second is the distribution of demand. Lower lumbar moment and higher knee moment means that under the same load the lower back does less and the legs do more. The third is handle height. A higher starting position reduces the hip and hamstring range required, which makes it easier to build a start position while keeping a neutral spine.

  • The cost is equally clear. With the load at your sides, the movement shifts toward a squat. It teaches the hinge less well than the conventional pull
  • If powerlifting competition is the goal, the straight bar has to be learned eventually, and going there first avoids the detour
  • If the goal is whole-body strength and the ability to lift from the floor, starting with a trap bar costs almost nothing
  • No direct study was found showing the trap bar lowers injury rates in beginners. What was cited is a comparison of moments and output

Grip - where you lose the bar and what it costs

The deadlift is the lift where grip gives out first. The back and legs still have something left when the hands open. That makes grip not a preference but the variable that decides where a set ends.

GripWhat it givesWhat it costsWhere it fits
Double overhandFull symmetry. Grip strength grows alongsideYou are stopped by the hands firstMost of the learning period
Alternated (mixed)The bar does not roll, so it holds longerAsymmetry. Biceps tendon load on the supinated armThe few genuinely heavy sets
Hook gripSolves the grip problem while keeping symmetryThe thumb hurts. Adaptation takes timeAdding load without losing symmetry
StrapsStops grip from capping training volumeGrip grows less. Letting go is harderHigher-repetition work aimed at back, glutes, hamstrings

One point about the mixed grip is worth stating plainly. The arm whose palm faces forward sits in the position typical of a distal biceps tendon rupture. A 2022 video analysis in Bone and Joint Open reviewed 57 recorded ruptures and found that deadlifting was the most common mechanism, at 38 cases (67%). At the moment of rupture the forearm was supinated in 56 cases (98%), and no rupture occurred with a pronated forearm. The elbow was between 0 and 10 degrees of flexion, essentially straight, in 51 cases (89%).

This is not grounds for banning the mixed grip. Rupture is a rare event, and the analysis examined posture at the moment of injury rather than incidence. What it does show clearly is the position the supinated arm occupies. The judgment that follows is about allocation, not prohibition.

  • If you use a mixed grip, keep the supinated elbow straight. The instant you try to pull with the arm, that arm moves closest to the position above
  • Most sets are fine with a double overhand grip. The first time to consider a tool is when grip stops you finishing the intended repetitions
  • The hook grip solves the same problem while keeping symmetry. Many people abandon it over the early pain, but it stays once adaptation is done
  • Straps make sense when grip is not the goal. If grip strength itself is the target, straps remove that stimulus

Adding weight - finding today's ceiling

There is one principle. The point at which the position starts to break down is today's ceiling. That is not sentiment, because the measurements above support it: as load rises, flexion and posterior pelvic tilt increase. So the ceiling is not a fixed number. It moves with sleep and residual fatigue, and today's version comes from observation rather than from a plan on paper.

The common beginner sequence runs like this. Weight goes up fast. The setup changes every time. Some days it works and some days it does not, and there is no way to tell what differed. What gets lost there is not only safety but learning.

  • Film from the side. How much the back is rounding is nearly impossible to feel from inside
  • Compare the trajectory of the first repetition with the last. If the last one visibly differs, that is not a weight for another rep today
  • How much to add varies widely between people. The condition that the setup does not break down comes before any number
  • When progress stalls, change something other than the weight. Repetitions, sets, starting height and variation are all adjustable

What a belt actually does

A belt is not a brace that holds the lower back for you. What it does is raise intra-abdominal pressure and stiffen the trunk. When you draw in air and expand the abdomen, the belt gives the abdominal wall one more surface to push against; trunk stiffness rises, and less of the force the legs generate leaks away before it reaches the bar.

A condition follows from that description. A belt amplifies bracing you can already produce without it. Wrap one on before you have learned to brace and what you learn is mostly how to push your belly into the belt.

  • No primary source was found while writing this for the claim that belts lower injury rates. Reports that intra-abdominal pressure rises and claims that injuries fall are statements at different levels
  • Position it where there is still room for the abdomen to expand. Cinched so tight that you cannot draw air in, the belt blocks the very thing it was meant to do
  • There is no need to wear it on every set. Using it through the light work removes the place where you would have built the capacity to hold without it
  • It starts to earn its place around the point where the load itself demands a braced trunk to move at all. That point differs from person to person

Shoes - why a raised heel works against you

There are only two requirements: a sole that does not compress, and one that does not slip. That is why a heavily cushioned running shoe works against you. Cushioning exists to compress. When running that is the point, but when you are driving a load into the floor, the ground under you collapses slightly differently on every repetition. If repeatability of the setup is the condition for learning, a floor that changes each time erodes that condition.

Heel height creates a separate problem. A raised heel shifts the centre of mass forward, which makes it harder to keep the bar close to the body. That a heeled lifting shoe helps in the squat and hinders in the deadlift are two faces of the same reason.

  • A thin, firm sole is enough. This is not a lift that needs dedicated footwear
  • Barefoot or socks is an option where it is permitted, though it is hard to recommend anywhere plates might be dropped
  • No study directly comparing footwear types in the deadlift was found. The explanation above is mechanical reasoning, not a measured result

Six common mistakes

The mistakes that recur early are mostly the same list. What matters is not what went wrong but what it is the result of. Fix only the symptom and it returns on the next set.

What you seeUsual causeWhat to change first
The hips shoot up firstStarting hips too low, or the leg drive arrives lateReset the starting height, remove slack, then go
The bar drifts off the bodyLats not engaged, or the torso rises firstBuild the pull toward the armpits into the setup
Leaning back at the topTreating lockout as extensionRedefine the finish as driving the hips forward to stand
Bouncing the bar off the floorFinishing the rep count became the goalRemove slack on the floor before every repetition
Craning the neck to look upExecuting 「chest up」 with the neckLet the neck follow the trunk, eyes a few metres ahead
Holding the breath all setTreating bracing and breath-holding as the same thingRefill and rebuild the brace between repetitions

The last item needs more explanation. Holding the breath while straining is called the Valsalva manoeuvre; intrathoracic pressure rises, venous return to the heart falls, and blood pressure swings hard. At the moment of release, pressure drops sharply and then overshoots back above baseline. Using it briefly and holding it through an entire set are different things, and in the presence of cardiovascular disease the manoeuvre itself is treated as a caution.

  • Several of the six usually appear together. Working from the setup-stage items first is faster
  • Three of them (hips, bar path, neck) are hard to correct without video. They are not detectable by feel
  • If a mistake appears in proportion to load, it is a loading problem rather than a technique problem. Drop the weight and it goes with it

When you do not need this lift

The only people who must deadlift are those competing in powerlifting. For everyone else it is one option among several. What needs training is not the name of an exercise but the pattern of folding and extending at the hip while the trunk resists load, and lifting from the floor is one implementation of it. That is also the criterion for choosing a substitute.

  • Trap bar - the same movement with the demand shifted away from the lower back
  • Rack pull - a raised start that reduces the range required. The knack is starting at a height where neutral can be held
  • Romanian deadlift - isolates the hinge without the floor. The most commonly used substitute while learning
  • Kettlebell swing, good morning, 45-degree back extension - the magnitude and direction of load differ, but hip extension is common to all of them
  • Dropping this lift does not leave you behind. Producing the same stimulus through substitutes does mean managing range and load separately

If there is a history of spinal disease or pain that has not settled, the alternative route is the more sensible one. Which side fits your situation is settled by talking to a professional, not by reading material like this.

Pulling it together

Three things survive the early period. The same setup order every time, a bar that comes up against the body, and an eye for your own ceiling. The weight follows from those.

On the folklore side it comes out like this. The claim that slight flexion equals injury is not supported that strongly in the literature, no evidence was confirmed that belts prevent injury, and the mixed grip is a tool to be used knowing what position it creates. There is a certain side too. Position changes as load grows. Reading that change is half of what this lift teaches.

  • The bar stays close not through magic at the start position but to protect the trajectory
  • Flexion is a gauge, not an alarm. If it has grown, that weight is at the ceiling
  • Gear amplifies what you can already do. It does not substitute for a capacity you lack
Worth restating. This article is general information, not a diagnosis or prescription for any individual. If you have pain, or any of the warning signs listed above, get seen before deciding whether to continue training. Where there is existing spinal or cardiovascular disease, the order is to consult a medical or exercise professional first and set the individual conditions there.

  • The 2020 JOSPT meta-analysis on lumbar flexion during lifting and low back pain refused access to its full text. It is not linked, and only the 2021 PLOS ONE study from the same group was used as evidence
  • The 2000 MSSE paper reporting numeric stance width, foot angle and bar travel for sumo and conventional sits behind a paywall, so none of its specific figures are used here
  • The systematic review of injury rates per 1,000 hours of powerlifting and weightlifting training could not be opened, so its numbers were not carried across
  • Several reports exist that belts raise intra-abdominal pressure, but the original papers were paywalled. No evidence was confirmed that belts lower injury rates
  • Reports exist that position changes within a set as fatigue accumulates, but the originals were inaccessible, so only load-dependent change is cited in the body
  • No study directly comparing footwear types in the deadlift was found. The account of heel and cushioning is mechanical reasoning
  • Assigning variations by arm or torso length is a widespread recommendation, but no study validating it was found
  • No outcome study was found showing the trap bar lowers injury rates in beginners. What is cited is a comparison of moments and output
