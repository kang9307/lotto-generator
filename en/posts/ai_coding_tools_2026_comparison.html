AI Coding Tools 2026 Comparison

May 14, 2026 Tech & IT

If you are about to put an AI coding tool in front of a team, the first question is not which one is smartest. It is whether you can tell today what the invoice will say three months from now. Through 2026 this market moved to credit-based billing almost in unison, and a plan advertised at ten dollars a month is now ten dollars a month plus fifteen dollars of usage budget riding inside it. That is why teams that thought they bought a flat fee keep running into usage limits.

This piece sorts ten tools along four axes: what the plan actually meters, what spend you can see and where, how each one reaches your codebase, and which data-handling clauses stop a rollout at security review. Per-million-token model pricing is not here. That lives in The 2026 AI Model Comparison, and mixing tool plans with model rates in one table gets both of them read wrong.

Blank cells in the tables are not oversights. They are values the vendor does not publish. Where the blanks cluster tells you something about the product.

The short answer — constraints pick the tool

Tool choice looks like a matter of taste until you nail down two constraints, at which point the shortlist collapses to two or three names. First: can you change editors? Second: do you need a hard ceiling on spend? Settle those and then compare model quality. Do it the other way round and you will pick a strong tool that never clears procurement.

  • The editor is fixed → GitHub Copilot, JetBrains AI, Amazon Q Developer. They attach to what your team already runs
  • The editor is negotiable → Cursor, Devin Desktop, Zed. Dedicated editors become viable
  • The work happens in a terminal or in CI → Claude Code, OpenAI Codex CLI, Gemini CLI
  • You want to choose the model provider → Cline, Zed, Aider. You supply your own API key
  • You already have an AWS contract and hate onboarding vendors → Amazon Q Developer. It lands on an invoice you already receive
  • A spending ceiling is non-negotiable → GitHub Copilot with budgets and alerts, Claude for Teams and Enterprise with seat allowances plus spend limits, Amazon Q Developer at a flat seat price
  • You want to read spend in whole dollars → JetBrains AI. One credit equals one dollar, so no conversion is needed
  • You want to start at close to zero → Gemini CLI with 1,000 requests a day on a Google account, Copilot Free with 2,000 completions a month, or Cline and Aider where the client is free and only the model bills

If that settled it, treat the rest as evidence. If it did not, sections 5 and 7 are the ones that will make the decision.

What actually changed since May 2026

The first version of this article was written in May 2026. In three months the billing structure shifted and one product lost its name.

Windsurf became Devin Desktop

On June 2, 2026 Cognition relaunched Windsurf as Devin Desktop. Existing users got it as an over-the-air update and kept their plans and settings. The Cascade local agent ran only through July 1; after that the work moves to Devin Local, rewritten in Rust. The substance of the change is not the name but the Agent Command Center, a Kanban view that manages several agents at once, plus Spaces for sharing context between them. The product moved from single editor to agent orchestrator.

What bites in practice is automation. If a CI pipeline or a script invoked cascade directly, that call is already dead. If your internal wiki pinned Windsurf documentation links, they now redirect to devin.ai, so the anchor text needs a pass too.

GitHub Copilot moved from premium requests to AI Credits

Copilot used to count in requests, as in three hundred premium requests a month on Pro. Now each plan carries AI Credits denominated in dollars. Pro is 10 dollars a month with 15 dollars of credits, Pro+ is 39 dollars with 70 dollars, and Max is 100 dollars with 200 dollars. Max is new on the individual side, and Business and Enterprise get an organisation-wide credit pool.

Moving from requests to dollars favours the customer. Under the old scheme you had to know each model multiplier to work out what was left; now the remaining credit is the remaining money. The catch is that an expensive model burns the same request faster. Copilot code review carries a model multiplier of 13 as of June 1, 2026.

Cursor split individual pricing into three tiers

Above the 20 dollar Pro plan there is now Pro Plus at 60 dollars and Ultra at 200 dollars, carrying 20, 70 and 400 dollars of third-party model usage respectively. Ultra buys 400 dollars of usage for 200, which is the best unit rate on the list and also the fastest way to waste 200 dollars if the team does not consume it.

The training default changed on Copilot free and individual plans

Since April 24, 2026 GitHub may use inputs, outputs, code snippets and associated context from Free and Pro users to train and improve AI models. There is a setting labelled Allow GitHub to use my data for AI model training, and turning it off is the user's job. The same document states that GitHub does not use Copilot Business or Copilot Enterprise customer data for training. If people at your company touch company code on personal plans, this single line is what stops the rollout first.

Two families — terminal agents and in-editor assistants

Ten names look complicated, but there is really one fork in the road. Does a person watch the code and call the AI, or does the AI finish a job and a person review it? That difference determines the billing shape and the way the team works around it.

This is not a choice of one over the other. Real deployments converge on an in-editor tool for everyone and an agent tool for the people who need one. Hand agents to the whole company and the cost distribution stops being manageable; hand them to nobody and the migration work stays on the backlog forever.

Devin Desktop has moved to blur this line. It started as an editor and now leads with a Kanban board for managing agents. Claude Code is arriving at the same place from the other side: it began as a CLI and added a VS Code extension, a JetBrains plugin, a desktop app and a browser surface, with CLAUDE.md files, settings and MCP servers shared across all of them.

Three things in that table matter more than the rest.

  • The same twenty dollars buys different goods. Cursor Pro buys twenty dollars of model usage. Claude Pro buys a usage window that resets on a rolling five-hour and weekly cycle. Exhaust the first and you get an overage line; exhaust the second and you wait for the reset. If you need a predictable invoice, take the window. If you need to finish tonight, take the budget.
  • Only two products still sell a plain seat. Amazon Q Developer Pro at 19 dollars and Copilot Business at 19 dollars per seat are the shapes a finance team recognises. Everywhere else a metered layer sits on top of the seat.
  • Only Zed put a number on the markup. Past the included five dollars, usage bills at API list price plus 10 percent. Everyone else says at API rates and leaves the vintage of that list price unstated.

How to read a credit

JetBrains made this the easiest to read. One credit is pinned to one dollar, so the remaining credits are the remaining money. The documentation even gives rough conversions for a single credit: about 10 code generations in AI Chat, about 40 code generations in the editor, about 25 code explanations, about 280 documentation writes, and about 140 commit messages. The same dollar buys 140 commit messages or 10 chat code generations. That fourteenfold spread is what credit billing really is.

Copilot also denominates credits in dollars but applies model multipliers on top. The documentation says each model carries a premium request multiplier based on complexity and resource usage, and pins Copilot code review at a multiplier of 13 from June 1, 2026. Wire that review into every pull request automatically and the credit balance evaporates far faster than anyone budgeted.

The operating rule falls out of this without much argument. Expensive features run only when a human asks for them; automatic triggers are reserved for cheap features. Skip that and the credit-balance chart goes over a cliff in the first week of the month.

Working from token rates up to an organisation-level budget is a separate exercise, covered in Governing AI Token Spend at the Org Level.

Where this table splits the field

The emptier the row, the harder that tool is to defend in a budget review. Cursor's team analytics leans on activity metrics and does not put spend on the same screen. You get chats sent, tab completions accepted and the AI share of committed code, but not who spent what. Activity is visible and money is not, so when an overage lands there is no clean way to attribute it to a person.

Claude Code sits at the other end. /usage puts session token statistics next to plan usage bars and breaks recent consumption down by skill, subagent, plugin and MCP server as a share of the total. When long context or cache misses account for 10 percent or more of recent usage, it flags that too. On the organisation side, OpenTelemetry exports metrics such as claude_code.cost.usage in USD, claude_code.token.usage and claude_code.lines_of_code.count into your own observability stack. That is the only path here that lets you build a per-developer cost panel in your own Grafana instead of waiting for a vendor dashboard.

The documented baseline is useful too. Anthropic puts enterprise deployments at roughly 13 dollars per developer per active day and 150 to 250 dollars per developer per month, with 90 percent of users staying under 30 dollars per active day. Use those numbers to check a pilot against, not to quote a budget from. A team averaging 40 dollars per active day has a usage-pattern problem; a team averaging 5 dollars has a tool nobody is actually using.

When a tool shows no dollar figures at all, there is a procedure for measuring cost yourself, written up in The Dollars Vanished From Your AI Coding Tool.

Where context turns into cost

In agent-style tools the thing that inflates a bill is habit, not the model. The Claude Code documentation is specific about the causes.

  • Long sessions. The full conversation ships with every request, so a one-line question in a session left open all day still draws usage for the entire day's history
  • Cache misses. The first message after a break longer than the cache lifetime reprocesses the whole context. That lifetime is an hour on a subscription, five minutes once you are drawing on usage credits, and five minutes by default on an API key or cloud provider
  • Too many MCP servers. Tool definitions eat the window. The documentation recommends CLI tools such as gh, aws and gcloud as more context-efficient than MCP servers
  • Agent teams. Teammates running in plan mode use roughly seven times the tokens of a standard session, which is why the feature ships disabled
  • Compacting a large context. /compact has to read the conversation it summarises, so it is itself a large request. When you do not need continuity, /clear costs nothing

Paste that list onto the first page of your onboarding document and pilot costs drop visibly. Most overspend comes not from hard work but from a session nobody cleared.

Questions to hand straight to security review

  1. On our plan, are code and prompts used to train models? The answer varies by plan, so never answer this at the brand level
  2. Does the switch that turns training off sit with the org admin or with each developer?
  3. How many days do conversations and code persist on vendor servers? Is a no-retention option part of the standard contract or a separate negotiation?
  4. Can we extract per-user usage and spend? Screen only, or an API as well?
  5. Can ceilings be set per organisation, per group and per member?
  6. Is there a filter that catches suggestions resembling public code?
  7. Does the contract carry an indemnity clause for IP disputes over generated code?
  8. Do SSO, SCIM and audit logging integrate with our identity provider?
  9. What stays on the laptop? Claude Code documents that session transcripts are cached in plaintext under ~/.claude/projects/ for 30 days by default, with the period configurable
  10. Can telemetry be turned off, and is one environment variable enough to do it?

Question nine is the one organisations skip. Signing a no-retention agreement on the server side while plaintext session logs pile up on developer laptops for a month means that agreement is half honoured.

That last row gets misread constantly. An open-source client says nothing about whether your data trains a model. Cline, Gemini CLI and Aider are all open, and the prompt still travels to some provider's servers. The document security review needs is that provider's terms.

Cursor's Privacy Mode is inherited: new members join a team with the team's setting already applied. That beats asking each person to enable it. Copilot's training toggle on Free and Pro is a personal setting, so a company that wants to enforce it has to move to a paid organisation plan. A policy that says everyone uses personal plans but turns training off is not enforceable, because there is no way to verify it.

Designing a two-week pilot

One two-week pilot is more accurate than three selection meetings. It has exactly one requirement: decide the measurements before you start. Decide them afterwards and the criteria drift toward the results.

MeasurementHow to capture itHow to judge it
Spend per person per dayRecord the tool's usage screen or telemetry dailyCompare against the documented baseline: about $13 per active day, 90 percent under $30
Spend distributionTrack the median and the top decile separately, not the meanIf one person accounts for half the team's spend, that is a usage-pattern problem

Revert rateShare of tool-authored changes that were reverted or reworkedA high value cancels out the speed gain

Rules for running the pilot

  • Five to eight people, and fill half the group with people sceptical of the tool. A pilot staffed by enthusiasts always returns a good result
  • Do not run the same task through two tools. It doubles the time and adds little comparative information. Give each tool a different class of work instead
  • At the end of week one, publish the usage screen to the team. Behaviour changes the moment spend becomes visible
  • Put context commands such as /clear and /compact and a model-selection rule on the first page of the onboarding document
  • Open agent teams and parallel execution in week two. Enable them in week one and the cost data is contaminated
  • Do not sign after the pilot; during the pilot, actually click the spend ceiling and the spend report. Having a screen and having a usable screen are different things
  • Point the pilot at real production code. Sample projects carry small contexts, so both cost and quality come out unlike the real thing
  • In the closing retrospective, read the metrics first and collect impressions second. Reverse that order and impressions overwrite the numbers

Where costs spike and how to stop it

Budget overruns are rarely one dramatic event. They are small repeated habits. A table that maps symptoms to causes is more useful in practice than a list of tips.

SymptomCauseResponse
Half the credits gone in the first week of the monthAn expensive feature is wired to an automatic trigger, typically code reviewTurn off the automatic trigger and run it on request. Copilot code review carries a multiplier of 13

That last row is the summary of the whole article. The first seven are fixable in operations, but choosing a tool with no attribution is not fixable in operations. Which is why cost observability belongs alongside model quality as a selection criterion, not below it.

Lock-in and the cost of leaving

After about a year, switching gets hard. Knowing in advance what holds you lets you build those parts in a portable shape.

Asset you accumulateDoes it move?Cost to rebuild
Project instruction files such as CLAUDE.mdYes, it is textLow. Only the format changes
Custom skills and slash commandsThe format differs per toolMedium. The logic survives, the wrapper is rewritten
Hooks and automation scriptsHook event names are tool-specificMedium
MCP server configurationMoves between tools that support MCPLow
CI pipeline integrationThe invocation command is tool-specificHigh. This is exactly what bit people when Cascade ended
Team habitsThey do not moveThe most expensive item on this list
Usage and cost historyUsually does not moveYou rebuild the baseline from scratch
Having switched editorsExtensions, shortcuts and settings all come into playHigh

Two practical moves lower the exit cost. First, have automation call a shell script rather than the tool command. If the tool is only invoked inside that script, switching means editing one file. When Cascade shut down, the teams in trouble were the ones calling it straight from the pipeline. Second, commit instructions to the repository. Configuration that lives only inside a vendor account disappears with the contract.

Licensing and data handling

Two licences apply to every tool here and they get conflated constantly. The client licence governs the program on your machine. The service terms govern what happens to the prompt after it leaves. An open-source client settles only the first.

Client licences

  • Apache-2.0 clients: OpenAI Codex CLI, Gemini CLI and Aider are all Apache 2.0, so you can fork them, audit them and run modified builds internally
  • Open extension, commercial service: the Cline extension is free for individual developers and you pay only for inference, either through your own provider keys or through Cline credits
  • Proprietary clients: Claude Code, Cursor, GitHub Copilot, Devin Desktop, JetBrains AI and Amazon Q Developer ship as products, and their terms arrive with the subscription
  • An open client grants no data rights. Read the terms of whichever provider the key points at

Output rights and indemnity

  • Amazon Q Developer Pro includes IP indemnity. Of the ten tools here it is the one that states this on the pricing page, and for legal teams that is often the deciding line
  • GitHub Copilot offers a duplicate-detection filter: with public code suggestions blocked, it checks a suggestion together with about 150 characters of surrounding code against public code on GitHub
  • Cursor provides a SOC 2 Type II attestation on request, plus at-least-annual third-party penetration testing summaries through its trust portal
  • Anthropic offers Zero Data Retention for Claude Code on Claude for Enterprise, enabled per organisation after an eligibility check rather than bundled by default
  • Amazon Q Developer bills through an existing AWS account, which for many organisations removes the vendor onboarding and payment-method review entirely. That procedural fact decides more rollouts than any benchmark
  • Seat minimums belong in the same conversation. Zed Business order-form contracts start at 25 seats, with self-serve available below that, so a six-person team and a sixty-person team are not evaluating the same product

Practical combinations

The arrangement that clears legal fastest in most organisations is a flat-seat tool for everyone, with agent-style tools approved individually. The first has a fixed budget and an easy explanation; the second stays small enough that overages remain contained. Open metered tools to everyone and the second month's invoice starts the conversation you were trying to avoid.

Frequently asked questions

If I have to pick one, what do I look at?

Settle two things first: whether the editor can change, and whether you need a spending ceiling. Those two cut the shortlist to two or three, and then you compare model quality. Start with quality and you will choose a strong tool that stalls in procurement.

I pay a flat fee, so why do I hit limits?

Most individual plans now wrap a usage budget inside the flat fee. Copilot Pro is $10 with $15 of credits, Cursor Pro is $20 with $20 of model usage, JetBrains AI Pro is $10 with 10 credits, and Zed Pro is $10 with $5 of tokens. Spend the budget and you stop or start paying overage.

Is running several tools wasteful?

Not when the axes differ. One in-editor tool and one terminal agent do not do the same job. Two tools on the same axis is the waste.

Does using an open-source tool make my data safe?

An open-source client and a no-training guarantee are unrelated. Cline, Gemini CLI and Aider all send prompts to some provider's servers. The document to read is that provider's terms.

What if a script still calls Cascade?

It stopped working after July 1, 2026 and needs to point at Devin Local. While you are in there, sweep internal documentation for windsurf.com links, which now redirect to devin.ai.

Where do I find model pricing?

Tool plans and model API rates are different axes. Per-million-token pricing, along with cache and batch discounts, is in The 2026 AI Model Comparison.
