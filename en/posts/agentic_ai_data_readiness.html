Fix Your Data Before Unleashing AI Agents - Martin Fowler Staged Autonomy Principles

Replacing implicit human intuition with explicit data contracts, audit lineage, and governed access layers

2026-09-14 Dev & Infra

Introduction: The Hidden Bottleneck Behind the Model Performance Race — Data Infrastructure

Much of the contemporary excitement surrounding artificial intelligence centers on foundation model scaling, multimodal token processing, and elaborate agent orchestration frameworks. Engineering teams routinely celebrate newly released reasoning models, believing that wrapping these engines with autonomous tool-calling loops will instantly unlock enterprise task delegation. Yet the moment these autonomous agents connect to corporate relational databases, enterprise resource planning (ERP) suites, and customer relationship management (CRM) systems, initiatives routinely stall. The root cause is rarely the cognitive capacity of the underlying language model. Rather, it is because the enterprise data fed into these systems was never engineered for autonomous machine consumption.

For more than three decades, enterprise data architectures have been designed exclusively for human analysts. Dashboards, business intelligence reports, and ad-hoc SQL exploratory queries were built on the fundamental assumption that a human being sits in front of the screen. Human workers inherently possess institutional memory, tribal knowledge, and healthy professional skepticism. They know which database tables contain legacy artifacts, recognize when a daily aggregate is suspiciously rounded, and double-check with colleagues over team chat whenever an anomaly surfaces. Imperfect, malformed, or stale data existed across all organizations, but the implicit context supplied by human judgment quietly absorbed the errors before decisions materialized.

Autonomous AI agents operate under a fundamentally distinct paradigm. As machines, they accept whatever data they are handed as unquestioned ground truth. When confronted with flawed schema representations or outdated numerical records, agents do not hesitate. They proceed with complete, unwavering confidence, computing results and firing external API side effects. When human intuition is removed without an architectural replacement, minor data defects cascade into immediate operational disasters. This reality was systematically unpacked in an architectural guide published on Martin Fowler's platform by Thoughtworks engineers Pramod Sadalage and Premanand Chandrasekaran. Their core thesis is direct: the most critical investment in agentic systems is not another orchestration framework, but a trustworthy data foundation coupled with disciplined data governance.

Relationship to Prior Discussions: The foundational theory of data contracts and how schemas fail to prevent semantic drift was examined in detail in our previous article, Moving Data Is Easier Than Agreeing What It Means. This current analysis moves beyond analytical warehouse storage to address the requirements of autonomous agentic systems that actively read, interpret, and execute state-changing write operations across enterprise infrastructure.

This article examines the four pillars of engineering data readiness for agentic systems: establishing data contracts with automated quarantine circuits, preserving decision rationales through agentic lineage, defining nouns, numbers, and verbs via declarative context layers, and safely expanding agent access across a spectrum from passive retrieval to live execution.

The Fundamental Difference Between Human and Machine Consumers: Agents Cannot Smell Bad Data

Human analysts possess an instinctive smell test for compromised data. When an analyst observes an inventory balance that has remained unchanged across peak season, a transaction timestamp recorded during a national bank holiday, or an unrevised unit price, they pause. They recall that pricing adjustments were agreed upon during recent committee meetings and seek verification before dispatching quotations. This innate capacity to detect subtle inconsistencies represents a massive volume of invisible context processing that human operators provided without explicit software engineering.

Large language models, by contrast, are gullible by design. As technologist Simon Willison observed, models place complete trust in whatever context is injected into their execution prompts. Consider an autonomous pricing agent tasked with quoting Product X. Yesterday, leadership updated the base price from $49.99 to $59.99, but due to caching or an uncompleted replication pipeline, the data table accessed by the agent still lists $49.99. The agent does not pause. It retrieves $49.99, presents the quotation, executes the checkout transaction, and costs the business $10 on every unit sold. Every algorithmic step the agent took was technically flawless according to its workflow definition, yet the business suffered quantifiable financial damage because the underlying data failed silently.

A stark divergence exists between executive confidence and operational reality. In the 2026 State of Data Integrity and AI Readiness report conducted by Precisely and Drexel University's LeBow College of Business across 505 data and analytics leaders, 88% believed their enterprise data was prepared for AI initiatives, yet 43% acknowledged data readiness as the single greatest bottleneck to realizing actual business value. Similarly, the KPMG Global AI Pulse survey of 2,145 executives revealed that nearly half of leadership teams now report AI deployment expenses outstripping financial benefits. Enterprisewide exposure to stale data remains a pervasive vulnerability.

Bridging this gap requires engineering five fundamental attributes into the data infrastructure itself, compensating for the implicit cognitive labor humans previously contributed.

2.1 Silent Data Errors and the Risk of Confidently Wrong Actions

Traditional software failures are typically loud. Missing database columns, syntax violations, and broken network connections emit fatal error codes and trigger immediate operational alarms. In stark contrast, semantic drift and data staleness represent quiet failures. Pipeline runs conclude with green status codes, metrics calculate without crashing, and user interfaces render smoothly. When an agent consumes these quietly corrupted records, it proceeds without doubt, propagating miscalculations throughout downstream inventory, billing, and scheduling workflows.

2.2 Turning Human Tacit Knowledge into Explicit Rules for Data Infrastructure

Deploying agentic systems safely requires translating unwritten organizational knowledge into machine-executable software constraints. The informal understanding that a batch job occasionally lags must become a programmatic freshness SLA. The unspoken consensus that internal testing accounts must be excluded from gross margins must become a permanent filter within the version-controlled semantic layer. Without encoding these implicit realities directly into the data foundation, scaling agentic autonomy inevitably amplifies operational risk.

  • Automated trust verification: Validating record schemas and pipeline arrival timestamps before agent query execution
  • Explicit semantic contextualization: Defining business concepts and formulas in version-controlled declarations
  • Reconstructible decision pathways: Preserving full reasoning chains to satisfy retrospective internal and regulatory audits
  • Architectural access boundaries: Discarding monolithic service accounts in favor of scoped, delegated user permissions
  • Graduated operational empowerment: Advancing agents through strictly monitored autonomy tiers based on empirical performance

Data Contracts and Quarantine Patterns: Blocking Contaminated Inputs Upstream

Preventing corrupted records from reaching autonomous agents requires treating data schemas as strict law rather than advisory guidelines. While the past decade favored flexible, schemaless architectures for human exploration, loose schemas introduce catastrophic failure points for autonomous machines. Implementing data contracts as code via industry specifications such as the Open Data Contract Standard (ODCS) establishes non-negotiable boundaries enforced directly within continuous integration pipelines and live ingestion layers.

The pricing contract presented in the Thoughtworks analysis provides a concrete reference. It establishes rigid logical and physical data types, enforces an SQL-based business constraint ensuring unit prices exceed zero, restricts currency strings to valid ISO identifiers (USD, EUR, GBP), and defines a freshness SLA requiring the ingestion timestamp to reside within the preceding 24 hours.

apiVersion: v3.1.0
kind: DataContract
id: product-pricing
name: Product Pricing
version: 1.0.0
status: active
schema:
  - name: product_pricing
    physicalType: table
    properties:
      - name: product_id
        logicalType: string
        physicalType: varchar(64)
        required: true
        unique: true
        primaryKey: true
        primaryKeyPosition: 1
      - name: price
        logicalType: number
        physicalType: decimal
        required: true
        quality:
          - type: sql
            description: Every price must be greater than zero
            query: SELECT min({property}) FROM {object}
            mustBeGreaterThan: 0
      - name: currency
        logicalType: string
        physicalType: varchar(3)
        required: true
        quality:
          - type: sql
            description: Currency must be a supported ISO code
            query: SELECT count(*) FROM {object} WHERE {property} NOT IN ('USD', 'EUR', 'GBP')
            mustBe: 0
      - name: ingested_at
        logicalType: timestamp
        physicalType: timestamp
        required: true
        slaProperties:
          - property: latency
            value: 24
            unit: h
            element: product_pricing.ingested_at

Enforcement occurs along three distinct vectors: strict schema compliance checking data types and structural integrity, automated freshness SLAs monitoring ingestion latency, and continuous deployment gates that block infrastructure updates whenever data contracts fail validation tests. Tying freshness SLAs to successful data ingestion rather than record modification ensures that stable catalog items are not falsely labeled stale while simultaneously preventing frozen pipelines from masquerading as healthy.

3.1 Combining Schema Enforcement with Freshness SLAs

Data contracts achieve operational efficacy only when structural validation is coupled with temporal constraints. Even if column data types conform perfectly, data that has languished past its freshness threshold represents toxic input for real-time customer-facing agents. While a human executive reviewing weekly trends might tolerate a one-day caching window, an autonomous agent issuing live quotes requires verified freshness. Setting distinct SLAs per dataset and consumer profile ensures appropriate boundaries.

3.2 The Role of the Quarantine Pattern and Dead-Letter Queues

Declaring a contract is incomplete without establishing circuit breakers for contract violations. The quarantine pattern provides this necessary mechanism. Raw data streaming from enterprise APIs, transactional databases, and message queues passes through validation gates before touching agent-accessible data stores. Records passing all structural, freshness, and quality rules flow into certified analytical stores. Records failing any single constraint are immediately routed to a dead-letter queue for human triage.

Crucially, bad data remains entirely invisible to the agent. In the pricing scenario, if the ingestion timestamp exceeds 24 hours, the record is quarantined. When queried, the agent responds with a graceful failure mode: "Current verified pricing data is unavailable." Failing safely by declaring missing information and escalating to human staff protects the organization far more effectively than allowing an agent to confidently quote stale numbers.

3.3 Separating the Agent-Access Layer (Gold and Above) in a Medallion Architecture

The medallion architecture, widely popularized by Databricks for organizing lakehouse systems, must be adapted to enforce agent boundaries. Raw, immutable Bronze layers and validated Silver layers exist exclusively for data engineering, lineage auditing, and human debugging. Autonomous agents must be strictly restricted to Gold tiers or above, where datasets are certified, semantic models compile, and metrics are guaranteed.

Furthermore, the Thoughtworks authors highlight an emerging 'Adaptive Gold' tier. At DataHub's CONTEXT 2025 conference, Apple presented autonomous agents acting as digital stewards of its enterprise data catalog, continually monitoring metadata, identifying documentation gaps, and suggesting catalog revisions. Adaptive Gold extends this pattern to analytical views, allowing curated agents to materialize frequently queried data combinations into governed warehouse views. These architectural guarantees must equally apply to unstructured RAG pipelines, validating chunk metadata, indexing timestamps, and embedding distribution shifts before retrieval occurs.

  • Declarative contract authoring: Adopting ODCS standards to programmatically define types, constraints, and ranges
  • Ingestion-based latency monitoring: Benchmarking freshness against actual pipeline load timestamps rather than raw values
  • Automated quarantine routing: Shunting invalid records into dead-letter queues to prevent agent exposure
  • Graceful operational degradation: Requiring agents to acknowledge missing data rather than guessing stale metrics
  • Restricted medallion visibility: Isolating raw Bronze and Silver tiers from agents while exposing only certified Gold tiers

Audit Trails and Agent Lineage: Recording Not Just What, But Why

Providing verified data addresses only half of the architectural equation. Once agents execute autonomous decisions across corporate environments, governance requirements escalate dramatically. Consider an agentic trade finance system processing commercial letters of credit. The agent evaluates customer KYC compliance, verifies international sanctions registries, analyzes credit risk limits, and approves a $2.4 million letter of credit within 30 seconds. If an internal audit or banking regulator inquires six months later why that specific transaction was cleared, standard infrastructure logs will prove entirely insufficient.

Traditional audit logs only preserve what took place. They show that an automated service account queried compliance tables, touched an external sanctions API, and updated a database row at specific timestamps. What they cannot explain is why. Traditional logging fails to capture why sanctions verification preceded credit checks, why documentation discrepancies were categorized as non-material, or which alternative decisions were evaluated and discarded. This disconnect between what and why represents an existential governance hazard.

Resolving this challenge requires implementing 'agentic lineage'—an evolution of traditional data lineage borrowed from distributed observability. By employing traces and spans, the entire lifecycle of a transaction (such as letter of credit LC-4892) is captured as a unified trace. Within this trace, individual spans record discrete operations: Span 1 verifies customer KYC credentials, Span 2 confirms sanctions clearance via the OFAC API, Span 3 checks credit limits against the policy engine, and the concluding span records the final approval, including a 94% confidence metric and the complete chain of reasoning.

Regulatory mandates are rapidly turning these architectural patterns into binding law. Under the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act (EU AI Act), Article 12 explicitly mandates that high-risk AI systems maintain automated event logs throughout their lifecycle to ensure traceability. Article 19 mandates that providers retain these operational logs for at least six months. Non-compliance triggers severe penalties reaching up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher. Regardless of geographic jurisdiction, the ability to reconstruct why an agent acted remains essential for debugging failures, defending legitimate actions, and maintaining enterprise trust.

4.1 The Critical Gap Between Traditional Logs and Agentic Lineage

Standard infrastructure metrics record deterministic events. Agents, however, are non-deterministic, selecting tool execution paths based on prompt contexts and model reasoning. Reconstructing past agent behavior requires recording not just input-output payloads, but the causal chain of intermediate conclusions that motivated subsequent actions. Capturing the relational causality between discovered facts and selected tools is the foundation of agentic lineage.

4.2 Distributed Tracing (Trace and Span) and Regulatory Compliance (EU AI Act)

Leveraging distributed tracing abstractions like those in Jaeger and Zipkin provides a proven mental model for agent engineering. Emerging platforms such as OpenTelemetry for AI, Langfuse, and Arize Phoenix capture multi-step agent trajectories with granular span data. Meeting the statutory requirements of EU AI Act Articles 12 and 19 requires that these traces remain structured, searchable, and stored in long-term retention infrastructure capable of producing complete retrospective audits on demand.

4.3 Defending Against the Lethal Trifecta With Delegated Access and Just-in-Time Credentials

Security architectures must dismantle monolithic service accounts. If an agent executes an operation on behalf of Alice, it must operate strictly with Alice's delegated permissions rather than possessing omnipotent administrative access across the entire customer base. When an auditor asks who accessed an account, the answer must definitively reflect Alice's agent acting on Alice's specific behalf.

Coupling delegated access with just-in-time (JIT) credentials mitigates severe attack vectors. Instead of long-lived API keys, agents receive short-lived tokens scoped specifically to individual tasks (such as a five-minute read token for an OFAC query). This architecture dismantles what Simon Willison defines as the 'lethal trifecta': concurrent access to private data, exposure to untrusted external text, and external communication channels. Restricting credentials and privileges ensures that prompt injection attacks cannot compromise broader corporate systems.

  • Causal decision logging: Preserving intermediate reasoning spans that explain why specific actions were chosen
  • Trace-and-span instrumentation: Standardizing telemetry using OpenTelemetry and dedicated AI observability frameworks
  • Statutory compliance retention: Retaining comprehensive decision traces for at least six months under EU AI Act mandates
  • Delegated user identity: Ensuring agents inherit the exact permission scope of the human user initiating the request
  • Just-in-time token issuance: Discarding persistent credentials in favor of task-scoped, short-lived authorization tokens
  • Lethal trifecta mitigation: Enforcing least-privilege access boundaries to prevent prompt-injection exfiltration

Three Axes of the Context Layer: Declarative Versioning for Nouns, Numbers, and Verbs

Even with verified data and granular telemetry, autonomous agents will falter if business concepts remain ambiguous. When asked to compute third-quarter revenue for Product X, human analysts know instinctively which tables to join, that revenue denotes net receipts after deducting promotional rebates, and that the corporate fiscal calendar begins in February. Deprived of this domain context, an autonomous agent either hallucinates column mappings or generates syntactically valid but commercially incorrect SQL queries.

The context layer bridges this institutional knowledge deficit. Expanding beyond traditional BI semantic layers, the context layer provides a declarative framework defining enterprise entities (nouns), metric calculations (numbers), and executable operations (verbs). Agents never interact with raw database schemas directly; instead, they query and act through version-controlled definitions that constrain their operational boundaries.

Model TierLinguistic RoleFunctional DefinitionExecution Target
Domain ModelNounsDeclares business entities, relationships, and conceptual constraintsConsultative knowledge graph; contains no direct query execution path
Semantic ModelNumbersDefines version-controlled metric and dimension formulas compiled to SQLAnalytical warehouse; guarantees identical calculation logic across consumers
Capability ModelVerbsDeclares permitted live operations, preconditions, and reversibility classesLive operational systems; manages guarded state changes via tools and APIs

Managing business metrics as code is illustrated through dbt MetricFlow declarations. In the snippet below, the revenue metric is explicitly codified as `order_amount - discount_amount`. This definition is committed to source control, reviewed via pull requests, and compiled deterministically whenever an agent processes a financial query.

semantic_models:
  - name: orders
    model: ref('orders')
    defaults:
      agg_time_dimension: order_date
    entities:
      - name: order_id
        type: primary
      - name: customer_id
        type: foreign
    dimensions:
      - name: order_date
        type: time
        type_params:
          time_granularity: day
    measures:
      - name: revenue
        agg: sum
        expr: order_amount - discount_amount
        create_metric: true

The contrast between unconstrained agent SQL generation and semantic model compilation is profound. Without governance, an agent guesses table structures and omits essential deductions. With a semantic model, the query engine compiles the request against verified definitions, producing accurate, constrained SQL.

-- Unconstrained agent guess without a semantic model
SELECT SUM(amount) FROM sales_data WHERE product = 'Product X' AND quarter = 'Q3';

-- Deterministically compiled SQL constrained by semantic definitions
SELECT SUM(order_amount - discount_amount)
FROM orders o
JOIN products p ON o.product_id = p.id
WHERE p.name = 'Product X'
  AND o.order_date BETWEEN '2025-07-01' AND '2025-09-30';

5.1 Separating Domain, Semantic, and Function Models With a Shared Vocabulary

A common architectural misstep involves managing metric entities and operational tools in isolated silos. If the customer entity in the semantic reporting model diverges from the customer identifier utilized by refund tools in the capability model, severe discrepancies arise. The domain model must establish a shared enterprise vocabulary underpinning both quantitative aggregation and live state manipulation.

Every model within the context layer must reside in version control. When business logic evolves—such as altering refund windows or updating fiscal calendars—updates occur through formal code reviews, automated CI verification, and staged promotion across environments. Codifying business rules eliminates fragmented interpretations across ad-hoc scripts and rogue dashboards.

5.2 Exploring Dynamic Relationships and Temporality With Knowledge Graphs

While semantic models excel at tabular aggregations, complex agent workflows require navigating multi-hop relationships across time. Tracing how a customer purchased a product, submitted support tickets, and churned following a subscription overhaul involves traversing unpredictable relationship depths. Storing the domain model within a knowledge graph enables agents to traverse these pathways naturally.

Technologies such as Microsoft GraphRAG and Graphiti (both highlighted on the Thoughtworks Tech Radar) empower agents to perform hierarchical community detection and temporal reasoning. Combining semantic metric models with temporal knowledge graphs equips autonomous agents with durable institutional memory comparable to experienced staff members.

  • Declarative metrics as code: Maintaining metric definitions in Git rather than buried within BI tools or ad-hoc SQL
  • Deterministic SQL compilation: Eliminating agent schema guessing by routing queries through governed semantic compilers
  • Unified domain vocabulary: Aligning analytical metric models and operational capability models on identical entity keys
  • Adversarial test validation: Correcting missing semantic model definitions rather than endlessly tweaking prompts
  • Temporal knowledge graph navigation: Supplementing relational tables with graph representations for multi-hop reasoning
  • Focused initial scope: Beginning context layer implementation on contested core metrics before attempting full enterprise ontologies

From Read to Write: Staged Autonomy Expansion and Its Entry Barriers

Once data foundation trustworthiness and semantic context are established, engineering focus shifts to access architecture. Many organizations remain restricted to passive retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), where agents locate documents but cannot interact with live infrastructure. A complete agentic architecture spans a spectrum from passive retrieval, to real-time queries, and ultimately to state-altering write-back capabilities.

Formalized in Microsoft's Cloud Adoption Framework for AI as RAG plus read and write primitives, this spectrum balances escalating business capability against operational risk. Access boundaries must be segregated and governed deliberately, whether exposed via the Model Context Protocol (MCP) or native enterprise APIs.

Access TierCore FunctionalityMCP PrimitiveInherent Risk & Governance Guardrails
Tier 1: RetrievalVector search, document lookup, handbook referencesResources (Read-Only)Lowest risk. Enforces chunk freshness SLAs and metadata provenance
Tier 2: Real-Time QueryLive database reads, infrastructure health checksTools (Read-Only)Moderate risk. Requires query performance throttling and semantic constraints
Tier 3: Write-BackTicket generation, payment execution, record updatesTools (State-Changing)Highest risk. Enforces live precondition validation and reversibility gates

A purchase order payment failure scenario demonstrates how these tiers orchestrate within a unified workflow. When an employee reports an issue, the agent retrieves troubleshooting documentation (Tier 1: Resource), checks the live operational status of the payment gateway (Tier 2: Read Tool), and creates a support ticket in Jira or ServiceNow (Tier 3: Write Tool). Unifying these capabilities into a single governed pipeline delivers true operational automation.

6.1 The Limits of RAG and the Risks of Real-Time Query and Write-Back Operations

Retrieval-augmented generation remains safe because errors manifest as incorrect text on a screen. Write-back actions, however, modify database state, transfer funds, or trigger hardware workflows. Consequently, write permissions must be granted incrementally across a staged autonomy ladder.

Organizations should advance agents through four distinct operational phases: Shadow Mode (agent recommends, human decides and executes), Supervised Mode (agent stages action, human approves or rejects), Autonomous with Guardrails (agent executes within predefined parameters and escalates exceptions), and Fully Autonomous (agent acts independently with retrospective sampling). Agents must earn promotion up this ladder through empirical test evaluations and demonstrated production accuracy.

6.2 The MCP Anti-Pattern: Moving Past Naive 1:1 API Wrapping to Business-Function Design

A prevalent engineering mistake involves naively wrapping existing REST endpoints one-to-one as MCP tools. Exposing dozens of granular endpoints like `get_po_status`, `create_po_ticket`, and `create_network_ticket` leads to tool proliferation. Language models experience marked degradation in routing accuracy when selecting among dozens of near-identical tools. For this reason, the Thoughtworks Tech Radar placed naive API-to-MCP conversion on HOLD.

The superior pattern consolidates endpoints into 5 to 10 parameterized business capabilities accompanied by rich natural language descriptions. A unified `check_service_status` tool accepting service and region parameters, coupled with a parameterized `create_support_ticket` tool, enables models to reason accurately about tool selection.

6.3 Precondition Validation and Reversibility-Based Access Control

State-altering capability models require two foundational guardrails. First, preconditions must be checked deterministically against live system state at the exact moment of execution. For example, processing a refund requires confirming that an unrefunded original charge exists within the user's authorization threshold. Retrieved policy text can inform recommendations, but it must never act as an authorization gate. Hardcoded precondition logic prevents injected text from granting unauthorized access.

Second, permissions should be categorized by operational reversibility rather than transaction size alone. A $50,000 internal ledger reclassification can be reversed via accounting adjustments, representing low operational exposure. Conversely, an irreversible $200 wire transfer to an external account carries permanent loss risk. Irreversible operations must mandate human approval regardless of the agent's autonomy stage.

  • Spectrum progression: Advancing from read-only Resources to real-time Tools only after verifying governance infrastructure
  • Capability-driven tool design: Avoiding 1:1 API endpoint wrapping in favor of 5 to 10 rich, parameterized capabilities
  • Decoupled text authorization: Using retrieved documents for context while enforcing authorization via deterministic rules
  • Live precondition evaluation: Validating operational prerequisites against real-time state immediately prior to execution
  • Reversibility-based escalation: Structuring approval gates around action reversibility rather than transaction amount
  • Staged autonomy promotion: Requiring empirical evaluation benchmarks before advancing agents from shadow to autonomous modes

An Adoption Strategy for Korean Enterprises: Data as a Product and Priorities

A persistent challenge in enterprise AI implementations is the organizational void surrounding data ownership. Centralized data platform teams maintain pipeline infrastructure but lack the business authority to define operational semantics. Meanwhile, domain teams in finance, sales, and logistics modify database columns without realizing their schema updates break downstream autonomous agents.

Sustaining an AI-ready architecture requires adopting the Data-as-a-Product discipline established by Data Mesh. Every data contract, semantic metric, and capability tool must have an identified organizational owner. When a contract violation blocks a continuous integration deployment or two departments dispute metric formulas, designated owners must possess the authority and accountability to resolve definitions. Without human ownership, data architectures inevitably decay.

Architectural dependencies operate strictly from the bottom up. A context layer built upon untrusted data collapses, and autonomous tools unleashed without semantic context generate chaos. Observability, however, is not a sequential phase to be deferred; distributed telemetry must be embedded across every workflow from day one.

Engineering leadership should prioritize the following tactical execution roadmap:

7.1 A Pre-Flight Checklist for Connecting Internal Systems (ERP, CRM, DB)

Before connecting autonomous agents to core enterprise databases, organizations must isolate high-risk datasets (pricing, inventory, customer billing) and establish explicit freshness SLAs. Pipelines must incorporate automated quarantine dead-letter queues to catch stale or malformed records before agents consume them.

7.2 Why Data-as-a-Product Ownership Matters More Than Tool Adoption

Prioritize codifying contested business metrics into version-controlled semantic models over experimenting with new agent orchestration libraries. Establishing explicit semantic consensus across business stakeholders represents the single highest-leverage investment for boosting agentic task accuracy.

  • Day-one telemetry instrumentation: Deploying OpenTelemetry traces and spans across all agent workflows before deployment
  • Targeted data contract adoption: Establishing strict contracts and freshness SLAs on mission-critical transactional datasets
  • Semantic context prioritization: Achieving greater accuracy gains through version-controlled metric models than model swaps
  • Segregated read-before-write access: Rolling out read-only resources first and introducing write capabilities under strict oversight
  • Designated product stewardship: Assigning named domain owners accountable for contract maintenance and metric definitions
