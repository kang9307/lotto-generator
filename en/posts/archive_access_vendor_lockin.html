Outsourcing Custody - Access Breaks Long Before the Media Does

Contract checkpoints, catalogs and restore drills that keep the ability to retrieve on your side of the table

Aug 16, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: The disk is fine, the files just will not come out

Bring up long-term storage and most people picture a dead drive. The way archives are actually lost is far less dramatic. The server is running, the disk is healthy, the files are exactly where they were put. You simply cannot reach them.

In late July 2026 that scenario went to court in the United States. Nine PBS, a public television station in St. Louis, was reported to have sued data center operator Iron Mountain in a Denver court. According to the complaint, the station had contracted with a cloud storage provider, Open Source Storage, to hold more than 50 terabytes of broadcast, video and photographic material accumulated over more than seventy years, and that data physically resided in the data center operator's facility. Access was reported to have been cut off in March 2026, after which the storage provider went defunct.

According to reporting, the data center operator said it could not simply hand the material back because its contractual counterparty was the storage provider rather than the station, and because the equipment holding the data belonged to that provider. The same coverage quoted the company as saying it had met its contractual obligations at every step. A court was subsequently reported to have barred deletion or alteration of the material and set out a framework for retrieval, but no ruling on the merits has been issued.

This article does not take a side in that dispute. Which account is correct is for the court to decide, and the case appears here only as an illustration. What follows sits upstream of it: when you hand custody to someone else, how do you build a state in which you can still get the material back, on both the contractual and the technical side?

This is not legal advice. Contract terms below are presented as checkpoints for where to look, not as wording to adopt. Actual drafting and the applicability of any statute should be settled with a lawyer.

1. The real risk is not damaged media

Discussions of outsourced storage risk usually drift toward media lifetime, and that is where they go wrong. Tape longevity, annualized drive failure rates, bit rot. All real problems, all well covered already, and all defended probabilistically by keeping several copies.

What actually renders material unusable does not arrive as a probability. It arrives as a switch.

  • An invoice goes unpaid, or a price increase is not agreed, and the account is locked
  • The provider closes, or retires that particular service line
  • An acquisition or bankruptcy replaces your counterparty, and the new owner never made your promises
  • The files are there, but the index, the decryption keys or the proprietary tooling are the provider's assets
  • A dispute begins and a period opens in which nobody may touch anything

All five share one property. No hardware failed. Media strategy alone does not stop any of them. You can hold three copies, but if all three live inside the same provider they lock together. A large share of organizations that believe they satisfy 3-2-1 have in fact copied the same thing three times inside a single contract.

Borrowing a metric from backup design makes the gap obvious. Recovery time objective asks how many hours it takes to bring things back after an incident. Outsourced custody demands the question one step earlier: can it be brought back at all, and who holds that switch? Without an answer to the first, the number attached to the second means nothing. Any design promising recovery in hours assumes access rights are still alive.

2. Five paths to losing access

Splitting the risk into types shows which defense answers which failure. The two right-hand columns carry the weight: the types that extra copies solve and the types they do not require completely different responses.

Read the table down its middle column and every entry says healthy. And the two rows where adding copies does not help, economic cutoff and technical lock-in, are defensible only through contract and format. That is where the center of gravity of this article sits.

3. When your counterparty and the owner of the media are different

This is the structure that gets missed most often. The company whose name is on your agreement and the owner of the building and equipment your data physically sits in are frequently not the same. Nothing in the service interface reveals the layers.

  1. You, the owner of the material
  2. The service provider, the party you contracted with, running the interface, API, billing and support
  3. The infrastructure provider supplying racks, power and cooling, sometimes the storage hardware as well
  4. The hardware owner, whoever actually bought the equipment, which may be layer two, layer three, or a leasing company

When layer two disappears, layer three does not know you. From their position you are a third party with no contractual relationship, and opening their customer's equipment on your say-so would breach that customer's agreement. This is not a story about anyone acting in bad faith. It is structural: the contracts form a chain and you are holding exactly one link.

Sketching the layers does not take long either. Compare the counterparty name on the first page of the agreement with the name on the status page or the data center location notice, and half the picture appears. If they differ, at least one contract you never negotiated sits in between, and its terms were written without you in the room.

So the questions to ask before signing widen one step beyond service features, out to where the physical thing sits and whose it is.

  • Will they tell you the physical location and the operator of the facility?
  • Are there subprocessors, and is a change to them notified in advance?
  • If the service provider vanishes, does any document name a party you can approach directly?
  • Are ownership of the data and ownership of the media separated in writing?

That last item is the point reported as the crux of the case above. When the owner of the data and the owner of the equipment underneath it differ, the fight is not about ownership but about the route to access. Being the rightful owner does not by itself put files in your hands.

4. Contract terms worth checking

What follows is a list of where to look when the agreement is open in front of you. It does not prescribe wording; it pairs each item with what happens later if that item is missing.

Right to exportCan you demand a full export during the term and after termination?Your leverage at termination is zero

Two additions. First, an export clause you have never exercised is not far from having none. The clause guarantees a right, not a speed. Second, read the agreement from its ending. Contracts are written by the people starting the relationship, and the trouble arrives when it ends.

5. Keep the inventory yourself: catalogs and checksums

Of all technical preparations, the one with the best return is keeping your own record of what you handed over. The data is heavy; the inventory is light. Replicating tens of terabytes is hard, but a table listing which files those are and what each one hashes to fits on a laptop.

  • A path or identifier assigned under your own scheme, not the key the provider issued
  • File size and creation and modification timestamps
  • A checksum, preferably SHA-256 family, with the algorithm name recorded alongside the value
  • The device, software and settings that produced the original: capture codec, scan resolution, encoding parameters
  • At least one line of description, because a filename alone means nothing twenty years later
  • An export priority tier, so you know what comes first if you can only pull half of it in a hurry

Checksums matter for two separate reasons. One is integrity. The other is negotiation. If you can prove that what came back matches the original, you can test the claim that everything was returned, and you can name precisely what is missing and ask again. Receive material without an inventory and you will not even know it is short, sometimes for years.

A catalog built once and forgotten is stale within six months. Two forms survive in practice: bind the inventory update into the procedure for adding material, or pull the provider's own listing periodically and reconcile it against your copy. The second turns reconciliation itself into a check. The moment the file count or total size diverges, you have grounds to ask what happened, and you ask that month rather than years later.

Settling on a packaging convention pays off later. In digital preservation, BagIt, which ships a manifest and checksums alongside the payload, is widely used and is documented as RFC 8493. Whatever shape the provider hands you, receiving it into that form on your side leaves a record of what existed at which point in time.

6. Spreading copies and choosing open formats

The 3-2-1 rule is well known, and the fundamentals are covered in Complete Cloud Backup Guide — Keep Your Data Safe. What changes when you reread it in a custody context is how you count two different media.

  • A second region at the same provider counts as one copy. Lock the account and both go
  • Anything tied to the same payment method or administrator account is also one copy
  • A genuinely separate copy is separated by contract, not by medium: a different company, or physical media you hold yourself
  • One offline copy defends against account disputes as well as ransomware. What is not on a network cannot be locked

The objection that a second contract costs money always comes up, and it is fair. There is no need to duplicate everything. Grade it instead. Material that can be remade and material that can never be remade deserve different treatment. Camera originals, signed agreements, unique scans and similar irreplaceable items often amount to a few percent of the whole, and that few percent is most of what is actually lost when something goes wrong.

The format rule is simple: prefer formats with a published specification, multiple implementations, and at least one of those open source. The US Library of Congress publishes preferred and acceptable formats by material type in its Recommended Formats Statement, and while there is no obligation to follow it, seeing how far your choices sit from that list is worth the hour.

  • Record container and codec separately, since MP4 is a container name and says nothing about how the content was encoded
  • State lossy versus lossless explicitly, and keep preservation masters separate from access copies
  • If a proprietary format is unavoidable, archive the version, licence and installer of the software that opens it
  • Encryption creates format dependency by itself, so document the algorithm and key management in a form a human can read

7. Restore drills: cadence and scope

Storage you have never pulled from is not storage. Backup practice has repeated that for years, and in outsourced custody it carries more weight. A backup you run yourself throws an error when it fails. Outsourced custody gives you no signal at all when something goes wrong. The signal appears the first time you try to retrieve, which is usually the worst possible moment.

DrillCadenceScopeWhat it verifies
Sample pullMonthlyA few dozen random filesThe access path is alive and checksums match your records
Partial restoreQuarterlyOne collection or projectMetadata travels with the files and they actually open
Export rehearsalAnnuallyOne to five percent of the wholeMeasured throughput, the basis for estimating a full export
Full exportBefore renewal, or at the first sign of troubleEverythingWhether the right actually functions

The number a drill must produce is time: request to first byte, and first byte to completion. With those two you can compute how many days a full export takes, and that figure becomes your negotiating ground. A calculation showing the current rate implies several weeks is far stronger than asking someone to hurry.

A drill must also be executable by someone else working only from the written steps. If the same person runs it every time, you are testing their memory rather than the procedure. Staff turnover is certain to happen, and the procedure document surviving while the ability to execute it quietly disappears is the most common silent failure in outsourced custody.

8. Surviving decades: obsolescence and metadata

What separates an archive from a backup is the time axis. A backup cares about yesterday and last month; an archive cares about twenty years from now. That gap creates several distinct problems.

  • Media obsolescence — the tape is fine but drives for that generation are discontinued, and tape formats typically stop reading back after a few generations
  • Codec obsolescence — the file opens, but the decoder for it is dropped from current operating systems
  • Software obsolescence — the asset management system that organized the material is retired, the files remain and the relationships between them do not
  • Organizational obsolescence — nobody left knows what the material is. The most common and the quietest

Metadata dies first. Files follow when you copy them, but what was shot where and when generally lives in a management database, and moving systems leaves it behind. The remedy is modest: keep metadata as text next to the files. Sidecar file or manifest, one human-readable form must always travel with the payload even if every database disappears.

Periodic migration belongs in the plan too. Long-term preservation is not a matter of depositing something and forgetting it, but of repeatedly moving it onto new media and new formats every few years. As a maturity reference, the NDSA Levels of Digital Preservation is widely cited, with v2.1 released in March 2026. It works well for locating yourself on axes such as storage, integrity, control and metadata. Storage without a migration plan is not preservation but postponement.

Write the cost and effort of migration into the budget in advance. If storage appears in the ledger only as a monthly figure, the day you must move media or formats that work becomes an unbudgeted task, and unbudgeted tasks slip until a staff change erases them entirely. Simply noting the next migration window beside the storage line makes the conversation far faster when the moment arrives.

9. What statutes give you, using Korea as an example

In Korea, Article 27 of the Act on the Development of Cloud Computing and Protection of Users addresses protection of user information. It requires a provider to return user information and destroy its own holdings when a contract ends, and where the provider intends to wind down its business, to notify users and return their information before the closing date. Where return is effectively impossible, destruction is required, and violations carry administrative fines.

Even so, one article does not resolve a situation like the one above. Practical gaps remain.

  • An abrupt collapse leaves nobody in place to perform the notification duty
  • The statute does not fix the format or the speed of return. A pile of files with no structure is still a return
  • The relationship does not reach directly into facilities and equipment your provider subcontracted
  • With a foreign provider under foreign governing law, applicability itself is the first question

Statute is the floor and contract is the ceiling. The presence of a provision is not a reason to relax; it marks a minimum on top of which the checkpoints in section four must be stacked contractually. For international agreements, the EU Data Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854) devotes a dedicated chapter to switching between data processing services and is worth consulting. To repeat, this section is not legal advice, and verifying statutory text and its applicability belongs with a lawyer.

10. The same questions for individuals and small teams

At a small scale this can read like someone else's problem, but the structure is identical. A person with twenty years of photos in one cloud account and a five-person office keeping contracts and drawings only inside a collaboration tool face the same set of questions.

  • If the service you use closes next month, can you download everything within days?
  • If the card on file expires and the account suspends, what happens? How long is the grace period and what is deleted after it?
  • Do downloaded files retain capture dates and album structure, or do you get an unnamed heap?
  • What happens to shared material when the person who uploaded it deletes their account?
  • Have you actually used the bulk export feature? Most services have one and most people never have

Three minimums are within reach for a small organization. Run a full export once a year onto an external disk, and treat that day as the restore drill. Write down the file count and total size of what came out, which is the beginning of a catalog. Then pick the most important five percent and place a second copy with a different company. There is no need to duplicate everything, and attempting to is a far more common route to doing nothing at all.

That digital ownership is thinner than it looks has recurred on the purchased-content side too. PlayStation Deletes 551 Purchased Movies — The Illusion of Digital Ownership covers platform dependency, and Escaping AI Vendor Lock-In covers dependency at the service layer. The physical, long-horizon custody discussed here runs on a longer clock than either, which makes mistakes correspondingly more expensive to undo.

Conclusion: You can outsource storage, not the ability to retrieve

Outsourcing custody is a rational choice, and nothing here argues that doing it yourself is always better. What transfers, though, is the work of storing, not the ability to retrieve. That ability has three components: the contractual right to pull material out, an inventory telling you what exists, and the experience of having actually pulled it.

If only one of the three can start today, make it the last. Reopening a contract requires approvals and building a catalog takes time, but picking ten random files and retrieving them right now takes half an hour. Those thirty minutes will set the priority of the other two for you. If the files come out cleanly, the contract is next. If they do not, that is the most urgent thing on your desk today.

What this article does not settle

  • The case in the introduction is ongoing and no ruling on the merits has issued. Only the facts as stated in the complaint and in reporting appear here, and no view is taken on which side is right. Details such as contract wording, amounts and durations could not be confirmed
  • Whether the Korean cloud computing statute applies in a given situation depends on the contract form and the type of provider. Verify the text at the national law information portal
  • Individual article numbers and per-item commencement dates for the EU Data Act switching provisions are not specified here
  • Per-provider export fees and measured throughput are difficult to generalize from public material and were left out. Your own contract and rehearsal will give a more accurate figure
