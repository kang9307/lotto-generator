When AI Starts to Look Like the Semiconductor Industry

What actually converges, and where the analogy breaks

Jul 30, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: The claim that two industries resemble each other cannot be checked as written

The observation that AI development has taken on the shape of a capital intensive industry is now commonplace. The most common way to express that observation is to compare it to the semiconductor industry. Upfront investment is enormous, generations turn over quickly, a small number of players hold the bottlenecks, and governments intervene. Lined up like that, the overlap looks convincing.

But the sentence they resemble each other cannot be judged true or false on its own. Without separating which properties of the two industries are converging, you end up using one overlapping item as grounds for declaring that non overlapping items also match. The danger of an analogy is not that it is wrong but that it never states how far it holds. So this article inverts the usual order. Instead of assuming the analogy holds and drawing conclusions from it, it splits the analogy into five separate claims and checks each one.

The starting point could be identified. The essay behind the GeekNews topic is a piece Bilal Zuberi published on Medium on July 13, 2026, arguing why frontier AI has begun to rhyme with semiconductors. The author identifies himself as being with the venture firm Red Glass Ventures. The page opened without a paywall and I read it in full. The summary below is in my own words, and I do not reproduce the author's phrasing.

One principle up front. Every fact in this article was confirmed directly from a primary source on July 30, 2026. Dollar amounts, market sizes, and share figures that could not be confirmed through official documents or financial disclosures were omitted entirely rather than presented as estimates. Section 11 lists exactly what was left out. And this article offers no industry forecast, no investment judgment, and no assessment of any specific company or country. It deals only with structure.

1. What the original argues, and what kind of evidence supports it

Compressed into my own words, the argument runs like this. When large upfront capital overlaps with short technology cycles, you get a structure in which finishing one generation means immediately starting the next. The author describes this as being on a treadmill and sees it as rhyming with what the semiconductor industry has long lived through. Then the conclusion turns. The place to win is not the frontier model itself but the application layer built on top of it. The author also acknowledges that the application layer carries a dependency risk on the providers beneath it.

The first thing worth doing here is neither agreeing nor disagreeing, but sorting the evidence by grade. The numbers the author cites come from sources of very different reliability. Without that separation, a verifiable trend and an unverifiable dollar figure end up carrying the same weight in the same paragraph.

  • Estimates from a research organization — methodology and confidence intervals are published. Usable as evidence of a trend, but not readable as an invoice for any single model.
  • Design cost estimates from a market research firm — the underlying report is paid and the calculation assumptions are not public. This article does not use them.
  • Revenue figures for private companies — firms with no disclosure obligation cannot be traced back to a primary confirmation, however far you follow the citation chain. Also not used.
  • A company's description of itself — a firm's own account of its business model and customer structure can be cited as a factual statement. Claims about competitive position, however, are self assessment and must be marked as such.
  • Official government announcements — the existence of a regulation and its mechanism are confirmable from the announcement. Whether the regulation is effective or justified is a separate question this article does not take up.

Take the training cost trend as the example. The research organization in question states that frontier model training costs have grown roughly 2.4 times per year since 2016, and it publishes a 95 percent confidence interval of 2.0 to 3.1 times alongside it. There is more than one estimation method. One amortizes hardware and adds energy, another applies cloud hourly prices, and a third computes development cost including experimental compute and research staff, with the documentation stating that the cloud pricing approach yields roughly twice as high a figure on average. Under the development cost approach, the composition is reported as hardware 47 to 67 percent, research staff 29 to 49 percent, and energy 2 to 6 percent.

That last breakdown is the most useful number in this whole article. It means a little over a third of the cost goes to people, which is a different property from a pure facilities industry. Labor does not occupy that share of the cost structure of a semiconductor fab. In other words, the very source the author cites already contains the clue that the analogy is incomplete.

2. Splitting what converges into five axes

Now to decompose the analogy. The five axes below are independent of each other, and each can be judged true or false separately. The last column of the table is this article's verdict, and the grounds are taken up one at a time from section 3 through section 6.

AxisForm it takes in semiconductorsCorresponding thing in AIThis article's verdict

2. Yield and economies of scalePhysical defect rates and output volume set unit costTraining failures and retries, plus unit cost of servingPartial. The same word points at different things
3. Supply chain bottleneckDependence concentrates in specific process equipment layersDependence concentrates in accelerator, power, and network layersCorresponds in form. Exclusivity claims unconfirmable

Split this way, something is visible immediately. Not one of the five holds unconditionally. The single word resembles is in fact bundling five distinct claims, each with a different degree of certainty. Erase that difference and you get writing in which the conclusion of the weakest axis is propped up by the evidence of the strongest one.

3. Axes 1 and 2 - capital intensity, and the trap inside the phrase economies of scale

The capital intensity axis corresponds well in form. Both industries share the property that upfront investment determines whether entry is possible. You cannot enter a leading edge process without building a fab, and you cannot attempt a frontier training run without securing large scale compute. Both also share the property that the moment of investment and the moment of return are far apart. The decision is made now and the result arrives several generations later.

That said, this article does not compare the dollar figures on the two sides. Semiconductor capital expenditure exists in corporate disclosures, but I could not locate the capital expenditure line on the official quarterly results page I opened today. Training cost on the AI side is, as shown above, a research estimate that varies by a factor of two depending on method. The moment you place two numbers produced by different methods side by side, the comparison stops being a fact about two industries and becomes a fact about two estimation methods. Such comparisons get quoted often and prove nothing.

Axis 2 demands more care. Economies of scale and yield are physically defined concepts in semiconductors. Yield is how many working chips come off a wafer, and it connects directly to defect density, a physical quantity. As volume rises, fixed costs spread and process data accumulates so that the defect rate itself falls. That structure does not carry over intact to training and serving.

  • Failure on the training side is not defect density — large runs do get interrupted or fall short of expectations, but that is not a statistically stable physical phenomenon like per wafer defects. Repeating the same conditions does not reproduce the same failure rate.
  • Borrowing the word yield creates a misunderstanding — the moment you call the success rate of training attempts a yield, you smuggle in the false implication that it can be managed upward the way a process is tuned.
  • Growing scale does not lower unit cost by the same mechanism — the unit cost of inference serving is far more sensitive to request characteristics and optimization level than to volume. Fixed cost spreading alone does not explain it.
  • Over a third of training cost is people — the composition from the previous section bites here again. Activities where labor takes a large share of cost do not follow the scale curve of a facilities industry.
  • One thing genuinely does overlap — sunk upfront investment distorts the next decision. That is a general property of capital intensive activity, not something specific to semiconductors.

That last item matters. Much of what looks like a successful analogy is not the same as semiconductors but simply how capital intensive activity behaves in general. Shipbuilding, aircraft, and telecom network construction share the same properties. If so, the reason semiconductors were chosen as the comparison must be the speed of generational turnover, and section 7 shows that what sets that speed is entirely different on the two sides.

4. Axis 3 - is the bottleneck really concentrated in a few hands

The supply chain bottleneck axis is the one most often cited and most inaccurately described. The form itself corresponds. Both industries share a structure in which dependence is concentrated in specific layers of the chain, and the supply pace of those layers sets the pace of everything else.

The problem is the exclusivity claim usually attached at this point. You frequently see assertions that only one company in the world makes a given tool, or that lead times run to several years. So I opened that toolmaker's own official product page, and the result did not match expectations.

  • Confirmed — the official page describes the exposure technology as unique to the company itself. There are technical descriptions of how the tool operates: multiple multilayer mirrors, a high vacuum environment, and positioning accuracy adjusted twenty thousand times per second.
  • Not confirmed — there was no explicit sentence on the official page saying the company is the only maker of such tools in the world. Describing a technology as unique to yourself and claiming to be the sole supplier are two different statements.
  • Not confirmed — lead time figures, annual unit output, and market share were all absent from the official page. That is why those numbers do not appear in this article.
  • Not confirmed — there was no accounting of component counts or supplier counts either. The widely quoted numbers of that kind do not come from this page.
  • Confirmed timing information — the page notes that the hundredth system shipped in 2020, and that a particular platform is expected to support high volume production somewhere in 2025 to 2026.

This outcome is itself the lesson. The more dramatic a number is at reinforcing the analogy, the likelier it is to be absent from the primary source. That a bottleneck exists and that the bottleneck is a single supplier are entirely different claims, and the latter is far stronger. Pass along a strong claim without checking it, and every conclusion resting on that claim wobbles with it.

The AI side bottleneck deserves the same discipline. The structural observation is sound that dependence concentrates in accelerators, power, data center sites, and high speed networking layers. But which layer is exclusive and to what degree is hard to pin down from public material, and contract terms and allocation priorities are generally not disclosed. What this article can say about this axis stops at similarity of form, and beyond that nothing was confirmed.

5. Axis 4 - division of labor, where the analogy fits best

Of the five axes, the one that corresponds best is division of labor. It is also the only axis where both sides can be confirmed from primary sources.

On the semiconductor side the company's own account is explicit. The largest contract manufacturer states in its official company profile that it created the dedicated semiconductor foundry business model when it was founded in 1987. The same page describes its scale as having manufactured 12,682 products using 305 distinct technologies for 534 different customers. Those three numbers show the division of labor directly. The party that designs and the party that builds are separated, and the building side serves many designing parties.

Why that structure arose is the heart of the correspondence. The layers split so that the burden of facilities investment would not become the entry barrier for design capability. Without the capital to build a fab, a good design can still reach the market. Conversely, the building side does not stake its fate on any one design but spreads across many customers.

What corresponds on the AI side is the separation between foundation model providers and application developers. The grounds for that correspondence are as follows.

  • The upfront burden concentrates in one layer — securing compute and executing training belongs to the base layer, and the application layer enters without carrying that burden.
  • The layers join through a contract of interface — where the foundry side has design rules and process information, the model side has interfaces and usage terms. In both cases the specification is the layer boundary.
  • A many to many structure emerges — one base provider serves many applications, and one application may use several bases.
  • Value arises differently at each layer — if competitiveness at the manufacturing layer lies in process and yield, competitiveness at the design layer lies in understanding the use case. That distinction holds between models and applications too.

Where the correspondence breaks is equally clear. On the semiconductor side, a dedicated manufacturer entering its customers' design business is structurally suppressed, because competing with your customers destroys the contract manufacturing model itself. The model layer has no such suppressing mechanism. A base provider shipping products directly into the application layer can happen naturally, and the original author acknowledged that risk. In other words, the foundry analogy explains why the layers split but not why the boundary is respected. The application layer's position is structurally less safe than a chip designer's. The contractual and portability side of that problem is worked through separately in AI vendor lock in and exit strategy.

6. Axis 5 - how geopolitics intervenes in the technical path

The regulatory axis is comparatively easy to verify, because government announcements are published as primary sources. Let me state this article's stance plainly, though. It does not evaluate whether regulation is justified or effective, only that it exists and how it operates. Judging whose policy is right is outside this article's scope.

What is confirmable from the official announcement of the United States Bureau of Industry and Security is the following. The rule dated October 17, 2023 covers advanced computing chips, semiconductor manufacturing equipment, and supercomputing items. As a control mechanism, a performance density threshold is introduced as a new criterion in addition to the existing performance threshold. The announcement also describes a license exception requiring prior notification, a worldwide licensing requirement for companies headquartered in certain countries, and presumption of approval and presumption of denial categories. It states its purpose as addressing national security concerns and separately references the twenty two countries subject to a United States arms embargo.

The structurally important point is that the target is a measurable performance metric, not a company name. Once a metric is the criterion, three consequences follow.

  • Design starts referencing regulatory documents — what can be built is no longer decided by engineering judgment alone. When a performance metric is the line, designs appear that are conscious of that line.
  • The rule becomes a thing that gets revised — metric based criteria get adjusted when routes around them are discovered. The announcement explains the addition of the performance density criterion in connection with room for working around the prior one.
  • The effect reaches each layer differently — controls apply directly at the manufacturing equipment and chip layers, and reach layers above indirectly through availability and procurement terms.
  • Regional technical paths can diverge — pursuing the same goal under different constraints leads to different design choices. That is not an evaluation but a structural consequence of the constraints.

On this axis the correspondence between AI and semiconductors is not really an analogy at all. Because a single control regime covers both layers, it is not that two separate industries are converging but that they are being treated as one chain. That distinction is not trivial. Saying they resemble each other implies convergence of two independent things, whereas in reality they are exposed to the same conditions because they sit above and below each other in the same chain. The regulatory debate around whether weights are published has a different character, and that is handled separately in open weight AI regulation.

7. The three points where the analogy breaks

This is the center of gravity of the article. Getting drunk on the analogy and drawing conclusions from it is the signature failure mode of this topic, so the line for how far it can be pushed needs to be drawn clearly. At three points the analogy does not merely bend, the property itself is different.

Point of comparisonSemiconductorsSoftware and modelsWhat the difference produces
Cost of one more unitConsumes wafer, process time, and yieldReplication cost effectively zeroInventory and production capacity are not meaningful concepts
What sets the pacePhysical law and equipment delivery schedulesCapital, staff, data, research resultsNone of the predictability a physical ceiling provides
Signal of a generation changeAnnounced as a process generationNo equivalent announcement unitRoadmap matching does not work the same way
Definition of performanceMeasured by physical metricsTask dependent, with the evaluation set standing in for the definitionHard to reach agreement on what better means
ReversibilityOnce facilities are built, reversal is hardWeights and code are replaceableLosses from a wrong generation are of a different kind
Cost for a customer to leaveRequires design revalidation and requalificationMay amount to swapping an interfaceLock in strength differs by layer

The first row is the decisive one. The fact that replication cost is near zero changes the logic of a capital intensive industry at its root. In semiconductors, rising demand makes production capacity the issue, and capacity takes time to add. With models, the same weights can be replicated without limit, so the bottleneck moves from production to service capacity allocation. That is a different problem requiring a different response. Borrowing the semiconductor phrase supply shortage misidentifies the nature of the problem.

The second row concerns predictability. Physical law and equipment lead times are constraints but also information. They let you roughly calculate when the next generation arrives and how large the step will be. The model side has no such external ceiling. So any account that borrows the semiconductor notion of a generation cycle to estimate the timing of the next model generation has no basis. The treadmill analogy is valid for describing a state and loses its footing the moment it is used to predict a pace.

The fourth row is the most immediate for practitioners. Because what counts as better is task dependent, any performance comparison must state its evaluation criteria alongside the result. This property leads straight into the discussion of application layer assets below. If the definition of performance is not fixed, then whoever holds the definition is the one making the judgment.

Summarized, the line goes here. The analogy is valid up to explaining capital formation and layered division of labor. It becomes invalid the moment it moves on to generational pace, unit economics, or performance comparison. Writing that crosses this line, however plausible it sounds, is a case of a fact about the semiconductor industry being wrongly transplanted as evidence about AI.

8. The misjudgments that come from pushing the analogy all the way

Having drawn the line, it is worth writing down concretely what goes wrong when it is crossed. Below are conclusions that can actually emerge from overextending the analogy, each with the reason it does not hold.

  • Only a few will be left standing, as with leading edge nodes — the shrinking number of players in semiconductors rests on the irreversibility of physical facilities investment. A layer with zero replication cost does not face the same pressure in the same way. Worse, this conclusion lumps the application layer in with everything else.
  • Generations follow a fixed cycle, so the next leap can be calculated — process generations are announced on roadmaps and models have no equivalent. Borrowing the word cycle is not the same as a cycle existing.
  • Capital is everything, so small teams are pointless — what capital determines is whether you can enter the base layer. The very reason a division of labor exists is to create a layer that can enter without carrying that burden.
  • The bottleneck holders are fixed, so the outcome is fixed — the existence of a bottleneck is confirmed, but its degree of exclusivity was not established from public material. A conclusion drawn from an unestablished premise is not a conclusion.
  • Manage model quality the way you improve yield — yield is a stable physical statistic and model quality depends on task definition. The things being managed differ in kind, so the same management approach does not transfer.
  • Larger scale must lower unit cost — fixed cost spreading does occur, but inference cost is far more sensitive to request characteristics and optimization. The moment the word must appears, the sentence is false.
  • Therefore we must build our own model right now — this conclusion does not follow from the analogy. The basis for deciding on in house training is not an industry comparison but your own data, required accuracy, and operations staffing.

One shared failure pattern is visible. An analogy is a tool for explaining structure, and it becomes a misjudgment the moment it is used as grounds for a conclusion. That single step from observing that structures are similar to predicting that outcomes will match is the unverified leap.

9. What actually changes for practitioners

This is where the article earns its keep. If the division of labor is real, then for the large majority who will not build a foundation model, the question narrows to one. What becomes an asset at the application layer. More precisely, what cannot be bought immediately with capital and survives a change of model generation.

AssetWhy capital cannot substitute for itHow you lose itWhat you can do now
Domain dataObservation opportunity comes from time and touchpoints and cannot be generated retroactively with moneyNo contractual basis for use, so the data is unusableWrite the collection basis and permitted scope into contracts
Task definitionWhat counts as success is settled only in the fieldIt leaves with the person who held itFix boundary cases in writing along with the deciding rule
Evaluation setFailure cases come only from real operationsIt goes stale fast once updates stopBuild a routine that adds a case after every incident
Distribution channelDirect contact with users cannot be replicatedGrowing single channel dependence lets the channel set termsCount and record the share of each channel quarterly
Operating procedurePeople, rules, and exception handling are entangled in itUndocumented, so it cannot be reproducedLeave failure response paths behind as a runbook

The domain data row is easy to misread. What matters here is not volume but exclusivity of the observation opportunity. Having collected a lot of public data is not an asset. The dividing line is whether a signal is repeatedly observed only inside your own workflow, and whether the right to use it is secured contractually. When data does and does not function as a defense is examined more closely in is data really the moat.

Conversely, what fails to become an asset deserves to be written down too. Mistaking this list for assets is the more common error in practice.

  • Prompt tuning fitted to a specific model — much of it has to be rewritten when the generation changes. The effort is real but does not accumulate.
  • Post processing rules fitted to one model's output habits — change the model and the rules actively get in the way.
  • A deployment structure entangled with vendor specific features — you gain convenience by deferring the cost of switching into the future.
  • Tracking benchmark scores as such — a score not connected to your own task is not a basis for judgment. Without an evaluation set, the score belongs to someone else's criteria.
  • Up to date knowledge of the model lineup — this goes stale fastest. Lists age; judgment criteria age more slowly.

Put the two lists side by side and a rule falls out. Only what survives swapping the model is an asset. That one sentence classifies most items. If you do reach a point where model performance comparison is needed, that is separate work, and the AI model comparison hub on criteria and the API pricing comparison on cost structure are the places for it.

10. What you can defend without capital - a review procedure

Applying the previous section's criteria requires a procedure, not a list. Below is a review sheet built on the assumption that you run it once a quarter. Copy it and fill it in as is.

[APPLICATION LAYER ASSET REVIEW SHEET]   As of: ____ / __ / __

Axis 1. Domain data
  A signal observed repeatedly only in our workflow?     Yes / No
  Right to use that data written into a contract?        Yes / No
  Stored in a form that survives a model change?         Yes / No

Axis 2. Task definition
  Boundary between success and failure written down?     Yes / No
  Boundary calls not left to one person's instinct?      Yes / No

Axis 3. Evaluation set
  Contains cases pulled from real incidents?             Yes / No
  Ran the same criteria across a swapped model?          Yes / No
  Has this set ever actually caught a regression?        Yes / No

Axis 4. Distribution channel
  Some share of users reached through our own channel?   Yes / No
  Single channel dependence stays under half?            Yes / No

Verdict
  Three or more No answers  → fill these gaps before chasing capital
  Zero to two No answers    → schedule a model swap trial this quarter

The box that most often fails is the third one under axis 3. Plenty of organizations will answer that they have an evaluation set, but far fewer have a record of that set actually catching a regression. A set that has never filtered anything out is just a rubber stamp, and it will not serve as a basis for judgment when the time comes to swap models.

It also helps to fix how the procedure is run. The order below finishes inside half a day.

  1. Pin down one representative task — it must be something running in real operations. A pilot task makes the conclusion optimistic.
  2. Write the success criterion for that task as a sentence — if it is not written, there is no criterion. This step usually surfaces disagreement inside the team.
  3. Collect twenty boundary cases — not clear successes and clear failures, but the ones where judgment splits. Only those earn their keep.
  4. Run the same evaluation on a different model — the experiment itself measures portability. Whether you can even execute it is information before the result is.
  5. Mark the parts that depend on model specific handling — the places that broke during the swap are that list. You do not have to guess; the experiment tells you.
  6. Verify data usage rights in the contract — read the document, not the custom. This check gets more expensive the longer it is deferred.
  7. Count inbound share by channel — if one channel exceeds half, register that fact as a risk item.
  8. Schedule next quarter's review now — a review with no date on it happens exactly once.

This procedure earns its keep regardless of any forecast about industry structure. Whether the analogy holds or fails, confirming what survives a model swap never becomes a loss. Conversely, believing the analogy's conclusion and postponing preparation leaves you unprepared both if the analogy was wrong and if it was right.

11. Figures left out of this article because they could not be confirmed

Inaccurate numbers circulate unusually well on this topic. So rather than hiding what was dropped for lack of confirmation, here it is as a list. If you meet these numbers elsewhere, check the grade of the source first.

  • Chip design cost by process generation — the widely quoted figures originate with a market research firm whose underlying report and calculation assumptions I could not open. Dropped.
  • Training cost figures for specific models — not confirmable from corporate disclosures. Dropped. Only the research organization's growth rate estimate is cited, with its confidence interval.
  • Total data center capital expenditure — quoted in many places but not confirmed from a primary disclosure. Dropped.
  • A manufacturer's capital expenditure amount — I opened the official quarterly results page and the line was not on it. Dropped.
  • Lead times and annual unit output for exposure tools — absent from the maker's official page. Dropped.
  • Component counts and supplier counts for the tools — absent from the official page. Dropped.
  • The claim of being a sole supplier — the official page's wording was that the technology is unique to the company, with no sentence claiming to be the only one in the world. Not used in the strong form.
  • Yield figures for leading edge processes — not confirmable from public official material. Dropped.
  • Revenue figures for application layer companies — private firms with no disclosures. Dropped.
  • Market share at the accelerator layer — varies widely by calculation basis and could not be confirmed at first hand. Dropped.

That this list runs ten items long is itself part of the conclusion. Most of the impressive numbers that support the analogy do not reach a primary source. That does not make the structural similarity between the two industries disappear. It means the similarity has to be argued through structure rather than through dollar amounts. That is exactly why this article could still judge five axes with the numbers removed.

Conclusion: an analogy explains structure, it does not substitute for a conclusion

The observation that AI development is taking on the shape of a capital intensive industry is sound. But the range over which comparing it to semiconductors actually explains that observation is narrower than it looks. What this article established is that among the five axes, division of labor and geopolitical intervention correspond best, capital intensity corresponds only in form, and yield plus bottleneck exclusivity correspond partially or not verifiably at all.

More important is where the analogy collapses. Replication cost is zero, no physical ceiling sets the pace, and the definition of better is not fixed. Because of those three, results observed in the semiconductor industry cannot be transcribed as AI's future. The treadmill analogy is valid for describing the present state and invalid for predicting the next point.

What remains for practitioners is simpler than expected. Not having the capital to enter the base layer is a starting point, not a conclusion. The very reason a division of labor exists is to create a layer that can be entered without that burden. What can be defended at that layer is domain data, task definition, the evaluation set, and the distribution channel, and the test reduces to a single sentence. Does it survive swapping the model.

Finally, the limits of this article. Every fact here was confirmed from a primary source on July 30, 2026, and regulatory provisions and corporate descriptions do get revised. What was dropped for lack of confirmation is left in section 11 as it stands. What is worth taking away is not the numbers but the method of splitting the axes. Whatever industry analogy you meet next, separate out what is converging and draw the line where it breaks, and that analogy becomes usable as a tool.
