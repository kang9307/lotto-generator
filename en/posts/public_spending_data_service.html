When Data Published for Oversight Becomes a Map - Mandatory Disclosure Data in a Second Life

The purpose of disclosure fixes the shape of the data. What that shape measures, and what it quietly leaves out

Sep 1, 2026 Tech & IT

A great deal of what public bodies publish was never meant to be read for pleasure or convenience. It was made so that somebody could check it. Once that is the purpose, the purpose decides the shape. Certain fields are non-negotiable, and certain other fields never enter anyone's mind, because nobody performing the check would ever need them.

So the moment you try to turn such a dataset toward some other end, the first thing you hit is not a technical wall but the residue of a design intent. You fill the absent columns by inference, you collapse inconsistent spellings into single entities, and then you put a label on the result. This piece walks through what gets quietly distorted along the way, using a recent Korean example as the occasion rather than the subject.

This article is a general account of how mandatory disclosure data behaves when reused, and of the limits involved. It is not an assessment of any particular institution, business or service. The statutes and directives cited are Korean and reflect what could be verified at the time of writing; none of this is legal advice for a specific project.

The occasion - 1.21 million receipts became a map

At the end of August 2026 a personal project appeared on a Korean developer forum. It is a static site that gathers the hospitality and entertainment expense records published by 75 public bodies across 15 Korean provinces and metropolitan cities, and lays the result out as a restaurant ranking map. The builder describes the pipeline plainly: crawl 83 institutional bulletin boards, parse tables that arrive as word processor documents, PDFs and HTML in no consistent format, normalize the venue names, and end up with roughly 1.21 million ledger rows across a rolling twenty-four month window. On top of that sit 2,846 restaurants.

The scoring is the interesting part. Rank is assigned not by number of visits but by how many distinct departments went there. The reasoning is that a place one department visited a hundred times is that department's regular haunt, whereas a place fifty separate departments each found their way to has no obvious reason for its patrons' tastes to coincide. Comparison is confined within a single district or city. There are no reviews and no star scores, only receipt counts. The site sells no advertising and runs with no login, no accounts and no server.

What deserves attention here is not the artifact but the inversion itself. This data did not enter the world so that anyone could pick a place for lunch. It was published for an entirely different reason, and that reason determined every column it has. From the moment you use it for something else, you are negotiating with that design, continuously and on its terms. What follows is an inventory of that negotiation.

This data does not exist to find you dinner

Article 7(1) of the Korean Act on the Disclosure of Information by Public Institutions enumerates the categories a public body must publish proactively, on a schedule it announces in advance. Item 3 of that list reads: 「information necessary for administrative oversight, such as the content of budget execution and the results of project evaluations」. It is an unusual case where the purpose is written into the statutory text. This data exists in order to be checked.

The local government side is more specific. Article 121 of the Ministry of the Interior and Safety directive on local government accounting provides that bodies shall endeavour to publish expense execution records on their websites and comparable channels. That wording, endeavour rather than shall, returns later when we talk about missing data. For public corporations and agencies there is a separate integrated disclosure system grounded in Article 12 of the Act on the Management of Public Institutions, and the head of institution's hospitality expenses are among the items disclosed there.

The fact that oversight is the purpose determines the form. Oversight has exactly one question to answer: was this money spent in accordance with the rules? Answering it requires the amount, the stated purpose, the person who spent it, the timing and the payment instrument. Conversely, several things are simply not needed to answer it.

  • Oversight does not need to know where the venue is. So no address and no coordinates are required
  • Oversight does not need to know whether two rows are the same venue. So there is no business registration number or any other stable identifier
  • Oversight does not need a category taxonomy. Whether the place is a restaurant, a cafe or a stationery shop can only be guessed from the free text purpose field
  • Oversight does not need continuity across time. A rule violation is visible within a single quarter, so nothing is designed to stitch quarters together

Those four lines account for nearly every problem that follows. The data is not deficient; it is built to answer a different question. Missing that distinction leads straight to the wrong conclusion, namely that public bodies are publishing carelessly, when in fact they are publishing exactly what the rule asks for.

The list of disclosure fields is the schema

Item 4 of Table 2 attached to the accounting directive specifies that expense execution records be broken out by spender, date, place, purpose, number of people covered, amount, payment method and budget line. The publication cycle is at least quarterly, with a deadline of one month after the close of each quarter. Anyone who has worked with databases recognizes what that sentence is. It is not a regulation, it is a table definition.

Disclosure fieldWhat it does for oversightWhere it falls short for analysis
SpenderIdentifies who authorised the spend and whether the per-rank ceiling heldWhether it records a name, a job title or a team varies by institution, so the baseline for any department-level aggregation wobbles
DateShows the day and time band, catching spending outside permitted occasionsOften only the date is present with no clock time, so lunch cannot be separated from dinner
PlaceLets a human eye confirm where the money wentNo rule says whether this is a trade name, a building or a neighbourhood, and address, coordinates and registration number are all absent
PurposeEstablishes whether the spend matches a permitted useFree text. A single word like 「meeting」 satisfies oversight but supports no classification
Number of peopleYields a per-head figure to check against the capMay diverge from actual attendance, and nothing inside the dataset can confirm it
AmountCatches ceiling breaches and split transactionsA unit price can be derived, but with no menu, no composition and no price index the comparison stays coarse
Payment methodConfirms the card-first principle was followedAlmost unused in analysis, beyond spotting where cash transactions cluster
Budget lineReconciles against the budget accountAn internal classification, so the same label does not carry the same meaning across institutions

Read the list to the end and the absence becomes sharp. The two columns analysis most needs are not there. Nothing tells you where the venue is, and nothing tells you whether two rows describe the same venue. Both have to be manufactured later by inference. Manufactured columns carry error, and that error propagates into every figure derived downstream.

What the ranking actually measures

This is the centre of the article. Everything before it leads here.

A restaurant ranking built from expense ledgers gets labelled a restaurant ranking. But what the formula counts is not the quality of the cooking. It is the number of times public money arrived somewhere. Once you list what sits between those two things, the list turns out to be long, and almost none of it has anything to do with food.

Confounding factorHow it lifts the rankingRelation to food quality
ProximityIt is within walking distance of the office. A one-hour lunch break sets the radiusEffectively none. Distance has nothing to do with cooking
CapacityIt has a room that seats twenty. Places without one never enter the candidate setNone. This is a question of floor plan
Receipt handlingThe corporate card works, tax invoices are issued, group settlement is painlessNone. This is accounting convenience
Price bandIts per-head price fits inside the allowance ceilingWeak, and partly inverted. Anything above the cap is excluded automatically
Protocol and habitIt has a reputation as a safe choice when hosting visitorsA choice that cannot fail is not the same as the best choice
InheritanceA successor keeps using what the predecessor usedNone. This is pure institutional momentum
Whether the body publishesOnly the surroundings of publishing institutions appear in the data at allNone. This is the sample being cut

Compressed into one sentence: the denominator of this ranking is not 「all restaurants」 but 「restaurants near a public office that take groups, handle receipts and fit the price band」. A ranking with a narrowed denominator carries meaning only inside that narrowed denominator. If you cannot write the denominator in one sentence, you should not be producing a ranking at all.

There is a subtler issue on top. This dataset is not a pure observational record; it is partly a self-reinforcing one. The remark that a place worked out well travels between departments, and the consequence of that travel gets stamped into the next quarter's ledger. Where observation influences the observed, the data alone cannot separate a top ranking that reflects merit from one that reflects the spread of information.

None of which makes the ranking worthless. Read precisely, it has a value that is genuinely hard to find elsewhere. It approximates a list of places where a party of twenty can be taken without incident. That information is difficult to extract from star ratings, and it has the virtue of being uncontaminated by advertising. The problem is not the data but the label. When the label points at something other than what the formula counts, accurate data manufactures inaccurate belief.

Counting departments changes some things and not others

Choosing to count distinct departments rather than raw visits is a design that takes direct aim at one bias. Changing the unit of aggregation is a standard defence when a sample is skewed, and several things really are caught by it.

  • One team's habit can no longer monopolise a venue's position in the table
  • A department with a large budget no longer imposes its preferences on the whole ranking by sheer volume
  • Restricting comparison to a district or city reduces the automatic advantage of areas dense with public offices
  • A single large transaction can no longer swing the result, since the question becomes counts rather than sums

What it cannot remove is larger. Counting departments erases within-department bias while passing bias shared across departments through untouched.

  • Proximity survives intact. Fifty departments housed in the same building still describe one radius. This is by far the largest residue
  • Capacity and receipt handling survive too. Increasing the department count does not widen the set of venues that clear those conditions
  • Reputation travelling between departments actively pushes the department count upward. The metric chosen as a defence is breached along precisely that path
  • Departments in institutions that do not publish are never counted in the first place. A truncated sample cannot be fixed by changing the unit of aggregation

None of this is a design failure. What is absent from the data cannot be removed by changing the metric. As long as the location of each office and each department is nowhere in the records, no formula can produce a proximity correction. That is the boundary between what a choice of metric can do and what it cannot.

Turning scattered files into one table

The directive says to publish on websites and comparable channels, and says nothing about format or location. The whole difficulty of the collection stage grows out of the space left by that one line.

From crawl to load

  • There are as many bulletin boards as institutions, each with its own list structure and pagination rules
  • Attachments arrive as word processor documents, PDFs, spreadsheets and inline HTML tables, mixed together
  • Even within one institution the template changes when the responsible officer changes. Column order and column names shift silently
  • Merged cells, subtotal rows and headers repeated on every page all obstruct table extraction
  • A PDF is not a table but glyphs placed at coordinates. Column boundaries have to be reconstructed geometrically
  • Scanned PDFs do not even offer that. Running character recognition adds another layer of error on top
  • Amount fields mix commas, currency marks and stray whitespace, and date formats differ from file to file

Where the time actually goes

Writing the parser takes a few days. What consumes time is the list of exceptions, and that list grows every quarter. When new files land, a rule that worked perfectly last quarter breaks for one or two institutions, and the only way to notice is that the row count suddenly drops. Which is why a pipeline like this has to record parse success rates per institution. An institution that silently falls to zero rows does not announce itself.

And the work does not end. As long as the source is refreshed quarterly, the derivative has to repeat the same process quarterly. This is where most personal projects stop. The cost of building it the first time is smaller than the cost of keeping it alive.

Name normalization - two directions of error

With no identifier, matching venues means matching strings. The nature of the problem is not 「grouping things that are the same」 but 「grouping things that are the same without mixing in things that are not」. Those two requirements pull in opposite directions.

Forms one venue takes across ledgers (same place, different spellings)
  A Restaurant
  A Restaurant (Main)
  A  Restaurant
  A Co., Ltd.        <- recorded under the registered corporate name
  A Restaurant #2    <- this may be a different place entirely
  A Restaurant resv  <- purpose text leaking into the place field

Loosen the rules and one venue splits across several rows. Tighten them and separate venues collapse into one. The two errors present completely differently in the output.

  • Under-merging — the same venue scatters and each fragment sits low in the table. Nothing on screen looks wrong. It fails silently
  • Over-merging — distinct venues fuse and the combined row is inflated. It rises to the top, so it fails in the most visible position available
  • For a ranking, over-merging is considerably more dangerous. The top of a table is what people read, and a read error attaches to a real business
  • Which is why in work like this the default is to bias toward precision over recall. Missing a match is preferable to fusing two identities
  • Branch naming is the hardest case. A second branch of the same brand is effectively a different venue, and string evidence alone gives you little to separate main from branch
  • The project that occasioned this article states plainly that normalization errors may group different venues into one row. This is not a class of problem that effort eliminates

Where the coordinates come from

With no address field, position has to be recovered from the venue name alone. Trade names are not nationally unique, and the more common the name, the longer the candidate list. This is where the decision to rank only within a district quietly does a second job. It looks like a fairness device for the ranking, but it is also a strong constraint that narrows geocoding candidates to a single administrative area.

Problems remain even so.

  • Two venues with the same name inside the same district cannot be separated automatically
  • If a venue has relocated, historical transactions and current coordinates disagree. The ledger does not record relocations
  • Closure is not represented anywhere in the source. A ledger says 「a payment happened then」 and never says 「it exists now」
  • The instant a pin appears on a map, users read that pin as current information. The tense of the data and the tense of the interface diverge
  • The project acknowledges that closed venues may still be listed. That is not an implementation defect but the absence of a column in the source

Lines to draw before republishing

Published and freely reusable in any form are not the same statement. Article 24-2 of the Korean Copyright Act permits use, without permission, of works created and released by the state or a local government in the course of official duties, while carving out information touching personal privacy or business secrets, information whose disclosure is restricted under other statutes, and registered works managed as state or public property. The question to settle before reuse is not 「is it public」 but 「on what terms is it public」.

Personal identification sits closer than people expect. The first disclosure field is the spender, and some institutions record only a job title, some add the team, and some record a personal name. Republishing such a ledger verbatim changes the character of the derived work. The project behind this article cites privacy protection as its reason for keeping the source repository closed, which is the same hazard, avoided.

But the thing most often overlooked in this topic is not privacy. It is that the party under exposure changes. The original subject of scrutiny was the institution that spent the money. The party named in the derivative is not the institution but the venue. A ranking is a reputation, and a low position, a wrongly merged row and a row surviving after closure all attach to a real trade name. That migration of scrutiny from public bodies to small businesses is a decision nobody made and it happens anyway.

Lines that hold in practice

  • Treat redistribution of the source separately. Showing a processed result and republishing the harvested ledger wholesale are different acts
  • Drop identifying columns at load time. Not rendering them is insufficient. What stays in storage eventually escapes it
  • Provide a correction channel. With nowhere to report a bad merge or a closed venue, errors become permanent
  • Match the label to the formula. One sentence stating what was counted is the cheapest defence available
  • Do not foreground negative rankings. A bottom-of-the-table list carries a different magnitude of risk than a top-of-the-table one
  • Monetisation changes the calculus. Once advertising or affiliate arrangements enter, the ranking shifts in character from information toward commerce

Traps this whole family of data shares

None of this is specific to expense ledgers. Data produced by a periodic disclosure duty shares almost the same set of traps wherever you find it.

  • Publication lag — apply at least quarterly, within one month of quarter end, and the most recent available record can be four months old. The project noting that its last month or two is thin is a direct consequence of this structure
  • The weight of 「shall endeavour」 — an institution that did not publish has not necessarily broken a rule. No data and no spending are entirely different facts, yet both appear as an empty cell in an aggregate
  • The size of what is out of scope — not every body follows the same rule, and not every won an institution spends falls under this category. Spending booked to other budget lines is simply absent
  • Template changes — when field composition or column names change, time series comparison breaks silently. Values before and after carry different definitions while looking identical
  • Amendments — the underlying rules and directives get revised. Stitching pre- and post-amendment periods into one table is itself the error
  • Missingness is not random — this is the big one. The institutions that publish late, publish in awkward formats and fail to parse are the same institutions every quarter. Gaps do not scatter; they clump by region
  • You cannot see outside the sample — looking only at the rendered list, there is no way to notice that a region is missing at all. Publishing the list of harvested institutions alongside the output is the minimum honest gesture

What inverts and what does not

Some disclosure datasets take this treatment and some do not. The fork comes down to four conditions.

  1. Is it published row by row? A dataset released only as summary tables offers nothing to invert in the first place
  2. Is there a key to join on? An address, a code, coordinates, something. At least one column has to reach outside the file
  3. How far does the sample overlap the population you care about? When the overlap is thin, the output describes the sample rather than the world
  4. Does the cycle keep pace with the decision? Data four months old can support retrospective analysis but not a choice being made today
Disclosure datasetOriginal purposeWhat it can invert intoWhere it stalls
Public hospitality expense ledgersAdministrative oversight of budget executionThe distribution of venues that can host official group dinnersNo address or identifier, and the sample is cut to the radius around offices
Apartment management fee disclosureResidents' right to know and management transparencyCost comparison between complexes and outlier detection by line itemWithout adjusting for complex size and facilities the comparison does not hold
School information disclosureThe right of parents and citizens to knowThe distribution of educational conditions across regionsAggregated at school level, so no descent to individual-level inference
Public institution management disclosureManagement transparency and citizen oversightCross-institution indicator comparison and trend linesAccounting practice differs by body, so identically named items are not identical
Public contract and procurement recordsOversight of the procurement processOrder flow and seasonal concentration by sectorContracts differ from delivery, and amendments are recorded apart from the original

The cases that resist inversion are equally clear. Data published in bands to protect individuals cannot be un-banded. Data whose key column is masked is the same story. And data published long after the events it describes may serve retrospective study while contributing nothing to a present decision. Whether something can be built is, in most cases, knowable before you build it, by checking these four conditions.

Questions to settle before you start

Before writing any code against a disclosure dataset, there are questions that head off most of the trouble if you answer them first. Six is enough.

  • Is the purpose of disclosure written into the statute? If it is, that sentence explains the schema, including why the missing columns are missing
  • Which columns you need are absent from the disclosure list? That list is exactly the set you will fill by inference, and therefore the inventory of where error originates
  • Can you write the denominator of your sample in one sentence? If not, do not build a ranking or a ratio. The same applies to taking an average
  • Is the missingness random? If not, write down which way it leans. You cannot correct for a direction you have not identified
  • Does the label point at the same thing the formula counts? The distance between 「best restaurants」 and 「where public money went」 is the exact size of the misreading
  • If a result attaches to a real business or person, is there a way to correct it? This last one is not an engineering question, which is why it goes missing most often

Summary

Data created by a disclosure duty carries the shape of that duty. Records published for oversight hold only the columns oversight requires, and the list of those columns functions in practice as a table definition. Turning such data toward another purpose means negotiating with that definition continuously. Absent columns get manufactured by inference, unstable spellings get bound by rules, and the output ships carrying the error generated by both operations.

The hardest part technically is parsing and normalization, but that is not what ruins results. What ruins results is attaching a ranking to a sample you have not described. A ranking built from expense ledgers measures where public money went rather than what tasted good, and that spending has distance, seating, receipts, protocol and inertia mixed into it in unknown proportions. When the formula is exact and the label points elsewhere, accurate data produces inaccurate belief.

Which is why the most valuable output of work like this is not the table but a single paragraph saying what the ranking counted and what it did not. Write that paragraph and the remainder is an engineering problem. Fail to write it, and no matter how correct the code, the artifact goes out into the world without knowing what it is.

What I could not verify

  • The project's source repository is closed, on stated privacy grounds. Its normalization rules, geocoding approach and list of harvested institutions are described here only within the range the public write-up covers, and the code itself was not inspected
  • The full text of the ministerial rule on local government hospitality expenditure could not be opened. The disclosure fields and cycle are stated here only within the range of Article 121 and Item 4 of Table 2 of the accounting directive, as quoted in a Ministry of Government Legislation opinion
  • The proportion of attachment formats across institutions could not be verified. That word processor documents, PDFs and HTML are mixed together is stated in the project description; no percentage figures are used here
  • The number of bodies that do not publish, and the true size of what falls outside the disclosure scope, could not be verified. The direction of the missingness is described without any estimate of its magnitude
  • For the integrated disclosure system, it was confirmed only that hospitality expenses appear among the disclosure items; the field-level template was not verified
  • For apartment management fees, school information and procurement records, only the existence and stated purpose of each regime were confirmed. Their detailed field lists are not used in this article
