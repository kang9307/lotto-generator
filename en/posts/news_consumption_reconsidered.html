How the Form of News Shapes the World You See - Where the Smoking Analogy Holds and Where It Breaks

Once you know what becomes news, you can also see what never does

Aug 28, 2026 Trends & Culture

Opening: the headlines from yesterday that survived to today

Of the headlines you scrolled past yesterday, one or two are still in your head today, and almost nothing from last month is. Yet every one of them arrived as if it were something you needed to know right then. That mismatch is the starting point.

There is a piece behind this. Psychologist Adam Mastroianni published Reading the news is the new smoking in his newsletter Experimental History on 14 June 2022, and it resurfaced in a Korean developer community in August 2026, drawing fresh discussion. The skeleton of the argument runs like this.

  • He has read almost no news since the summer of 2020 and reports less anxiety and less anger since
  • He argues that news repeatedly exposes rare and dramatic events, making the world feel more dangerous than it is
  • Most stories that seemed momentous at the time vanish within days, he writes, and genuine changes reach you eventually without daily checking. The properties he says news shares with smoking are habit formation, effects on the people around you, and an absence of benefit
  • He attaches his own caveats. He grants the value of investigative reporting and local news, and says he cannot promise that quitting news extends life the way quitting cigarettes does

One nail has to go in before anything else. An analogy is an analogy. What Mastroianni offers is observation and argument, not epidemiology, and he says as much himself. Smoking and news sit on completely different levels of accumulated evidence. Miss that distinction and everything downstream of it goes wrong. Section 6 treats the difference on its own.

What follows is not about whether news is bad for you. It is about what the form of news selects and what it discards.

This is a general explanation of media form and not medical advice. If anxiety or low mood persists and interferes with daily life, speak with local mental health services or a medical professional.

News is not what happened but what becomes news

Only a sliver of what happens in a day becomes an article, and that selection follows rules rather than taste. Journalism scholarship calls them news values.

The classic statement of the idea is The Structure of Foreign News by Johan Galtung and Mari Holmboe Ruge, published in Journal of Peace Research volume 2, issue 1, pages 64–90, in 1965. They analysed how four Norwegian newspapers covered three international crises and extracted twelve selection factors, the first of which is frequency: how well the time scale on which an event unfolds matches the publication rhythm of the medium.

That single item explains most of the rest. Something that resolves within a day fits a daily paper's day exactly. Something that unfolds over thirty years fits no date at all. It fails to become an article not because it is unimportant but because the shape does not match.

AxisLikely to become newsUnlikely to become news
Time scaleResolves within a day or an hourChange spread across decades
FrequencyHappens rarelyHappens every day
ShapeDiscrete event (crash, announcement, ruling)Background condition (prices, disease, norms)
VisibilityThere is something to photographThere is nothing to photograph
DirectionSudden deteriorationGradual improvement
ActorNamed people and institutionsAn unnamed many
ClosureThe sentence closes todayStill ongoing, so it never closes

The right-hand column has something in common. Improvement is usually slow and visible only in statistics, so it does not become news. A ten-year decline in road deaths has no photograph, no announcement date and no name attached. A single crash has all three. Same subject, and only one side passes the form of reporting.

This is not a conspiracy but a property of the form. No editor decided to pick only bad news; it happens automatically the moment you cut the world into units called events. The same property shows up in company newsletters, in journal press releases and in a personal timeline. The fork is not which outlet you choose but whether you see the world in event-sized pieces.

The resulting distortion: treating what comes to mind easily as common

What happens when you watch a selected list for long enough? Cognitive psychology has a name for it: the availability heuristic. Asked how often something happens, people reach not for statistics but for how easily an example comes to mind. Things that happen often do come to mind often, so it usually works. The trouble starts when what comes to mind tracks exposure frequency rather than real frequency.

One classic experiment put numbers on the gap: Judged frequency of lethal events by Lichtenstein, Slovic, Fischhoff, Layman and Combs, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory 4, 551–578, 1978, five experiments with 660 adult participants.

  • They judged accidents to kill about as many people as disease. Disease in fact kills roughly sixteen times as many
  • They judged homicide to be more frequent than suicide. Suicide is in fact about twice as frequent
  • Rare causes were consistently overestimated and frequent ones underestimated

Follow-up analyses of newspaper coverage found that dramatic risks are over-represented relative to their real frequency.

One step back is warranted, though. The conclusion that media distorts risk perception joins two observations rather than measuring the join itself. The perception of dramatic risks: Biased media, but unbiased minds by Thorsten Pachur, Cognition volume 246, 2024, argues exactly what its title says: coverage may be skewed while people's frequency judgments are less distorted than the classic reading suggests. That selection criteria exist and how far they reshape an individual mind are still questions of different sizes.

Negativity bias, an old story that is not only the media's fault

The observation that bad news holds attention better is older than media criticism. The broadest measurement in this direction is Cross-national evidence of a negativity bias in psychophysiological reactions to news by Stuart Soroka, Patrick Fournier and Lilach Nir, PNAS, 2019: an experiment across 17 countries and 6 continents in which participants watched real broadcast news video while psychophysiological responses were recorded. On average, negative stories produced greater activation.

The part that matters most is not the direction of the result but the caveat the authors emphasised alongside it. Individual variation was very large. This is a statement about averages, not about everyone. And if negativity bias also sits on the audience side, then an abundance of bad news is less a one-way editorial choice than the product of selection and demand reinforcing each other.

  • Editors pick what gets read and readers open what grabs them. Each behaviour becomes the other's justification
  • Once clicks and dwell time became measurable in real time, the loop's rotation shortened from days to minutes. The same loop runs on personal accounts, where people also forward the more heated item
  • So the story that blames only the media erases half of it. The other half sits with the reader

The economics of breaking news: fast arrives before accurate

The second property of the form concerns time. The value of a first report is extremely time-sensitive; the same content thirty minutes later is worth much less. Verification, meanwhile, takes time. Put those two sentences side by side and one conclusion follows. Early reporting is structurally less verified. That is not laziness but the way value is assigned.

Corrections sit under the opposite conditions. They arrive late, and what arrives late scores low on news value. Apply the table from section 2 to a correction and it lands in the right-hand column on every row.

The asymmetry of spread has been measured too. The spread of true and false news online by Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy and Sinan Aral, Science 359, 1146–1151, 2018, analysed roughly 126,000 rumour cascades involving about 3 million people on Twitter between 2006 and 2017, and reported that false information travelled farther and faster than true. What that study measured, though, is the difference between true and false rumours, not the difference between an original article and its correction. It cannot be used to prove how far corrections travel.

What happens after a correction arrives has been studied separately. Misinformation and Its Correction by Stephan Lewandowsky and colleagues, Psychological Science in the Public Interest 13, 106–131, 2012, reviews the continued influence effect: people accept a correction and go on reasoning from the original information anyway. The practical conclusion is simple. What you read in the first 24 hours is a draft. Not filing a draft as a conclusion is the response this form calls for.

Where the smoking analogy holds and where it breaks

Now back to the analogy that prompted all this. Half of it is useful and half of it is dangerous. Where the line falls is the most important thing here.

Where it holds: the shape of the habit

  • A short stimulus repeated often, reached for without any particular reason
  • The environment lowers the cost of the next contact. Notifications and infinite scroll occupy the same position as individually wrapped units
  • People who quit report that things got quieter, and both sides show the same pattern of switching to something else when they try to stop

Where it breaks: the level of evidence

Smoking has four things behind it. There is a large long-term cohort (Richard Doll, Richard Peto and colleagues enrolled roughly 34,000 male British doctors in 1951 and followed their mortality for fifty years; BMJ 2004, volume 328, page 1519). There is an established dose-response relationship in which risk rises with cigarettes per day and years smoked. There is a biological mechanism identifying which substances damage which tissues. And there is risk reduction after cessation, confirmed by long observation. News consumption has none of these four at a comparable level.

Evidence axisSmokingNews consumption
Long-term cohortExists at fifty-year scaleNo study at that level
Dose-responseEstablishedExposure unit not even defined
Biological mechanismIdentifiedNone proposed
Change after stoppingConfirmed by long observationMostly short-term self-report
Homogeneity of exposureCigarettes are broadly uniformContent varies, so it cannot be summed
Experimental feasibilityObservational data sufficedLong randomised assignment barely possible

The second row matters most. News is not countable the way cigarettes are. The moment you count ten minutes of disaster coverage and ten minutes of a weekend long-form piece as the same unit, the question of how many minutes a day is harmful stops meaning anything. Cigarettes could serve as a unit in smoking research because they were broadly uniform.

The conclusion of this section. An analogy is a starting point for thought, not evidence. The moment you step from like smoking to as harmful as smoking, you are writing as though research exists that does not. The more persuasive the analogy feels, the more easily that boundary gets erased.

Correlation and cause: what the studies show and what they cannot

People who consume more news are more anxious and news makes people anxious are different sentences. The first observes two values moving together; the second asserts a direction.

One attempt to gather the data is COVID-19 news consumption and distress in young people by Strasser, Sumner and Meyer, 2022, a systematic review of COVID-19 news consumption and distress in young people covering 13 studies and 760,474 participants. Of those, twelve were cross-sectional and only one was longitudinal. That composition sets the character of any conclusion. The limits the authors state are these.

  • Because all but one were cross-sectional, causal inference was impossible and reciprocal relationships could not be explored
  • All mental health outcomes were self-reported, carrying a risk of recall bias
  • No reliable, validated instrument for measuring news consumption exists
  • Methods differed across studies, making results hard to compare

At least four explanations remain alive against the same data.

  • Forward causation. News exposure raises distress
  • Reverse causation. Already anxious people check more often. Anxiety is a state of poor tolerance for uncertainty, and checking is the easiest available coping move
  • Feedback. Worry drives checking and checking feeds worry. Such cycles have been reported in trauma-related media exposure
  • A third condition. Job loss, illness or living in a dangerous area raises news consumption and anxiety at the same time

The limits of self-report matter here too. Answers to how much news do you consume per day often diverge from actual usage logs, and people in a low mood have been noted to recall higher usage. If so, part of the correlation may be an artefact of measurement.

So the honest sentence available right now is roughly this. A correlation between news consumption and anxiety is repeatedly observed, and the direction and size of the arrow are not settled.

The cost of quitting: not knowing is also a cost

One claim in the prompting essay deserves scrutiny: you eventually hear about anything important. True on average, false in the tail, because the items whose loss hurts most are usually concentrated in the tail.

  • Weather warnings and disaster alerts make no sense once they arrive late
  • Rule changes and application deadlines cannot be recovered once the date passes
  • Local information about roadworks, power and water outages and school schedules has almost no substitute channel
  • Recalls, outbreak notices, election days and early voting periods all lose their value when late

And some people are positioned so that information access is safety itself: residents of a disaster area, people carrying caregiving duties, small business owners directly exposed to regulatory change. Telling them less is easier is not advice. So the practical goal is not to cut the total but to separate information by type, because each type tolerates a different delay.

Type of informationWhen you need itMatching form

Politics and world affairsWeeklyWeekly digest

Changing the form rather than quitting

Once that table exists, the points of adjustment become visible. Three axes.

From stream to cycle

Checking the same story ten times a day means reading ten drafts; reading it once a week means reading one settled account. There is a range in which cutting the number of checks does not reduce what you learn and may increase it, because you no longer have to count each draft and each correction separately.

From event to context

Instead of what happened, ask where this sits on which trend. Long-form articles, white papers, statistical tables and books belong here. The items in the right-hand column of section 2, the ones that never became news, are the body text in this form.

From notification to retrieval

A notification lets the sender choose the moment; retrieval lets the receiver choose it. Even with identical content, that difference moves control of attention. So the object of adjustment is less what you read than who sets the timing. In practice it looks like this.

  • Restrict push notifications to things that become impossible to act on if late
  • Decide per topic how often you need to know, then pick a medium that matches that cycle
  • Treat the first 24 hours of coverage as a draft and defer conclusions
  • Pair different forms covering the same story, and build a habit of opening the primary source one step further at least once per topic: agency statements, statistical tables, court rulings
  • After reading, ask not what you learned but what changed. That answer becomes the basis for the next choice

This is not about cutting the total but adjusting how information arrives and in what unit. Attempts to force the total down rarely last, while a form set once costs almost nothing to maintain.

Asking yourself what never becomes news

The table in section 2 works as a tool. Read an article while also asking why this became an article, and the other side comes into view.

  • Where does this event sit on the trend? How does it compare with the same month last year?
  • What is the denominator behind this number? How many in total, and how many of them?
  • What part of this subject never becomes an article precisely because it happens every day?
  • If this same subject improved, when and in what form would it be reported? Usually the answer is it would not have been

Answering requires forms outside the news: regular statistics, long time series, periodic institutional surveys, administrative records. In the 1978 experiments from section 3, the reason real ratios of accidents and chronic disease diverged so far from people's judgments is that only one side arrived daily. The side that does not arrive has to be fetched.

This account falls into the same trap

Closing honestly requires this section. News distorts perception is itself a rare and dramatic story. It is tidy, easy to remember, and counterexamples do not readily come to mind. The very selection criteria criticised above are criteria this account also passes.

  • Warnings aimed at media have recurred with printing, novels, newspapers, radio and television. Some were right and some were overstated, and which kind the current one is cannot be settled from inside the current one
  • I read less news and felt better carries selection bias. People in a position to stop are the ones who stop, and people who felt better are the ones who write it up
  • The individual variation from section 4 applies here as well. What was covered above is averages only. And the proposal that changing the form helps is a reasoned position, not a validated prescription: none of the studies cited has tested it

So what remains here is not a conclusion but the shape of a question. Once you know what becomes news and what does not, the seat you read from changes even when the article is the same. That is the share an understanding of form can deliver.

What could not be confirmed

  • The total participant count and measurement details in Soroka et al. (2019). Public summaries state only that it was an experiment across 17 countries and 6 continents, so no figure appears in the text above
  • Sample size and study design in Pachur (2024). Only the bibliographic record (Cognition volume 246, article 105736) is open, so only the direction of the argument is cited
  • The figure behind the prompting essay's claim that a substantial share of articles contain factual errors. Its original source and method could not be traced, so it is not used
  • Long-term tracking studies of Korean-language audiences, and large studies measuring news consumption by actual usage logs rather than self-report

Summary

  • News is not a sample of the world but a selected list. The criteria are rarity, sudden change, visibility and named actors, and steady slow things do not pass
  • That leaves room for risk perception to diverge from real frequency. The size of the divergence is contested, and papers arguing the other way exist
  • The smoking analogy is useful for describing the shape of a habit and cannot carry a claim about magnitude of harm. The fifty-year cohort, the dose-response relationship and the biological mechanism exist on one side only
  • The correlation between news consumption and anxiety is repeatedly observed but its direction is not settled. Reverse causation and feedback remain live explanations, and most of the data rests on cross-sectional designs and self-report
  • Blocking has a cost. So the direction of adjustment is not blocking but replacing the form: stream to cycle, event to context, notification to retrieval

All of it compresses into one proposal: set why did this become an article alongside what happened. It is one extra question, and it is enough to make the same page read differently.
