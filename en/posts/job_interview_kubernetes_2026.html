What Job Interviews Taught Me About Kubernetes

How K8s Interview Questions Reveal What Actually Matters in Production

Jun 18, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: The Interview Room as an Unlikely Teacher

Engineers who have worked with Kubernetes for years often share a common experience: the moment you start preparing for a job interview, concepts you use every day without thinking suddenly feel unfamiliar. What exactly is the difference between a Pod and a Deployment? Why does etcd exist? How does an Ingress Controller differ from a Service? These things work fine in the cluster, but explaining them clearly turns out to be surprisingly hard.

As of June 2026, Kubernetes has become the de facto standard for cloud-native infrastructure. Startups and enterprises alike run their services on K8s. As a result, Kubernetes questions in DevOps, SRE, and backend engineering interviews have become unavoidable. What's interesting is that these interview questions — regardless of their original intent — paradoxically point with uncanny accuracy at the concepts that genuinely matter in production.

This post draws on insights from Kubernetes interview experiences to map out the core concepts that keep showing up, the difference between what to memorize versus what to understand, and what preparation strategies actually work. This is not a memorization sheet — it is a map of real K8s knowledge revealed by interview pressure.

1. Concepts Interviews Keep Testing — and Why

1.1 Pod vs Deployment — Unit vs Manager

One of the first questions in almost every Kubernetes interview: "Explain the difference between a Pod and a Deployment." It sounds simple, but the question is probing whether you understand K8s abstraction layers.

A Pod is the smallest deployable unit in Kubernetes. One or more containers share the same network namespace, storage, and a single IP address. But Pods are fundamentally ephemeral — when they die, they don't come back on their own.

A Deployment is the Pod's manager. You declare a desired state, and the Deployment controller continuously reconciles the actual state toward it. It restarts dead pods, handles rolling updates, and enables rollbacks. In practice, you rarely create Pods directly. Almost everything goes through a higher-level controller like a Deployment or StatefulSet.

The reason this question appears in every interview is straightforward: if you don't understand this distinction, you haven't grasped Kubernetes' core philosophy of declarative state management.

1.2 Service Types — Choosing How to Expose Your App

Questions about Service types are equally common. Because Pod IPs change constantly, you need a stable access point. That's what Services provide. Interviews typically test your knowledge of three main types:

  • ClusterIP: A virtual IP accessible only within the cluster. Used for internal microservice communication.
  • NodePort: Exposes a specific port on each node. Useful for development and testing, but has limitations for production use.
  • LoadBalancer: Automatically provisions a cloud provider load balancer. The go-to for receiving external traffic in production, though attaching a load balancer to every Service adds cost.

Simply memorizing these types misses the point. What matters is knowing when to use which. In production, the typical pattern is: internal services use ClusterIP, and external exposure is handled by Ingress — with a LoadBalancer attached to just the one Ingress Controller. Being able to walk through this flow in an interview changes the tone of the conversation entirely.

1.3 Ingress — The HTTP Routing Gateway

Ingress handles HTTP/HTTPS traffic routing to Services inside the cluster — path-based and host-based routing, TLS termination. Nearly every web-facing service in production goes through an Ingress.

A common point of confusion in interviews: Ingress is a Kubernetes object (rules definition), but the Ingress Controller (nginx-ingress, AWS ALB Ingress Controller, etc.) is what actually implements those rules. Without an Ingress Controller running in your cluster, creating an Ingress object does nothing. Failing to understand this distinction leads directly to the real-world problem of "Why doesn't my Ingress work?"

1.4 Scheduling — Which Node Gets the Pod

Scheduling questions appear regularly in mid-to-senior level interviews. The kube-scheduler selects nodes in two phases:

  • Filtering: Eliminates nodes that cannot accommodate the pod. Insufficient resources, taint/toleration mismatches, and unmet affinity rules are common filters.
  • Scoring: Ranks remaining nodes and selects the best fit.

The number one cause of a pod stuck in Pending in real clusters is either resource requests set higher than any node can provide, or deploying to tainted nodes without the corresponding tolerations. Understanding this mechanism means you can read a kubectl describe pod output and immediately spot the problem. That's exactly why interviews ask about scheduling: it is a proxy for troubleshooting ability.

1.5 RBAC — Who Can Do What

RBAC (Role-Based Access Control) is the foundation of Kubernetes security. Interviews frequently probe whether you understand how ServiceAccounts, Roles/ClusterRoles, and RoleBindings/ClusterRoleBindings connect.

In practice, the principle of least privilege means giving pods and users only the permissions they actually need. CI/CD pipelines accessing the cluster, monitoring agents scraping metrics — a misconfigured RBAC creates either a security hole or an operational failure. This is not interview-only knowledge. It comes up every day in real cluster operations.

2. What to Memorize vs What to Understand

2.1 Things Worth Memorizing

The list of things worth actually memorizing for a Kubernetes interview is shorter than most people think. Primarily short, frequently-used keywords:

  • Pod status codes: Pending, Running, Succeeded, Failed, Unknown
  • Failure reason codes: CrashLoopBackOff, OOMKilled, ImagePullBackOff, ErrImagePull
  • Core kubectl commands: get, describe, logs, exec, apply, delete, rollout
  • Service type names: ClusterIP, NodePort, LoadBalancer, ExternalName

These you need to recall instantly. They signal to the interviewer that you have actually used Kubernetes in practice.

2.2 Things You Must Actually Understand

The following concepts cannot be memorized — they require understanding the flow and the reasoning:

  • Control plane architecture: The roles of kube-apiserver, etcd, kube-scheduler, and controller-manager, and how they interact. You should be able to explain why etcd exists.
  • Declarative state management: K8s works by declaring desired state and having controllers reconcile actual state toward it. Understanding this philosophy changes how you approach debugging.
  • Pod networking model: Every pod can reach every other pod without NAT. Services, DNS, and CNI are built on this foundation.
  • Controller patterns: Why Deployment, ReplicaSet, StatefulSet, and DaemonSet each exist. Not as a list to memorize, but as a judgment: "What should I use for this workload?"

This is why the CKA exam is open-book. Looking up exact field names in the documentation is something anyone can do. What both the exam and interviews are testing is whether you can use that documentation to solve real problems.

2.3 What Interviewers Actually Evaluate

A recurring observation in interview experience write-ups from the engineering community: "The most common failure point isn't an obscure question about etcd internals — it's being unable to clearly describe the difference between a Pod, a Service, and a Deployment." More candidates stumble on foundational objects than on advanced topics like operators or custom resources.

Interviewers are evaluating two things. First, fundamentals: why pods have IPs, why services exist, why deployments are convenient. Second, troubleshooting mindset: how do you approach a Pending pod, what do you check when a service isn't connecting. When both are solid, the rest grows in proportion to experience.

3. The Real-World Importance Map Revealed by Interviews

3.1 Why Organizations Actually Adopt Kubernetes

When you hear why various companies adopted Kubernetes, an interesting pattern emerges. Organizational reasons often come first, ahead of technical ones:

  • Standardization: When every service deploys the same way, a new engineer can read a YAML file and understand the architecture. Dependence on any individual's tribal knowledge decreases.
  • Traceability: Combined with GitOps, every deployment is recorded in git. Who deployed what and when becomes auditable, and compliance can be automated.
  • Shared language: "The pod is in CrashLoopBackOff" becomes a shared vocabulary. Problems can be communicated and collaborated on without requiring deep background knowledge from everyone involved.

Understanding this context helps you avoid a common interview mistake: answering "Why do you use Kubernetes?" with only technical advantages. The decision to adopt K8s often starts with a CTO's desire for standardization, and the technical benefits follow.

3.2 The Failure Modes You Will Actually Encounter

The failure states that appear as interview staples are exactly what happens most often in real clusters. This is not a coincidence.

  • CrashLoopBackOff: Container starts and immediately dies. Usually caused by app errors, missing environment variables, or config file path issues. Diagnose with kubectl logs.
  • OOMKilled: Container exceeded its memory limit and was forcibly terminated. Requires understanding requests vs limits.
  • ImagePullBackOff: Cannot pull the image. Common causes: typo in image name, missing imagePullSecret for a private registry.
  • Pending pod: Over-specified resource requests or node taint issues. The Events section of kubectl describe pod gives the answer.

These patterns are not things to memorize for interviews. You need to create these situations on purpose in a local cluster and recover from them — only then do you speak about them naturally in both interviews and production.

3.3 Controller Selection — A Common Interview Stumbling Block

Which controller to use comes up frequently in both real work and interviews:

ControllerWhen to UseKey Characteristic
DeploymentStateless apps (web servers, APIs)Rolling updates, rollbacks
StatefulSetStateful apps (databases, queues)Stable network identity, ordered scaling
DaemonSetOne pod per node (log collectors, monitoring)Exactly one pod per node
Job/CronJobOne-time or scheduled batch tasksTerminates on completion

Answering "Deployment" to "What controller would you use for a database on Kubernetes?" is a red flag in any interview. Being able to explain StatefulSet and why — stable pod names, ordered scaling, persistent volume binding — is what separates someone who knows Kubernetes from someone who has looked it up.

4. Interview Preparation Strategies That Actually Work

4.1 Break Things on a Local Cluster and Fix Them

Setting up a local cluster with Minikube, Kind, or k3s and deploying a real app is more effective than any book. The key is not just deploying — it is intentionally creating failures:

  • Set resources.requests higher than any node can provide to trigger a Pending pod.
  • Use a wrong image name to trigger ImagePullBackOff.
  • Introduce a bug in your app to experience CrashLoopBackOff.
  • Deliberately mismatch the service selector to break connectivity.

After going through this process, kubectl describe and kubectl logs stop being commands and become a language for reading problems. Interviewers distinguish people who have been through this experience from those who haven't within a few exchanges.

4.2 Use Official Documentation as a Tool

The fact that the CKA exam is open-book carries a lesson. The Kubernetes documentation (kubernetes.io/docs) is meant to be open in a tab during real work. The goal is not to memorize every field — it is the ability to find what you need quickly.

Manifest examples, RBAC configurations, and Ingress rule syntax are things you look up when needed. In an interview, a brief pause of "let me think about the exact field name..." is actually a signal of practical experience, not a weakness.

4.3 Be Able to Explain 'Why,' Not Just 'What'

The differentiating factor in interviews is the ability to explain 'Why' rather than 'What'. Not "what is a pod" but "why is a pod a larger unit than a container," not "what is a Service" but "why Services exist (because pod IPs change)," not "what is etcd" but "why etcd is managed separately from the cluster (to avoid a single point of failure)."

Preparing this way creates a depth of understanding that is qualitatively different from rote Q&A memorization. And that depth shines in both interviews and production work.

5. As of June 2026 — Trends in Kubernetes Interview Questions

5.1 The Rise of Advanced Topics

The baseline difficulty of Kubernetes interviews has risen. Pods, Services, and Deployments are now assumed knowledge, and senior positions regularly include:

  • Networking depth: Differences between CNI plugins (Calico, Cilium, Flannel) and selection criteria; restricting pod-to-pod communication with NetworkPolicy.
  • Autoscaling: Differences between HPA, VPA, and KEDA.
  • Helm and Kustomize: Differences and practical selection criteria for manifest management.
  • GitOps: Declarative deployment pipeline design with ArgoCD or Flux.
  • Security: Pod Security Standards, OPA Gatekeeper, secret management with Vault.

5.2 The Influence of Managed Kubernetes

With managed K8s services like EKS, GKE, and AKS becoming standard, more companies now ask for "experience effectively operating a managed environment" rather than "experience building a cluster from scratch." IAM-integrated RBAC, autoscaler configuration, and load balancer controller integration vary per cloud, so preparing with the specific cloud environment you have worked in is the practical approach.

5.3 Interviews as a Learning Tool

One of the most valuable insights from going through multiple Kubernetes interviews at different companies is that good interview questions turn out to be excellent learning guides. "Why StatefulSet," "the difference between Ingress Controller and Service," "why etcd backup matters" — these questions simultaneously encode both the design philosophy and the operational reality of Kubernetes. Trying to genuinely understand these questions while preparing for interviews is itself a path to raising your K8s competency by a level.

Conclusion: Interviews Are a Mirror Reflecting Your Knowledge Map

The concepts Kubernetes interviews test — whether intentionally or not — align precisely with the problems you encounter most often in production. The ephemerality of pods, the stable access points provided by services, the resource dependency of scheduling, the principle of least privilege in RBAC. These are not interview topics — they are the core principles of operating Kubernetes.

As of June 2026, Kubernetes is a default, not an option. If that's true, then a Kubernetes interview is not merely a gate to pass — it is an opportunity to audit your own current standing. Rather than spending time memorizing, breaking and fixing things on a local cluster while building up the 'Why' is the fastest path to preparing for both the interview and the job.

The most important lesson the interview taught me is paradoxical: don't study Kubernetes for the interview. Truly understand Kubernetes, and the interview will follow.
