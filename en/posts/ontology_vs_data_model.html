When one word means three things in three departments

The warehouse runs fine. The pipelines do not fail. And yet a single question about how many customers there were last month produces three different numbers from marketing, finance and support. None of them is wrong. Marketing counts registered accounts, finance counts legal entities that received an invoice, support counts people who filed a ticket. The problem is not the data. It is that the word customer is not defined anywhere.

Attach a language model to that situation and the crack does not close, it widens faster. People silently patch context in a meeting. Models do not. A great many of the moments where someone wires up the internal document store and concludes that the results disappoint are not retrieval quality problems at all, they are problems where the meaning of the question was never settled inside the organisation.

That is the backdrop for ontology reappearing in data conversations. In August 2026 a GeekNews topic pointed at a learning site that organises Palantir Foundry and the ontology concept in practitioner language (checked 2026-08-19), and that site opens by insisting an ontology is not a rebranded data model. That sentence is the starting point here. What exactly separates an ontology from a schema, and why is the distinction being argued again now. For the modelling language and tooling comparison I already wrote about Malloy; this piece stays one level above that, on the conceptual axis.

Start with the words - what each of the four actually names

Half the confusion is not conceptual difficulty. It comes from four words being used in place of one another. When someone says data model in a meeting and nobody has fixed whether that means an ERD, a conceptual definition or a metric definition, two hours pass and nothing is decided.

Schema - the shape of storage

A schema decides the shape data takes in storage. Tables and columns, types, primary and foreign keys, indexes and constraints all belong here. It answers one question: where and in what form does this value get written and read. Because a schema follows the storage arrangement, splitting a table for performance changes the schema even though nothing about the business concept moved.

Data model - the broadest and haziest of the words

Data model is the widest of the three terms and therefore the one that misleads most often. The tradition splits it into conceptual, logical and physical layers, but in practice, when someone says data model in conversation, they almost always mean the logical or physical one. In other words the term reaches up to the conceptual layer while its actual usage is glued to storage. That mismatch is why anyone meeting ontology for the first time asks whether this is just a data model. I have covered the conceptual side from a design angle in the piece on domain modelling.

Semantic layer - one source for the arithmetic

A semantic layer keeps metric and dimension definitions in one place so that dashboards and notebooks alike compute from the same definition. dbt describes its semantic layer as moving business metric definitions such as revenue out of BI tools and down into the modelling layer, so that when a definition changes, every place that calls it refreshes and consistency follows (checked 2026-08-19). The question it answers is how this number is computed.

Ontology - existence and relation

What an ontology fixes is not arithmetic but what exists and how those things relate to one another. W3C describes OWL, the ontology language, as a semantic web language designed to represent rich and complex knowledge about things, groups of things and relations between things (checked 2026-08-19). A customer exists, an order exists, and a customer owns orders. There is no table and no join in that sentence.

LayerWhat it definesWho mainly uses itWhat breaks without it
SchemaThe shape of storage: tables, columns, types, keys, constraintsData engineers, backend developers, DBAsThe same value accumulates in several shapes and every join requires remembering a fresh rule
Semantic layerHow metrics and dimensions are computed: revenue, active users, conversionAnalysts, BI users, data consuming applicationsRevenue differs per dashboard and there is no basis for deciding which one is right
OntologyConcepts and relations, plus the actions possible on top of themOwning business teams, rule setters, programs that operate the systemThe same word names different things per department and nobody can say how far automation may reach

One thing becomes clear from the table. The three layers are not competitors. You cannot put a semantic layer on top of nothing, and having an ontology does not retire the schema. What does hurt is misdiagnosing which missing layer causes the pain you feel today, because then the money and the months go to the wrong layer.

The core difference - an ontology sits apart from the data

What separates an ontology from a schema is not expressive power but the direction of coupling. A schema is attached to storage and moves when storage moves. An ontology is designed to sit apart from it. The concept of an order must be one thing whether the data lives in one table, is split across three, or flows through an event stream.

That separation shows up in product documentation in the same words. Palantir public docs call the Foundry Ontology an operational layer for the organization and state that it sits on top of the digital assets integrated into the platform, namely datasets, virtual tables and models (checked 2026-08-19). Sitting on top is simply another way of naming the separation. The goal is that you can swap the layer below and the conceptual definition above survives.

What that separation actually buys is three things.

  • Restructuring storage does not shake the concepts. Change partitioning, split a table, and the definition of customer is untouched
  • The same concept across systems gets one name. There is finally a place to write down that the user in the account database and the payer in the billing system are the same person
  • The definition survives as a sentence people read. Unlike a wiki page this one is actually consulted by systems, so neglect shows up quickly

The cost is equally clear. One more layer means one more thing to maintain, the mapping. Rename a column below without fixing the mapping above and the system does not raise an error, it quietly returns a wrong answer. That is a slower failure to notice than a schema change. So any decision to adopt an ontology has to arrive with an answer to who owns the mapping and when it gets verified. Without that answer, the added layer becomes exactly that much debt.

Once actions arrive, the character changes

Concepts and relations alone amount to a representation of knowledge. It resembles a dictionary, something to read. The moment cancel an order joins it, it stops being a dictionary and becomes a list of an operable world.

This split is not a conceptual game; it is reflected directly in product design. The public documentation cited above divides the ontology into semantic elements and kinetic elements. The semantic elements are object types, link types and properties; the kinetic elements are action types and functions; and interfaces are added for polymorphism across object types (checked 2026-08-19). The design intent is to keep named nouns and named verbs on the same layer.

Defining actions means three practical things.

  • Write paths acquire names. Instead of an UPDATE statement there is order cancellation, and validation rules can hang off that name
  • Permissions attach to verbs. The question stops being whether someone can write to a table and becomes whether this person may cancel an order
  • Audit records land in business language. Whoever reads the log knows what happened without reverse engineering SQL

Read the other way, an ontology with no actions defined is closer to documentation. That is not worthless, but the thing that usually justifies the cost is this action layer. A read-only concept dictionary can be built in a wiki.

Why now - agents need verbs, not columns

The experience of connecting internal documents to a language model and finding the result underwhelming is common by now. It is tempting to blame retrieval quality, but the cause is often that a reading problem and a writing problem were treated as one lump.

What the refund policy says lives in a document. A request to cancel this customer's unpaid orders placed since yesterday lives nowhere in any document. That is reading current state and changing it, not finding a document. Asking the machinery that is good at the first to do the second is expected to fail.

Doing the second means handing the model named verbs, and the tool-calling specifications have exactly that shape. The Model Context Protocol defines a tool as a name, a human readable description and an input schema, with an optional output schema. For trust and safety it states that there should always be a human in the loop with the ability to deny tool invocations, asks servers to validate inputs, implement access controls and rate limit invocations, and asks clients to prompt for confirmation on sensitive operations and log tool usage for audit purposes (checked 2026-08-19).

Here the problem surfaces. Build tools one at a time and definitions and permissions scatter across as many places as there are tools. If three teams each build their own order cancellation, the cancellable conditions get encoded three different ways. That is precisely where the ontology is floated as the point of contact. Define concepts, relations and actions once on one layer and derive the tool list from it, and the definition stays in one place while the permission boundary is decided on that same layer.

That said, this is a candidate and not a conclusion. A well designed domain API does the same job. What the ontology claims is not a capability but whether definitions converge in one place, and that claim is only realised when an organisation actually intends to converge them. Buying a tool does not create that intent.

The habit of holding relations between concepts explicitly also shows up outside data work. I covered treating a codebase as a graph in the piece on code knowledge graphs, and the problem shape is similar. Finding something similar with vectors and knowing exactly what calls what are different kinds of information.

Not a new idea - twenty years of RDF and OWL

Ontology is sometimes discussed as if it were invented in 2026, which is not the case. The project of writing down concepts and relations in a machine readable way has existed as a standard for over twenty years.

  • RDF is a framework for representing information on the web, and its core unit is the triple made of subject, predicate and object. RDF 1.1 became a W3C Recommendation on 25 February 2014 (checked 2026-08-19)
  • OWL is the ontology language built on top of it, offering richer expressive power. OWL 1 arrived in 2004 and OWL 2 in 2009, with the second edition published on 11 December 2012 (checked 2026-08-19)
  • Per W3C, knowledge written in OWL can be used so that programs verify consistency or make implicit information explicit

What vendor documentation now calls object types and link types occupies the same position that classes and properties have occupied in the standards world. The vocabulary forked; the problem statement did not. Knowing that lineage materially improves adoption judgement, because twenty years of failure and success is already published.

And that record is half a failure. Retrospectives on the semantic web field conclude that the original web-scale vision was not broadly realised, but that its technical foundations survived in the form of knowledge graphs. The spread of open structures such as schema.org and Wikidata, and the knowledge graphs built in-house by large internet companies, are offered as the evidence.

What blocked it then - agreement cost, not build cost

The obstacles those same retrospectives list still read as current. The cost of maintaining annotations, the absence of an incentive to publish, the loop where no applications meant no data and no data meant no applications, verbose standards, reasoning engines that could not scale against the noise and contradiction of the web, thin tooling, and the difficulty of agreeing on an ontology at all. Setting that list down again and sorting what changed from what did not is the only honest way to approach the question of whether this time is different.

Obstacle back thenHow much it changedWhat still remains
Building and maintaining definitions takes too many handsSubstantially improved. Extraction and first drafts can be delegated to modelsDrafting got faster, but judging whether it is right is still human work
No incentive because nothing consumed the definitionsGreatly changed. There is now something that consumes structured definitions immediatelyA consumer appearing does not automatically raise the quality of the definitions
Applications and data waiting on each otherOne side filled. Demand from the application side arrived firstData inside an organisation still has to be defined by that organisation
Standards are verbose and hard to learnPartly improved. Products wrap them in friendlier vocabularyThe friendlier vocabulary is usually that product's vocabulary
Reasoning did not scaleImproved. Compute and graph processing advancedDrawing logically sound answers from contradictory input is as hard as ever
Difficulty of agreeing on an ontologyBarely changed at allCross-department agreement on definitions is the same class of problem it was

Read the table down its columns and the judgement writes itself. The technical rows mostly improved and the human row did not improve at all. Since the thing that actually collapsed these projects historically was the human row, it is fair to assume the same trap is still open.

The bill goes to the organisation, not to engineering

Building an ontology means moving organisational agreement into code. Inverted, it is even clearer: with no agreement there is nothing to move. A tool gives you a place to write the agreement down; it does not produce the agreement.

Which is why ontology projects usually stall somewhere other than the engineering room.

  • The meeting to settle one definition of customer drags on for weeks, because each department's definition is entirely correct within its own work
  • Agreement fails, so the effort retreats to defining only our own department's version for now
  • Departmental ontologies appear. The original problem has simply been reproduced one layer up
  • Attach automation in that state and you get agents running on different rules per department. Divergence spreads faster than it did when people were doing the work

One useful decision rule: if more than three departments have a stake in a single definition, it is not an engineering task but a decision task. Engineering tasks can be handed to whoever is good at them; decision tasks need someone named as the decider first. Skip that order and the definition meeting never ends, and an organisation that endures a few of those remembers ontology itself as a failed fashion. What failed was the sequence, not the concept.

There is a cheap way to test whether agreement exists. Write the minimum definition of one concept as sentences and circulate it to three departments. What follows is a conceptual example for explanation and was not executed or verified in any product.

Concept: Order
- Identity: what uniquely designates one order
- State set: placed / paid / shipping / completed / cancelled
- Transitions: which state may move to which
- Ownership: whose order is this (and what is the definition of that whose)
- Cancellability: from which state, by whom, until when

Five lines. If three departments cannot give the same answer to those five, it is not yet time to choose a tool. If the five are agreed, deciding which format to write them in becomes a much smaller problem.

How to start small - one workflow, not the whole company

A plan to draw the company-wide ontology first bills the maximum agreement cost all at once. Worse, nothing visible ships while that bill is being paid, so the project is usually cut or shelved at the first progress review.

The alternative is plain. Take as the starting unit one workflow people already run every day, define only the concepts that workflow touches, and lift only the actions that actually occur in it. Criteria for picking one:

  • High frequency. A workflow that runs often exposes a wrong definition quickly
  • Rules already written down somewhere. Refining an existing document is far cheaper than manufacturing agreement from a blank page
  • Visible cost of mistakes. If you can say who loses how much when it goes wrong, improvement is measurable
  • A single owning department. Starting cross-department agreement in the very first unit walks straight into the previous section's trap
  • No system replacement required. Bundle ontology adoption with a platform migration and failure causes become impossible to separate

For a sense of scale, one more example. Again, an illustrative example that was not executed or verified.

First unit example: returns handling
3 concepts - Order / ReturnRequest / Refund
2 relations - a ReturnRequest belongs to an Order, a Refund comes from a ReturnRequest
3 actions - open return, approve return, issue refund
For each action: who may perform it / under what conditions / what gets recorded

Three concepts, two relations, three actions. That size can be defined within weeks and attached to a live workflow. And setting the abandonment criterion at the start matters more than it looks. Something like: if that workflow is not actually running through this definition within the agreed period, we stop. Projects started without such a criterion survive without results and eat the budget of the next attempt.

What being tied to one platform ontology means

Platforms that sell an ontology generally deliver definition, execution and screens as one package, and that integration is part of the value. The trouble is that only when you try to leave does it become visible what was tied to what, and how tightly. To judge in advance, split the implementation into layers.

LayerWhat lives hereCost of moving itWhat to secure now
DefinitionWhich concepts, relations and actions exist and what their rules areRelatively low. The agreed content is independent of formatKeep a human readable copy of the definition too. If it only lives inside a format, it moves with that format
FormatThe data structure and file shape that hold the definitionMedium. Converters move it, but expressiveness gaps cause lossCheck whether it exports in machine readable form and whether the export can be imported back
RuntimePermission decisions, action execution, screens, audit records, integration pointsHighest. In practice close to rebuildingConsider keeping the real action logic in an external service rather than inside platform functions

Adoption decisions mostly look at the first layer, and the bill mostly arrives from the third. That mismatch is why lock-in discussions always happen after the fact. I have covered the same structure, where the real lock-in accumulates outside the repository, in the piece on moving code hosting.

So the things to ask are less a product comparison table and more these four.

  • Can the definition be exported whole in a machine readable form, structure intact, not as a screenshot or a document render
  • Can the export be imported back to reproduce the same state. A one-way export is half a feature
  • How deeply is the action definition tied to the platform's own function syntax. Logic expressed as calls to an external service is that much lighter
  • Is the permission model expressed in standard concepts, or in concepts that only mean something on that platform

For balance, the other side. Adopting standard formats such as RDF and OWL does not make migration free. Standards carry the definition; they do not carry the runtime. Even with a definition written in a standard, permission decisions, action execution and screens still have to be rebuilt. The value of standard adoption is not eliminating migration cost but keeping the longest-lived of the three layers outside the vendor. That is the practical meaning of separating the concept from the implementation.

  • I could not find independent research comparing automation success rates between organisations that adopted an ontology and those that did not. The judgements here rest on standards documents, published retrospectives and structural reasoning

Conclusion - three things to take away today

An ontology is not a superset of a schema; it is a different layer answering a different question. Schemas fix the shape of storage, semantic layers fix how metrics are computed, ontologies fix what exists, how it relates and what may be done to it. The moment those three are used interchangeably, the discussion stops moving.

Why it returned is equally clear. As more programs operate systems rather than report on them, what is needed is meaning and permission boundaries at the level of cancel an order rather than this column in this table. That does not make the ontology the only answer, nor does it guarantee this attempt succeeds. Of the things that blocked this project twenty years ago, the technical items mostly improved and the human items did not.

  • Fix the vocabulary first. Next time data model comes up in a meeting, establish whether it means storage, concepts or metrics before proceeding. That alone reclaims a large share of meeting time
  • Before choosing a tool, write one definition as sentences and circulate it. Order or customer, five lines is enough. If three departments cannot answer alike, this is not a product selection problem but a missing-decider problem
  • Start with one workflow, and set the abandonment criterion at the same time you start. A company-wide ontology bills the agreement cost all at once, and a project without a stopping rule survives without results

What an ontology ultimately demands of an organisation is that things which have been running along ambiguously get settled explicitly. That demand holds whether or not a tool is purchased, and no purchase pays it on your behalf. Which is why the first real step on this topic always sits outside the software.
