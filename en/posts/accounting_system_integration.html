The Payment Succeeded but the Books Are Empty - Where Accounting Follows Your Service

Why authorization, capture, settlement and refunds fall out of step, what evidence and filing deadlines demand from code, and where to keep the ledger

Aug 20, 2026 Dev & Infra

1. Introduction: the payment succeeded but the books are empty

Once the payment integration is wired up, it usually feels like the hard part is behind you. The authorization clears, the order flips to paid, and one more shipment lands in the queue. The screens look right and the logs are clean. Then, months later, somebody asks what last month's revenue was, and you discover there is no table that answers that question as written.

The payments table is right there. But it is a record of payments, not a record of revenue. The two overlap enough to look identical at first. They separate at a predictable moment: the first refund arrives, a partial cancellation gets mixed in, and the settlement deposit from the payment provider lands at a number that does not match the sum of authorizations. That divergence is not a bug. It is what happens when two different things were modelled as one.

This article maps the places where people building services run into accounting. It does not do the accounting for you. It is about which facts have to be preserved, and in what shape, for accounting to be possible later. Three conclusions up front.

  • Payment events and accounting facts sit on different clocks. Put them in one table and one of them is necessarily wrong
  • Evidence and filing are code requirements. Not a feature to bolt on later, but a constraint on which values must be final by when
  • What you deleted, you cannot prove. In accounting a correction is not a deletion but a record in the opposite direction

What this article is. What follows is a general explanation for people designing systems and is not tax advice. Which evidence a transaction requires, at what point and in what amount revenue is recognised, and which filing obligations apply all vary with business form and contract, and are matters for a qualified professional such as a licensed tax accountant and for the competent tax authority. The institutional details below are specific to Korea and were confirmed against public sources as of August 2026; rules change, and readers elsewhere should treat them as one worked example of a general pattern. This article only covers meeting obligations. Nothing here is about understating revenue or skipping evidence.

2. Where developers meet accounting is not the API

The first instinct when scoping an accounting integration is to look for tooling. Which accounting software exposes an application programming interface, which library will build journal entries. That step rarely blocks anyone. The blockage sits earlier.

A payment produces revenue. Revenue carries a recognition point, it carries evidence, and it carries tax. No library decides those three. They are decided by what actually happened: what you sold and in whose name you sold it. So three questions have to be answered before the schema.

  • Who sold it. Are you the seller, or did you connect a seller with a buyer? This answer changes the size of the revenue number outright
  • When was it sold. At order time, at payment time, at delivery, or in proportion to the subscription period that has elapsed
  • What proves it. Whether the counterparty is a business or a consumer changes the kind of evidence you must retain

Build tables without those answers and you will rebuild them when the answers arrive, because the accumulated data will not contain enough to reconstruct them. Settle the answers first and the schema gets simpler, not harder. What you need is not a sophisticated accounting model but a raw record that can be re-aggregated under any later definition.

3. Payment data and accounting data are not the same thing

This is the load-bearing claim of the article. Payment data is a record of money moving. Accounting data is a record of a transaction becoming real. They watch the same event on different clocks.

3.1 One payment is not one event

A single card payment splits into at least three. Authorization reserves headroom. No money has moved, and cancelling it leaves only a trace. Capture turns that authorization into a claim, and that is where the claim actually comes into being. Settlement is the payment provider wiring money to the merchant account days later, net of fees. The three happen on different days and carry different amounts.

On top of that sit refunds and partial cancellations. A refund does not remove the original transaction; it is a new event pointing the other way. A partial cancellation splits the original and forces the remaining amount to be recomputed. If a multi-line cart carried a discount, the question of which line gives up how much appears immediately, and if that allocation was never stored it cannot be reconstructed. The mechanics of allocations drifting by a single unit are covered separately in the won has no decimal place.

3.2 The three most common misreadings

  • Treating the deposit as revenue. The settlement deposit is already net of fees. Booking that net figure as revenue makes the expense disappear from the books
  • Treating the authorization time as the sale time. Authorization is a beginning, not a completion. Authorizations that are never captured genuinely exist
  • Deleting the original when a refund arrives. The moment you delete, last month's revenue changes retroactively. The number you reported and the number you can produce now diverge, and the evidence that would explain the gap is gone too

So the structure worth recommending is plain. Store payment events raw, exactly as they arrived, and put a separate translation layer between those events and accounting facts. With the original intact, a change of definition can be replayed. Overwrite the original with a processed version and there is no way back from that point on.

4. Six places where the timing diverges

The table below puts payment-side events next to the questions accounting asks. The right column is the mistake that actually recurs at each point.

Payment-side eventWhat accounting asksCommon mistake here
AuthorizationNothing has completed yet. Is there a basis to call this revenue?Booking revenue on authorization, so uncaptured items sit in the revenue total
CaptureWhen did the claim become final?Never collecting capture results, so aggregation runs on authorizations alone
Settlement depositHow does this split between revenue and fees?Storing only the net deposit, so gross and fee cannot be recovered
RefundWhich period's revenue is reduced?Editing or deleting the original row, so past aggregates shift
Partial cancellationWhat is the composition of the remaining amount?Not storing per-line allocations, so recomputation differs from the original
Processing feeIs this a reduction of revenue or a separate cost?Netting the fee out of revenue up front, so the expense line vanishes

All six rows can land on different days. That is why one payment to one accounting record does not hold as a mapping. The shape that works in practice is many payment events, many accounting records, joined by an order identifier and an event identifier.

Subscriptions add a layer. Collecting a year up front does not make the whole amount that month's revenue. Recognising it in proportion to the elapsed period is the general principle, and for that the data must retain the start and end of the subscription period, the cancellation point, and any plan change point. Store the charged amount alone and no method will reconstruct the period allocation.

5. Korea's rules, part one - evidence: electronic tax invoices and cash receipts

Rules decide what code must have finalised by when. The following is public guidance from Korea's National Tax Service, confirmed in August 2026, and applies to businesses operating in Korea. Which rule applies to a specific transaction was outside what could be confirmed here, and that call belongs to a professional and the competent tax office.

5.1 Electronic tax invoice

This is the evidence used in business-to-business transactions in Korea. The National Tax Service explains that the rule is to issue at the supply time set by the Value-Added Tax Act, with monthly aggregate tax invoices as an exception issuable up to the tenth day of the month following the month of supply. If that day is a Saturday or a public holiday, the deadline moves to the next business day. After issuance, the issuance details are to be transmitted to the National Tax Service by the day after issuance. The governing provision is Article 32 of the Value-Added Tax Act.

The scope of mandatory issuance has widened in stages. On the National Tax Service's own account, all corporate businesses have been covered since January 2011, while for sole proprietors the threshold on the previous year's supply value per place of business has kept falling and stands at 80 million won from July 2024. The guidance adds that the obligation begins on 1 July of the year following the year in which the threshold was crossed.

What these deadlines impose on code is concrete.

  • If you issue monthly, the previous month must be in a finalizable state in the first days of the new month. You need a policy for corrections that arrive after the close
  • If transmission is daily, you must collect issuance results within a day and retry failures. Fire-and-forget issuance quietly produces gaps
  • Whether an item is in scope depends on whether the counterparty is a business, which means that flag has to exist in the order data
  • When a correction is needed, the original and the correction must survive as a pair. Overwrite the original and you lose what was changed and why

5.2 Cash receipt

This is the evidence used for cash transactions with consumers in Korea. Per the National Tax Service, a business in a mandatory issuance industry that supplies goods or services for 100,000 won or more per transaction including value-added tax and receives the payment in cash must issue a receipt even if the consumer does not ask. Where the consumer declines or their details are unknown, the guidance directs self-issuance under a designated code, within five days of receiving the cash. The governing provisions are Article 162-3 paragraph 4 of the Income Tax Act and Article 117-2 paragraph 4 of the Corporate Tax Act, with the covered industries listed in a schedule to the Enforcement Decree of the Income Tax Act.

What engineering tends to miss is the scope of cash. If there is even one path by which the service receives money other than by card, check whether that path is in scope too. Once payment methods multiply into bank transfer, virtual accounts and stored balance, the design has to absorb the fact that evidence paths branch by payment method.

6. Korea's rules, part two - the VAT filing cycle sets your close

If evidence is a per-transaction problem, filing is a per-period problem. The filing cycle decides when the system has to have that period's numbers final. The table below is the National Tax Service's guidance for sole proprietors in Korea, confirmed in August 2026.

FilingPeriod coveredFiling and payment windowNote
First half, preliminary1 Jan to 31 Mar1 Apr to 25 AprTaxable period 1 Jan to 30 Jun
First half, final1 Jan to 30 Jun1 Jul to 25 Jul
Second half, preliminary1 Jul to 30 Sep1 Oct to 25 OctTaxable period 1 Jul to 31 Dec
Second half, final1 Jul to 31 Dec1 Jan to 25 Jan of the next year
Simplified taxpayer1 Jan to 31 Dec1 Jan to 25 Jan of the next yearTaxable period is one year

The guidance describes value-added tax as filed and paid on a six-month taxable period, with each taxable period divided again into three months to create a preliminary filing window in the middle. It adds that in the ordinary case corporate businesses file four times a year and sole proprietors twice. Sole proprietors and corporations below a certain size are described as paying by assessment notice instead of filing a preliminary return, but who falls into which treatment is a case-by-case determination.

What this calendar demands of a system is a finalizable state. Asking what the last six months of revenue were, just before the deadline, must not return a different answer each time.

  • Period boundaries must be unambiguous. Whether a transaction belongs to 30 June or 1 July cannot depend on when the query runs
  • Closed periods must be locked. If a closed period's numbers move, they no longer match what was filed
  • Events arriving after the close need a rule. A refund this month against last month's sale will keep happening
  • The same question must return the same answer. If rerunning the aggregation changes the value, it is an estimate, not an aggregate

7. Brokered or sold - whose revenue is it

The same screen and the same payment can belong to you or not, and that split changes the size of the revenue number outright.

Under Korean law, Article 2 paragraph 4 of the Act on Consumer Protection in Electronic Commerce defines mail order brokerage as permitting the use of a cyber mall, or providing advertising means for mail order in your own name or with your name displayed on them, so as to perform part of a mail order transaction such as supplying information or accepting offers. Article 20 of the same Act requires a mail order broker to inform consumers in advance, in a way they can easily notice, that the broker is not a party to the transaction, and where that notice is not given, imposes joint liability for property damage caused to consumers by the intent or negligence of the party who requested the brokerage.

Accounting has a parallel split. If you promised to provide the good or service yourself, the gross transaction value is revenue; if you arranged for another party to provide it, only the fee you received is. The specific criteria in the standards are not quoted at paragraph level here, and the reason is recorded below under what could not be confirmed.

The point is that engineering does not make this determination. The job is to split the values so either conclusion can be reconstructed.

  • Keep gross transaction value and brokerage fee in separate columns. Do not derive one by subtracting the other
  • Name the settlement counterparty. Recording where the money finally goes is what makes both gross and net presentations available
  • Retain the seller identifier and per-seller settlement records. In a brokered structure your revenue and the seller payout are separate flows
  • If direct sales and brokerage are mixed, tag each transaction and log which notice was shown to the consumer and when. Classifying in bulk after the fact does not work, and the presence of that notice on screen may have to be proven later

8. Where to put the ledger - four choices and what each costs

The design decision converges on who holds the ledger. There is no single right answer; it turns on scale and on where the responsibility for the close sits.

ChoiceWhat you gainWhat it costsWhere it fits
Own ledger in the service databaseFast queries and no drift between product screens and the numbersYou own the chart of accounts and closing rules, and rule changes need deploymentsEarly stage, simple transaction types, no accounting staff yet
Integration with accounting SaaSRegulatory change is absorbed on that sideIntegration failure and lag become new failure modesModerate volume with a standard selling structure
Integration with an ERP systemPurchasing, inventory and accounting agree in one placeHeavy to adopt and maintain, with a slow change cycleBusinesses where inventory and supply chain move together
Raw record in service, journals outsideReplayability and delegated rules at the same timeYou must define when the two systems are supposed to agreeSeveral transaction types and the close owned by finance

The fourth is where most teams settle. The service side holds the raw record of what happened plus a reconciliation summary, and journal entries and the close go to the accounting system. The division matches the nature of each side: the service knows the instant an event occurred more precisely than anything else, and the accounting system manages the rules for mapping that event to accounts better than anything else. Rules can then change without touching service code.

Whichever choice you make, one condition does not go away: persist a reconciliation result between the two systems every day. Skip it and the first sign of a gap arrives at quarter end, when identifying which item on which date caused it takes days on its own.

9. Auditability - offset instead of delete, reverse instead of edit

What separates accounting data from ordinary operational data is that the past must not change. Edit a figure that has already been closed and used as the basis of a filing, and past aggregates shift retroactively, so a report pulled earlier and the same report pulled today no longer agree. The evidence that would explain the difference goes with it.

So the principle is simple. Keep the ledger append only. When a correction is needed, post a reversing entry that offsets the original and a correct entry at the current time. The wrong entry survives, the reversal survives, and the new entry survives. When someone later asks why a number is what it is, you show three lines in order.

9.1 Three places developers get this wrong

  • Updating a status column so the history is overwritten. Flipping status from paid to refunded erases the fact that it was ever paid. Status should be a projection folded from events, with the original living on the event side
  • Hiding rows behind a deleted flag. Hidden rows drop out of totals silently, and because no reason is recorded, the difference cannot be explained afterwards
  • Letting a batch rerun insert the same entry twice. Double counting is discovered later than omission in accounting, because a total that grew does not look wrong

The third is structural. Make the source event identifier part of the unique key on the accounting record and a rerun cannot insert twice. Using the identifier the payment provider sends is safer than one you generate, because yours may be regenerated during a retry while theirs stays the same for the same event.

Add one more thing and auditability is essentially covered: every accounting record carries a reference to the raw record it came from. Which payment event produced this journal entry, which original entry does this reversal offset. With both threads intact, any number can be walked back to its source.

10. Reconciliation and close - a working checklist

10.1 What is reconciled against what

Reconciliation is comparing records held in two different places. Unless it is decided what is being compared with what, a reconciliation batch is useless no matter how well it is built.

  • Your payment records against the provider's settlement file. Do counts and amounts agree, and is anything present on only one side
  • The settlement file against the actual bank deposit. Differences between expected and received, and delayed deposits
  • Accounting records against payment records. Items that never made it into accounting, and entries with no source event
  • Refunds against their original transactions. Refunds larger than the original, and refunds with no original at all

Reconciliation is not about eliminating differences but about explaining them. The day the difference is zero never arrives. Settlement lag, authorizations not yet captured and disputes in flight are always in the mix. Good reconciliation classifies the difference by reason and records it, so that only unclassified differences reach a human.

10.2 Close, currency, rounding, time zones

  • Lock closed periods. Events arriving after the lock go to the next period, and that fact is recorded
  • Make the pre-close checks code. Show unreconciled counts, items that failed to reach accounting, and authorizations with no capture, on a screen
  • Handle amounts as integers and round in exactly one place. Multiple layers rounding independently make totals disagree, and discount or split allocations must be stored for a recomputation to return the original figures
  • With multiple currencies, store the timestamp of the rate alongside it. Which rate at which moment has to survive for anything to be reproducible
  • Store timestamps in universal time and shift to local time at aggregation. Filing period boundaries are defined in local time, but storing in local time collapses when regions with daylight saving are mixed in
  • Aggregation must be rerunnable. If rerunning the same period changes the value, it is not an aggregate

11. What I could not confirm

Everything left out for lack of a source, collected in one place.

  • The principal versus agent criteria in the accounting standards are not cited to paragraph level. The standard text itself could not be opened, so only the principle that gross and net diverge is stated
  • Settlement cycles and fee structures by payment provider vary by contract and have no published standard. No specific company's settlement structure or fee rate appears in this article
  • Who issues a tax invoice to whom in a payment-facilitated transaction depends on the contractual form, and the individual determination belongs to a tax professional and the competent tax office
  • The enforcement decree article number behind the mandatory electronic tax invoice threshold was not stated in the guidance consulted, so it is not given. Only the threshold amount and effective date are carried over as published
  • Penalty and surcharge rates are not covered. They do not connect directly to system design and are the kind of number that does harm when stated loosely

12. Questions that keep coming up

Does an early-stage service need accounting design at all

Not all of it, but the irreversible parts are worth holding from day one. Store raw events unprocessed, do not overwrite status, and persist allocation results. Journal rules can be added later; facts you did not record cannot be manufactured.

Missing a webhook throws the books off

The answer is not to trust webhooks alone. Use event delivery as the fast path and add a slow path that pulls the provider's list once a day and compares it against yours. Merging on the same unique key backfills anything lost and blocks duplicate delivery at the same time.

The dashboard and the ledger disagree on revenue

Usually the definitions differ rather than one being wrong. Dashboards commonly count authorizations while books count captures or recognised revenue. The first move is not to reconcile the numbers but to write down what each number counts.

13. Conclusion: three things to settle before the schema

An accounting integration is not a choice of library. It is settling what you sold, when it counts as sold, and what proves it. Once those three are fixed the schema is just a container for the answers; leave them open and any schema you write gets rewritten.

  • Store raw payment events unprocessed. They are the only basis from which a changed definition can be rebuilt
  • Only append to accounting records. Correct with reversing entries and keep a reference back to the source
  • Reconcile daily and classify differences by reason. The goal is not zero difference but zero unexplained difference

Accounting follows a service not because of regulation but because taking money creates an obligation to explain. What code can do reaches as far as not losing the facts that explanation needs. Past that point it belongs to the people who do this for a living.

Once more. This article is a general explanation for system design and is not tax advice. Filing procedures, tax computation and the treatment of individual transactions are outside its scope. Actual treatment must follow a qualified professional such as a licensed tax accountant and the competent tax authority, and the institutional details are specific to Korea as confirmed in August 2026.
