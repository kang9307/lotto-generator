Dutch Police Dismantle a 17-Million-Device Botnet - How Botnets Work and How to Stay Clean

Inside a 17-Million-Device Botnet Takedown and the Simple Habits That Prevent It

Jun 4, 2026 Tech

Introduction: What a 17-Million-Device Takedown Warns Us About

In early June 2026, Dutch authorities reported that they had dismantled a massive botnet built from 17 million devices and seized its command-and-control (C2) servers. As a number alone, it sounds like a distant event in another country. But 17 million is not an abstract statistic. Hidden inside it are someone's home router, an IP camera in the corner of an office, a forgotten NAS in a warehouse - and quite possibly the aging router sitting in your own home right now.

This article does not stop at "a big event happened." Instead it analyzes how the botnet threat actually works, what the number 17 million means in security terms, how international cooperation brings down this kind of criminal infrastructure, and most importantly, what ordinary users and system administrators must do to keep their devices from being hijacked into bots. The short version: a botnet this large is not the product of cutting-edge hacking. It is the sum of something far more mundane - unchanged default passwords and unapplied firmware updates.

1. What Is a Botnet - The Zombie Device Army

Let us lay the foundation first. Botnet combines "robot" and "network," and refers to a collection of devices infected with malware that follow an attacker's remote commands. Each infected device is commonly called a bot or a zombie. The zombie metaphor is apt because the real owner of an infected device almost never realizes their machine is acting on someone else's orders.

A botnet has three parts:

  • Botmaster: The attacker who controls the entire botnet - the person or group issuing the commands.
  • C2 server (command and control): The relay hub that passes the botmaster's commands to the infected devices. It is the "head" of the botnet.
  • Bots (infected devices): The hands and feet that execute the C2's commands. Almost any internet-connected device - PCs, servers, routers, IP cameras, NAS units - can become one.

What makes a botnet frightening is the economy of scale. The traffic from a single infected PC is trivial, but when millions hammer the same target at once, almost any defense collapses in seconds. A botnet is essentially distributed power, and when that power is turned to crime, it is amplified to a scale a single society can barely absorb.

2. The C2 Server - How a Botnet's Heart Beats

The core of any botnet is the C2 (command-and-control) server. Driven by its malware, an infected device periodically "checks in with C2 and waits for orders." This communication pattern determines a botnet's survivability.

2.1 Centralized C2

Early botnets had a simple structure in which every bot connected to a single server or IRC channel. Control was easy, but blocking that one server paralyzed the whole botnet. As in this Dutch case, a C2 seizure becomes a decisive blow precisely because it targets the weakness of this structure.

2.2 P2P and Distributed C2

To evade investigators, more advanced botnets use peer-to-peer structures where bots relay commands to one another, or a Domain Generation Algorithm (DGA) that changes domains every day. Cut off one head and another grows, hydra-style, so dismantling such botnets demands far more sophisticated work and international coordination.

The fight against botnets is part technology and part endurance. Even when one server is blocked, the threat does not vanish as long as the infected devices remain - it merely goes dormant.

3. Infection Paths - How an Ordinary Device Becomes a Zombie

"I never visit shady sites, so I'm fine" is a dangerous illusion. Botnet infection paths are surprisingly ordinary.

  • Unchanged default passwords: The most common path. IoT devices left with factory accounts like admin/admin or root/12345 are found and captured by automated scanners within minutes.
  • Unpatched known vulnerabilities: When firmware flaws in routers, NAS units, and cameras are disclosed but updates are delayed, attackers walk in using public exploits.
  • Malicious attachments and installers: Phishing attachments, pirated-software cracks, and fake update files hide malware.
  • Exposed remote-access ports: Telnet (port 23), administrative SSH, and UPnP left open to the internet become targets of brute-force attacks.

Especially alarming is large-scale automated scanning. Attackers do not pick targets by hand; the botnet itself continuously scans the entire internet, absorbing vulnerable devices automatically. Already-infected zombies hunt the next zombies - a self-propagating structure.

4. IoT and Ordinary PCs - The Two Targets a Botnet Wants

Botnet prey splits into two groups, each valuable for different reasons.

Target Why it's attractive Typical use
IoT devices Always on, weakly secured, unmanaged DDoS attacks, proxy networks, traffic relays
PCs and servers Powerful compute, holds personal data and credentials Credential theft, cryptomining, ransomware footholds

It is clear why IoT devices are especially vulnerable. Routers, cameras, and set-top boxes run 24/7, users forget they even exist once installed, and manufacturer firmware support ends quickly. An unmanaged computer permanently connected to the internet is exactly what a botnet loves. Ever since the 2016 Mirai botnet generated a massive DDoS using only IP cameras and routers, IoT has been the botnet's primary base.

5. The Weight of the Number 17 Million

Now back to the key question. What does 17 million devices mean in security terms?

First, firepower. If 17 million devices pour traffic at one target simultaneously, you get terabit-scale DDoS capable of paralyzing even national infrastructure. Second, an ocean of anonymity. Using infected devices as proxies, an attacker can hide criminal traffic behind 17 million ordinary home and business IPs, disguising it as legitimate. Third, a data goldmine. The credentials and financial data scraped from infected PCs alone sustain a sprawling underground market.

Above all, the figure lays bare the scale of the IoT security gap. That this many devices were left to be infected means tens of millions of homes and businesses worldwide are exposing devices whose default passwords were never even changed. A botnet is not a cutting-edge threat - it is a mirror of accumulated security neglect.

It is also worth remembering that 17 million is usually a conservative floor, not a ceiling. Investigators can only count devices that were still checking in with the C2 server at the moment of seizure; machines that were powered off, sitting on a different network segment, or already overwritten by a second strain of malware never make the tally. The real infected base is almost certainly larger. This is also why the same vulnerability tends to be exploited repeatedly by different criminal groups - a weak device is rarely infected just once; it is occupied in turn by rival operators like a piece of no-man's-land. Grasping this is what makes the difference between thinking a takedown solves the problem and understanding that real cleanup takes months or years of cross-border cooperation plus device-by-device remediation on the user's side.

6. What Botnets Are Used For - Multipurpose Crime Infrastructure

The danger sharpens when you see what a dismantled botnet is actually used for.

  • DDoS attacks: Flooding a target server with massive traffic to paralyze it. "DDoS for hire" is sold by the hour in underground markets.
  • Spam and phishing: Using infected devices as mail-sending hubs to evade tracing.
  • Credential stuffing: Automatically trying leaked ID/password combinations across countless sites to hijack accounts.
  • Residential proxies: The backend of "legitimate-looking" residential-IP proxy services is, not infrequently, a botnet.
  • Cryptomining: Stealing the compute of infected devices to mine coins in secret (cryptojacking).

In short, a botnet is not a single-purpose weapon but rentable criminal infrastructure. Rather than committing crimes directly, the botmaster profits by leasing this vast zombie army to other criminals by the hour. 7. International Cooperation - How to Bring Down a Giant Botnet

Dismantling a 17-million-device botnet is impossible for one country's police acting alone, because the infected devices and C2 servers are scattered across many jurisdictions. A takedown of this kind typically requires several elements to interlock.

  1. Long-term tracking and intelligence: Security firms and researchers trace the botnet's communication patterns and C2 infrastructure for months or years to draw a map.
  2. International judicial cooperation: Europol, Interpol, and national cybercrime units execute warrants simultaneously, seizing servers in different countries at the same hour.
  3. Seizure and sinkholing: Investigators take over the C2 domains (sinkholing) so infected devices can no longer receive attacker commands.
  4. Victim notification: Using seized C2 connection logs to build a list of infected devices, national CERTs guide affected users through malware removal.

It is no accident that the Netherlands often sits at the center of such operations. Amsterdam hosts a world-class internet exchange (AMS-IX) and is a data hub, which is exactly why cybercrime infrastructure clusters there too. Seizing the C2 server is a decisive strike at the botnet's head - but as the next section shows, it is not the end.

8. After the Takedown - Infected Devices Still Remain

Here we must correct the most important misconception. Seizing the C2 server does not make the botnet vanish entirely. Only the head is cut off; the 17 million infected bodies still sit on the internet.

This residual threat continues in three forms. First, re-absorption: another criminal group can target the same vulnerability and round up the abandoned zombies again. Second, backup channels: sophisticated botnets attempt to resurrect via backup domains when the primary C2 dies. Third, lingering malware: if a user never removes the malware, that device remains a potential asset for the next attack forever.

So true resolution comes only when the authorities' seizure meets individual malware removal and patching. At exactly this point, responsibility leaves the hands of the police and passes to each of us.

9. Seven Rules for Ordinary Users to Block Infection

So what should an ordinary user do? You do not need grand security expertise. These seven basic hygiene rules block the vast majority of botnet infections.

  1. Change default passwords immediately. The moment you install a router, camera, or NAS, replace the admin password with a strong one. This single act is half of botnet defense.
  2. Keep firmware up to date. Turn on automatic updates for routers and IoT devices, and decisively replace old devices that are no longer supported.
  3. Close unused ports and features. Disable Telnet, UPnP, and remote (WAN) management if you do not need them. Admin ports exposed to the internet are the botnet's front door.
  4. Do not delay OS and app security patches. Security updates on PCs and phones are the cheapest insurance you can buy.
  5. Never run files from unknown sources. Cracks, fake updates, and suspicious attachments are the usual malware delivery routes.
  6. Keep antivirus and a firewall on. Even a basic defensive line blocks a large share of known malware.
  7. Watch for anomalies. Sudden slowdowns, unknown upload traffic, and increased heat or fan noise can be infection signals.

10. Advanced Defense for System Administrators

If you are responsible for many devices, you need to go a step further. Structural defense beyond home rules is required.

  • IoT network isolation: Confine IoT devices like IP cameras, sensors, and set-top boxes to a separate VLAN or guest network so an infection cannot spread into the internal network.
  • Outbound traffic monitoring: Botnets reveal themselves through internal devices "reaching out" to external C2. Detecting abnormal outbound connections is essential.
  • Least privilege and segmentation: Carve the network into small segments so one device's infection cannot spread to everything. For practical firewall-rule examples, see 50 practical iptables rules.
  • Credential hygiene: Give every device a unique, strong password and apply key-based authentication and multi-factor authentication (MFA) where possible.
  • Supply-chain checks: Backdoors hidden in firmware and libraries also become botnet entry points. Obtain firmware only from trusted sources.

The administrator's core mindset is not "make infections zero" but "make sure infections cannot spread." On the premise that perfect prevention is impossible, the realistic goal is to narrow the blast radius through isolation and detection.

11. The Local Picture - Is Your Home Router Safe?

Mapping this onto a high-connectivity country makes several implications clear.

  • The double edge of high internet penetration: World-class broadband and IoT adoption also mean an abundant pool of potential botnet resources.
  • The neglected-router problem: Many households use a carrier-default or cheap router bought long ago, never changing the password, for years. These are prime zombie candidates.
  • Blind spots in small businesses: CCTV, NAS, and POS systems at small shops without dedicated security staff are especially vulnerable.
  • The role of national CERTs: Agencies that notify owners of infected devices and guide malware removal exist in most countries. If you receive such a notice, do not ignore it - act immediately.

The most realistic first step for any household is to log into your router's admin page today, change the password, and update the firmware. Those five minutes do far more for botnet defense than any elaborate security product.

Another widely overlooked blind spot is second-hand and resold hardware. Plenty of people pass on or shelve an old router or camera without ever performing a factory reset, let alone scrubbing any malware that may already be hiding inside. The moment such a device reconnects to a network, it can drag the infection straight into a new environment. So when you retire an old device, make "factory reset, update to the latest firmware, confirm remote access is off" a fixed routine. For organizations, the deeper fix is to put IoT devices into a formal asset inventory - you cannot protect a device you do not even know exists, and what botnets love most are exactly these shadow devices: unregistered, never inspected, yet always online.

12. The Fight Against Botnets - An Unending War

This Dutch achievement is clearly a meaningful victory. But the war against botnets does not end with one takedown. There is a structural reason new botnets keep growing even after a head is cut: vulnerable IoT devices ship daily, unpatched devices keep piling up, and criminal groups rebuild seized infrastructure elsewhere.

Still, there is hope. International cooperation is getting faster, regulations forcing secure defaults on manufacturers (such as bans on default passwords) are spreading, and above all, user awareness is rising bit by bit. A botnet is a problem of habit before it is a problem of technology - and habits can be changed.

13. Conclusion + 3 Things You Can Do Today

The lesson of the 17-million-device takedown is paradoxically simple. This enormous criminal infrastructure was not the work of genius hackers; it was the sum of small acts of negligence by tens of millions of people who "never changed the default password" and "never ran the update." The power to stop botnets comes from the same place: not grand security spending, but the ordinary hygiene of everyone tending their own devices a little better.

Let us close with three things you can do today.

  1. Change your router password today. The five minutes it takes to log into the admin page and replace the default with a strong password is the starting point of botnet defense.
  2. Update the firmware on every IoT device. Turn on automatic updates for cameras, NAS, and set-top boxes, and consider replacing anything no longer supported.
  3. Turn off remote access you do not use. Simply closing unnecessary exposed paths like Telnet, UPnP, and WAN remote management dramatically shrinks your attack surface.
