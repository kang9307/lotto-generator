Calluses vs. Corns: Complete Foot Care Guide — Causes, Differences, Self-Care & When to See a Doctor

What separates a callus from a corn, safe home care, risky self-treatment mistakes to avoid, and why diabetic patients need a podiatrist

2026-07-10 Health & Wellness

Introduction: Is That Thick Patch of Skin a Callus or a Corn?

Have you ever taken off your shoes and noticed a hard, thickened patch of skin on your toe or the sole of your foot? In most cases, this is a callus or a corn — changes in the outer skin layer that your body builds up to protect itself from repeated friction and pressure. Both are common and usually not serious, but they form in different locations, cause different pain patterns, and call for slightly different care.

This guide draws on patient information from the Mayo Clinic and Cleveland Clinic to summarize the differences between calluses and corns, what causes them, safe self-care, risky self-treatment mistakes to avoid, and when professional treatment is needed, as of July 2026. If you have diabetes or a circulation disorder, self-treatment can lead to a foot ulcer, so extra caution is essential. This article does not constitute medical diagnosis or treatment advice. If pain is severe or infection is suspected, see a healthcare professional.

1. Calluses and Corns — Definitions and Key Differences

1.1 What Is a Callus

A callus is a broad, flat area of skin where keratin builds up in layers to protect the area from prolonged friction or pressure. Calluses commonly form on weight-bearing parts of the foot — the heel, the area under the big toe, and the ball of the foot — where pressure concentrates, and on the hands in areas repeatedly exposed to tools or musical instruments. Calluses have poorly defined edges, spread out over a wider area, and are usually not painful.

1.2 What Is a Corn — A Small Lesion With a Hard Core

A corn is smaller and deeper than a callus, and is defined by a hard central core surrounded by inflamed, swollen skin. Corns tend to form on bony areas like the top of or between the toes, wherever friction concentrates against the inside of a shoe. A corn that forms in a moist area, such as between the toes, is called a soft corn, while one on a drier area like the top or side of a toe is a hard corn. The central core can press on a nerve, often causing sharp pain when walking or when pressure is applied.

1.3 Telling Them Apart by Location, Pain, and Shape

Three clues distinguish the two: location, pain, and shape. A callus is flat and spread out over a large weight-bearing area with little pain. A corn is small and round, forms in a narrow area like a toe, and causes distinct pain when pressed — and on close inspection, you may be able to feel or see a hard core at its center. If you can't tell them apart, or if there is pain, it's safer to have a dermatologist, orthopedist, or podiatrist confirm it rather than relying on self-diagnosis.

2. What Causes Them — Repeated Friction and Pressure

2.1 Ill-Fitting Shoes and Socks

The most common cause of calluses and corns is footwear that doesn't fit properly. Shoes with a narrow toe box or high heels squeeze the toes together, while loose shoes let the foot slide and rub repeatedly inside the shoe. Thick or poorly fitted socks can also concentrate pressure on specific spots.

2.2 Foot Deformities and Gait Abnormalities

Structural foot changes such as a bunion (deformed big toe), hammertoe (a bent toe), or bone spurs create conditions in which certain spots press more forcefully against the shoe, making corns and calluses more likely. Abnormal walking mechanics or an uneven distribution of weight, as seen with flat feet or high arches, also raises the risk.

2.3 Repetitive Activity and Occupational Factors

Jobs that involve prolonged standing or walking, as well as repetitive high-impact activities like running, accumulate friction and pressure at specific spots over time. Walking barefoot or wearing shoes without socks also increases friction.

3. Safe Self-Care — What You Can Do

3.1 Soaking in Warm Water and Softening the Skin

The first step is soaking the affected foot in lukewarm, soapy water for around 10 minutes to soften the thickened skin. Once the skin has softened sufficiently, it can be thinned naturally without applying excessive force.

3.2 Using a Foot File or Pumice Stone

After soaking, gently rub the thickened surface with a pumice stone, foot file, or emery board to thin it out. Rub gently in one direction, and stop immediately if the skin turns red or feels sore. Don't try to remove it completely — smoothing the surface is the safe goal.

3.3 Moisturizing and Padding

Afterward, apply a plain moisturizer free of urea or salicylic acid to keep the skin soft. In areas with heavy friction, applying a nonmedicated callus or corn pad can help distribute pressure and may reduce pain and size over time.

3.4 Choosing Proper Footwear

Switching to well-cushioned shoes with enough room in the toe box and forefoot is the single most effective way to prevent recurrence. Choose socks that are evenly cushioned and fit well too.

4. What Not to Do — Dangerous Self-Treatment

4.1 Cutting It Off With a Blade or Knife

Cutting a callus or corn directly with a razor blade, knife, or scissors is very dangerous. It's hard to see exactly where the thickened tissue ends and healthy skin begins, and this can lead to deep cuts, bleeding, or infection. Even in medical settings, this kind of procedure is only performed with sterilized instruments and by trained hands.

4.2 Misusing Medicated Corn Pads (Salicylic Acid)

Over-the-counter corn pads and liquid corn removers sold at pharmacies mostly rely on salicylic acid to chemically dissolve the thickened skin. The problem is that this acid cannot tell the difference between the lesion and healthy skin. If a pad isn't cut to the exact size needed, or if liquid runs onto surrounding skin, it can cause a chemical burn or an ulcer. People with diabetes, circulation disorders, or nerve damage should never use these products and must consult a clinician before considering any use.

5. When Professional Treatment Is Needed

5.1 Signs of Pain, Inflammation, or Infection

Stop self-care and see a doctor if you notice any of the following: pain severe enough to make walking difficult, redness and swelling around the lesion, warmth to the touch, or pus or discharge. These signs can indicate a bacterial infection that may require antibiotics or in-office treatment.

5.2 Repeated Recurrence Without a Clear Cause

If a callus or corn keeps coming back in the same spot despite consistent self-care, or keeps growing larger, it's time to identify the underlying cause — a foot deformity, gait abnormality, or ill-fitting shoes. Without addressing the root cause, the lesion will keep returning no matter how carefully you manage it at home.

5.3 What Treatment Looks Like at the Doctor's Office

A clinician can precisely pare down thickened skin with a scalpel (professional debridement) or remove a corn's central core if needed. If an underlying deformity such as a bunion or hammertoe is the cause, custom orthotics or corrective devices may be considered, and in rare, severe cases, surgery to reposition the underlying bone may be discussed — though this remains a last resort.

6. Diabetes and Circulation Disorders — Always See a Specialist

6.1 Why It's Risky — The Threat of Foot Ulcers

Diabetes can damage peripheral nerves and blood vessels. When nerves are damaged, a wound may go unnoticed because pain isn't felt, delaying treatment; when circulation is poor, wounds heal slowly. An ulcer can develop underneath a callus or corn and remain hidden beneath the thickened skin, so by the time it's discovered, it may have already progressed to a serious infection or tissue damage.

6.2 For Diabetic Patients — Self-Treatment Is Strictly Off-Limits

If you have diabetes or peripheral vascular disease, avoid filing down skin yourself with a pumice stone or foot file, using salicylic acid corn pads or liquid removers, and cutting lesions off with a blade or scissors. Instead, check your feet daily for wounds, discoloration, or temperature changes, and if a callus or corn develops, get regular foot care from a podiatrist or an endocrinology or orthopedic specialist. Getting professional care early is the most reliable way to prevent foot ulcers and the serious complications that can follow.

7. Prevention — Footwear, Socks, and Foot Care Habits

Choose shoes with a roomy toe box and good cushioning and support, and break in new shoes gradually rather than wearing them for long stretches right away. Wear well-fitting socks that absorb moisture and reduce friction. Wash and dry your feet daily and apply moisturizer to prevent cracking or excessive dryness. Regularly checking between your toes and areas of concentrated pressure helps you catch changes early, reducing both recurrence and the risk of the lesion worsening.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Q. What's the most reliable way to tell a callus from a corn?

Location and pain are the most practical clues. Calluses form as flat, spread-out patches on weight-bearing areas like the sole and heel and are rarely painful. Corns are small and round, tend to appear on narrow areas like the top of or between the toes, and are usually distinctly painful when pressed because of a hard core at the center. If you're unsure, or if there's pain, it's safer to have a dermatologist or podiatrist confirm it rather than self-diagnose.

Q. Is it okay to use medicated corn pads with salicylic acid?

Healthy adults can use them in limited amounts following the product instructions, but the pad must be cut to the exact size of the lesion so it never touches healthy skin, and repeated long-term use should be avoided. If you have diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, or nerve damage, these products carry a real risk of chemical burns and ulcers, so consult a clinician before using them at all.

Q. Can I cut a callus or corn off myself with a blade or scissors?

This is not recommended. It is difficult to see exactly where the thickened tissue ends and healthy skin begins, which creates a real risk of deep cuts, bleeding, and infection. Limit home care to softening the skin in water and gently smoothing it with a pumice stone or foot file, and leave full removal to a medical professional.

Q. I have diabetes and developed a callus on my foot. What should I do?

Do not try to cut it or use chemical removers yourself. See a podiatrist or your treating clinician as soon as possible. Because diabetes can damage nerves and blood vessels, even a small wound can progress to an ulcer or a serious infection, so regular professional foot care is essential.

Q. What treatment can I expect from a doctor or podiatrist?

A clinician can precisely pare down thickened skin with sterile instruments or remove a corn's central core. If an underlying foot deformity is the cause, custom orthotics or corrective devices may be prescribed, and in severe, recurring cases, surgical correction may be discussed. The exact treatment plan is decided after an in-person evaluation.

Summary as of July 2026

Calluses and corns are the skin's natural response to repeated friction and pressure, and most cases improve once you change footwear and manage the thickened skin properly. However, self-treatment such as cutting the lesion off with a blade or misusing salicylic acid products can invite wounds and infection instead, and should be avoided. If you have diabetes or a circulation disorder, get regular foot care from a specialist, and if you notice pain, inflammation, or signs of infection, see a doctor without delay.
