Costco Is the Anti-Amazon - Why the Opposite Strategy Wins

If Amazon is infinite choice, Costco is deliberate reduction - an analysis of how the opposite strategy built a retail powerhouse

Jul 5, 2026 Trends & Culture

Introduction: The Company Winning by Doing the Opposite of Amazon

As of July 2026, Amazon remains the reference point for e-commerce worldwide. Its weapons are a near-infinite catalog, personalized recommendations, next-day or same-day delivery, and the vast data pipeline that makes all of that possible. Yet in the same retail arena, one company has built an even more loyal following by moving in exactly the opposite direction: Costco. A Costco warehouse carries roughly 4,000 items, a number so small it's barely comparable to Amazon's catalog. Its advertising budget is among the lowest in the industry, and its revenue still centers on physical warehouse stores rather than online sales. This piece reads Costco not as an old-fashioned warehouse club, but as a rare case that chose to stand directly against the spirit of the Amazon era - and is winning anyway.

1. Two Companies Standing at Opposite Poles

Start with the numbers. Amazon, including third-party marketplace listings, is widely described as offering hundreds of millions of products. Costco, by contrast, stocks only about 4,000 active SKUs per warehouse - a fraction of the 30,000 to 50,000 SKUs found in a typical supermarket. This gap is not an accident. It is a design choice.

1.1 Amazon's Playbook: Infinite Choice and Data

Amazon's core grammar is more, more personalized, and faster. A single search returns dozens or hundreds of near-identical options, and its algorithms use purchase history to pre-select what you're likely to buy next. Its logistics network exists to get that chosen item to your door in the shortest possible time. It is a model that sells expanding choice and the data-driven means to narrow that choice, at the same time.

1.2 Costco's Playbook: Deliberately Narrowing Choice

Costco does the exact opposite. In most categories, it stocks only one or two brands, sold in bulk at razor-thin margins. Faster turnover concentrates buying power, which in turn drives prices down further. The table below makes clear just how differently the two companies are solving the retail equation.

2. The Paradox of Choice: Why Less Sells More

2.1 The Fatigue That Grows With More Options

Psychologist Barry Schwartz's paradox of choice observes that satisfaction can actually decline as the number of options increases. Facing 20 near-identical options in one category, shoppers deliberate longer and are more likely to regret their pick afterward, wondering if another option would have been better. Online shopping's infinite scroll only amplifies this fatigue.

2.2 Costco Sells the Trust That the Choice Was Already Made

A single detergent option on a Costco shelf isn't a shortage - it's a service. It means the company has already weighed price, quality, and size, and picked the best one. Shoppers are freed from the cognitive labor of comparing near-identical brands, and instead buy the trust that "if it's at Costco, it's already been vetted." Costco isn't just selling products; it is selling a service that pays down the decision cost on the customer's behalf.

3. The Economics of Membership: Selling Trust, Not Margin

3.1 The Membership Engine, By the Numbers

Over the first 24 weeks of fiscal 2026, Costco's membership fee revenue reached roughly $2.68 billion, exceeding the roughly $2.4 billion in operating income the company generated from merchandise sales on $134.2 billion in net sales over the same period. In other words, pure membership fee income outweighed the profit made from selling goods. Reports suggest membership fees have accounted for somewhere in the 60-70% range of total operating income in recent fiscal years, while merchandise margins are pinned at an industry-low roughly 11%. Worldwide renewal rates hover around 90% (about 92% in the U.S. and Canada), and Executive members are reported to drive a large majority - reportedly in the high-70% range - of total sales.

3.2 The Psychology of an Already-Paid Fee

Once a customer pays the annual fee upfront, a "get your money's worth" mentality kicks in, pushing up both visit frequency and basket size. If Amazon Prime sells the convenience of a subscription, Costco sells the loyalty of "I trust this company and shop only here" as the subscription itself. For the company, this shifts the competitive game from a margin war to a trust-and-relationship war.

4. The Physical Warehouse and the Treasure Hunt: An Analog Weapon in a Digital Age

4.1 The Efficiency Built From Constraint

A Costco store is, quite literally, a warehouse with almost no interior decoration. Goods move straight from the truck to a forklift to the pallet on the sales floor, minimizing the number of times human hands touch each item along the way. This apparent "limitation" is in fact a design that directly cuts labor costs and speeds up turnover.

4.2 The Treasure Hunt: Turning Unpredictability Into a Product

At the same time, Costco rotates limited-quantity seasonal items, so every visit carries a "what will I find this time" anticipation. The industry calls this the treasure hunt experience. Where Amazon's algorithm tries to predict what you'll buy next, Costco turns unpredictability itself into entertainment - a kind of value that's hard for an online store to fully replicate, precisely because it emerges from the physical constraints of a warehouse.

5. Why Minimal Marketing Works: Trust Instead of Ads

Costco is known as one of the lowest advertising spenders in retail, channeling that budget instead into lower prices and the quality of its private label, Kirkland Signature. The logic is simple: because it's a membership model, there's less need to keep telling customers who already paid to "come shop here" - word of mouth and repeat visits do that job instead. Where Amazon fights an intense advertising war for the top search result, Costco cuts marketing costs altogether through a structure where, once someone becomes a member, they rarely leave.

6. Lessons for Business and Product Design: The Power of Curation

6.1 Applying It to Startups and Products

A common trap in product design is assuming that adding more features makes a better product. Costco's model proves the opposite: deciding what to cut is harder than deciding what to add, and a small, well-curated set of options reduces a customer's decision cost enough to actually raise conversion. Selling the trust of "we already chose for you" instead of piling on options applies just as well to SaaS pricing tiers and content curation services as it does to retail.

6.2 A Note From the Korean Market

In Korea, Costco entered as an unfamiliar membership warehouse format and spawned local competitors like Traders. Korean shoppers, already accustomed to online dawn delivery, have turned Costco's "inconveniences" - weekend crowds, bulk packaging, a near-absence of a smooth online experience - into a premium experience of their own. The takeaway for domestic commerce operators is that in a market already saturated with convenience competition, deliberately designed constraint and trust-based curation can become the actual point of differentiation.

7. Limits and Counterarguments: Costco Is Changing Too

This piece shouldn't be read as a conclusion that "Costco is always right." Costco itself is expanding online through Costco.com and its own delivery and logistics operations, and it continues to test membership fee increases to adjust its revenue mix. Conversely, Amazon keeps absorbing curation elements of its own, through Amazon's Choice, private labels, and subscribe-and-save. In other words, it's more accurate to say the two companies started at opposite extremes and are gradually borrowing each other's strengths. The binary claim that "fewer choices always win" or "infinite choice always wins" is an oversimplification; the right answer differs by category, customer segment, and purchase frequency.

Conclusion: Neither Amazon Nor Costco Is the One Right Answer

What Costco proves isn't that "analog is better," but that the trade-off between choice and trust can be deliberately engineered. Infinite choice is powerful but breeds fatigue; extreme curation limits freedom but builds trust. As of July 2026, both models are still working well in their respective markets, and the real winner isn't a model - it's whoever reads most accurately what their own customers are tired of. If you're designing a business or a product, the practical lesson here is to decide whether to widen like Amazon or narrow like Costco based on your own customers' fatigue points, not on whichever approach happens to be trendy.
