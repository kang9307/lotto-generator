What Gets Copied and What Stays - Measuring a Vertical AI Defense Line in Years

Regulatory approval, switching costs, process integration, liability contracts and the budget line - how many years is each one worth

2026-09-07 Finance & Economy

You ship a product that handles one narrow job properly, customers start signing, and not long afterwards the renewal notice for the large piece of software wrapped around that job carries the same capability as a single new bullet point. Features get copied. And because copying costs far less than it used to, they get copied faster.

So the question worth asking is not is our feature better but what is left once the features are the same. And then: how many years is each of those things worth. There is no shortage of lists of defense lines. A list on its own does not settle anything. Regulatory approval, switching costs and contract clauses all get filed under the single word moat, yet their lifespans differ and so do the triggers that end them.

The piece that put this question back in front of me is a16z, The Incumbents Are Coming, published on 3 September 2026. The author works at that venture firm. Explaining where vertical AI startups can win overlaps with growing the market the firm invests in. So everything from that piece is carried over below as the piece argues that, never as a settled fact.

What follows is general information and does not recommend investing in any particular company or financial product. The company names that appear below are the examples the source article used, carried over as they stand, and are not a judgment about who wins or loses. Actual decisions about contracts, regulation and procurement are matters to settle with a lawyer, tax adviser or comparable professional who knows the specifics of your situation.

The trigger - reading a venture firm piece as a venture firm piece

The argument runs like this. Incumbent software vendors are moving from products that store information toward products that do more of the work themselves, the piece argues. The examples it reaches for are Iris from Docusign, Rovo from Atlassian and Composer from Klaviyo. It also notes that incumbents have an incentive to control access to their own data and to charge for it.

So where does that leave a vertical startup. The place the piece points to is doing the customer job end to end across several systems and several teams. Winning goes to whoever does the job best rather than to whoever owns the record, which is roughly where the piece lands. The reason it gives is the learning loop: memory in a general purpose agent can recall a customer preference, but it does not tell the agent whether the work was any good.

One thing carries over from that piece here: the opposition between owning the record and doing the work. Two things do not. First, any verdict on individual companies. Second, the assumption that this opposition applies with equal force across every industry. Section eight is about exactly that second point.

The caveat the piece attaches to itself deserves the same weight. Incumbents will also learn from the work performed inside their products, it states plainly, except that the learning covers only their part of the job. So the claim is not that incumbents cannot learn but that the coverage differs. That distinction matters. The first version is a prediction; the second is a conditional description. Carrying a source sentence over at a higher intensity manufactures a claim the source never made.

The half-life of a feature lead, and why that line is short

A lead built out of features gets worn down by three forces.

  • Model generations turning over - the gap you were filling with prompt design, post-processing and validation rules shrinks by exactly as much as the model starts doing on its own. A correction layer built with great care on the product side becomes unnecessary one day, and this repeats
  • The falling cost of copying - the cost of building a capability first and the cost of building it second were never the same, because the second builder already knows what to build. As the tools improve, that spread widens
  • The asymmetry in the cost of selling - the largest of the three in practice, and worth a paragraph of its own below

When an incumbent attaches the same capability, it is not selling a new product. It is adding one line item to a renewal negotiation on a contract that already exists. The arithmetic runs the same way on the buying side. Instead of vetting a new vendor, pushing them through security review and creating a new approval chain, it is cheaper to let one more line appear on the invoice of a vendor who already cleared all of that. This asymmetry operates independently of how good the two products are, because the comparison never reaches the point where quality is examined.

So a feature is not a defense line; it is a ticket at the door. Without one you do not get in. Holding one guarantees you no seat. Half-life here is a metaphor rather than a physical quantity. No public measurement exists of how long a feature lead takes to halve. What does follow from the structure is that the interval is bound to whichever is shorter, the model generation cycle or the procurement cycle. Neither of those is controlled by the people building the product.

What verticalization is, and why a wrapper ends up walking that road, was covered in The AI Wrapper Is Dead, and why accumulated data on its own is not a sufficient condition was covered in Data Is Your Only Moat. This piece does not re-explain either axis. It looks only at where you read the expiry date of the defense lines that remain.

The original sentence behind the word moat

As the word moat travelled into general business usage it mostly came to mean a technology gap. The sentence in the original source does not read that way. Here is the passage from the Berkshire Hathaway 1993 chairman letter.

The might of their brand names, the attributes of their products, and the strength of their distribution systems give them an enormous competitive advantage, setting up a protective moat around their economic castles.

Three sources are named. The might of the brand names, the attributes of the products, and the strength of the distribution systems. One of the three sat outside the product from the very beginning, and technology is not among them at all. That is why the selling-cost asymmetry in section two is not a new observation. Distribution was one third of the moat from the start.

The same image appears once more in the 1995 letter, in a sentence about looking for economic castles protected by unbreachable moats, followed by the remark that the GEICO moat widened that year. That the verb widened works at all means a moat is not a state but something maintained and extended. And the evidence offered for the widening was not a new feature but cost structure: underwriting expenses had fallen as a share of premiums against the prior year.

The academic tidying points the same way. The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy, which Michael Porter published in Harvard Business Review in January 2008, organises the sources of entry barriers into seven.

  • Supply-side economies of scale
  • Demand-side benefits of scale, commonly called network effects
  • Customer switching costs
  • Capital requirements
  • Incumbency advantages independent of size
  • Unequal access to distribution channels
  • Restrictive government policy

Of those seven, the only one a new entrant can effectively manufacture is customer switching costs. Demand-side benefits of scale appear only where the market already has that property, and the remaining five are either handed out by the market or already held by the incumbent. While the vertical AI conversation concentrates on data and learning loops, items five through seven sit untouched.

Five things that do not get copied, and how long each lasts

Stopping at a list of what does not get copied leaves you with nothing usable. Each entry needs what breaks it and how long the breaking takes attached before it becomes material for a decision.

What sets the lifespan sits outside the product

Read the last column downward and one thing surfaces. Every trigger sits outside the product. Rule amendments, standard formats, reorganisations, a single incident, a change of budget owner. Not one of them is controlled by a product roadmap. Which is why measuring the lifespan of a defense line is not a product meeting but an exercise in reading contracts, regulations and org charts. The rest of this piece is spent locating those reading positions one at a time.

A regulatory line comes with a published expiry - three Korean gates

Regulatory approval looks like the sturdiest of the five. Without the certification a competitor cannot enter no matter how well built their product is. It is also the most dangerous. When the scheme changes it disappears in one piece, and the schedule for that change is usually published in advance. Three positions in Korean rules show how that structure surfaces.

The gate you clear before you can sell to the public sector

Supplying a cloud service to national and public institutions in Korea requires a cloud service security certification. The legal basis is Article 23-2 of the Act on the Development of Cloud Computing and Protection of Its Users, and the criteria used in the assessment are set by Article 15 of the public notice on cloud computing service security certification. The Korea Internet and Security Agency is the certifying body.

The character of this gate is that it sits ahead of any product competition. What a procuring institution may choose from is the set of providers currently holding the certification. However good a vertical product is, absent from that list it is not even a comparison candidate. Read as a defense line, what matters is not that you passed but that you are currently holding it, and the lifespan of that line equals the period the scheme remains in its present form.

How SaaS came to be usable inside a financial internal network

The internal business network of a Korean financial company sat under the network separation requirement set by Article 21 of the Electronic Financial Transactions Act and Article 15(1)3 of the Regulation on Supervision of Electronic Financial Transactions. In plain terms, using external cloud-based application software inside that network was blocked in principle. For a vertical product aimed at the financial sector, that requirement was simultaneously a defense line and a wall.

The Financial Services Commission announced a network separation improvement roadmap for the financial sector on 13 August 2024. Stage one was a regulatory exception for generative AI use plus a widening of the SaaS scope, stage two was expanding the use of personal credit information after safety verification, and stage three was a separate financial security statute establishing autonomous security with accountability for outcomes.

Then, from 20 April 2026, an amendment to the enforcement rules of the Regulation on Supervision of Electronic Financial Transactions took effect, opening an exception for using SaaS inside the internal business network on the condition that a set of security disciplines is observed. Advance notice ran from 20 January 2026 to 9 February 2026, and the Regulatory Reform Committee review was on 7 April. The conditions attached to the exception are these.

  • The SaaS must have been assessed by an incident response body such as the Financial Security Institute
  • Protective measures must be established for the connecting terminals
  • An assessment must be run once each half-year and reported to the information protection committee
  • The exception does not apply where personal credit information is processed

Two things are worth reading here. First, roughly one year and eight months passed between the roadmap announcement and the effective date. For a provider whose defense line was that regulation, that interval was the remaining life, and the schedule was public from the outset. The expiry date of the line was printed in the official gazette. Second, easing a rule does not remove the gate. A new list appeared, the SaaS that has been assessed, and the personal credit information boundary stayed where it was. The gate did not vanish; it moved. Whoever stands at the new position first is advantaged again.

The line drawn between prime contractor and subcontractor

In Korean public software projects the contract structure itself decides where the defense line sits. Article 51 of the Software Promotion Act restricts subcontracting. Where a software business concludes a software project contract with the head of a national institution, it may not subcontract more than fifty hundredths of the project value excluding the cost of purchased goods. A business that received the subcontract is barred from subcontracting again. The head of the institution carries a duty to supervise compliance and to demand correction from a business in breach.

What that clause means for a vertical provider is clear enough. Because the prime cannot hand the whole project onward, the vertical product typically enters as a partial supplier. In that position the counterparty is the prime rather than the procuring institution, and the approval chain that decides renewal also sits on the prime side. You do not stay because the product is good; you stay by being written into the next proposal the prime submits. The question section seven raises, whose budget the money comes out of, bends once more in the Korean public market into whose proposal you appear in.

The four layers of switching cost - what you can build and what you should not

Of Porter's seven items, the one a new entrant can manufacture directly was customer switching costs. The point of that tidying is that switching costs are partly handed out by market structure and partly created by firms. The trouble is that getting the method wrong turns the thing into a liability rather than a defense line.

Lock-in and switching costs are not the same thing

The moment switching cost is understood as stopping people taking their data out, the direction is wrong. That is lock-in, and the pushback arrives from contractual export clauses and from procurement requirements. Public and financial procurement documents routinely carry clauses on data return at termination and on the format it must take, and one such clause turns a lock-in strategy into a deduction at the review stage.

The switching cost you can build is not what stops people leaving but what they lose by changing. The difference shows up when a customer walks. The first builds a structure in which you fight a departing customer; the second makes the departing customer run the numbers themselves. In the second structure, a customer who decides to stay does so without the relationship being damaged.

  • Data migration - formats, history, labels. The thinnest of the four layers and the first to thin further. Once standard formats and migration tooling exist it can disappear within months
  • Retraining people - proportional to how many staff have grown used to the screens and the vocabulary. It vanishes all at once during a reorganisation or a change of owner
  • Rebuilding integrations - thickens with the number of points where neighbouring systems attach. It thins again once those points are tidied into standard interfaces
  • Re-earning audits and approvals - where an audit, a certification or an internal review has to be obtained again. The thickest layer and the longest lasting

The fourth layer lasts longest for a single reason. It is the only one where time does not convert into money. A certification review and an internal approval process do not accelerate because you spend more on them. It works by delaying a competitor rather than blocking one, and the length of that delay is the length of the defense line. Which is why the question to ask when designing switching costs is not how to make leaving our product hard, but what the customer would have to obtain again in order to replace us.

Which budget the money comes out of decides renewal

Sell the same product at the same price and the probability of renewal still varies with which budget line the money leaves from. This is a purchasing question rather than a product question, and a different axis from the one the source piece draws between owning the record and doing the work. That axis is drawn from the selling side; this one from the buying side.

  • Cost-cutting budget - the first thing cut when conditions worsen. Worse, a product sitting in this line is impaled on its own logic, because removing a tool bought to reduce costs also counts as reducing costs
  • Compliance budget - not spending it carries a penalty, so it holds regardless of the business cycle. In exchange, when the underlying rule changes the whole line disappears. Section five is that shape
  • Revenue-linked budget - grows fastest while the results are visible and shrinks fastest the moment they are not. The largest swing of the three

This axis is a defense line because a budget line is harder to move than a product. Budget planning hardens on an annual cycle, and moving an item between lines takes its own approval process. A decision to replace a product can end inside the owning team; a decision to move a budget item travels upward.

The answers sit with purchasing and finance rather than with the account manager. Three things to look at: the approval chain on the contract, the renewal cycle and any auto-renewal clause, and which department code the invoice is issued against. The broader set of founder capabilities is covered in The Four Core Competencies of a Founder, and the move in pricing from seats to outcomes in The New SaaS Playbook for the AI Agent Era.

The same strategy produces opposite results in different markets

The five items above behave entirely differently depending on the properties of the market. The same strategy, doing the job end to end, is the only road out in some markets and a trap in others. Six axes produce that divergence.

In a market where incumbents are strong, doing the work well does not get you access at all. The record sits in one place, contracts are long, and there is a gate. In exchange, once you are inside, those same three become the defense line. Entry takes a long time and so does exit. What this market rewards is not speed but patience and capital, and whether the funding structure can carry that patience has to be settled first. How the way a fund earns changes the terms a founder is offered was covered clause by clause in How a Fund Makes Money Decides What a Founder Is Offered.

In a weak market entry is easy. For the same reason so is exit. Contracts are short and no gate exists, so competitors arrive at the same speed you did. Counting contract length or regulation as a defense line here means putting something into the calculation that does not exist. What remains is the accumulated outcome, and for that accumulation to become a defense line the customer has to be able to measure the difference. A difference that cannot be measured does not exist inside a purchase decision.

Korean procurement adds one more axis

Tendering commercial software separately, then evaluating and contracting for it on its own, puts a vertical provider in a completely different seat from tendering it bundled into a systems integration project. Under the first method the product itself is what gets assessed; under the second, being selected by the prime comes first. The subcontracting cap from section five overlays this. Because the prime cannot hand the entire project onward, the space a vertical product can occupy is structurally limited to a part, and the size of that part is set by the contract rather than by the product. Why domain expertise outlasts coding as an asset was handled separately in The Real Moat Is Domain Expertise, Not Coding.

The four conditions in the source piece, and how far to trust them

The piece organises the places a vertical AI company can win into four questions, as it presents them.

  • Can an expert quickly say what the AI got right and what it got wrong, and explain how to improve it
  • Is the work hard enough that judgment matters, rather than being handled by simple rules
  • Does the work happen often enough for the product to learn from it
  • Can it start with one assignment and grow into doing an entire job

The example given is Harvey. The piece states that Harvey built roughly 1,750 legal task environments, each carrying on average about 50 specific evaluation criteria. It also states that the post-training of an open-weight model used synthetic data, public legal data and expert-created data rather than customer data.

The scope needs to be drawn clearly here. These are one company's own figures, carried over by the piece, with no third-party verification attached. And the fact that there are 1,750 environments is a different proposition from the claim that they block competitors. The first is an input quantity and the second is an outcome, and no measurement joining the two appears in the piece. Carrying a figure over from the source article and quietly recasting it as evidence of defensibility is the most common distortion in this subject.

Condition in the pieceWhat the piece offers as groundsThe reservation to attachWhen the condition turns into a defense line
An expert can judge right from wrong quicklyThe distinction between memory and learning. Memory recalls preferences but does not say whether the work was goodNo figure measuring the speed of that judgment is offeredWhen the route by which that judgment feeds back into the product is fixed in a contract or a process
Work hard enough to require judgmentThe contrast with simple rulesWhat counts as hard varies by industry and the piece supplies no scaleWhen a clause accepting liability for that judgment enters the contract
Frequent enough to learn fromThe remark that long running agents especially benefit from learning loopsNo threshold frequency is givenWhen the customer measures the accumulated difference themselves and uses it as a renewal argument
Expansion from one assignment to a whole jobThe seat that performs work spanning several systems and organisations end to endNo data showing the rate at which such expansion succeedsWhen the expanded scope takes a place inside the approval chain of the process

The reservation the piece places on itself deserves the same weight when carried across. Incumbents will also learn from the work performed inside their products, and that learning simply covers only their part of the job. Rewriting that sentence as incumbents lose on learning manufactures a prediction that is not in the source.

A defense line lifespan checklist

Six questions. Filling in the answers reveals roughly how long the defense line runs. The purpose is not to manufacture a number but to fix which document you open to find the answer. The third and fourth columns are the scale for reading which way each answer points.

  • Total 0 to 3 - the defense line is effectively features alone, and the half-life in section two applies directly. In this band, working on contract terms and budget placement returns more than the next quarter of feature planning
  • Total 4 to 7 - contracts are standing in for the defense line. The renewal date is the exam date, so knowing how your name reaches the renewal approval chain carries the same weight as engineering work
  • Total 8 to 12 - rules and processes are the defense line. It looks stable, but as in section five the schedule on the regulatory side may already be public. Watching amendment notices on the underlying rule is the homework for this band

The total is an ordering, not a number of years. If Q1 scores two while the amendment schedule for that scheme is already public, those two points are valid only up to the published effective date. The financial example in section five had exactly that shape. Scoring high and lasting long are not the same statement, and what joins them is always a date written down somewhere outside.

Where this frame could be wrong

  • Assigning lifespans in years may itself be overconfidence - rule changes get pulled forward and they get postponed indefinitely. Even in the Korean case in section five, one year and eight months separated the roadmap from the effective date, and that gap was not a figure announced accurately in advance
  • Features get copied can also be overstated - what gets copied is the feature list, not the outcome. Domains where two products with identical lists produce different results plainly exist. The condition attached is that the customer must be able to measure the difference for it to become a defense line
  • The source piece has interests running one way - an argument about where vertical startups can win comes from a party that gains when that market grows. A piece on the same subject from a party with the opposite interest deserves reading against the same standard for the balance to hold
  • The Korean examples lean toward the public and financial sectors - where regulatory gates are weak or absent, the logic of section five barely operates. In such markets it is closer to the truth to treat only sections six and seven as remaining

The five-item split used here is not an absolute list either. Brand and reputation are missing from it, because the method for measuring their lifespan differs enough from the other five that they did not fit the same table. Brand resembles the liability contract in that a single event is the trigger, but the time it takes to recover is long in a way that has nothing to do with the renewal cycle.

Stating it once more. What is set out here is general information about market structure and Korean rules, and it does not recommend investing in any company's shares or in any particular fund. The checklist is material for a business judgment and guarantees no result, and how contracts and regulations apply to an individual business varies case by case and is a matter to settle through review by a lawyer, tax adviser or comparable professional.

What could not be confirmed

  • No official statistic exists showing the survival rate of Korean vertical software providers competing against incumbents. So the Korean material here stops at the existence and the timetable of the rules and makes no outcome comparison
  • The validity period and post-assessment cycle figures for the cloud service security certification are not on the certifying body's public guidance page. They require the text of the public notice, and since the argument here does not rest on those numbers they were left blank
  • The number of financial companies and cases designated as innovative financial services for SaaS circulates as a quoted figure but is not carried in the body of the relevant press release. So it was not brought into the text
  • The Harvey figures are the company's own numbers as carried by the source piece, with no third-party verification attached. No measurement showing those numbers translating into actual defensibility appears in the piece either
  • No public measurement in years exists for the half-life of a feature lead. The lifespan column in the section four table is not a grounded estimate but a statement of what sets that lifespan. It should not be read as a number
  • No aggregate data yet shows how much internal-network SaaS adoption actually rose after the April 2026 amendment. Too little time has passed since the effective date
  • The article number governing direct purchase of commercial software could not be pinned down. The existence of the scheme is established, but without a primary source fixing the article number the text describes only the method and omits the number
