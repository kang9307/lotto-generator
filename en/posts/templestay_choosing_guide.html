How to Start a Templestay in Korea and Choose the Right Temple

Start by deciding whether you are going to rest or to learn, then work through programme types, fees, booking and refunds, temple selection, packing and the daily timetable — as of August 2026

2026-08-06 Life & Practical

Decide one thing before you press book

Search for templestay and the results arrive as a list of temple names. This one has the best mountain view, that one sits beside a famous waterfall, someone else swears by a coastal hermitage. Yet when people come back disappointed, the cause is almost never the temple. It is the programme type. They booked hoping to sleep in and read a book, and found a timetable that started before dawn. Or they booked hoping to be taught something, and were shown to a quiet room and left alone. Neither picked the wrong temple. Both picked the wrong type.

So it helps to invert the usual order. First, decide whether you are going to rest or to learn. Second, choose the programme type that matches that answer. Third, filter the temples that run that type by travel time and practical conditions. The temple name comes last, not first. This guide follows that sequence.

Templestay is coordinated nationally by the Korea Buddhist Cultural Service under the Jogye Order, while each participating temple designs and runs its own programme. It started in 2002, when the football World Cup left Korea short of accommodation for overseas visitors and temples opened their guest quarters for a trial month. That first run involved 33 temples and around two and a half thousand participants. It is now a programme of national scale: official figures record roughly 180,000 participants across 170 operating temples in the first half of 2026 alone, of whom about 20,000 came from abroad. Cumulatively, more than four million people have taken part since 2002.

Everything below reflects the situation as of August 2026. Fees, timetables and cancellation terms are set by individual temples and change without central announcement, so this guide stops at the level of judgement criteria. The confirmed numbers live on the booking page of the temple you are applying to, and nowhere else.

Which of the three programme types fits you

The official classification has three branches. The names sound interchangeable, but what separates them is the scope of compulsory participation. That single difference decides whether the stay lands the way you hoped.

Following a fixed timetable together: the experience type

Built around one night and two days, this type carries you through a schedule from arrival to departure. An etiquette briefing, the formal monastic meal, 108 prostrations, tea and conversation with a monk, and a walk through forest trails or cultural heritage sites follow one another at set times. A coastal temple may add a tidal flat excursion; a mountain temple may add a forest programme. Content shifts with the season and with who is attending, and special editions appear around the Lunar New Year, the autumn harvest holiday, the turn of the year and sunrise gatherings. The defining feature is simple: you are expected to join everything on the schedule.

Using the time as you see fit: the rest type

Here the point is to stay at the temple and let your body and mind settle. After check-in you follow your own plan, and apart from the orientation walk and meal times almost everything is unstructured. Some temples keep meals and the chanting service as the only fixed points; others advertise their rest programme as fully self-directed. Length is flexible too, running from a single night up to three nights and four days at temples that offer it, which makes this the easier type to stretch or shorten around a travel itinerary.

Finished within an afternoon: the day type

Designed for people who cannot give up a whole day, and explicitly for overseas visitors who want a taste of Korean Buddhist culture in a short window. It typically includes a guided walk of the grounds, a seated meditation session, a tea ceremony and a craft activity such as making a lotus lantern, a prayer bead string or copying a sutra by hand. Sessions usually begin at a fixed time, often 10 in the morning or 1 in the afternoon, and run for two hours or more. If an overnight stay in a monastery feels like too big a first step, this is the low-commitment way to find out how it feels.

What an overnight stay actually looks like hour by hour

The skeleton of an experience type stay is broadly similar across temples. Knowing it in advance removes most of the surprise, and for a visitor unfamiliar with monastic routine it also makes the schedule announcements easier to follow even when they arrive in Korean.

  • Check-in, around 2 to 3 in the afternoon: you register, receive your room and collect the loose training clothes worn for the duration.
  • Orientation and etiquette briefing: a walk through the grounds plus the basics, including how to press your palms together in greeting and how to enter a dharma hall. Because this session exists, arriving with no prior knowledge is completely normal.
  • The four instruments: the drum, the great bell, the wooden fish and the cloud plate are sounded as part of an evening ritual. Some temples let guests watch, some let them take part, and some skip it entirely.
  • Evening meal: in a temple a meal is called an offering. The food is vegetarian by default, which is worth knowing before you arrive rather than after.
  • Evening chanting service: you join the ritual held in the main hall.
  • 108 prostrations or meditation: depending on the temple it is one, the other, or both. Prostrations are physically demanding, so flag any knee or back problem beforehand.
  • Lights out: early. Around 9 in the evening at many temples, which is a genuine adjustment if you have just flown in across several time zones.
  • Dawn chanting service: the next morning starts early, generally somewhere between 4:30 and 5:40, with the exact hour varying by temple and season.
  • Morning meal: this is where the formal monastic meal, eaten from a set of nested bowls, usually takes place.
  • Communal work: sweeping the courtyard or preparing vegetables together. It is a short, symbolic part of the day rather than real labour.
  • Tea with a monk: a conversation over tea. Coming with a question or two changes the depth of it considerably.
  • Reflection and departure: most programmes close after the midday meal, wrapping up between noon and 1 in the afternoon.

A rest type stay is easiest to picture as the same skeleton with almost all the compulsory markers removed. You keep the meal times and spend the rest reading, walking the grounds or sleeping.

Do you need to be Buddhist

The short answer is no. Templestay is operated as a cultural experience open to anyone regardless of faith, and nothing in the process asks you to convert or to state a belief. Given that the programme was born out of a World Cup accommodation shortage, that openness is structural rather than a recent concession.

There is a distinction worth holding on to, though. Not requiring belief is not the same as having no rules. These are functioning monasteries where people live and practise, not visitor attractions. The following expectations come with the territory, and they are worth reading twice if this is your first time inside a Korean temple.

  • Photography and phone calls are discouraged inside dharma halls and practice areas. Where photography is permitted, signage or staff will say so.
  • Keep your voice down within the grounds. Loud conversation carries a long way in a quiet valley.
  • Many temples ask guests to avoid visible displays of affection, including holding hands or draping an arm over someone.
  • Alcohol and tobacco are restricted, and some temples also restrict bringing meat onto the grounds.
  • If you enter a chanting service, you are not obliged to bow, but standing quietly and attentively is the expected minimum.
  • Addressing a monk is simpler than visitors fear: the Korean honorific seunim works for any monk regardless of rank or gender.
  • Pressing your palms together at chest height is the standard greeting. It functions as a courtesy, not as a profession of faith, so returning it commits you to nothing.

The reverse also holds. If joining a ritual at all feels like more than you want to take on, there is no need to force it. Choose a rest type programme with a narrow compulsory scope, or a two to three hour day programme. Asking the temple before you book is not rude, and staff at temples that host overseas guests regularly are used to the question.

Six filters that narrow the temple list fast

With well over a hundred participating temples, browsing by name goes nowhere. These six filters cut the list down quickly, and the first one matters more than most travel guides admit.

1. Travel time, which in practice dominates everything

Mountain temples often sit a long way past the last public transport stop. Check-in is typically 2 to 3 in the afternoon, so work backwards from that to find when you actually need to leave. If a one night stay costs you six hours of return travel from Seoul, you are buying a short stay at a long price. Without a car, the decisive question is how the final leg from the nearest station or bus terminal is covered. Some temples note a shuttle or pickup arrangement in their listing. For visitors arriving from Incheon Airport, it is usually more realistic to spend a night in a city first and travel out the following morning than to attempt the journey on landing day.

2. Type and compulsory scope

Covered above. Open the timetable and look for the compulsory markers. Whether the dawn service is required or optional will shape your impression of the whole stay more than any other single line on that page.

3. How physically demanding it is

Prostrations, pilgrimage routes that include hill walking, and an early wake-up all cost energy. If your knees or back are unreliable, avoid programmes where 108 prostrations are compulsory, or tell the temple in advance. Travelling with children or older relatives makes gradient and walking distance within the grounds a real consideration, not a detail.

4. Room conditions

Accommodation quality drives satisfaction more than the programme content does. Check these before booking.

  • Whether the room is a single, a two or three person room, or a large shared hall
  • Whether the bathroom and washing facilities are inside the room or communal
  • Whether you sleep on bedding laid over a heated floor or on a bed frame
  • How heating and cooling are handled, since mountain temples do not feel like the city in either direction
  • Whether hot water is available at all hours or only during set windows
  • Whether there is wifi, and how reliable the mobile signal is in the valley

5. Language support

For an overseas visitor this filter is often decisive. A defined group of temples runs programmes aimed at foreign participants, and some venues hold regular sessions conducted in English, including weekly dharma talks that draw a mixed audience of visitors and local participants. Most temples, however, run entirely in Korean, which means an unaccompanied visitor will miss the etiquette briefing and the schedule announcements. The official booking site maintains a separate English portal, and starting there rather than on the Korean pages saves a great deal of time.

6. Season and weather

The same temple is a different programme in a different month. Summer plays to valleys and forest shade; winter offers snow-covered courtyards but complicates both heating and the drive in. Monsoon rains can restrict mountain access, and the autumn foliage weeks and the period around the Buddha's Birthday celebrations bring heavy booking competition. For a first visit, a season with moderate temperature swings is the safer choice, which in practice points to late spring or early autumn.

Where to book and what to check before you pay

The anchor point is the official integrated booking site operated by the Korea Buddhist Cultural Service. You filter by region, date and type, then apply through the individual temple's page inside that system. An English portal runs alongside the Korean one. Some temples maintain their own websites in parallel, but where details conflict, the official site is the one to trust.

Checking the following before payment prevents most of the problems visitors report.

  • Check-in and check-out times: arriving late means missing the etiquette briefing, and some listings state what happens if you do.
  • Which items are compulsory: the dawn service, the prostrations, the formal meal — find out which of them you cannot skip.
  • How rooms are allocated: a solo booking may be placed in a shared room with strangers unless a single is specified.
  • Age restrictions: some programmes bar unaccompanied minors or set a minimum age for children.
  • Dietary needs: meals are vegetarian by default, but allergies and specific restrictions need a direct enquiry rather than an assumption.
  • Parking and extra charges: some sites levy a separate cultural heritage admission fee or parking fee on top of the programme price.
  • Cancellation and refund terms: covered in the next section, and worth reading before you pay rather than after.
  • Training clothes and sizing: they are usually lent out, but the available size range can be limited, which affects taller visitors in particular.
  • A contact number: save the temple office line. Reception in mountain valleys is patchy and you may need to ask for directions on the final approach.

Popular dates disappear quickly: weekends, public holidays, the foliage season and the turn of the year above all. Weekdays, by contrast, have space and are sometimes priced lower, so if your travel dates are flexible, a Tuesday night is easier and cheaper than a Saturday. Overseas visitors should also confirm early whether the payment page accepts non-Korean cards and whether a Korean mobile number is needed to complete the booking, since some Korean reservation systems still assume a local number for verification.

Why the fee differs by temple, and the rough range

There is no national price table for templestay, because the entity designing and running each programme is the individual temple. Any source that states a single definitive price is quoting one temple's one programme.

Across published programmes, the numbers tend to move within these brackets.

  • One night, two days: most commonly somewhere between roughly 70,000 and 100,000 Korean won per person.
  • Occupancy tiers: within a single rest programme, the per-person price typically rises as you move from a three person room to a two person room to a single. A private room is the most expensive option.
  • Adult and student rates: some temples publish a lower student price.
  • Weekday and weekend: weekends and public holidays are commonly priced higher.
  • Day programmes: with no accommodation involved, these sit far below the overnight bracket, with group rates published around the 30,000 won level at some temples.
  • Extras: heritage admission, parking and craft material fees are sometimes charged separately.

Layered on top of standard pricing, subsidised campaigns aimed at a defined group appear in some years. In 2026 a programme for adults aged 19 to 34 offered one night and two days at 98 temples nationwide for 30,000 won, with 7,000 places filled on a first-come basis. Campaigns like this have short announcement windows and fill within minutes of opening, so the only workable approach is to watch the official site notices and the Korea Buddhist Cultural Service announcements if you fall inside the eligible group. There is no guarantee the same terms return the following year. Overseas visitors should note that eligibility conditions for such campaigns, including residency or age verification, are set per campaign and need checking against the specific announcement.

What you get back if you cancel

Refund policy is not standardised nationally either. Each temple sets its own. What is consistent is the shape: the closer you cancel to the arrival date, the less comes back.

The table below reproduces one temple's published policy. It does not transfer to other temples. Read it as an illustration of the structure, not as a rule you can rely on elsewhere.

The procedure adds its own friction, and more of it if you are abroad.

  • Cancelling immediately after booking usually works online, but after some time has passed you may have to telephone the temple office, which is a real obstacle without Korean.
  • Card payments take additional days to appear as a reversal, and the exact period depends on your card issuer, longer again for overseas cards.
  • Bank transfers may require you to submit account details before the refund is processed, and the transfer itself takes further time.
  • Whether you can change dates rather than cancel and rebook varies by temple, and changing is often the cheaper route.
  • Cancellations initiated by the temple due to typhoons, heavy snow or landslides are handled under separate terms announced at the time.

Some things are easier left in your suitcase at the hotel.

  • Revealing clothing: sleeveless tops, very short shorts and cropped tops are discouraged. Even in training clothes, what you wear arriving and leaving still counts.
  • Strong perfume and scented cosmetics: shared indoor space in close quarters makes these a burden on everyone else.
  • Heeled shoes: stone steps and packed earth paths are the norm.
  • Alcohol and tobacco: bringing them onto the grounds is generally restricted outright.
  • Your work laptop: if you pack it you will use it. If the point is to rest, leave it behind.
  • Large amounts of snack food: meal times are fixed, and some temples restrict bringing meat onto the site.

Five moments that catch first-timers off guard

Knowing these in advance halves the awkwardness, and for a visitor unfamiliar with East Asian monastic custom they are the points most likely to feel bewildering in the moment.

1. The formal meal, where you drink the water you rinsed your bowl with

In a temple, eating is called making an offering, and the formal monastic version follows a fixed sequence. There is a way to unfold the set of four nested bowls, a way to signal how much food you want, and a closing step where you clean the bowls with water and a piece of kimchi and then drink that water. Leaving nothing behind is the principle, which makes taking less than you think you want the practical strategy for a first attempt. An explanation always precedes the meal, so nobody is left to guess.

2. Noble silence means lowering your voice, not going mute

The term suggests total silence, but the usual instruction is closer to keeping your voice down and avoiding loud conversation except when circumstances require otherwise. Some programmes do impose full silence as a rule, and the timetable will say so. If you have travelled with a companion, the thing that surprises people is not the silence itself but how much of the trip you had planned to spend talking.

3. The dawn service, where the real problem is bedtime

The morning service generally runs between 4:30 and 5:40. Waking at that hour is hard less because the hour is early than because sleep the night before rarely goes well: lights out comes quickly, the bedding is unfamiliar and the room is shared. For a first visit, avoid stacking a demanding daytime itinerary in front of it. Earplugs and an eye mask are more useful than they sound, and if you have recently flown in, jet lag can cut in either direction.

4. The 108 prostrations, where the number matters less than your knees

The practice carries the meaning of acknowledging and releasing 108 afflictions, one at a time. Completing the count is not the point, so stopping partway is acceptable. It puts real load on knees and lower back, so anyone with a relevant condition should take it easy and say so beforehand. Muscle soreness the following day is entirely normal, including for people who exercise regularly.

5. The room is small and the floor is hard

This is practice accommodation, not a hotel. Bedding laid over a heated floor is common, and a shared hall with several other guests is a real possibility. Measuring it against hotel expectations guarantees disappointment. If those conditions worry you, the fix is available at booking: choose a programme with an en-suite bathroom or a single room option. The fee goes up and the experience changes substantially.

Going alone, with family, or without Korean

Travelling alone

  • The rest type is the safe default. Nothing binds your schedule, so solitude stays intact.
  • A single room costs more but removes the question of sharing with strangers. Decide which matters more, budget or privacy.
  • A solo application can still be merged into a shared room, so confirm the allocation policy before booking.
  • Tea sessions and group activities sometimes involve introducing yourself. If that is unwelcome, pick a programme with fewer compulsory items.

With family or children

  • Some programmes set a minimum participant age or restrict infants, so check before planning around it.
  • Family-oriented and children's editions appear at certain times of year. School holidays and long weekends widen the choice considerably.
  • For children, silence and an early lights-out are the real hurdles. Within the experience type, programmes weighted toward crafts and outdoor activity are gentler.
  • With older relatives, the practical variables are gradients and steps within the grounds, bathroom location, and getting up from a floor-level bed.
  • Check whether family rooms exist, or whether the temple separates accommodation by gender, which would split a couple or a parent and child.

If you do not speak Korean

  • A defined set of temples runs programmes aimed at overseas participants, and some venues hold regular sessions conducted in English.
  • The official booking site keeps a separate English portal, which is far faster than filtering the Korean pages for programmes that can host you.
  • Most temples operate entirely in Korean. Attending one without interpretation means missing the etiquette briefing and the schedule announcements.
  • Day programmes are short and activity led, so the language barrier weighs less. They make a sensible first experience.
  • Mountain temples are weakest on the final public transport leg. If Korean geography is unfamiliar, start with temples that are easy to reach from a city centre.
  • Say up front that meals are vegetarian and alcohol is restricted, so nobody in your group discovers it at check-in.

Frequently asked questions

Q. Do I have to be Buddhist to join?

No. Templestay runs as a cultural experience open to anyone regardless of religion, with no conversion step and no declaration of faith. What does apply is the setting: these are working monasteries, so rituals such as the chanting service and the formal meal sit on the timetable, and depending on the type some are compulsory. You need not believe in their meaning, but you are expected not to disrupt them. If joining a ritual feels like too much, a rest type programme or a two to three hour day programme is the comfortable choice for everyone.

Q. How much does it cost?

There is no single figure, because each temple sets its own. An overnight stay most commonly falls somewhere between roughly 70,000 and 100,000 Korean won per person, moving with room occupancy (three person, two person or single), adult versus student rates, and weekday versus weekend. Day programmes sit far below that. Subsidised campaigns aimed at a specific age group appear in some years and cut the price sharply. The only reliable number is the one on the booking page of the temple you are applying to.

Q. What should I pack?

Training clothes are usually lent, but the cotton t-shirts and socks that go underneath are yours to bring. A toothbrush and toothpaste are often not provided, so treat them as essential, and add a towel, sleepwear, a charger and a personal cup. Mountain temples run cooler than the city, so bring an extra layer whatever the season. Leave behind revealing clothing, strong perfume and heeled shoes; flat, easily removed shoes are far more practical.

Q. How do I book, and what if I cancel?

Use the official integrated booking site run by the Korea Buddhist Cultural Service, filtering by region, date and type; an English portal runs alongside the Korean one. Cancelling immediately usually works online, while later cancellations may require calling the temple office. Refunds follow each temple's own policy, with one published example running full refund four days out, 70 percent at two to three days, 50 percent the day before and nothing on the day. Card reversals take extra days, and bank transfers may require submitting account details first.

Q. Are programmes available in English?

Some are, most are not. A defined set of temples runs programmes for overseas guests and some hold regular English-language sessions, but the majority of participating temples operate entirely in Korean. Without interpretation you will miss the etiquette briefing, the schedule announcements and the tea conversation with a monk. Use the official English portal to filter, and consider a short day programme as a first step, since activity-led sessions carry less language load.

Closing

What ruins a templestay is rarely the temple. It is the gap between expectation and programme type. Decide whether you are going to rest or to learn, choose the type that matches, and open the timetable to see where the compulsory markers sit. Doing only that removes most of the mismatch. Choosing the temple name afterwards is still early enough.

For a first attempt, aim lower rather than higher. A two to three hour day programme tells you how the setting feels, and a weekday rest type night is an easy way in. Move up to the experience type after that, and the dawn service and the formal meal both land far more gently.
