Why Public Restrooms Disappeared - And Where the Cost Actually Went

The costs that get written into a budget, the benefits that never do, and the people who make up the difference

Aug 26, 2026 Trends/Culture

Introduction: The habit of checking for a restroom before leaving

Some people plan an outing by locating the restrooms first. They scan the area around the destination on a map app, pick a cafe to stop at halfway, and if that still feels thin, they drink less water before heading out. This is not fussiness. It is a function the city used to provide, now filled in by private planning.

Ask why that habit exists and the subject shifts from hygiene or manners to where money gets recorded and where it does not. A public restroom is built once and maintained forever. The maintenance shows up as a figure in the budget every year. The benefit of it existing shows up in no ledger at all. That single asymmetry is what erased these facilities from many cities, and this piece is about where the erased cost travelled to.

To be clear up front, the conclusion is not a slogan about building more toilets. It is that the cost did not disappear, it moved onto someone else's ledger, and that we can name whose.

1. What the source essay traced - Parisian urinals and American comfort stations

The piece that brought this back into circulation is a short history essay in JSTOR Daily. It moves in three steps.

First, nineteenth century Paris lived through cholera and came to read public urination as a public health emergency. After banning it in 1850, the city installed street urinals for men. These structures, wrapped in decorative ironwork, were part of the streetscape, but they were built for men only. The assumption of the era, that women did not linger in public space, was written straight into the equipment list.

Second, the facility was used in ways nobody designed for. Privacy was almost nonexistent, and partly because of that it became a site for anonymous encounters, which in turn drew police patrols and blackmail. The physical form of the infrastructure shaped how it was used, and that use pattern generated both the maintenance burden and the political one.

Third, in the United States the temperance movement of the early twentieth century adopted public restrooms as its own cause. Saloons were effectively serving as the city's toilets, so replacing the saloon meant replacing that function first. But the wave of large comfort stations did not continue, and while the existing stock aged, nothing new arrived. The result, the essay argues, is that people walking the city came to depend on semi private spaces such as hotels, shops and restaurants.

One caveat is worth flagging. This history is American and French, and as we will see, the situation in South Korea differs. What does not vary by region is the structure underneath: what happens when cost and benefit are recorded in different ledgers.

2. Why they shrank - cost gets a budget line, benefit gets none

Decisions to remove public restrooms rarely come from bad intent. Picture how each item looks in a budget meeting and the reason becomes obvious.

The spending side is sharp. Cleaning wages, water and electricity bills, consumables like paper and detergent, plumbing repairs, safety inspections and structural upkeep each exist as a line with a number attached. The officer responsible can quote those numbers precisely.

The benefit side has no such line. The fact that people stayed in a commercial district thirty minutes longer scatters into shop revenue. Street cleaning that never became necessary is not tallied, because it never happened. Someone with a bladder condition keeping a clinic appointment instead of postponing it is buried somewhere in health insurance data. Benefits spread thin, costs concentrate on one party — the classic shape of a public good.

CategoryWritten into the budget as a figureRecorded nowhere
Labour Wages and contracts for cleaning and patrol inspection Complaint handling time that never occurred because the place was clean
Operations Water and power, paper and detergent Savings in street cleaning and odour complaint response
Facilities Plumbing repair, damage restoration, safety fittings Dwell time gained because visitors stayed downtown longer
Safety Installing and testing emergency call buttons, inspection costs Mobility retained by people who did not have to cancel a trip
When it becomes visible Every year, at every budget review Only after it is gone, and only as private inconvenience

The last row decides everything. Costs are conspicuous on a repeating annual cycle, benefits become visible only once removed. And the inconvenience that follows removal appears in residents' days, not in any department's ledger. It counts toward nobody's performance record.

One more general property of budgeting piles on. When money is tight, the first thing reviewed is not statutory spending but discretionary spending. Restroom upkeep tends to be classified closer to the latter. Cutting it violates no rule immediately, and in the first year nothing appears to happen. The trouble comes next. Longer cleaning intervals reduce usage, lower usage makes neglect easier, and neglect accumulates into a case for closure. The reduction manufactures its own justification.

In one line: this facility has no presence when it is working well. Things with no presence always lose budget arguments.

3. What the maintenance burden actually is

None of that means the burden is exaggerated. It is genuinely heavy. But you have to see which part is heavy before you can find the lever.

  • Labour dominates. Construction is spent once, cleaning recurs daily. Over ten years, total upkeep typically exceeds construction cost, and most of it is human time.
  • Cleaning frequency is a function of usage. A facility that thousands pass through daily cannot be held together by one cleaning a day. The busier it is, the more it costs per hour, and busy also means needed. The places that need it most are the most expensive.
  • Safety has been added as a line item. Under Korean rules, the Act on Public Toilets requires emergency call buttons and other safety equipment to prevent crime and accidents, with the scope defined by local ordinance. Installation is not the end of it; testing follows permanently.
  • Damage repair resists forecasting. Recurring costs can be planned; breakage varies in both timing and scale. It draws on contingency funds, which makes it the least comfortable category for any manager.
  • Siting decides cost. Whether a cleaner can park nearby, whether water supply and drainage are close, how far supplies have to be carried — identical facilities diverge sharply in upkeep on these grounds. None of it is visible at the moment of construction.
  • Budget cycles and asset lifespans do not align. The facility lasts decades; the budget is drawn one year at a time. Saving this year enlarges next year's invoice, and the person receiving that invoice may not be the person deciding now.

There is one explanation that comes up constantly and needs addressing. The claim that a particular group of users makes maintenance impossible. It is convenient and it predicts nothing. Within the same city and the same population mix, some facilities stay intact for years while others break repeatedly. The variable is not who the users are but the condition the facility sits in: whether the shared area has open sightlines, whether lighting works, whether cleaning frequency tracks usage, whether a fault can be reported to somewhere with a defined response time.

The Paris case from the previous section is exactly this story. A structure with almost no privacy produced a particular pattern of use, that pattern drew enforcement, and enforcement shortened the political life of the facility. The cause sat in the design and the response was aimed at people. Treated as a people problem, the only option left is closure. Treated as a design problem, there are things to fix.

4. The share pushed onto private hands - when a purchase becomes admission

What actually filled the gap left by public facilities is the restroom in the cafe, the shop and the department store. It looks like a natural drift, but examine it and you find a public function transferred with no contract, no standard and no compensation.

Access conditions change first. A public facility is open to anyone in principle; a shop restroom generally assumes a purchase. Buying one drink sounds trivial, yet the condition filters people out reliably.

  • Money — if you have to buy something every time, several times a day adds up to a real figure.
  • Time — in an urgent moment, ordering and waiting becomes the gate. With a small child in tow, that gate is especially hard.
  • Information — which venues are open and which use keypad locks is known only to regulars. Visitors and travellers have to test it every time.
  • Hours — it disappears when trading stops. For anyone working at night, it may as well not exist.
  • Permanence — a closure removes it without notice. It does not leave the city plan, it just stops being there.
  • Atmosphere — even without an explicit rule, the character of a venue makes some people feel they cannot walk in. This exclusion is never recorded.

The cost moved too. Cleaning time, consumables, lock management and the awkward conversations all sit with the shop. Some of it returns as revenue, but only from paying customers; a passer by's use is pure cost. This is why the spread of customers only signs and keypad locks should not be read as a decline in generosity. When a burden lands entirely on one party, that party shuts the door. It is a rational response.

The phrase the JSTOR Daily essay closes on captures the situation precisely: bodily privacy became a purchasable commodity. The problem there is not the size of the price. It is that something previously granted without conditions acquired a condition at all.

5. Who pays the largest share

The cost of this shift is not evenly split. If you use a restroom once or twice a day and spend most of your hours at an office or at home, essentially nothing happens to you. The burden concentrates on people with particular circumstances.

  • Workers whose job is movement — delivery, transport, home visit services, outdoor trades. When most of the day is spent in motion, restroom access is part of working conditions. No fixed workplace means no fixed facility.
  • People with chronic conditions — digestive and urinary conditions, or dialysis schedules, where frequency and urgency are high. The distance to a restroom becomes the radius within which leaving home is possible.
  • Older adults — frequency rises while walking speed falls. Those two changes together demand a far denser network.
  • Pregnant people and carers with young children — if the changing table sits only in the women's room, a father cannot use equipment that exists. Presence of a fitting and access to it are separate questions.
  • Disabled people — an accessible cubicle that is locked or used as a storeroom is the same as no cubicle. For users who need an adult changing bench, the options narrow further.
  • People without stable housing — the circumstance with the fewest alternatives. This is frequently framed as a cause of the problem when in fact it is the position most affected by it.

How that burden turns into behaviour has been surveyed publicly. The 2019 report Taking the P from the Royal Society for Public Health reported that one in five adults in the UK do not go out as often as they would like because of a lack of toilets, rising to two in five among people whose conditions require frequent access. In the same report, more than half of respondents said they deliberately restrict how much they drink, and 74% said there are not enough public toilets.

That last behaviour matters most. Drinking less because there is nowhere to go looks like a personal workaround, but dehydration is a health risk in itself, more so in heat. Here you can see the path by which money saved on facilities relocates into health costs elsewhere. That transferred amount is recorded as a minus in no budget document anywhere.

6. Where South Korea stands - the public toilet act and designated open restrooms

Turn to South Korea and the picture changes considerably. Under Korean rules there is a standalone statute, the Act on Public Toilets. Its purpose clause covers installation and use, hygienic management, and the creation of a safe usage environment, in service of public convenience and welfare.

The interesting part is that it splits restrooms into five kinds: public toilets, open toilets, mobile toilets, simple toilets and paid toilets. The open toilet category speaks directly to this discussion. It covers restrooms in public institution buildings that are opened to the public, plus restrooms designated by a mayor, county head or district head. The law requires heads of public institutions to make their restrooms available absent security or structural grounds, and permits designation of restrooms in privately owned buildings above a defined size after agreement with the owner or manager. The designation procedure and operating method are set by local ordinance.

The scale looks like this, from the nationwide figures published by the Ministry of the Interior and Safety in its public toilet system guide, as of the end of December 2025.

What stands out is that open toilets exceed a third of the total. Alongside building new facilities, South Korea has legislated a route for folding existing restrooms into public use. That is the decisive difference from the unpaid delegation described earlier. There is a designation procedure, signage is required, and an ordinance sits underneath. The relationship exists on paper rather than by implication.

This does not mean the model is free of problems. Within what can be verified, the points worth flagging are these.

  • Designation carries a size threshold. Only buildings above a defined scale are eligible, so smaller premises fall outside it and opening remains the owner's choice.
  • The detail devolves to ordinance. Procedure and operation are set locally, so structural room for regional variation exists even under one national statute.
  • Count and access are different metrics. Night closure, uneven siting and missing signage do not appear in a facility count. A locked facility on a map is, at that moment, not a facility.
  • The installation standard includes an allocation rule. The number of stalls in the women's restroom must be at least the combined number of stalls and urinals in the men's. It is an attempt to address the gender gap in queueing through fixture counts.
  • Safety entered the statute itself. Emergency call button provisions and the safe usage environment language in the purpose clause both sit in the law. Upkeep grew heavier, but neglect became harder to justify.

In short, transplanting the decline curve from the source essay onto South Korea would be wrong. The country sits on a different institutional path. What does not change is that the asymmetry between cost and benefit pushes in the same direction everywhere. What institutions do is not remove that force but build something that holds against it.

7. Approaches elsewhere and what each one costs

Cities have taken roughly four paths on the same problem. Each buys something and pays for it.

ApproachHow it worksVerified cost
City builds and runs it free
(Paris)		 The City of Paris operates 435 automated street toilets. They were paid when introduced in 1981 and became free from February 2006. A full replacement programme ran across 2024 and 2025. The count is capped by contract and budget. 435 is not a number that blankets a city densely.
Charging a fee
(the former British station model)		 Usage fees cover upkeep. The body running Britain's major railway stations charged for access for many years. It abolished the charge itself. London Paddington removed fees on 9 January 2019, with a plan announced to extend that to every station by April. The judgement was that the people turned away mattered more than the sums collected.
Subsidised private opening
(German speaking countries, UK)		 The local authority supports participating venues financially and users enter free, with no purchase. The German language open restroom network states that more than 400 cities and municipalities take part, and it has extended into Austria and Switzerland. The British community toilet approach follows the same idea. It is tied to trading hours and disappears when a business closes. Districts with no participating venue simply stay blank, and the model is criticised for not substituting fully in either quantity or distribution.
Heavy investment in design and upkeep
(Shibuya, Tokyo)		 THE TOKYO TOILET redesigned 17 public restrooms in Shibuya, aiming to change the perception of such places as dark, dirty and frightening through design. Facilities are inspected regularly with specialist inspectors. Upkeep cost rises with design ambition. And 17 is a fraction even of Shibuya. This is less a solution to density than an experiment in perception and standards.

Policy choices split outcomes even within one country. RSPH research published in April 2026, drawing on freedom of information responses from 221 of England's 309 local authorities, put England at 15,481 people per public toilet. The same research puts Scotland at 8,500 and Wales at 6,748. That is close to a threefold gap on one island. It also reported a 14% fall in English facilities since 2016, against a 26% drop in local authority spending power compared with 2010.

What those figures say is simple. Decline is not a natural phenomenon but an accumulation of fiscal decisions, and where the decisions differed, so did the outcome.

8. What design changes - openness, lighting, service routes

Upkeep and safety look like operational matters, yet a large share of both is decided at the design stage. The room to change things after construction is narrower than people expect.

  • Open the shared area, close the cubicle. When basins and circulation are visible from outside, natural sightlines make it hard for a problem to persist unnoticed. At the same time the individual cubicle keeps full privacy. What the Paris case showed was what happens when those two axes are not separated.
  • Lighting is both surveillance and a maintenance signal. A dark space hides its own condition, and a hidden condition invites the next round of neglect. In bright space, cleaning quality is immediately legible, which puts pressure on the people responsible.
  • Service routes set the real cleaning interval. Carrying distance for supplies, position of water and drainage, and vehicle access all govern time per visit, and that time is labour cost. Build somewhere hard to service and you have created a closure candidate for a few years out.
  • Materials and joints cut cleaning time. Fewer seams and simpler surfaces mean less time per pass. The higher initial material cost is often recovered in a few years of wages.
  • Fully separate individual rooms reduce exclusion. Independent rooms make accompaniment easier and queue management more flexible. In exchange, area and fittings per unit go up. No option is free.
  • A reporting channel breaks the damage chain. A way to report faults, with a defined response time, shortens the neglect window. Given that unrepaired damage invites more damage, this is a cheaper intervention than adding cleaning staff.

The transparent cubicles most often cited from Shibuya are best read in this frame. Before entry the interior is visible, so you can see whether it is free and whether it is clean; once the door is locked the glass turns opaque. Two requirements that conflict, verifiability and privacy, are separated along time rather than space. Nobody has to copy the glass itself, but the lesson that a conflict between two requirements does not oblige you to abandon one of them travels well.

As an aside, what urban infrastructure produces outside its intended purpose came up from a different angle in a piece on rereading already installed equipment for other ends. The point that physical form governs use is the same point.

9. Five questions that tell you the real state

Judging a district's restroom situation by facility count alone gets it wrong often. What decides whether something is usable comes down to five things.

  • Is it open right now. A facility that closes at night does not exist for people who move at night. Counts do not distinguish day from night.
  • Is there a condition on entry. Fee, purchase or membership — any condition creates people who cannot meet it. The presence of a condition matters before its size.
  • Can you find it. Without signage or a map entry, a facility serves only those who already know. To a first time visitor it is absent.
  • Are the necessary fittings unlocked. Whether the accessible cubicle, the changing table and running water are actually usable is the point. Installed and usable are different questions.
  • Is there somewhere to report a problem. A reporting channel with a defined response time means the facility is being managed. Without one, whether it is managed is left to luck.

Passing all five is what makes it a restroom you can use within fifteen minutes from where you are. What a city owes is that state, not a list of facilities.

What could not be verified

Collected here are the points this piece could not ground.

  • The survey sample size and regional composition of the 2019 RSPH report could not be confirmed. The proportions quoted are the summary figures the organisation published; the underlying sampling method was not examined here.
  • How South Korea's public toilet count has moved year by year could not be confirmed. The figures here are a single snapshot published for the end of December 2025, and no claim about direction of change is made.
  • The split between public institution restrooms and designated private ones within the 21,639 open toilets was not found in public sources.
  • The revenue the British station operator collected during the paid era could not be verified from a primary source, so it is not included. What was confirmed is the abolition date and the stated reason.
  • The official payment rates councils pay venues under subsidised opening schemes could not be confirmed. Only the existence, mechanism and participation scale were verified; unit amounts were left out.

Conclusion: the cost that vanished did not vanish

Remove public restrooms and that budget line gets smaller. That much is true. But the money did not leave the world. It moved to another ledger.

The destination is nameable. The time spent checking a map before leaving, the decision to drink less and the health risk attached to it, the threshold of having to buy something to pass through, and the cleaning hours absorbed by small shops with no compensation at all. The largest share is paid by the people who decide not to go out. Because that item is tallied nowhere, the saving looks like an unqualified success from the side that made it.

So the question worth asking is not how many restrooms to build. It is whether someone walking the city right now can reach one within fifteen minutes, with no conditions attached. Answer that and the count is a number that follows. Fail to answer it and the count tells you nothing.

If nineteenth century Paris left one lesson, it is that infrastructure freezes the assumptions of the people who built it and carries them a long way forward. Just as the assumption that women did not linger in the street got written into an equipment list, today's decisions to build or erase record who is being assumed. That record lasts far longer than we tend to imagine.
