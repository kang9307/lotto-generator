Tech Interviews in the AI Era 2026 - What to Assess When Coding Tests Are Obsolete

When candidates use AI, what exactly are we measuring about a developer?

Jun 3, 2026 Tech

Introduction: When the Candidate Uses AI, Does the Coding Test Still Mean Anything?

There is an invisible extra guest in the 2026 interview room: AI. When a candidate hesitates on a video call, another monitor may already be displaying the answer, generated by Claude or ChatGPT. Applicants who score perfectly on online coding tests increasingly stall on trivial debugging once they are hired. A quiet dread has spread among hiring managers, and it reduces to a single question: has the thing we have been measuring all along actually been the candidate's ability?

This article is an opinionated analysis of what tech interviews should assess in an era when candidates freely use AI, written from the vantage point of the Korean tech hiring market but applicable anywhere. It is not a lament about how AI ruined everything. It is a concrete map of what has been neutralized, what we should measure instead, and how interviewers and candidates each need to adapt. The central message is simple: the obsolescence of the coding test is not a crisis but an opportunity, because it finally exposes that we were measuring the wrong thing all along.

In early June 2026, "Tech Interviews in the AI Era" rose to the top of GeekNews (news.hada.io), Korea's most-read developer community, with a strong score. That is not a coincidence. Now that AI coding tools are mainstream, hiring has moved past the first defensive phase ("how do we stop cheating?") into the harder second question: "what should we have been assessing in the first place?" I hope this piece is a practical answer.

1. The Collapse of the Traditional Tech Interview: The End of LeetCode and the Whiteboard

For the past fifteen years, the tech interview at big tech and the countless companies that imitated it converged on a single format: solving isolated algorithm problems under a time limit. LeetCode-style puzzles, whiteboard coding, and data-structure quizzes became the standard. The reasons it survived so long are clear: grading is objective, it scales easily, and it gives a minimal signal that the person knows algorithms.

But this format carried one enormous flaw from the start: the ability it measures differs from the ability the job requires. Working developers almost never need to invert a binary tree or recall a dynamic-programming recurrence on the spot. We measured it anyway, because it was easy to measure. This gap was criticized even before AI, but it survived behind the excuse that it was "still a proxy for filtering smart people."

AI demolished even that last excuse. LeetCode Hard problems are now solved by AI faster and more accurately than the average passing candidate. The proxy no longer proxies human ability. The fact that a candidate solved the problem in the interview carries no information, as long as you cannot tell whether the person or the AI solved it. The moment the last virtue, the objectivity of measurement, disappeared, the traditional coding interview lost its reason to exist.

There is a subtler casualty worth naming. For a decade, the standardized coding test also served as an unspoken social contract: candidates invested hundreds of hours grinding problems, and that investment itself was read as a signal of seriousness and conscientiousness. The grind functioned as a costly signal in the economic sense, hard to fake precisely because it was expensive. AI collapses the cost of that signal toward zero. When a candidate can prepare in a fraction of the time, or simply route around preparation entirely during the interview, the test no longer separates the diligent from the lucky. What remains is a ritual whose original information content has quietly drained away, even though its form looks unchanged. Recognizing that the form survived while the substance evaporated is the first honest step toward redesign.

2. Five Ways AI Has Neutralized the Coding Interview

Vaguely blaming "AI" misses the substance. There are five concrete paths by which AI neutralized the traditional coding interview.

  • Instant answers. AI produces the correct answer to isolated algorithm problems immediately, driving the problem's discriminating power toward zero.
  • Generated explanations. AI generates not just the answer but a plausible explanation of why, breaching the follow-up questions interviewers used as a backstop.
  • Stealthy real-time assistance. Secondary monitors off-screen and voice-driven live hints make detection hard.
  • Flattened preparation. The hundreds of hours once needed to learn problem patterns are compressed by AI, weakening the use of preparation effort as a signal of diligence.
  • The fall of take-home tests. For at-home coding assignments, verifying who actually solved them has become essentially impossible.

These five paths share a clear theme: they all target tasks where a single right answer exists and the goal is to produce it quickly. Conversely, assessments where the answer is not predetermined, where judgment matters more than production, or where contextual dialogue is required are not easily neutralized by AI. The direction of new interview design follows directly from this.

3. So What Should We Assess? Five New Competencies

In an era when AI handles code production, which human abilities remain, and have become more expensive? Across the hiring processes I have been involved in, five stand out.

3.1 Problem Definition

AI solves well-defined problems well. But it cannot define, amid ambiguity, what the real problem is. The ability to ask the right questions and structure a fuzzy problem is the most expensive skill of the AI era.

3.2 Code Verification and Debugging

AI code pours out fast but is frequently plausibly wrong. Reading a given piece of code and catching faulty assumptions, missing edge cases, and security holes now matters more than writing code from scratch.

3.3 System Design and Trade-off Judgment

Designing the whole structure, owning its consistency and scalability, and judging the trade-offs between options remain human work. AI is locally smart but globally irresponsible.

3.4 Domain Understanding

To judge what a correct answer even is, you must know the field, whether finance, healthcare, or commerce. Domain knowledge is the baseline that catches plausibly wrong AI code.

3.5 The Ability to Use AI

Paradoxically, the ability to use AI well has itself become a core competency. The meta-skill of supplying precise context, verifying output, and selecting the correct candidate from several directly determines real-world productivity.

4. Five Interview Formats for the AI Era

So how do we measure these competencies? Here are five formats I have used and recommend to other teams.

4.1 Practical Pairing

The interviewer and candidate tackle a realistic problem together. You assess the thinking process, the quality of questions, and collaboration, not whether the right answer was produced. Allowing AI lets you observe how the candidate uses it too.

4.2 System Design

Instead of asking the candidate to write memorized answers on a whiteboard, you work through vague requirements together and discuss the design. The ability to explain and defend trade-offs out loud is hard for AI to do on someone's behalf.

4.3 Code Review

Give the candidate code with deliberately planted defects (ideally AI-generated) and have them review it. What they catch, and in what priority order, reveals their real ability.

4.4 Domain Conversation

Talk in depth about a domain the candidate claims to have worked in. The details that only people who have actually wrestled with it know are hard to fake through memorization or AI.

4.5 Assessing AI Usage

Let them use AI, but watch whether they take output at face value or verify, critique, and improve it. Given the same AI, the quality of results varies enormously between people. That difference is skill.

5. The "AI-Allowed" Interview: A New Paradigm

The most contentious shift is allowing AI use in the interview outright. The intuitive objection is "then AI just solves everything," but if the real job involves AI, then an interview that bans AI measures a skill disconnected from the actual work. It is like banning navigation in a driving test, which no longer accurately measures driving ability.

The key to the AI-allowed interview is to raise the difficulty so that no single AI call finishes the job. You give design problems with more than one answer, situations where multiple constraints collide, and tasks that require finding and fixing errors in AI output. Then AI is merely a tool, and the judgment of the person directing it is laid bare.

The core shift: when you change the question from "do you know the answer?" to "can you direct the process of reaching the answer?", whether AI is allowed stops being the crux of discrimination. The person who cannot solve it even with AI and the person who solves it better with AI separate clearly.

6. The Seven Real Signals Interviewers Should Watch For

Here are seven signals I actually pay attention to in interviews, the ones AI finds hard to imitate.

  1. Quality of questions. Faced with an ambiguous problem, what do they ask first? A good question is the fastest signal of a good engineer.
  2. Skepticism toward assumptions. Do they accept given conditions as-is, or do they test the premises?
  3. Reaction when wrong. When stuck, do they panic, or calmly form hypotheses and narrow down?
  4. Rationale for decisions. Can they say "I did this because..." rather than just "I did this"?
  5. Trade-off awareness. Do they know every choice has a cost, or do they believe in silver bullets?
  6. Critique of AI output. Do they trust the AI's answer as given, or doubt and verify it?
  7. Clarity of communication. The ability to explain something complex simply, which is itself the depth of understanding.

All seven signals surface only in real-time dialogue within context. An isolated multiple-choice test cannot capture them. That is why the format of the interview itself must move from test to conversation.

7. Cheating vs. Legitimate AI Use: Where the Line Is

The thorniest issue is the line between cheating and legitimate use. That line depends on what rule was set in advance. Using AI secretly in an interview that banned it is cheating; using it in an interview that allowed it is skill. So a company's first responsibility is to clearly tell candidates the AI-use policy before the interview.

Once the policy is clear, the next step is to make the "quality of use" the essence. Even when AI is allowed, the candidate who blindly trusts and submits the output and the candidate who verifies and refines it with their own judgment diverge in their results. Making the thinking process visible is a stronger line of defense than cheating detection. Ask the candidate "why?" relentlessly, and a borrowed answer separates naturally from an owned one.

Caution: over-relying on cheating-detection technology (gaze tracking, screen surveillance) gives honest candidates an unpleasant surveillance experience and damages your reputation. Designing problems that cannot be solved by copying is more fundamental than tightening surveillance.

8. The Candidate's View: How to Prepare for AI-Era Interviews

As a candidate, you need to change strategy. The traditional approach of memorizing hundreds of algorithm problems has sharply diminishing returns. Spend your time on these instead.

  • Practice reading and verifying. Train yourself to read AI-generated code quickly and find what is wrong. This is the core of both real work and the interview now.
  • Practice explaining. Practice clearly articulating your design and decisions out loud. The depth of understanding shows in the explanation.
  • Internalize an AI workflow. Build your own workflow for giving AI precise context and verifying the result.
  • Build domain depth. Real experience wrestling with at least one field. Those details are the differentiator that AI and memorization cannot manufacture.

The key is not to try to "beat AI" but to become "the person who directs AI." The interviewer is not really looking for someone who writes code faster than AI, but for someone who commands AI most intelligently.

9. The Hiring Company's View: Redesigning the Interview Process

As a company, you need to redesign the entire interview process. Here are the steps I recommend.

  1. Shrink isolated algorithm tests. Boldly reduce or retire the stages whose discriminating power has vanished.
  2. Expand work-realistic assessment. Shift the center of gravity to pairing, system design, code review, and domain conversation.
  3. Codify the AI policy. Specify for each stage whether AI use is allowed or banned, and notify candidates in advance.
  4. Train interviewers. Build consensus among interviewers on what is a real signal versus noise. Without this, even new formats fall prey to subjectivity.
  5. Refine the rubric. Grade against concrete criteria like the seven signals to secure fairness and consistency.

The caution is not to change everything at once. Keeping the existing process while inserting one or two new stages and accumulating data, a gradual transition, is the safer path.

10. Applying It to the Korean Tech Hiring Market (Enterprise, Startup, SI)

In Korea, application varies by organization type.

10.1 Large Enterprises

Large enterprises, accustomed to mass open recruitment and standardized coding tests, change the slowest. Strong demands for objectivity and fairness make it hard to abandon the test format. But that very standard coding test was neutralized first. The realistic path is a hybrid: keep the test only as a first filter and move the real assessment to pairing and design discussion.

10.2 Startups

Startups are the most flexible. With small teams, high-density assessments like practical pairing and domain conversation are easy to apply. But with insufficient interviewer training, they risk drifting into "gut-feel hiring," which is exactly why explicit criteria like the seven signals matter most here.

10.3 SI (System Integration)

SI firms do a lot of high-volume, fast hiring and rely heavily on standardized tests. In the AI era this produces the biggest mismatch, because the gap between test scores and real ability translates directly into degraded project quality. The more SI-oriented the work, the more strengthening code review and verification assessment reduces maintenance risk.

11. The Risks: Over-Change and Under-Change

Two opposite errors are both dangerous when handling this transition.

Over-change: the hasty abolition of "coding tests are dead, scrap them all." Unvalidated new formats can be more vulnerable to interviewer subjectivity and bias. Throwing out the objective filter entirely shakes the consistency and fairness of hiring. New formats should run in parallel with the old and be validated with data.

Under-change: the stance of "the coding test is still the most objective," clinging to a neutralized method. This causes you to miss genuine talent who use AI well and to select people who are merely good at tests, an adverse selection. Continuing to trust the scale when the measuring tool is broken is dangerous.

The right stance is in between: reduce the neutralized parts, replace them gradually with validated new formats, and keep the fairness safeguards.

One practical guard against both errors is to treat your interview process as an instrument that itself needs calibration. Track whether the signals you collect in interviews actually predict on-the-job performance six months later. If they do not, no amount of objectivity in the measurement matters, because you are objectively measuring the wrong thing. Conversely, if a new conversational format predicts performance well but feels less "objective," resist the temptation to discard it for that reason alone. The goal of an interview was never objectivity for its own sake; it was a reliable forecast of how well someone will do the actual work. AI did not change that goal. It only invalidated one particular instrument that had been mistaken for the goal itself. The teams that internalize this distinction will navigate the transition calmly, while those that conflate the instrument with the goal will lurch between panic and denial.

12. Conclusion + Three Things to Apply Today

Tech interviews in the AI era have shifted their center of gravity from "do you know the answer?" to "can you direct the process of reaching it?" The obsolescence of the coding test is not a crisis but an opportunity that exposes how we measured what was easy to measure. What we must measure now is judgment, verification, design, communication, and the ability to direct AI.

Here are three things interviewers and candidates can each apply right now.

For interviewers:

  1. Replace one isolated algorithm problem with a "review this defective code" task.
  2. Ask "why did you do it that way?" one more time on every answer to make the thinking visible.
  3. Tell candidates the AI-use policy clearly before the interview.

For candidates:

  1. Shift time from memorizing algorithms to reading and fixing AI code.
  2. Practice explaining your design decisions out loud.
  3. Go deep in at least one domain to build details AI cannot manufacture.
