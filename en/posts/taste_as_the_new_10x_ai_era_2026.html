Taste Is the New 10x - The Real Differentiator When AI Handles Execution

Why Taste Becomes the Engineer's Moat When AI Commoditizes Execution

Jun 9, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: The New Scarcity When Execution Becomes Cheap

In 2026, the axis along which an engineer's value is measured is quietly shifting. Only a few years ago, "writing code fast and correctly" was the very definition of skill. Now, ask AI in plain language and a working function, a plausible design mockup, or a polished document draft pours out in under a minute. Execution has become a commodity. And when something becomes cheap, it is not that thing but what stood next to it that becomes the new scarcity.

I believe that new scarcity is taste. In Silicon Valley it goes by a single word, "taste," and it sits somewhere between discernment, sensibility, and preference. It is the ability to judge what to build and, just as importantly, what not to build. This article starts from a trending topic but, instead of recycling its summary, works through the questions in my own voice: what taste is, why it matters more now, whether it is learned, how to cultivate it, and how far we should trust it. To put the conclusion first, taste is the capability AI will replace last, and also the word most easily inflated.

1. What Taste Is - And How It Differs From Preference

We need a definition first. Taste is often conflated with preference, but the taste this article means is a much firmer concept. Preference stops at the subjective "I like this," whereas taste is the discernment to judge that "this is better," with reasons.

In an engineering context, taste shows up concretely in judgments like these:

  • What to build: the discernment to recognize the three features that are truly needed among the hundred you could build.
  • What to cut: subtraction, which is harder than addition. The sense to spot excess and remove it decisively.
  • Which abstraction survives: the eye to distinguish a design that is convenient now from one that will hold up a year later.
  • Which detail builds trust: the difference in polish that users cannot articulate but clearly feel.

One discussion in the design field defines taste as the sensibility to understand how structure, hierarchy, tone, and visual language create clarity and trust. What is striking is that an output generated by AI can look polished on the surface while quietly missing emotional nuance, cultural context, brand personality, or strategic clarity. The ability to notice exactly that "missing thing" is taste.

2. Why Taste Matters More in the AI Era - The Commoditization of Execution

If taste was always important, why has it become the topic now? The core is the commoditization of execution.

2.1 The Technical Edge Is Flattening

The technical edge that once separated teams is flattening fast. Models converge in capability, APIs are available to anyone, and some tools produce a working app in under a minute. To borrow one analysis, production itself is becoming a commodity. When everyone can easily generate a "good enough" result, the judgment that separates which of those results is right becomes the last remaining edge.

2.2 "Anyone Can Be a 10x Now - As Long As..."

One line captures this shift most compactly. A data infrastructure lead at OpenAI said, in effect, "Everybody can be a 10x engineer now, as long as you have good software taste." The decisive part is not "if you have AI" but the conditional clause "as long as you have taste." The tools are handed out equally, so the variable that makes the difference has moved to the judgment of the person holding them.

Why does taste work as a multiplier? Because good decisions compound. A single good architecture decision saves the team months over the following year, and a single good product decision means the feature is actually adopted instead of ignored. Taste-driven decisions compound, while plain effort only adds linearly. That is why some engineers deliver five or ten times the value of their peers. This point connects directly to the question in our piece on how the economics of code shifted with AI: when the marginal cost of code approaches zero, where does value move?

3. The Resurgence of the Craftsman

Strikingly, this most futuristic discussion summons the oldest of words: craftsmanship. AI is clearly a powerful force multiplier, but as one discussion notes, it cannot replace the human judgment, deep understanding, and dedication to quality that define true craftsmanship.

Indeed, in 2026 a view is rising that the most valuable developer is the artisan-builder - a craftsman and a fast maker at once. The faster AI pushes up the pace of production, the more, paradoxically, the value of slowly honed discernment rises. It is like how a better hammer does not make the carpenter's touch meaningless but instead makes it stand out more.

The value is created in the ideas, the communication, the taste - knowing what to build, why to build it, and how to get the AI or your team to build it.

The key insight here is that a craftsman's taste is the crystallization of expertise and experience. Taste is not inspiration out of thin air; it is another name for discernment accumulated across countless cases. Which leads naturally to the question: is taste innate?

4. Is Taste Innate or Learned?

The most common misconception about taste is the belief that it is an innate sense. Yet the conclusion of most discussions on this subject is the opposite. Taste is not something you are born with but a cultivated competence - which is precisely why it can become a competitive advantage.

4.1 Exposure, Critique, Comparison, Repetition

The path by which taste grows is relatively clear. The elements most discussions converge on are:

  • Exposure: encountering a wide range of cases. Only those who have seen much that is good can recognize what is good.
  • Repeatedly being wrong: through trial and error, distinctions that were once invisible become obvious.
  • Critique: giving and receiving feedback to refine, in words, why one thing works and another does not.
  • Comparison and reflection: placing the good and the bad side by side, discerning between them, and revisiting your judgment.

One phrasing stays with me: "Taste can be taught, but it cannot be fast-tracked." It requires time, exposure, feedback, and the willingness to revise your own judgment. The myth of the "born tastemaker" is as much a fiction as the myth of the "born genius" - a point that is hard to argue against.

4.2 Hume's Old Insight

This insight is, in fact, not new. The eighteenth-century philosopher David Hume, discussing the standard of taste, held that a good judge possesses delicacy of perception, practice, the capacity for comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense. Notably, all of these are cultivated capacities, not innate gifts. The claim that taste is learned is not a discovery of the AI era but a long-standing conclusion of humanity's reflection on aesthetic judgment. AI has merely raised the price of that old truth again.

5. How Engineers and Designers Cultivate Taste

So what should a practitioner do? Translating "it is learned" into action looks like this:

  1. Deliberately read a lot of well-made things. Take apart good codebases, well-designed APIs, and polished products, asking "why did they do it this way?" Without exposure there is no discernment.
  2. Trace the outcomes of your own decisions. Revisit the design and product decisions you made six months ago and see what bill they returned. Taste grows when you reclaim the experience of having been wrong.
  3. Explain in words why something is good. Try explaining to a colleague why this design or this abstraction is better. Discernment you cannot explain is still instinct, not taste. The language of critique hardens judgment.
  4. Compare good and bad side by side. Analyzing the difference between two solutions to the same problem is the fastest shortcut to forming taste.
  5. Practice deciding what to remove. Adding is easy; subtracting is hard. Asking "what happens if I remove this?" on every task sharpens the blade of discernment.

One principle runs through all five: taste is not abstract inspiration but repeatable training. To the extent that AI handles execution, those who reinvest the saved time into sharpening judgment will pull ahead. The deep expertise that underlies taste is explored further in why domain expertise is the real moat.

6. The Shadow of Taste - The AI-Era Junior Crisis

There is a dark side to taste working as a multiplier. Multiplication does not work on zero. For someone whose taste coefficient is small, AI can be a burden rather than an amplifier.

One analysis puts it sharply. Generative AI amplifies engineers who already have systems judgment, but it imposes an "AI drag" on early-career developers who lack judgment and context. Without the taste to steer, verify, and integrate AI output, the flood of plausible code makes them lose their way faster.

This carries a particularly heavy implication for the field. In an environment where AI writes all the code, juniors easily lose the chance to learn, by struggling firsthand, "why design it this way?" The nutrients of taste - exposure, critique, repetition - dry up. Organizations can no longer expect taste to "grow naturally"; they must treat it as a scarce capability to be taught deliberately, with time set aside for it. This trend dovetails with the structural career shift discussed in the erosion of the backend developer career under AI.

7. A Caution Against Taste Maximalism

Read this far and it is easy to rush to the conclusion "taste is all you need." But for balance, the other side must be stated clearly. The taste discourse holds three traps.

7.1 Taste Does Not Replace Execution

First, taste does not exempt you from fundamentals. The eye that recognizes what is good comes from the hands that have actually built it. Someone who has never written code cannot recognize good architecture, and someone who has never written cannot discern a good sentence. Taste and execution skill are not opposed; they grow from the same root. The moment "AI handles execution" is misread as "you need not learn execution," taste loses the soil it grows in.

7.2 The Risk of Taste as a Gatekeeping Barrier and Self-Display

Second, because taste is hard to measure, it is easily abused as an instrument of power. "That has no taste" can be used to dress up baseless dismissal as something respectable. When taste degrades into abstract self-display or a ticket into an exclusive club instead of verifiable results, it becomes prejudice rather than discernment. It is worth remembering that Hume listed freedom from prejudice among the conditions of good judgment.

7.3 Taste Must Be Proven by Results

Third, taste is valuable not in itself but only when it leads to better outcomes. If something claimed as good judgment produces neither an adopted product nor a structure that holds, it is not taste but mere preference. So taste should always be discussed alongside measurable outcomes - adoption rate, maintenance cost, user trust. The greatest danger of taste maximalism is enshrining taste as a "virtue exempt from results."

8. A Practitioner's View - Treating Taste as an Asset

Mapping this discussion onto real practice yields a few actionable implications.

  • Shifting hiring and evaluation criteria: the axis of evaluation needs to move from "how fast you write" to "how well you judge what to build and what to cut." As noted in 7.3, though, taste must always be measured tied to outcome metrics so it does not degrade into self-display.
  • Designing junior development: to the extent AI handles execution, you must deliberately design scaffolding for newcomers to learn "why design it this way?" by struggling with it. Redefining code review as a venue for teaching taste is one approach.
  • Recalibrating the speed myth: AI is a powerful accelerator for a "move fast and break things" culture, but without taste about what to build, you will simply build the wrong thing fast. Organizations that ask about direction before speed pull ahead in the end.

9. Is Taste Individual or Organizational?

So far this discussion has treated taste as an individual capability, but in practice taste increasingly needs to be treated as an organizational asset. An organization that depends on one person's outstanding taste loses its standard of judgment the moment that person leaves. Just as the Winchester House sprawled and collapsed without one person's blueprint in mind, taste that stays only in one person's intuition never becomes an organizational asset.

That is why mature organizations codify and externalize taste. Design systems, coding conventions, architecture decision records (ADRs), and curated collections of good and bad pull requests are, in truth, "the organization's taste written down." Once one person's discernment is pulled out into a form the whole team can reference, even a newcomer can begin judging on top of that standard. In the AI era this externalization matters one step more, because a codified standard becomes the raw material for the prompts and guardrails you hand to AI. The more an organization's taste exists in writing, the more consistent the results AI produces by following it.

Conversely, when an organization whose taste exists only as tacit knowledge adopts AI, the AI follows not the organization's discernment but the average discernment of the internet. The output is fast but loses the quality of being "distinctly our product." Externalizing taste is therefore also a line of brand defense in the AI era.

There is a practical corollary here for leaders. If you want your team's taste to survive contact with AI, invest in the artifacts that carry it: write down why a past decision was right or wrong, keep a living gallery of examples that embody your standard, and treat every code review and design critique as a deposit into that shared account. Taste that is only ever spoken evaporates; taste that is written compounds, the same way good decisions do.

10. Conclusion + Three Things You Can Apply Today

In an era when execution is cheap, differentiation comes not from faster hands but from better judgment. Taste is the discernment that separates what to build from what to cut, and the more AI commoditizes execution, the higher its value rises. At the same time, taste is not an innate gift but a competence cultivated through exposure, critique, and repetition, and it holds real value only when backed by execution skill and results. Taste is the "new 10x," but it is not a master key.

Let me close with three things you can apply right away.

  1. Reinvest the saved time into judgment. To the extent AI reduces execution, spend that time reading good cases and reviewing your own decisions. Taste is a muscle that is trained.
  2. Explain in words "why it is good." Discernment you cannot explain is instinct; discernment you can explain is taste. Practicing giving reasons to colleagues hardens judgment.
  3. Tie taste to results. Prove taste not as a boast but through outcomes like adoption rate, maintainability, and user trust. Taste exempt from results is mere preference.

The more AI takes over execution, the more essential the question that remains for humans becomes: what, and why, will we build? The ability to give a better answer to that question - that is taste, and the real 10x of the coming era. The broader context of this shift becomes clearer read alongside the changing locus of design authority in the Cathedral, the Bazaar, and the Winchester Mystery House.
