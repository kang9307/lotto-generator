A Tumor Marker Came Back High, and the Same Number Means Different Things in Different Settings

Why these tests say almost nothing in asymptomatic screening yet are decisive in treatment monitoring - prevalence and predictive value

2026-07-30 Health & Wellness

Introduction: the same number can point in opposite directions

An optional health check-up panel includes a line item called tumor markers. A few days later the report arrives, and there is a flag next to CA-125 or CEA. One word appears in your head. And from that moment, the searching begins.

This article is not a substitute for that searching. It covers the one thing that searching tends not to surface. Here it is: the same test, the same number, carries completely different information depending on who was tested.

Concretely: a raised CA-125 in a person with no symptoms who received it as part of a check-up panel tells you almost nothing. A rising CA-125 in a person under surveillance after treatment for ovarian cancer is a highly important signal. This holds even when the numbers are identical, and it can hold even when the second person's number is the lower of the two. Why that inversion happens is the whole subject of this article.

The answer lives in statistics and it has a name: positive predictive value. And that value is not determined by test performance alone. It is co-determined by how much of the disease is present in the group being tested. Understanding that one sentence resolves most of the confusion around tumor markers.

Let us fix the starting point. The US National Cancer Institute defines a tumor marker in a way that includes substances produced in response to cancer or certain benign conditions. Non-cancer causes are written into the definition itself, not tacked on as an exception. And the same source carries one short, unambiguous sentence: having an elevated level of a tumor marker does not mean that someone has cancer.

The order of what follows: what a tumor marker is, why these tests are not used for screening (with a worked hypothetical), what is known marker by marker, why a normal value is not reassurance, the intended use in monitoring and recurrence surveillance, the real cost of false positives, how Korea's national programme is composed, what to do when a value comes back high, what this article could not verify, and a situation-by-situation table.

This article is written for general health information purposes and is not medical advice. 1. What a tumor marker is - and why the name misleads

Start with the National Cancer Institute definition. A tumor marker is anything present in or produced by cancer cells, or produced by other cells of the body in response to cancer or certain benign conditions, that provides information about a cancer. Traditionally these were proteins found in blood, urine, stool, tumors or bodily fluids; increasingly the category also includes genomic markers such as tumor gene mutations.

Two things in that definition deserve attention.

  • First, it says something that provides information. It does not say something that determines whether cancer is present. At the level of definition, this is already not a diagnostic instrument.
  • Second, in response to certain benign conditions sits inside the definition. That a non-cancer cause can raise the value is not an edge case; it is part of what the category means.

So the same source places three statements side by side. An elevated marker does not mean cancer is present. Noncancerous conditions can raise a marker. And not everyone with a given cancer will have a raised level of the marker associated with it. Those three correspond, in order, to false positives, the cause of false positives, and false negatives.

What are these tests for, then? The uses the Institute lists are: monitoring response to treatment, detecting recurrence after treatment ends, determining cancer type and stage, assessing prognosis, and identifying candidates for targeted therapy. And even where a marker contributes to diagnosis, it does not do so alone. The same source states that marker measurements are usually combined with the results of other tests, such as biopsies or imaging, to diagnose cancer.

It is faster to notice what is missing from that list. Finding cancer in people without symptoms is not on it. In fact the Institute states directly that circulating tumor markers do not work well for screening, and gives two reasons: they often fail to identify everyone with the disease because they are not sensitive enough, or they indicate the possible presence of cancer in people who do not actually have cancer.

Here are the officially listed uses of the four best-known markers, as published by the Institute.

All four rows repeat treatment and recurrence. None of the four says screening. That table summarises this article's argument on a single page. These tests were designed as instruments for following the timeline of someone whose diagnosis is already established.

2. The core question - why these tests are not used to screen

This is why the article exists, and there is a common misconception to clear first: that these tests are too inaccurate to screen with. That is only half right. The more accurate statement is this: even a reasonably accurate test produces mostly false positives when the disease being sought is rare.

2.1 Prevalence governs positive predictive value

Three terms, briefly.

  • Sensitivity: among people who truly have the disease, the proportion the test flags as positive.
  • Specificity: among people who truly do not have it, the proportion the test correctly passes as negative.
  • Positive predictive value (PPV): among people whose test came back positive, the proportion who truly have the disease.

The person holding the report wants to know the third one. What gets printed in test literature is usually the first two. And the third cannot be derived from the first two alone. You also need to know how much disease is present in that group.

2.2 A worked hypothetical - the arithmetic makes it visible

The number to read in that table is 4,998. A specificity of 95% is not poor performance. But because 99,960 people do not have the disease, misclassifying even 5% of them yields nearly five thousand false positives. Meanwhile there were only 40 real cases to begin with, so the best possible haul is 32. It is five thousand against thirty-two, and the outcome is not in doubt.

The second row shows the ceiling on test improvement. Lifting specificity from 95 to 99 is a substantial engineering achievement, and PPV still lands at 3.1%. That is still 31 false positives for every 32 positive results.

The third row is the heart of the article. Nothing about the test changed. Sensitivity and specificity are identical. The only thing that changed is who was tested, and PPV jumps from 0.64% to 45.7% - more than seventyfold.

That is what the same number, different information value actually means. The meaning of a result does not live inside the test; it lives in the probability that existed before the test was run. Which is why the first thing a clinician establishes when looking at a marker value is not the value but who this person is.

2.4 This has been confirmed by measurement, not just theory

The calculation above is a principle. What happened when large populations were actually screened? Ovarian cancer supplies the answer.

A large randomised screening trial in the United Kingdom divided more than 200,000 women into three arms: multimodal screening built around CA-125, transvaginal ultrasound screening, and no screening. On long-term follow-up, ovarian and tubal cancer deaths were not significantly reduced in either screening arm. The investigators' conclusion was that since screening did not significantly reduce these deaths, general population screening cannot be recommended.

The US Preventive Services Task Force points the same way. Its 2018 recommendation is against screening for ovarian cancer in asymptomatic women, carrying a grade D. In its evidence summary the Task Force states that there is adequate evidence that screening with transvaginal ultrasound, testing for the serum tumor marker CA-125, or a combination of both does not reduce ovarian cancer mortality. It also documented the harms: false-positive rates of 9.0% to 11.9% in first screening rounds, and the fact that false positives can lead to unnecessary surgical interventions in women who do not have cancer. In one major trial the proportion of false positives who went on to surgery reached 3.25%.

Put simply: the trials came out the way the arithmetic predicted. That alignment is not common, which is part of why the recommendation in this area is relatively firm.

3. Marker by marker - what actually raises these values

Why false positives are so numerous becomes clearer marker by marker. There really are a lot of non-cancer reasons.

3.1 CA-125 - the trap in the nickname

CA-125 travels with the name of ovarian cancer, but the patient-facing reference from the US National Library of Medicine lists non-cancer causes of elevation: endometriosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, uterine fibroids, liver disease, having a menstrual period, and pregnancy.

Read that list again. Endometriosis and fibroids are not rare, menstruation is cyclical, and pregnancy is not a disease. The common reasons CA-125 rises are mostly unrelated to cancer. The five thousand false positives in the previous section are not an abstract arithmetic artefact.

The same source sorts the test's uses into three: seeing whether ovarian cancer treatment is working or whether the cancer has come back, assessing whether a pelvic mass found on imaging could be ovarian cancer, and screening only where risk is very high. And that last item carries a restriction. The page states plainly that a provider cannot use CA-125 to screen for ovarian cancer in someone who is not at high risk.

That distinction matters in practice. It is used in a defined high-risk group and it is used on anyone are entirely different claims. If CA-125 arrived as part of a check-up panel, it was used in the second sense, and its information value in that setting is exactly what the previous section's arithmetic showed.

3.2 CEA - smoking raises the baseline

The Institute lists CEA as being used to check the effect of treatment and detect recurrence in colorectal and some other cancers. Again, not diagnosis and not screening.

CEA has one particular quirk. Baseline CEA is higher in smokers than in non-smokers - a finding repeatedly reported across studies - and some laboratories consequently apply a higher upper limit for smokers. The practical implication is simple: reading a single CEA line without knowing smoking status invites misreading it.

That said, this article did not succeed in opening a primary source to verify the specific cut-off difference between smokers and non-smokers. So the direction is stated here and the numbers are deliberately omitted. When interpreting your own report, the accurate move is to confirm the reference range that laboratory printed, together with smoking status, in consultation.

3.3 AFP - it occupies a special place in the Korean system

AFP (alpha-fetoprotein) appears in the Institute's list alongside liver cancer, ovarian cancer and germ cell tumors, used to help diagnose and follow response to treatment, and in germ cell tumors to assess stage, prognosis and response.

But AFP is the only one of the four markers discussed here that has actually entered a public screening programme - Korea's. With conditions attached. What those conditions are is set out in the national programme section below, because lifting the sentence AFP is in the national programme out of context creates a misunderstanding.

3.4 PSA - how not to misread a grade letter

PSA is the most debated marker in this area, and the grade letters attached to its recommendation are the most frequently misread objects in the whole topic.

The 2018 US Preventive Services Task Force recommendation splits by age.

  • Men aged 55 to 69: the decision to undergo periodic PSA-based screening should be an individual one - grade C
  • Men 70 and older: the Task Force recommends against PSA-based screening - grade D

Two pieces of interpretation must travel with those letters.

  • C does not mean do not do it. By the Task Force's own definition, C reflects a judgement that the net benefit is small; in practice it means decide after an individual discussion. Having the test and declining it are both defensible, and personal values and circumstances legitimately enter at that fork.
  • D is not a label saying this test is harmful. The definition is that there is evidence the service is ineffective or that harms outweigh benefits. The load-bearing words are in that population. It is a judgement about asymptomatic screening in men 70 and older; it is not a judgement about follow-up testing in someone already diagnosed with prostate cancer. The same test being grade D in one setting and a standard instrument in another is not a contradiction.

Non-cancer causes of a raised PSA are also documented. The Institute lists benign prostatic hyperplasia and prostatitis, notes that infection or inflammation of the prostate, or a recent prostate biopsy, can keep levels raised for a month or two, and that vigorous exercise such as cycling, and ejaculation, can raise the level transiently. PSA also tends to rise naturally with age, which is why different cut-offs are sometimes applied by age band.

Two numbers give a sense of scale. The Institute states that about 6% to 7% of men have a false-positive PSA test on any given screening round, and that only about 25% of men who have a biopsy due to an elevated PSA are found to have prostate cancer. Inverted: three of every four men whose raised value led to a biopsy did not have cancer.

Finally, on thresholds. The Institute states that there is no single threshold that distinguishes a normal from an abnormal PSA result, and no specific PSA level that means someone has prostate cancer. One number on a report does not settle anything.

4. Why a normal value is not reassurance - the other half of the symmetry

So far we have looked at one direction: a raised value may not be cancer. If the article stopped here it would have told half the story, and half of this particular story can be more dangerous than none. There is an opposite direction.

It is the Institute's third sentence: not everyone with a particular type of cancer will have a higher level of the tumor marker associated with that cancer.

For CA-125 the wording is more specific. The National Library of Medicine page states that a normal CA-125 result does not rule ovarian cancer out, and gives two reasons: levels may be low in the early stages of cancer, and not everyone with ovarian cancer makes high levels of CA-125.

The hypothetical showed this symmetry too. Negative predictive value looked excellent at 99.99% - yet 8 of the 40 real cases (20%) were classified as normal. That NPV was high not because the test was excellent but because the disease was rare to begin with. The same prevalence effect, this time working in the reassuring direction.

Which yields one practical conclusion. For a person with symptoms, a normal tumor marker is not grounds to postpone care. Symptoms must be evaluated along their own pathway, and that pathway may be imaging, endoscopy or biopsy. Skipping that pathway because a marker was normal is the most dangerous misuse of this test.

5. The intended use - decisive in monitoring, and trend beats absolute value

Now the other side. There is a setting in which this test is extremely useful, and it is the setting the test was built for.

Look again at the earlier table: assessment of response to treatment and evaluation of recurrence appear across all four markers. That is the intended use. And here the arithmetic from section 2 works in your favour.

The reason is simple. For someone already diagnosed with that cancer, the prior probability that the cancer is present is incomparably higher than in an asymptomatic member of the general public. If treatment is ongoing, presence is established. If treatment has finished, the probability of recurrence is on a different scale entirely from population incidence. It is the same structure as the 5%-prevalence row that produced a PPV of 45.7%, and in real surveillance the prior can be higher still.

Monitoring adds one more thing: the comparison group is yourself.

In screening, your value is compared against a population reference range, so every individual peculiarity becomes noise. In monitoring, your value is compared against your own earlier values. Individual variation drops out as a constant and what remains is change. That is why, in monitoring, trend matters more than the absolute number.

Concretely, what changes:

  • A value inside the reference range can still be meaningful. If a level that sat low after treatment is climbing steadily even while remaining within range, that climb is itself something to check.
  • A value slightly above the range can be unremarkable. A number that has not budged from that spot for a long time is read differently.
  • Several points beat one point. A trend requires at least two measurements and in practice several.
  • Consistency of measurement conditions is the crux. If the laboratory or the assay changes, the value can shift, and that shift can be misread as a change in disease.
  • Monitoring intervals are not adjusted at will. They are set by which cancer, which treatment, and how much time has passed.

The fourth point causes trouble particularly often in practice. Measuring the same marker at a different laboratory with a different assay can produce a different number. So for someone under surveillance, measuring under the same conditions can matter more than the value itself, and if you have changed testing facilities it is worth saying so at your appointment.

6. What false positives and false negatives actually produce

It is easy to treat a false positive as merely a repeat test. In reality costs attach. The ovarian screening evidence summary quoted above states that false positives can lead to unnecessary surgical interventions in women who do not have cancer, and in one trial 3.25% of false positives went on to surgery. On the PSA side, about 75% of men biopsied for a raised value did not have cancer.

And false positives are not the only problem. The Institute lists overdiagnosis among the harms of PSA screening: some cancers found by screening grow so slowly that they would never cause symptoms or become life threatening, yet treating them can cause harm. The post-treatment effects listed include urinary, bowel and sexual problems.

That is why the Institute writes that marker measurements are used together with biopsies or imaging. A marker is a signal that hands you on to the next test; confirmation happens elsewhere.

7. Does Korea's national cancer screening programme include tumor markers?

This question matters practically, because many people remember the public programme and an optional paid panel as one thing. The screening methods for the six cancers covered, as published by the Korean National Cancer Information Center, are as follows. Readers outside Korea should note that this is the Korean programme specifically; national programmes and recommendations differ by country, so check your own national guidance.

Stated precisely, what the table shows is this.

  • CA-125, CEA and PSA are not part of the national programme. Ovarian and prostate cancer are not among the six cancers at all, and colorectal screening uses a faecal occult blood test rather than CEA.
  • AFP is included. But only for the liver cancer high-risk group, paired with ultrasound rather than used alone, and repeated on a short six-month interval.

AFP's exceptional position actually reinforces this article's argument. Narrowing the target to a high-risk group raises the prior probability, which changes the information value of the same marker. Then ultrasound is paired with it so the two tests offset each other's false positives and false negatives, and the short interval builds a trend. All three conditions set out above are satisfied by that design. So AFP's inclusion is not evidence that tumor markers can screen for cancer. It is closer to a case study in the conditions under which one is used at all.

The practical conclusion: if a check-up gave you CA-125, CEA or PSA, that was a separately chosen test rather than a national programme item, and the interpretive frame differs. 9. What to do when you are told a value came back high

There is no step here in which you reach a verdict yourself. The only aim is to organise what you will bring to a consultation.

9.1 First, establish these

  • Which marker, and how high, from the original report - not a summary or a text message, but the original with the value and that laboratory's reference range printed together.
  • Whether there is an earlier value to compare against. If you have had the same test before, that value is central information for this judgement.
  • The circumstances on the day of the test. For CA-125 interpretation, where you were in the menstrual cycle, possible pregnancy, and any recent pelvic procedure may be relevant.
  • Smoking status. This is relevant to CEA interpretation.
  • Recent procedures and tests. PSA can sit raised for a month or two after a prostate biopsy or inflammation.
  • A complete list of medicines and supplements you take.
  • Existing diagnoses. Liver disease, endometriosis, uterine fibroids and inflammatory conditions can themselves be causes of elevation.

9.2 Where to go

There is no single answer; it depends on which marker and which symptoms, if any. But the starting point is usually one of two.

  • Request a results consultation at the facility that did the check-up. They know the reference range and the assay used, which makes them a natural first step.
  • If you have symptoms, the specialty that matches the symptoms. Symptoms decide the specialty, not the marker. And in that case the principle that you get the symptom evaluated even if the marker is normal takes precedence.

9.4 What not to do

  • Do not judge yourself against a threshold you found by searching. As shown above, some markers have no single cut-off at all, and reference ranges can differ between laboratories.
  • Do not repeat the test somewhere else and compare the two numbers. Different assays can shift the value, so the comparison does not hold. Whether and how to repeat is decided in consultation.
  • Do not go looking for products or procedures that claim to lower a tumor marker. A marker is not itself a treatment target. Lowering a number cannot be the goal.
  • Do not let a normal result paper over a symptom. This is the sentence this article has repeated most.
  • Do not try to buy reassurance by adding more markers to a panel. Adding tests also raises the chance of a false positive. What to add is a matter for consultation.

10. What this article could not verify

Some items belong in the open. The following are points where a primary source could not be opened directly, so no figures are given.

  • The CEA cut-off difference between smokers and non-smokers: the direction, that smokers run higher, is widely reported, but this article did not open a primary source for the specific cut-offs and reference ranges. Numbers were therefore omitted.
  • How often and how far each benign condition raises CA-125: which conditions raise it was verified; the frequency and magnitude for each were not.
  • Performance figures for AFP alone in liver cancer screening: that it is paired with ultrasound in the Korean programme was verified, but sensitivity and specificity for AFP alone were not.
  • Marker-specific prevalence and predictive value measured in a Korean population: the calculations in the text are hypothetical figures for illustrating the principle, not domestic measurements.
  • Results for multi-cancer early detection tests: the Institute notes this area is under research and outcomes remain unclear, so this article did not cover it.

Not writing unverified things as though they were verified seemed especially important in this particular topic.

Frequently asked questions

My check-up showed a high CA-125. Does that mean cancer?

That call cannot be made from this one number. The US National Cancer Institute states that an elevated marker does not mean cancer is present, and noncancerous conditions can raise a marker. For CA-125, the US National Library of Medicine lists endometriosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, uterine fibroids, liver disease, menstruation and pregnancy as causes, and states that a provider cannot use CA-125 to screen for ovarian cancer in someone not at high risk. The mechanism is clear: when the disease sought is rare, most positives are false positives even with an accurate test. The next step is not a self-verdict; it is to bring the original report, earlier values and your medication list to a clinician.

If my tumor marker is normal, does that mean I do not have cancer?

No. This is the point most often reversed. The Institute writes that not everyone with a given cancer has a raised level of its associated marker. For CA-125, the National Library of Medicine states that a normal result does not rule ovarian cancer out, because levels may be low in early stages and not everyone with ovarian cancer makes high levels. For PSA the Institute states that there is no single threshold separating normal from abnormal. So the important thing is not to postpone care because a marker came back normal when you have symptoms.

Why are tumor markers not used to find cancer early?

Because when the disease is rare, positive predictive value collapses even with good sensitivity and specificity. The Institute states that circulating tumor markers do not work well for screening. Measurement agrees. A randomised trial in the United Kingdom analysing more than 200,000 women found no significant reduction in deaths with CA-125-based screening, and the investigators concluded that general population screening cannot be recommended. The US Preventive Services Task Force also recommends against screening asymptomatic women for ovarian cancer (grade D), citing adequate evidence that such screening does not reduce mortality.

Should I have a PSA test or not?

The 2018 US Preventive Services Task Force recommendation splits by age. For men 55 to 69, periodic PSA screening should be an individual decision - grade C. For men 70 and older the Task Force recommends against it - grade D. Do not misread the letters. C reflects a judgement that net benefit is small and means decide after an individual discussion; D is not a label saying the test is harmful, but a judgement that in that age group harms outweigh benefits for asymptomatic screening. For scale, the Institute states that about 6% to 7% have a false positive on any round and that only about 25% of those biopsied for a raised value have cancer.

Does the Korean national cancer screening programme include tumor markers?

Almost none, with one exception. The six covered cancers use endoscopy or an upper gastrointestinal series for stomach, faecal occult blood testing for colorectal, mammography for breast, cervical cytology for cervical, and low-dose chest computed tomography for lung. No tumor marker appears. The exception is liver cancer: a high-risk group aged 40 and over, on a six-month interval, with liver ultrasound paired with a serum alpha-fetoprotein test. So AFP genuinely is included, but for a narrow group and never alone. A panel containing CA-125, CEA or PSA is therefore a separately chosen test. Programmes differ by country, so check your own national guidance.

Conclusion: read the setting before you read the number

Six lines for what this article kept returning to.

  1. A tumor marker is not a cancer diagnostic test. The definition says something that provides information, and benign conditions sit inside that definition. Across the four markers the Institute lists, screening never appears.
  2. Low information value in asymptomatic screening is not the test's fault. When disease is rare, the disease-free vastly outnumber the diseased, so even good specificity yields far more false than true positives. In the hypothetical, PPV was 0.64%.
  3. Apply the same test to a different group and the value changes. Changing nothing but prevalence moved PPV from 0.64% to 45.7%. Information value lives not in the test but in the prior probability.
  4. Theory and measurement agree. A 200,000-woman ovarian screening trial found no significant mortality reduction, and the Task Force recommends against screening asymptomatic women. PSA splits by age into C (individual decision) and D (recommend against), and D does not mean harmful.
  5. Normal is not exclusion. Levels can be low early, and some patients simply do not produce much marker. With symptoms, you get the symptom evaluated even if the marker is normal.
  6. In its intended use it is decisive. In treatment monitoring and recurrence surveillance the prior is high and the comparison is against yourself, so trend rather than absolute value becomes the signal - which is why repeating under the same conditions matters.

One sentence on this article's stance. Wrong answers here arrive from two directions. One is reading a raised value as a diagnosis and becoming frightened. The other is reading a normal value as a licence and burying a symptom. Both come from the same misconception - that this is a test which tells you whether you have cancer.

So the practical conclusion is short. Read the setting before you read the number. Three things to establish at your appointment: what this value means in my situation, whether a repeat is needed, and whether other tests are needed. Securing those three beats any amount of searching. And no article can substitute for that judgement - including this one.
