Living Alone Guide Part 18: Dealing with Loneliness

It's Okay, Everyone Feels This

Jan 25, 2026Health & Wellness

Living Alone Gets Lonely Sometimes

They say it's freeing and comfortable, but sometimes you have no one to talk to, weekends alone feel empty... These feelings are normal. Everyone experiences them.

As more people live alone, more people meet this feeling. In South Korea, where this series is written, single-person households passed 8.045 million for the first time in 2024 and now make up 36.1% of all households, an all-time high. More than one in three homes is a household of one, and the same shift is happening across most developed countries.

The 2025 national social survey makes the pattern clearer. Among people living alone, 48.9% said they feel lonely "often or sometimes" — more than 10 percentage points above the all-household average of 38.2%. Nearly half of people living alone are carrying this feeling. So if you were reading this thinking "is it just me?", statistically it is very much not just you.

This isn't a local story either. The World Health Organization's Commission on Social Connection published its flagship report, From loneliness to social connection, on 30 June 2025. Drawing on data from 2014 to 2023, it estimated that roughly one in six people worldwide (about 16%) experience loneliness. We tend to think of loneliness as an old-age problem, but the report found it was most common among adolescents and young adults — closer to one in five. The WHO framed social well-being as a third pillar of health alongside physical and mental well-being, treating loneliness as a public health topic rather than a personal character flaw.

One thing to be clear about before we go further: loneliness itself is not an illness. Just as hunger is your body signalling "you need to eat", loneliness is closer to your mind signalling "there's less connection here than I need right now." A signal is information, not a fault. This article focuses on how to read that signal and rebuild connection in everyday life. Broader mental wellbeing — sleep, meals, stress — is covered separately in Part 14: Mental Health Care.

Why Loneliness Happens - Being Alone vs Feeling Lonely

First, something worth sorting out. "Being alone" and "feeling lonely" are different things. Research usually separates them like this.

  • Social isolation: the objective state of having few people you actually see or talk to
  • Loneliness: the subjective feeling that comes from the gap between the relationships you want and the ones you have

That's why both of these exist: someone who spends the whole day alone and doesn't feel lonely at all, and someone who feels acutely lonely at a table full of colleagues. The first is isolated but not lonely; the second isn't isolated but is lonely. They also need different fixes. Isolation is solved by adding points of contact; loneliness is solved by filling in the quality of connection. Miss that distinction and you can attend every event on the calendar and still feel hollow on the walk home.

The psychologist Robert S. Weiss split loneliness into two kinds: emotional loneliness, from not having that one person you can say the real thing to, and social loneliness, from not having a group or community that takes you in. Later research broadly supports the split — emotional loneliness tracks with the depth of relationships, social loneliness with their number and structure. Knowing which one you're in makes your response far more accurate.

TypeWhat's missingWhen it shows upWhat actually helps
Emotional loneliness
(a depth problem)		 No one or two close people to say the real thing to — or they exist, but the connection has gone quiet Feeling empty even in a crowded room · No one comes to mind when you're sick · Something good happens and there's nowhere to put it Rather than hunting for new people, rewarm one or two you already know. Don't just ask how they are — share a line about your own week first, and the conversation actually continues

(a structure problem)		 No group that gives you a sense of belonging, no community whose faces you see on a regular basis Right after a move or a job change · Nowhere to go on weekends · "Nobody in this neighbourhood knows my name" Build one activity you return to at the same time, in the same place. ※ This table is a way to understand yourself, not a diagnostic tool. In practice the three types overlap more often than not.

Most people are a mix rather than one clean type. But knowing which box is emptiest right now lets you improve things with far less energy than a vague "I should see more people." Prescribe a social fix (go to more events) for emotional loneliness and you just get tired; prescribe an emotional fix (lean everything on one close friend) for social loneliness and you collapse again the week they're busy.

1. When Loneliness Hits

  • Weekends alone at home
  • Holidays when you can't go home
  • When you're sick
  • Good news but no one to share it with
  • Late nights when you can't sleep
  • Seeing others' gatherings on social media

Loneliness isn't something to be ashamed of. It's a natural human emotion.

2. Quick Fixes for Temporary Loneliness

Do Right Now

  • Go outside: Even to a store, cafe, or just a walk
  • Reach out: Call family or a friend
  • Video chat: Seeing faces makes a difference
  • Online communities: Connect even if it's virtual

Mood Boosters

  • Blast your favorite music
  • Order something delicious
  • Watch a movie or show
  • Take a warm shower
  • Move your body (stretch, walk)

Quality of Relationships Over Quantity

Adding contacts to fight loneliness works less well than people expect. The 2025 social survey has one more number worth noticing: among people living alone, 73.5% said they have someone to talk to when they feel down, 68.9% said they have someone to help when they're physically ill, and only 45.6% said they have someone to turn to if they needed to borrow money. All three were below the all-household average. What's short isn't the number of people you know — it's the number of people you can actually lean on when it matters.

So it helps to reframe the goal. Not "make lots of friends", but "have three people I could call if I got sick." Three is enough. What you need isn't a wide network, it's a few sturdy ropes.

Quality comes down to 'repeat' and 'first'

  • Repeat: relationships deepen through accumulated small contact, not grand events. A five-minute call every two weeks holds a friendship together better than one long night out every six months.
  • First: "I'll reply when they reach out" is what both sides are thinking. Most relationships fade simply because nobody went first. It's nothing to apologise for.
  • Be specific: "Let's grab a meal sometime" is a greeting, not a plan. "How about Thursday evening next week?" is what actually moves a relationship.
  • Weak ties count too: the barista who knows your order, the person you always pass at the gym, the neighbour who walks at the same hour. Even without depth, the sense that someone recognises me measurably takes the edge off loneliness. On finding the right distance with neighbours, see Part 13: Getting Along with Neighbors.
  • Practise receiving: relationships where you only ever give don't last. Asking for a small favour is itself a signal to the other person that you trust them.

There's a habit that quietly lowers quality too: only reaching out when you're struggling. It isn't wrong, but it teaches the other person that "a message from me = something heavy", which makes contact harder over time in both directions. The short, pointless check-ins you send when nothing is wrong are what build the place you can lean on when something is.

3. Preventing Loneliness Daily

Maintain Connections

  • Regular calls to family
  • Monthly meetups with friends
  • Join clubs or groups
  • Regular activities like exercise classes

Solo Routines

  • Regular schedule (routines provide stability)
  • Hobbies (something you can get absorbed in)
  • Exercise habits (endorphin effect)
  • Set small goals and achieve them

Realistic Ways to Build New Connections

If there aren't many people to call in the first place, you need new points of contact. The trouble is that "join a club" is uselessly vague as advice. Activities differ a lot in how hard they are to start (barrier to entry) and how hard they are to keep going (stickiness). The trick is picking one that matches the energy you actually have right now.

Rules that reduce failure when starting

  • Suspend judgement until the third visit: feeling awkward the first time applies to nearly everyone. You can't tell whether a group fits you until roughly the third time.
  • Start exactly one thing: sign up for three at once out of enthusiasm and you'll drop all three within two weeks. Add more only after one has settled.
  • Pick a fixed day: "I'll go when I have time" is the single most common route to never going. Put it in the calendar as a repeating event.
  • Take a small role: treasurer, photos, stacking chairs — anything. A role gives you a reason to show up, and people start using your name.
  • Close to home wins: past about an hour of round-trip travel, follow-through drops off a cliff. Distance predicts stickiness more than how fun it is.
  • Safety as standard: meet people for the first time in public places, and don't hand over your home address or detailed workplace information on a first meeting.

And lowering expectations slightly makes it last. The odds of meeting a lifelong friend at a new group aren't that high. But simply having somewhere to be on a Saturday changes how the whole week feels. That on its own is already a win.

4. Enjoying Time Alone

Change Your Perspective

  • Alone ≠ lonely
  • Solo time = free time
  • Peace with no interruptions

Solo Activities

  • Solo restaurant visits
  • Going to movies alone
  • Solo trips
  • Reading at a cafe
  • Self-improvement/studying

Learning to enjoy time alone reduces loneliness.

Turning Time Alone Into Recovery Time

Time alone isn't the problem. Used well, it's how you recover. The problem is that the same behaviour is restorative on one day and avoidant on another. An evening watching something is a recharge some nights and a way of letting hours drain away on others. What separates them isn't the activity — it's whether you chose it or drifted into it.

Same behaviourWhen it's recoveryWhen it looks more like avoidance
Watching shows or filmsYou picked what you wanted to watch, and you feel a bit better afterwardsAutoplay running while you barely register what's on · you feel emptier when it ends than when it started
SleepYou sleep and wake at consistent times and your body feels lighterYou keep lying down through the day, sleep doesn't refresh you, and day and night flip around
Gaming and social mediaYou enjoy the amount you meant to, then move on to something elseHours vanish · you keep scrolling and comparing even though it's making you feel worse
Staying homeA day you chose in order to rest. Tomorrow you feel like going out againErrands you need to do keep sliding, and you haven't been past the front door in days
Throwing yourself into work or studyThere's a goal, and when it's done you switch to something elseYou keep manufacturing tasks because empty time is frightening · resting makes you anxious

Small devices that turn alone time into recovery

  • Decide the start and the end: "two episodes, then shower" leaves the same two hours feeling completely different.
  • Move your body first: you don't move once you feel better; feeling better follows moving. Ten minutes of walking often beats thirty minutes of thinking.
  • Put human voices in the room: leaving a radio show or podcast on takes a surprising amount of weight out of the silence.
  • One weekend anchor: library on Saturday morning, groceries on Sunday evening — a single fixed point holds the whole weekend together.
  • Track it for two weeks: note when loneliness hits hardest and a pattern appears. It usually clusters on specific days and hours, and once you know, you can plan around it.
  • Get morning light: opening the curtains and getting outdoor light early, even briefly, helps steady your daily rhythm.

5. Warning Signs

Everything so far has been about everyday loneliness. But when it goes on long enough that daily life itself starts to wobble, that's a different situation. It isn't a question of willpower — it's a signal that this is the point to bring someone else in. The items below are a way of gauging when that point has arrived.

When to Seek Help

  • When loneliness turns to depression
  • Feeling down for more than 2 weeks
  • No motivation to do anything
  • Thoughts of self-harm

Resources

  • National Suicide Prevention: 988
  • Crisis Text Line: Text HOME to 741741
  • SAMHSA National Helpline: 1-800-662-4357
  • Local mental health centers (often free)

Signs You Need Help - When and How to Ask

The threshold for asking, put as simply as possible: ① has it lasted more than two weeks ② is daily life (work, sleep, meals, hygiene) coming apart ③ does it feel like you can't steer it yourself. If any one of those is true, there's no reason to wait for it to get worse first. Helplines aren't reserved for people in extremity — they're closer to a way of having someone alongside you while you get through a hard stretch.

🚨 If things are hard right now - lines open around the clock

  • 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (US) — call or text 988, or chat at 988lifeline.org. Free, confidential, 24/7.
  • Crisis Text Line — text HOME to 741741 if speaking on the phone feels like too much.
  • SAMHSA National Helpline — 1-800-662-4357, free 24/7 treatment referral and information service.
  • Local mental health or community centres — many offer free or low-cost counselling. Search your city name plus "community mental health centre".

If you are in immediate danger, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency room. If you live outside the United States, look up the crisis helpline for your own country and save it in your phone now, before you need it — numbers differ by country and change over time. (For readers in Korea: suicide prevention line 109, integrated from the former 1393 in January 2024, and the mental health crisis line 1577-0199.)

If picking up the phone feels like too much

  • You don't need to prepare what to say: "I'm just having a really hard time, that's why I called" is enough of an opening. The counsellor takes it from there.
  • "Is this bad enough to call about?" — yes, it is: non-emergency conversations are part of what these lines are for. You don't have to judge whether you've crossed a threshold.
  • If talking is the barrier: several services run text and online chat alongside the phone line. Check each organisation's website.
  • When telling someone you know: you don't have to ask them for solutions. "I'm having a rough patch — can you just listen for a bit?" makes it far easier on both of you.
  • If cost is the worry: community mental health centres, university counselling services and employee assistance programmes often provide free or sliding-scale sessions. Eligibility changes, so check current terms with the provider.

This article is not a substitute for medical diagnosis or treatment. Loneliness is not in itself an illness. What follows here is everyday information and a list of official support lines for people living alone who want to rebuild connection. It does not identify any condition and does not recommend medication or treatments, and none of the tables or checklists in this article are diagnostic tools. If things persist beyond two weeks or daily life becomes difficult, please speak with a mental health professional.

6. What Not to Do

  • Drinking to cope: Temporary, makes depression worse
  • Social media obsession: Comparison makes you lonelier
  • Suffering alone: You need to talk to release
  • Toxic relationships: Bad connections are worse than none

These four have something in common: they make right now easier and tomorrow's loneliness bigger. Alcohol makes falling asleep easier but degrades sleep quality, which drags the next day's mood down further. Social media sets other people's edited highlights next to your ordinary Tuesday. Late-night scrolling in particular is the combination that amplifies loneliness most reliably — putting the screen down for the last thirty minutes before bed genuinely changes how it feels.

"Toxic relationships" deserves a note of its own. Loneliness loosens judgement, and it becomes easy to decide that a relationship which doesn't respect you is still better than none. But the depletion that comes out of those relationships takes far longer to recover from than being on your own did. Choosing relationships matters more than adding them. Some people specifically target loneliness, so it's worth keeping distance from anyone who moves early to requests for money or unusually detailed personal information.

Regular Habits

  • ☐ Meet people regularly
  • ☐ Maintain hobbies/exercise

A four-week connection plan

Trying to change everything at once fails. Add one thing per week.

  • Week 1: send a check-in message to one person you've lost touch with (no reply still counts as success)
  • Week 2: research three candidate recurring activities near home and sign up for one
  • Week 3: attend for the first time. Stay to the end even if it's awkward
  • Week 4: go a second time to the same activity, and lock in one weekend anchor

Wrap-up

Loneliness is a natural feeling that everyone living alone experiences. Don't beat yourself up—find what works for you. And if it gets really hard, please ask for help.

One last thing worth holding on to. Feeling lonely means you still have the wish to be connected to people. If you didn't want connection, you wouldn't feel lonely in the first place. That wish isn't a defect — it's a resource. The reason the WHO called social connection a third pillar of health is precisely that it isn't something individual willpower produces; it's a condition we build together.

You don't have to do anything big today. Open a window, message one person, walk for ten minutes. That's a full enough day.
