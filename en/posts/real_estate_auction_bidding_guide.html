Korean Real Estate Court Auction - Bidding Procedure and the Basics of Rights Analysis

The risk is not the price, it is the rights the winning bidder inherits

2026-07-31 Law & Legal

Introduction: winning below market value and still losing money

Start with a hypothetical. An apartment appears at a court auction well below its appraised value, and someone wins it at that figure. The register carried a line of mortgages and attachments, but the bidder had been told that a successful bid erases all of it, so none of it looked like a problem. After the price was paid and ownership transferred, the person living in the apartment came and asked for the deposit back. That person had moved in and completed resident registration before the mortgage was created, and the distribution returned only part of the deposit.

What the bidder got wrong in that scene was not the pricing. It was assuming that the register shows everything. More precisely, it was not knowing that some rights die with the sale and some follow the buyer.

This article is not about how to buy cheaply at auction. It is about the other side: what you have to verify before a cheap purchase stops being cheap. The risk in a court auction does not live in the price. It lives in the inherited rights, and a good number of them never appear on the register. So what this article gives you is not a return calculation but a list of items to verify and the statute articles behind them.

Jurisdiction. Everything below describes the law of the Republic of Korea only. Court auctions, registration systems and tenant protection differ fundamentally between countries, and none of this describes the foreclosure or auction system of any other jurisdiction.

Disclaimer and verification date. This article is general information, not legal advice. The rights attached to a specific property can be judged completely differently depending on registration timing, the contents of the case file, and site conditions, so it must be reviewed by a qualified professional such as an attorney or a certified judicial scrivener. The decision to bid and its consequences are entirely your own responsibility. This article does not encourage anyone to participate in auctions.

1. An auction is not a sale - as-is acquisition and a contract you cannot undo

The first thing to correct is the habit of seeing an auction as a discounted listing the court happens to be selling. An ordinary sale and a court auction differ in the structure of the transaction itself.

An ordinary sale has a seller. The seller is a party to the contract, and if the property has a defect there is somebody to hold responsible. You can negotiate special terms, and if a problem surfaces before the balance is paid there is room to renegotiate or unwind. A court auction has no such counterparty. The sale is one part of an execution procedure run by the court, and the winning bidder is a participant in a procedure, not a party to a negotiation.

  • You take it as it stands. The property is sold in its current condition. There is nobody to ask for a reduction over a defect discovered after the win
  • Warranty claims are effectively limited. You cannot litigate a defect the way you would under an ordinary sale contract. How far you can dispute at all varies by case and needs professional confirmation
  • You cannot walk away because you changed your mind. Once you submit a bid form and become the highest bidder, the procedure moves only along the track the statute lays down
  • Failing to pay can cost you the guarantee you deposited. Re-sale is governed by Civil Execution Act art. 138. How the previous buyer's guarantee is treated must be confirmed in the statutory text itself
  • It arrives with occupants in place. Acquiring ownership and actually being able to use the space are two separate problems

This is where most accidents happen. The reasoning that says a cheap purchase can absorb a small loss only works if you know the maximum size of the loss, and leaving out an inherited right means bidding without knowing that maximum. Accepting an amount you have not measured is not buying cheaply.

2. The procedure - where you can still turn back and where you cannot

You do not need to memorise the procedure. You only need to know, at each stage, whether you are still free or already committed. The flow and the cited articles below follow the Ministry of Government Legislation guidance.

Three rows deserve attention. First, the documents go on file one week before the sale date, which means the research window is short. Second, submitting the bid form is the line: free before it, and only the statutory path after it. Third, the right is acquired when the price is paid in full, not when the registration is completed.

3. The heart of rights analysis - the baseline and the inherited rights

This is the centre of the article. There is a dividing line between the rights extinguished by the sale and the rights that pass to the buyer. Korean practice calls it the extinguishment baseline right.

The registration that sets the baseline. According to the Ministry of Government Legislation guidance, the baseline is the earliest registered item among a mortgage, a provisional attachment or attachment, and the auction commencement registration. Finding those three types on the register extract and picking the earliest receipt date is the first move of any rights analysis. The articles cited as the basis are Civil Execution Act arts. 88, 91, 135, 144 and 148 among others, while the nature of each individual right comes from the Civil Act and the various special statutes.

Once you have the baseline, you split what sits above it from what sits below. Anything registered after it is in principle extinguished, and anything before it may be inherited. That principle has exceptions, and some rights are inherited regardless of order entirely.

CategoryRightsWhat it means for the buyer
Extinguished as a ruleMortgage, provisional attachment or attachmentSettled through the distribution and erased from the register
Superficies or servitude registered after the baselineThe restriction on using the land falls away

Chonsegwon with a distribution demand filedSettled through the distribution
Provisional registration securing a claimBeing security, it is settled through the distribution

Statutory superficiesCan arise without registration, so you may win the land and be unable to touch the building on it
Customary grave site rightCan arise without registration, so use of the land can be restricted
Inherited on conditionSuperficies, servitude, chonsegwon or lease right registered before the baselineYou own the property carrying that burden

Chonsegwon with no distribution demandNot settled by the distribution, so it remains
Provisional disposition sought by a landowner to remove a buildingA signal that the building's very existence is being litigated

Read the table with one caution. It is a general classification, not the conclusion for any specific case. Rights carrying the same name are handled differently depending on registration timing, whether a distribution demand was filed, and the nature of the property. And where a cell says as a rule, that phrase means exceptions exist, not that they do not.

  • Pick the baseline wrong and every judgement beneath it is wrong too. The first move matters most
  • A provisional registration produces opposite conclusions depending on whether it secures a claim or preserves priority, and the label alone does not always distinguish them
  • For a chonsegwon the fork is whether a distribution demand was filed, which only the case file will tell you
  • For a provisional disposition you have to ask what it is trying to prohibit. One aimed at removing a building is a completely different animal
  • A right of retention, statutory superficies and a grave site right will not surface however carefully you read the register. Those three need a separate check of their own

4. Tenants with opposing power - how a deposit reaches the buyer

Back to the opening scene. The path by which a tenant's deposit reaches the winning bidder opens when three elements line up: opposing power, ranking, and distribution.

4.1 Opposing power comes from facts, not from registration

Under Housing Lease Protection Act art. 3 the requirements are two: delivery of the house and resident registration. Effect against third parties begins at midnight of the following day once both are satisfied. No separate registration is needed.

That single sentence carries enormous weight in an auction. Opposing power is not recorded on the register. However precisely you read the register extract, the tenant's move-in date is not in it. You have to find it through the statement of the property for sale, the on-site investigation report, and confirmation of registered households.

  • The midnight of the following day rule creates one-day differences that decide ranking contests
  • If a tenant moves out and moves back in, the guidance states that opposing power is acquired anew, which pushes the ranking back
  • For an apartment the building and unit number must be recorded exactly, while for a multi-household house the lot number alone is stated to suffice. An error in that entry risks losing opposing power
  • Commercial premises fall under the separate Commercial Building Lease Protection Act. Arts. 3, 5 and 8 are cited as the relevant provisions, and the thresholds for its application need separate confirmation

4.2 Priority repayment, distribution, and the part left over

Under art. 3-2 of the same Act, adding a fixed date stamp to the opposition requirements creates a priority repayment right, which allows the deposit to be recovered ahead of junior right holders. The guidance states that a fixed date stamp can be obtained at a town or township office, a community service centre, a district office branch, a district court or its branch, a registry office, or a notary (art. 3-6).

What the buyer should watch here is the part the distribution does not cover. If the tenant has opposing power and that opposing power predates the baseline, the unrecovered deposit can remain as the buyer's burden. Conversely, if the opposing power comes after the baseline, that lease right is settled on the extinguishment side.

  • The move-in date alone is not enough. The fixed date stamp and the distribution demand must be read together with it
  • Even with a fixed date stamp, missing the distribution deadline can shut the tenant out of the distribution
  • Even with a demand filed, senior right holders may leave nothing behind
  • A multi-household house with several tenants only becomes legible once every tenant is mapped on one page
  • For the tenant's side of deposit recovery, see the deposit refund lawsuit guide and lease renewal refusal grounds. This article covers only the buyer's verification items

5. Top-priority repayment - the front of the distribution queue

Small-deposit tenant protection rearranges the order of distribution. Housing Lease Protection Act art. 8 provides the basis, and the concrete figures are set by arts. 10 and 11 of the Enforcement Decree of the same Act. The reason this matters to a buyer is simple. The pot available for distribution is fixed, so a change in who takes the front of the queue leaves someone short at the back, and that shortfall can affect what gets inherited.

Do not treat the amounts as fixed. These thresholds differ by region and change when the Enforcement Decree is amended, and the applicable standard can itself differ depending on when the lease was signed and when the security interest was created. The figures below are the values printed on the Ministry of Government Legislation page read on 31 July 2026, which presented them on the basis of Presidential Decree No. 33254 of 21 February 2023. The current Enforcement Decree must be confirmed directly at the Korean Law Information Center.

RegionDeposit ceiling to qualify as a small-deposit tenantAmount repayable ahead of others
SeoulKRW 165 million or lessKRW 55 million
Overconcentration control region, Sejong, Yongin, Hwaseong, GimpoKRW 145 million or lessKRW 48 million
Metropolitan cities and similar areasKRW 85 million or lessKRW 28 million
ElsewhereKRW 75 million or lessKRW 25 million

More important than the figures are the requirements. The guidance attaches the following conditions.

  • The tenant must satisfy the opposition requirements, delivery of the house and resident registration, before the auction registration
  • Those requirements must continue to exist up to the distribution deadline
  • To exercise the right the tenant must file a demand for distribution with the execution court or notify the delinquency authority
  • Where the amount exceeds one half of the value of the housing, it is limited to that half
  • The cited provisions are Housing Lease Protection Act arts. 3 and 8, arts. 10 and 11 of its Enforcement Decree, and Civil Execution Act arts. 88 and 246

For a buyer the practical conclusion is one line. Estimate how the distribution schedule will fall, but never take comfort in that estimate. Distribution is confirmed by the court, and if the estimate is off, the difference can come back as an inherited burden.

6. What the register does not show - risks you verify on site

The reason the inherited-regardless-of-order row in the table above is frightening is that none of those items appear on the register. However carefully you read the register extract, they will not surface. You have to go to the site. And sometimes going to the site is still not enough.

TypeWhy it is not registeredWhy it is dangerousHow to check
Asserted right of retentionIt is not a registered rightStated to be inheritable regardless of registration order. The larger the claimed amount the heavier the burdenBanners, notices and changed locks at the site, entries in the statement of the property for sale, filings in the case record
Statutory superficiesIt can arise without registrationYou may win the land and be unable to remove the building on it, which changes the land's usefulness entirelyWhether land and building share an owner, which one is being sold, whether the building is registered
Customary grave site rightIt can arise without registrationUse of the land can be restricted, and a grave cannot simply be movedA site visit. It can exist even with no mention in the appraisal report
Unregistered buildingThere is no register at allWhether it forms part of the sale becomes disputable, and you may end up owning only the landNotes on structures outside the appraised scope, the investigation report, the building ledger
The real occupantOccupancy is not a registered matterA mismatch with the paperwork stretches out the handover and raises the costThe on-site investigation report, resident household confirmation, a site visit

The principle for all five is the same: distinguish what you confirmed to be absent from what you failed to check. Having gone and found nothing is a completely different state from never having gone, yet at the moment of writing the bid figure both tend to be filed under no problem.

  • An asserted right of retention complicates the procedure by the mere fact of assertion. Whether or not it is upheld, the handover takes time
  • Cases where land and building are sold separately have a completely different structure. Establish what is actually being sold first
  • A part of the investigation report marked impossible to investigate is a warning sign. It means the court could not confirm it either
  • Arrears on management fees and common areas are not rights but they still cost money, and need a separate check
  • Deciding from photographs and internet listings is not verification. Verification means going

7. Bidding in practice - the guarantee, the form, and an unrecoverable typo

Once the rights analysis is done, what remains is procedure. Accidents here have a different character. They come from a slipped hand, not from ignorance of the law, and the consequences are just as heavy.

7.1 Bidding methods

Under Civil Execution Act art. 103 the court chooses the sale method. The two forms described in the guidance are as follows.

  • Date bidding. You attend the court on the designated day, complete the bid form, and submit it to the execution officer together with the guarantee
  • Period bidding. Within a set window you complete the bid form, seal it in one envelope with the guarantee, and hand it to the execution officer or send it by registered mail
  • The execution officer opens the bids, and the name and price of the highest bidder are announced (Civil Execution Act art. 115, Civil Execution Rules arts. 65, 71)
  • The guidance states that the guarantee is returned immediately to those who did not win

7.2 The guarantee and the bid form

Items requiring your own verification. The amount and submission method of the purchase application guarantee, and the rules on completing and correcting the bid form, were not confirmed against the statutory text while writing this article. A commonly quoted ratio exists in practice, but it is not asserted here. There are two verification routes. First, the amount and method are stated in the sale notice for that case. Second, confirm the relevant articles of the Civil Execution Act and the Civil Execution Rules at the Korean Law Information Center, and the forms and procedure at the Supreme Court Electronic Civil Petition Center and Court Auction Information.

There is one structure you must understand about writing the bid amount. The figure on a submitted bid form is hard to take back. Why a misplaced digit that turns your intended figure into ten times that amount is catastrophic becomes clear from the following chain.

  • The mistaken figure is the highest bid, and you become the highest bidder
  • The sale is approved and a payment deadline is set (Civil Execution Act art. 142)
  • You cannot fund that amount, so you fail to pay
  • Non-payment sends the property to the next-highest bidder or to re-sale (Civil Execution Act art. 138)
  • How the guarantee already deposited is treated in that process must be confirmed in the text of art. 138 itself. This is where the outcome practitioners call forfeiture of the deposit comes from

Joint bids and proxy bids each have their own required documents and formalities. Those specific requirements were also not confirmed against statute for this article. Asking the relevant court in advance, or checking the Court Auction Information guidance, is the safe route. A single missing document on the day, invalidating the bid entirely, is a common occurrence.

8. After winning - payment, non-payment, delivery orders and handover

Winning is not the end but the start of a second procedure, and here too there are points you can still turn back from and points you cannot.

8.1 Payment and acquisition of the right

  • Once the approval decision is final, the price is paid by the deadline the court sets (Civil Execution Act art. 142)
  • The right that is the object of the sale is acquired when the price is paid in full (art. 135), not when registration is completed
  • After payment, the transfer of ownership and the cancellation of the rights not inherited are commissioned (art. 144)
  • Failure to pay within the deadline leads to approval for the next-highest bidder or a decision to re-sell
  • Even after a re-sale is ordered, the guidance states that paying the price with interest and procedural costs up to three days before the re-sale date can stop it. The interest rate is presented as 12 percent per annum (art. 138)

8.2 You cannot remove an occupant on your own

The core sentence of this section is this: acquiring ownership does not let you remove the occupant on your own. Opening the door, moving belongings out, or changing the locks is itself a legal problem. The statutory procedure has to be followed.

  • If the occupant refuses to hand over, the guidance states that an application for a delivery order may be made to the court within six months of paying the price (Civil Execution Act art. 136)
  • Missing that window forces you into a different procedure, so the schedule needs managing
  • A delivery order does not reach every occupant. An occupant holding a title that survives the sale can produce a different outcome, which needs professional confirmation
  • Handover costs time and money, and the inability to use the property during that period belongs in the burden as well
  • Where a tenant with opposing power remains, the question is not how to remove them but what to do about the deposit in the first place

9. Pre-bid checklist - what to read in each of the four documents

Here is everything above, reorganised by document. The knack is not reading the four separately but cross-reading them. An item present in one and absent from another is the warning sign.

DocumentWhere to get itWhat to look for

Statement of the property for saleThe court, on file from one week before the sale date (Civil Execution Act art. 105)Rights noted as possibly inherited, tenant move-in date, fixed date stamp, distribution demand, special sale conditions, any retention right filing
On-site investigation reportThe court, from the same dateThe occupancy the execution officer actually found, rent and deposit (Civil Execution Act art. 85), any part marked impossible to investigate
Appraisal reportThe court, from the same dateWhat was excluded, the valuation date, land and building separated, notes on structures outside the appraised scope (Civil Execution Act art. 97)

The combinations worth checking against each other are these.

  • Put the registration order from the register extract next to the tenant move-in date from the statement of the property for sale
  • Check whether the occupant in the statement matches the actual occupancy in the investigation report
  • Check whether the appraised object matches the building you saw on site
  • Wherever the word inherited appears in the statement, copy that sentence out whole and establish what it means
  • Case searches and sale dates are at Court Auction Information

### 1-3. Tenants

- [ ] I identified the occupant and whether that person is a tenant or the owner

- [ ] I actually went there (I did not decide from photographs and paperwork alone)

## 7. What not to do

- Judging from the gap between the appraised value and the minimum sale price — the risk is in the inherited rights, not the price
- Stopping at the register extract — a retention right, statutory superficies, grave site right, and unregistered buildings are not in it
- Not visiting the site — the real occupant can differ from the documents
- Skimming past the word inherited in the statement of the property for sale — that one line changes the number
- Checking the tenant move-in date and never checking the distribution demand
- Using an old source for the small-deposit tenant thresholds — they differ by region and are changed by enforcement decree
- Bidding without a funding plan — non-payment leads to re-sale
- Removing an occupant on your own after winning — the statutory procedure must be followed
- Bidding on the strength of this document alone — a specific property needs professional review

## 8. Points that are commonly misunderstood

- Opposing power comes from delivery of the house and resident registration, not from any registration, and it takes effect against third parties from midnight of the following day (Housing Lease Protection Act art. 3)
- Priority repayment right requires a fixed date stamp added to those two requirements (same Act art. 3-2)
- Even with opposing power, whatever the distribution does not cover can remain with the winning bidder
- Top-priority repayment for a small-deposit tenant requires the opposition requirements before the auction registration, and a demand for distribution (same Act art. 8)
- The top-priority amount is capped at one half of the value of the housing
- Commercial buildings fall under the separate Commercial Building Lease Protection Act (arts. 3, 5, 8)
- A delivery order can be applied for within six months of paying the price (Civil Execution Act art. 136)

  • The verification date is 31 July 2026

10.2 Not verified - items you must confirm yourself

  • The text of each article and its paragraphs. Article numbers and titles were confirmed, but the provisions themselves were not opened. Searching by statute name and article number at the Korean Law Information Center gives the text and the amendment history
  • The basis and submission method for the purchase application guarantee. It appears in the sale notice for the case, and the underlying provisions are in the Civil Execution Act and the Civil Execution Rules
  • How the bid form is completed and whether it can be corrected. Forms and drafting guidance are at the Supreme Court Electronic Civil Petition Center and Court Auction Information
  • The treatment of a deposited guarantee on non-payment. The text of Civil Execution Act art. 138 must be read directly
  • Documents required for joint and proxy bids. Asking the relevant court in advance is the reliable route
  • The length of the payment deadline. The court sets it case by case, so the decision and the notice must be checked
  • The scope of application of the Commercial Building Lease Protection Act. Whether it applies turns on deposit size, so the current Act and its Enforcement Decree must be checked directly
  • Case law. This article cites no court decisions, because reproducing a decision whose case number could not be confirmed would itself be an error. Areas where accumulated case law is decisive, such as rights of retention and statutory superficies, absolutely require professional review
