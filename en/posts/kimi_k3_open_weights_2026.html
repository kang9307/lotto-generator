Kimi K3 Open Weights - Can You Actually Deploy a 2.8T-Parameter Model

License terms, VRAM math and total cost - the things a deployment decision needs, not the things a press release contains

Jul 28, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: the gap between released and usable

In late July 2026, Moonshot AI made the weights for Kimi K3 available for public download. The headline number is 2.8 trillion parameters, an unusual scale for weights anyone can fetch, and that number is what filled most of the coverage.

From an adoption standpoint, though, the parameter count is close to the least useful piece of information on the page. A practitioner actually has to answer four questions. First, does the license let our company use this? Second, will it even run on the hardware we can get? Third, which workloads does it help and which does it hurt? Fourth, is it genuinely cheaper than paying for a commercial API? Without answers to those four, it does not matter whether the number is 2.8 trillion or twenty-eight trillion.

This article covers only those four questions. Rather than listing launch facts, it works from what is written in the model card and the license text, and it says plainly where something could not be confirmed. In particular, every figure is labelled with who measured it, because a vendor self-reported number and an independent evaluation carry very different weight.

One note on scope: the previous generation was already covered in our Kimi K2.7-Code analysis, so that ground is not repeated here. The policy and ecosystem debate around open-weight models lives in open-weight AI regulation and the Kubernetes moment for open weights. What follows is aimed squarely at the information one organisation needs to decide whether to adopt this model. For generational positioning, the AI model comparison hub is a useful companion.

1. What K3 actually is - the specifications released

Every figure below comes from the official Moonshot AI model card published on Hugging Face. These are taken from the distribution page itself, not from press coverage or secondary summaries.

1.1 Model structure

K3 is a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model. Total parameters come to 2.8 trillion, but only 104 billion participate in the computation for any single token. There are 896 experts in total, 16 are selected per token, and two shared experts are always active on top of that.

  • Total parameters: 2.8 trillion (2.8T)
  • Activated per token: 104 billion (104B)
  • Expert configuration: 16 of 896 selected per token, plus 2 shared experts
  • Layers: 93 in total (69 Kimi Delta Attention + 24 Gated MLA)
  • Context window: 1,048,576 tokens (one million class)
  • Vision encoder: MoonViT-V2, roughly 401 million parameters
  • Vocabulary size: 160K
  • Activation function: SiTU-GLU

The operationally meaningful number here is not 2.8 trillion but 104B, because activated parameters drive compute and response speed. Total parameters, by contrast, drive memory. In other words K3 consumes memory like a 2.8-trillion model while computing like a 104-billion one. That asymmetry dominates everything in the hardware discussion that follows.

1.2 Attention design - KDA and Gated MLA

The model card describes attention as a blend of Kimi Delta Attention (KDA) and Gated MLA. Of the 93 layers, 69 are KDA and 24 are Gated MLA. Stacking mostly linear-attention layers and mixing in a smaller number of global-attention layers reads as a design intended to contain the KV cache explosion that a million-token context otherwise produces.

That said, no independent verification of how well this works could be found. The architecture names and layer split are facts stated in the model card, but how much they contribute to long-context quality is a separate question. If long context is central to your plan, measuring recall on your own data is the safer route.

1.3 The MXFP4-native decision

This is the most operationally significant item on the card. K3 is distributed with MXFP4 weights and MXFP8 activations, and the card attributes this to quantization-aware training.

The distinction matters. Open-weight models are usually distributed at higher precision such as BF16, and users compress them to four bits afterwards. That post-hoc quantization typically costs some quality. K3 instead ships already trained with four-bit precision in mind. There is no separate quantization step for the user to perform, and the degradation risk that normally accompanies post-hoc compression is correspondingly reduced.

The side effect is footprint. Four bits per parameter is half a byte per parameter, so 2.8 trillion parameters occupy roughly 1.4 TB of weights. The same model distributed at FP16 would have been 5.6 TB. (Both figures are the author's own arithmetic from parameter count and bit width, not values printed on the model card.) Cutting the requirement to a quarter is welcome, but as the next section shows, 1.4 TB is still not a small number.

1.4 What changed from the K2 generation

Moonshot states that the new architectural components deliver a 2.5x improvement in overall scaling efficiency over K2. That is a vendor claim and has not been reproduced by a third party. The changes users are more likely to feel are the ones below.

ItemKimi K2.7-Code (previous line)Kimi K3

The biggest practical change is that activated parameters grew from 32 billion to 104 billion, more than tripling. Quality goes up, but per-token compute rises with it. K3 is therefore not lighter than its predecessor. A team running K2-class models to keep a coding agent cheap could see costs increase by switching straight to K3. A newer generation is not automatically a drop-in upgrade.

There is a second consideration: thinking mode cannot be turned off. The model card states that K3 always reasons and returns reasoning_content, with effort adjustable across low, high and max. Attach it to short classification or extraction jobs and you will keep paying for reasoning tokens you did not need, which makes this a real factor in workload placement.

2. Reading the license - open weight is not open source

This is the most important section of the article, because internal adoption reviews rarely stall on performance. They stall on licensing.

2.1 What the Kimi K3 License requires

The K3 code and weights ship under a custom license called the Kimi K3 License. It is not a standard license like MIT but a permissive core with several conditions bolted on. The following was confirmed against the license text itself.

  • Core grant: use, copying, modification, merging, publication, distribution, sublicensing and sale are permitted. Running, deploying, fine-tuning and creating derivative works are explicitly allowed.
  • Notice retention: the copyright notice must be included in all copies or substantial portions of the software. This is the baseline obligation.
  • Separate agreement at scale: if you offer the model as a service and aggregate revenue exceeds 20 million US dollars over any consecutive twelve months, you must enter a separate agreement with Moonshot AI before that commercial use.
  • UI attribution: for commercial products or services, including derivative works, above 100 million monthly active users or 20 million US dollars in monthly revenue, Kimi K3 must be prominently displayed in the user interface.
  • Exemptions: internal use, and access through official Moonshot AI products or certified partners, are carved out from both conditions above.

In short: for most organisations these conditions are effectively harmless. Twenty million dollars in monthly revenue or 100 million monthly active users is a threshold the vast majority never approach, and purely internal use is exempt outright. But having no conditions and having conditions that happen not to bind you are completely different things to a legal reviewer. The latter needs to be documented.

2.2 Four things to check before internal adoption

If you take the review forward, these are worth putting in writing.

  1. Is our usage internal, or an external service? A tool used only by employees likely falls under the exemption. The moment it ships inside a customer-facing product, the revenue and MAU thresholds become something you have to keep tracking.
  2. Does the roadmap cross a threshold? You may be clear today and over the line in three years. Knowing at design time that you will need UI attribution or a separate agreement is far better than discovering it later.
  3. How are fine-tuned artefacts treated? Derivative works are permitted, but the review should assume the same conditions travel with them. That matters especially if you intend to sell a model tuned on internal data.
  4. Where does the data go? This is a security question rather than a licensing one. Downloading weights and running them internally keeps prompts inside your network. Calling Moonshot's hosted API for convenience does not. Using an open-weight model does not by itself guarantee data sovereignty - self-hosting as a deployment choice is what guarantees it.

One more practical caveat: the summary above is the author's reading of the license text. Contractual decisions should rest on the original document rather than a summary, and any organisation plausibly near the monetary thresholds should route this through legal review. License terms can be revised, so check the version in force at the time of your review. Figures here are current as of July 2026.

2.3 Why calling it open source is a problem

Press and community usage blurs the two terms, but an adoption review needs them separated.

  • Open source conventionally follows the OSI definition, whose core requirement is no discrimination by field of use, by person, or by scale.
  • Open weight describes only the distribution method: the trained parameters can be downloaded. Conditions vary by license.
  • The K3 license varies obligations by revenue and MAU. At that point it departs from the OSI sense of open source.
  • In addition, training data and training code are not part of the release, so transparency for reproduction or bias auditing is not available.

This is not pedantry. Writing open source in a public sector or financial procurement document and having the license conditions surface later is an avoidable problem. Writing open-weight model under a custom license is the accurate and safer description.

This is the part of any model launch that deserves the most care. The tables below name the measuring party explicitly.

3.2 Third-party measurement

The independent evaluator Artificial Analysis reported the following. These carry different weight because an outside organisation ran multiple models under common conditions.

  • Intelligence index score of 57, placing it third at the time of publication, behind Claude Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol.
  • Artificial Analysis characterised K3 as at the top tier among open-weight models.
  • Across all nine evaluations it consumed 132 million output tokens, a 21 percent reduction versus the previous generation.
  • Average cost per task was calculated at 0.94 US dollars.

One caution applies. Indices like this reshuffle whenever the version is updated and new models are added, and later tabulations do show different scores and placements. Citing third place as a fixed fact is therefore fragile; understanding it as near-frontier among downloadable models will age considerably better.

3.3 Do not read only the flattering numbers - hallucination regressed

The same evaluator flagged one negative result, and for adoption purposes it may be the more important one: the hallucination rate rose from 39 percent in the previous generation to 51 percent. Accuracy metrics improved while the hallucination metric moved backwards.

That combination produces a specific failure mode in practice. The model is better at solving hard problems while being worse at admitting what it does not know. When writing code or driving a search agent, running the result exposes what is wrong, so the risk is largely absorbed. In factual summarisation, research assistance or customer-facing responses - anywhere the output is believed directly by a person - the risk is fully exposed. This is the central basis for the fit and non-fit judgements later in this article.

To be explicit: a benchmark advantage does not guarantee quality on your workload. For any model, there is no substitute for running a small evaluation set built from your own data.

4. What it takes to actually run it - starting with VRAM math

Many reviews end right here. The model card does not state minimum hardware requirements. So what follows is the author's own calculation, shown with its working so readers can check it. This is arithmetic, not a citation.

4.1 Calculating the weight footprint

The calculation is simple: multiply the number of parameters by the bytes each one occupies.

  • At MXFP4 (4-bit): 2.8 trillion x 0.5 bytes = approximately 1.4 TB
  • For reference, at FP8 it would be 2.8 trillion x 1 byte = 2.8 TB
  • For reference, at FP16 it would be 2.8 trillion x 2 bytes = 5.6 TB
  • So MXFP4-native distribution cuts the memory requirement to a quarter of a high-precision release

Now that 1.4 TB has to be spread across accelerators. On 80 GB class devices, 1.4 TB divided by 80 GB is about eighteen cards. Eighteen just to hold the weights, before KV cache, activations and framework overhead. Converted to eight-card nodes, three nodes or more is the starting line.

Note that this is a theoretical floor. Real deployments lose several gigabytes per device to tensor-parallel communication buffers, CUDA context, scheduler memory and fragmentation, none of which touch a parameter. Sizing with no headroom commonly produces a cluster that will not even start.

For what it is worth, a number of pages online suggest K3 can be run on a single server or five to six GPUs. That does not reconcile with the arithmetic above: 2.8 trillion parameters at four bits is 1.4 TB, which does not physically fit in six 80 GB cards (480 GB). Budget planning based on such sources is inadvisable.

4.2 KV cache and context - the price of a million tokens

Loading the weights is not the end of it. The KV cache remains, and it grows with the number of concurrent sessions and the context length of each.

  • A million-token context means you can use it, not that it is free.
  • A handful of concurrent long-context sessions can push the KV cache into the hundreds of gigabytes.
  • Capacity planning must therefore be weights + (concurrent sessions x context length per session).
  • Check whether your serving stack can compress the KV cache, for example to FP8, which buys real headroom.
  • K3's KDA-heavy hybrid design is aimed at reducing this burden, but the specific reduction could not be confirmed.

A more practical approach is this: do not size for the maximum context, measure your actual request length distribution and size for the 95th percentile. If million-token requests are one percent of traffic, scaling the entire cluster for that one percent is irrational. Routing those requests to a separate queue is cheaper.

4.3 The serving stack

The model card names vLLM, SGLang and TokenSpeed as inference engines, and states that an OpenAI/Anthropic-compatible API format is available.

  • MoE routing support is mandatory. Handling 896 experts requires expert parallelism in the engine.
  • Verify MXFP4 support at the engine version level. New formats generally only work above a specific release.
  • The compatible API format is a genuine practical advantage: existing OpenAI SDK code can often be pointed at it with little more than an endpoint change.
  • Because this is a multi-node deployment, inter-node interconnect bandwidth governs performance. Standard Ethernet can degrade token generation substantially.
  • Thinking is always on, so responses include reasoning_content. Existing parsers that cannot handle it will need work at integration time.

In summary, self-hosting K3 is not the act of downloading a model but the act of operating a small GPU cluster. That sentence is the realistic decision point in most reviews.

5. Judging total cost - self-hosting versus a commercial API

The fact that weights are public does not make them cheap. This section establishes the criteria.

5.1 The API price coordinates

Artificial Analysis lists the following for the Kimi API. (Current as of July 2026; pricing varies by provider and over time.)

  • Input: 3 US dollars per million tokens
  • Output: 15 US dollars per million tokens
  • Cached input: 0.30 US dollars per million tokens (a 90 percent discount)
  • Blended rate: 2.31 US dollars per million tokens (assuming a 7:2:1 cache, input and output mix)

One point deserves attention. The same evaluator assessed K3 as particularly expensive compared with other open-weight models. The common assumption that open weight equals low cost does not transfer to K3. With 104 billion activated parameters the serving cost is intrinsically high, so this is unsurprising. If low unit cost is the goal, an open-weight model with fewer activated parameters may be the better candidate.

The speed profile is also on record. The same evaluator measured output at 33.3 tokens per second and time to first token at roughly 148 seconds. The latter looks alarming, but the natural reading is that thinking is always on and the model reasons before responding. The practical implication is that this is a difficult latency profile to place behind a conversational UI.

5.2 How to think about the break-even

Whether self-hosting or the API is cheaper is decided by utilisation. The framework looks like this.

  • Self-hosting is a fixed cost. GPU rental, power and floor space are charged whether or not any requests arrive.
  • API spend is a variable cost. If you do not use it, it is zero.
  • Break-even is therefore set by the monthly token volume you can push through without leaving the cluster idle.
  • Conversational services with daytime peaks and empty nights have low utilisation, which works against self-hosting.
  • Bulk document processing that can fill an overnight queue can hold utilisation near 100 percent, which works in its favour.
Decision axisCommercial API winsSelf-hosting wins
Monthly volumeLow or highly variableLarge and predictable

5.3 Costs that are easy to leave out

Self-hosting estimates come in low mostly because the following are missing.

  • Staffing: a multi-node inference cluster needs an owner. That salary can exceed the GPU rental line.
  • Incident response: lose one node and the whole model goes down. Redundancy means two clusters.
  • Upgrade cost: when a better model arrives in six months, the cluster design is revisited. With an API you change a string.
  • Early inefficiency: before serving parameters are tuned, throughput often lands under half of expectations.
  • Power and cooling: on-premises, floor space and electricity are separate line items.

The realistic compromise is hybrid: self-host only the workloads carrying sensitive data and send the rest to an API. Because K3 exposes an OpenAI-compatible format, handling both paths from the same code is comparatively straightforward. Running the numbers for your volume with an LLM API price calculator first is a good way to build intuition for where break-even sits.

6. Which workloads fit, and which do not

Putting the evidence together yields a placement decision. Three facts drive it: thinking cannot be disabled, a third party flagged a regression in hallucination, and activated parameters are large enough that unit cost is not low.

6.1 Good fits

  • Multi-step agentic work: reported strength on tool-use and browsing benchmarks, and consistent with a design where reasoning is always engaged.
  • Long-horizon coding tasks: planning, editing and verifying across many files. Because results get executed, hallucination risk is absorbed.
  • Large document and codebase analysis: one of the few uses that genuinely needs a million-token context.
  • Document image processing: native vision, with document vision benchmarks reported at the top of the set.
  • Data that cannot leave the building: the ability to download weights and run them internally is decisive when there is no alternative.
  • Overnight batch pipelines: latency is not an issue and utilisation can be filled.

6.2 Poor fits, or cases needing caution

  • Simple classification, extraction and labelling: reasoning cannot be switched off, so unnecessary tokens accrue continuously. A small model is far cheaper.
  • Conversational UIs needing instant responses: measured time to first token is long, which is punishing on a screen where someone is waiting.
  • Unverified factual statements shown directly to users: given the hallucination regression, citation and verification steps must be designed in.
  • High-volume simple work where cost minimisation dominates: this is not a low unit cost option even among open-weight models.
  • A small team's first self-hosting attempt: multi-node MoE serving is difficult. Build operational experience on a smaller model first.
  • Procurement documents that must record open source: the license conditions above mean the wording has to be precise.

7. An adoption decision checklist

If you are running a real review, this order works well. Move down the list and stop wherever you get blocked.

  1. Would an API be sufficient? If yes, there is no reason to download weights. Self-hosting is a means, not an objective.
  2. Is there data that cannot be exported? If so, self-hosting is justified on its own terms. If not, decide on cost alone.
  3. Do the license thresholds apply? Document whether use is internal or external and whether the roadmap crosses the revenue or MAU lines.
  4. Can you obtain hardware to hold 1.4 TB? If not, the review ends here. Switch the shortlist to smaller open-weight models.
  5. Does the serving stack support MXFP4 and MoE routing? Verify down to the engine version.
  6. Have you measured quality on your own evaluation set? Thirty to a hundred items from your own workload will show the direction. Do not substitute a benchmark ranking.
  7. Is there enough volume to fill utilisation? If not, the API is cheaper.
  8. Is there a named owner for operations and incidents? A cluster started without one eventually gets abandoned.

Starting from 2.8 trillion parameters are public so let us grab it without clearing those eight usually means turning back at the hardware quotation stage.

8. Limitations and open questions

Here is what could not be confirmed while writing this. Leaving gaps open is preferable to filling them with estimates.

  • Official minimum hardware requirements: not stated on the model card. The 1.4 TB figure in this article is the author's calculation from parameter count and bit width.
  • Measured throughput: no reliable primary source was found for tokens per second or concurrent session counts on a specific hardware configuration.
  • Quantified KV savings from the KDA design: the architecture split is published, but the magnitude of the saving could not be verified.
  • Practical fine-tuning difficulty: permission to create derivative works and the practical ability to tune a model this size are different matters. No official guidance on required resources was found.
  • Korean-language performance: published benchmarks are largely English-centric. Quality on Korean workloads needs independent validation.
  • Current index standing: evaluation indices reshuffle as versions update. Figures here reflect the time of publication.

One further observation. As the scale race continues, the effective accessibility of open weights is arguably declining. When models in the tens of billions were being released, a well-equipped workstation could run them. At 2.8 trillion parameters that is no longer true. Anyone can download it; the set who can run it narrows to organisations able to operate multi-node clusters. How that shift changes the meaning of openness is explored further in open-weight regulation and the open-weight ecosystem analysis.

Conclusion - reduced to three sentences

First, Kimi K3 is open weight, not open source. Its custom license attaches conditions keyed to revenue and user scale. Harmless for most organisations, but procurement documents and legal reviews should say open-weight model under a custom license, precisely.

Second, released and runnable are different things. Even after MXFP4-native distribution cut the footprint to a quarter, the weights alone come to roughly 1.4 TB - about eighteen 80 GB accelerators, with multi-node cluster operations as the premise. If you stop here, the rest of the discussion is moot.

Third, a newer generation is not automatically an upgrade. Activated parameters more than tripled, thinking cannot be disabled, and third-party measurement shows hallucination moving in the wrong direction. It is a strong candidate for multi-step agents, long-horizon coding and large document analysis, and an overpowered, slow choice for simple classification or instant conversational service. The rule that this is a workload placement exercise rather than a model selection exercise applies here as it always does.

A closing note on method. For any model, the adoption call should come from a small evaluation set built from your own work, not from a benchmark ranking. Thirty to a hundred items is enough to see the direction. Use launch figures to narrow the shortlist and nothing more; let the final decision rest on numbers you measured yourself.
