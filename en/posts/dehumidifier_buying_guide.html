The Complete Dehumidifier Buying Guide — Types, Capacity, Electricity Cost

Compressor vs desiccant compared, how to calculate capacity by room size, key specs, and electricity cost math — a practical buying guide as of July 2026

2026-07-24 Life & Practical

Introduction: What Should Actually Decide Your Dehumidifier Pick

When the rainy season or a damp shoulder season rolls around, the musty smell from the closet, water stains from condensation on the wallpaper, and laundry that just won't dry send a lot of people shopping for a dehumidifier. Once you're actually browsing a store or a shopping site, though, terms like "compressor type," "desiccant type," "inverter," "16L," and "18L" pile up fast, and it's easy to lose track of what actually matters.

This guide walks through, step by step, the operating principle behind each dehumidifying method, how to calculate the right capacity for your space, the key specs worth checking before you buy, how to estimate the electricity cost, day-to-day usage and maintenance tips, and when to reach for a dehumidifier versus an air conditioner's dry mode versus a passive desiccant packet — based on general practical information as of July 2026. It doesn't recommend any specific brand or model; the goal is to give you criteria you can apply yourself. Exact wattage, pricing, and specs vary by product and over time, so always verify the manufacturer's current official specs before you buy.

1. Dehumidifying Methods: Compressor vs Desiccant/Zeolite

The core of any dehumidifier is how it pulls water out of the air. Residential dehumidifiers split into two main categories: compressor (refrigerant) type and desiccant (adsorbent/zeolite) type.

1.1 How a compressor dehumidifier works

A compressor dehumidifier works on the same principle as an air conditioner. Refrigerant is compressed and expanded to cool a set of coils (the evaporator); humid air passing over the cold coils condenses into water droplets. The compressor is the core component, hence the name.

1.2 How a desiccant (zeolite) dehumidifier works

A desiccant dehumidifier uses an adsorbent material such as silica gel or zeolite mounted on a rotating wheel. The wheel adsorbs moisture from the air, and a heater then regenerates the material, driving the moisture off so it can be condensed and collected as water. There's no compressor — a heater does the work instead.

1.3 Ideal temperature range - where each type performs best

The biggest practical difference between the two is the temperature range they perform well in. Compressor units generally work efficiently above roughly 18-20°C, but at lower temperatures, frost can form on the cold coils — a phenomenon that reduces dehumidifying efficiency. Desiccant units, being heater-based, are reported to stay relatively stable at low temperatures, which is why they're often favored for unheated balconies, storage rooms, semi-basements, or humidity control during a chilly shoulder season or winter.

1.4 Electricity cost and noise compared

Compressor units generally draw less power, so they tend to cost less to run, but the compressor's operation produces relatively more vibration and noise. Desiccant units, having no compressor, run more quietly, but the heater needed to regenerate the adsorbent means higher power draw and warm exhaust air. For a bedroom or study where quiet matters, desiccant has the edge; for drying out a larger space quickly while keeping the electricity bill down, compressor tends to win.

2. Choosing Capacity: Calculating by Room Size and Humidity

Dehumidifier capacity is expressed as daily dehumidification volume (liters per day) — the maximum amount of water the unit can pull from the air in 24 hours. The higher the number, the better suited it is to a larger space or a more humid environment.

2.1 A standard formula

A commonly cited baseline is floor area (pyeong) × 0.76L for apartments and × 1.02L for detached houses — detached houses use a larger multiplier because they're generally less airtight than apartments, letting in more ambient moisture. For a simpler practical estimate, floor area × 0.6-0.8L for a typical room, or floor area × 0.8-1.0L if the space is unusually humid or will double as a laundry-drying room, is also widely used. (Note: 1 pyeong ≈ 3.3 sqm.)

These calculations roughly line up with typical product lineups. The table below is a reference guide to commonly chosen capacity tiers by space type — since manufacturers state their own recommended coverage differently, always confirm the official spec before you decide.

2.3 Problems with undersizing and oversizing

If capacity is too small, the unit can never reach the target humidity and runs at full power nonstop — burning more electricity, wearing down components faster, and keeping the noise going continuously, which can be more uncomfortable than not having a target reached at all. If capacity is too large, you pay more upfront and it takes up more space, and it may reach the target humidity so quickly that it cycles on and off in short bursts — a pattern that's generally inefficient and can shorten compressor life. Calculate capacity for your actual space, and if it's an especially humid season or you plan to use it for laundry drying too, it's generally sensible to size up one tier.

3. Key Specs Checklist

Even at the same capacity, real-world satisfaction depends heavily on the finer specs. Check the following before you buy.

3.1 Water tank size vs continuous drainage

A small built-in tank may need to be emptied several times a day during a heavy humidity stretch like the rainy season. A continuous drainage feature, on the other hand, lets you connect a hose to a bathroom drain or balcony drain so the unit can run for extended periods — even unattended — without ever needing the tank emptied. Whether continuous drainage is supported can make a real difference in convenience if you'll be away from home for a while or managing humidity through a long rainy stretch.

3.2 Energy efficiency rating

Dehumidifiers sold domestically are labeled on a 1-to-5 efficiency scale, with grade 1 indicating the lowest power draw for a given dehumidification volume. A 16-18L, grade-1 unit, for example, is commonly reported to draw roughly 250-300W, and under a standard usage benchmark (171 hours a month), the monthly electricity cost difference between a grade-1 and a grade-3 unit is reported at around 3,000-4,000 KRW. If you plan to run it often over a long period, it can be worth paying more upfront for a higher efficiency grade.

3.3 Noise level (dB)

A dehumidifier is generally considered comfortably quiet at 40dB or below. For reference, 20dB is roughly a whisper, 30-40dB is about as quiet as a library, and 40-50dB is roughly typical office noise. If you plan to use it in a bedroom or study where quiet matters, be sure to check both the rated noise level and whether the unit has a low-noise (sleep) mode.

3.4 Portability (weight and wheels)

If you plan to move the unit between rooms, check its weight, whether it has wheels, and where the handle is positioned. Larger-capacity units tend to be heavier, so how much you'll actually move it around should factor into your priorities.

3.5 Automatic humidity control

Units with a built-in humidity sensor let you set a target humidity (for example, 50-60%); the unit stops automatically once it hits that level and restarts if humidity climbs again. This isn't just convenient — it also prevents both wasted electricity from unnecessary overrunning and over-drying the space.

3.6 Filter and cleaning convenience

The intake on a dehumidifier has a dust filter. As dust accumulates, it adds load to the fan, increasing noise and reducing dehumidifying efficiency, so regular cleaning matters. Whether the filter is washable and how easy it is to remove and reinstall varies by product, so check the manual.

4. Extra Features: Laundry Drying Mode and Air Purifier Combo

Newer dehumidifiers add a range of extra features — it's worth being clear-eyed about how useful each one actually is.

4.1 Laundry drying mode

Laundry drying mode combines strong airflow with dehumidification to shorten the drying time for clothes hung indoors and reduce that musty indoor-dried smell. It's genuinely useful during the rainy season, but it's more accurate to think of it as assisting indoor drying rather than fully replacing a dedicated clothes dryer.

4.2 Air purifier combo

Some units add a HEPA or similar filter to dehumidify and purify the air at the same time. This saves space and budget, but each function can be relatively more limited than a dedicated single-purpose product. If air quality is your top priority, a standalone air purifier may serve you better; if space is tight or you want both functions at a moderate level, a combo unit can be a reasonable choice.

5. How to Calculate Electricity Cost

You can estimate a dehumidifier's running cost yourself with the formula below.

Daily usage (kWh) = wattage (W) ÷ 1000 × hours used per day
Monthly usage (kWh) = daily usage × days used
Monthly cost = monthly usage (kWh) × electricity rate (per kWh)

For example, take a 16L, grade-1 unit (roughly 270W) run 8 hours a day: daily usage is 270 × 8 ÷ 1000 = about 2.16 kWh, and over a month (30 days) that's roughly 65 kWh. For a typical household, this level of usage is commonly cited as costing around 7,000-9,000 KRW a month. The actual bill, though, depends on which tier of your household's overall tiered electricity rate this additional usage falls into alongside your other appliances — so it's worth checking your total monthly usage bracket for an accurate figure.

6. Usage and Maintenance Tips

6.1 Target 50-60% humidity

A comfortable indoor humidity range is generally cited as 40-60%, and for periods where humidity control really matters — summer and the rainy season especially — 50-60% is commonly recommended as the target. Mold is reported to begin growing once humidity passes 60%, and mold spores become notably more active above 70%, so the key is keeping humidity from climbing past that 60% mark.

6.2 Keep doors closed while running it

Close the doors and windows of the space you're dehumidifying to keep humid outside air from getting in — this lets the unit reach the target humidity faster and more efficiently. Running it with doors open can leave the unit working at full power indefinitely, wasting electricity for little gain.

6.3 Filter cleaning schedule

Check the dust filter on the manufacturer's recommended schedule, typically every two weeks to a month, and clean it right away if you notice visible dust buildup. A clogged filter adds load to the fan, which increases noise and reduces dehumidifying efficiency at the same time.

6.4 Preventing mold and odor

Standing water in the tank or internal pathways is an easy breeding ground for bacteria and mold, so empty and rinse the tank regularly and let it dry, even if it isn't full. Avoid reusing water that's been sitting in the tank for a while, and get in the habit of leaving the lid open to air-dry the interior after use — it goes a long way toward preventing odor and mold.

6.5 Winter storage

If the unit will sit unused over winter, empty the tank completely, wash it, and let everything dry thoroughly before storing it — this prevents mold and odor from developing inside. It's generally recommended to store the unit in a cool, dry spot under a dust cover, rather than somewhere that gets direct sunlight or a hot, humid storage room.

7. Dehumidifier vs AC Dry Mode vs Desiccant Packets - When to Use Which

These three work on entirely different principles and serve different purposes. Matching the right one to the situation is the sensible approach.

7.1 Different operating logic

A dehumidifier runs based on target humidity, continuing to dehumidify regardless of temperature until it hits that target (say, 50%). An air conditioner's dry mode, by contrast, runs based on target temperature — once it hits the set temperature (say, 25°C), it switches to fan mode, so humidity may not drop as much as you'd like. In one consumer test, five hours of AC dry mode brought humidity down only to around 59%, while a dedicated dehumidifier reportedly brought humidity down substantially further under similar conditions.

7.2 Electricity cost and indoor temperature

A dehumidifier handles both cooling and condensation inside a single unit with no outdoor compressor unit, which keeps running costs relatively low — roughly 7,000-9,000 KRW a month for a 16L-class unit run 8 hours a day. An air conditioner's dry mode, which also runs the outdoor unit, is reported to run as high as 20,000-30,000 KRW a month for the same usage pattern. That said, an air conditioner vents heat outside through its outdoor unit and keeps the room cool, while a dehumidifier exhausts warm air back into the room, which can nudge indoor temperature up a bit while it's running.

7.3 The role of desiccant packets

Desiccant packets — silica gel and similar materials — passively absorb moisture with no electricity, which makes them a good fit for small enclosed spaces like closets, shoe cabinets, and drawers. Since they can't actively circulate air, they aren't suited to lowering the humidity in a whole room or living room, and they need to be replaced periodically once their absorption capacity is used up.

  • Have you chosen compressor vs desiccant (zeolite) based on the season and temperature you'll mainly be using it in?
  • Have you checked the energy efficiency rating (the cost gap between grade 1 and lower grades adds up with heavy long-term use)?
  • Have you confirmed the tank size and whether continuous drainage is supported?
  • Have you checked the noise rating in dB (40dB or below is recommended for quiet spaces like bedrooms)?

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q. Should I buy a compressor or a desiccant dehumidifier?

Generally, a compressor unit is the better fit above roughly 18-20°C, since it draws less power and dehumidifies faster. If you'll mostly use it somewhere cold — winter rooms, a balcony, a storage room, or a semi-basement — a desiccant (zeolite) unit may be more suitable, since it stays stable at low temperature and runs quietly.

Q. What capacity dehumidifier does my home need?

A common baseline is floor area (pyeong) × 0.76L for apartments and × 1.02L for detached houses, or floor area × 0.6-0.8L for a typical room and × 0.8-1.0L for a humid space or laundry-drying use. Too small a unit runs nonstop; too large adds unnecessary upfront cost — so check the manufacturer's recommended coverage area too.

Q. How much does it cost to run a dehumidifier per month?

A 16L-class, grade-1 unit (roughly 270W) run 8 hours a day uses about 65 kWh a month, commonly cited at around 7,000-9,000 KRW for a typical household. The actual bill depends on which tier of your household's overall tiered electricity rate the usage falls into.

Q. Should I use a dehumidifier, an AC's dry mode, or a desiccant packet?

Use a dehumidifier when you need to actively lower humidity across a larger space, an AC's dry mode when you only need supplemental dehumidifying while already cooling, and a desiccant packet — no electricity needed — for small enclosed spaces like closets or shoe cabinets.

Q. How should I maintain a dehumidifier?

Check and clean the filter roughly every two weeks to a month, and empty and dry the tank regularly, since mold can start forming once humidity around it passes 60%. If it will go unused over winter, empty the tank, dry it thoroughly, and store the unit somewhere cool and dry.

Conclusion

Working through a dehumidifier purchase in order — method, capacity, then key specs — cuts your odds of buyer's remorse dramatically. Pick compressor or desiccant based on the season and temperature of the space you'll use it in, calculate capacity based on floor area and humidity level, then run through a checklist covering energy rating, noise, continuous drainage, and automatic humidity control. Add the basics — targeting 50-60% humidity, keeping doors closed while it runs, and regular filter and tank maintenance — and it'll serve you well for a long time.
