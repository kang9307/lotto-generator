What a Camera Signature Does Not Vouch For - The File Can Be Genuine While the Scene Is Not

Feed a fake input into a genuine pipeline and a genuine signature comes out

Aug 27, 2026 Tech & IT

A photo lands in your inbox. Provenance information is attached, you drop it into a verification tool, and a green check appears. For most people the judgment ends right there. Yet the sentence that green check is actually uttering is much shorter than it looks. This file was sealed under the name of a party holding a particular certificate, and no byte has changed since. That is the whole of it.

Nowhere in that sentence is there room for what stood in front of the lens. The people who wrote the specification have never hidden that this box is empty. The verification screen simply does not show the empty box, and human beings who see a green check will fill in the rest themselves. A recently published piece of security research put that empty box back on the table.

The idea that marks on an image split into three layers - file metadata, signed provenance, pixel watermarks - and that each layer disappears under different conditions was worked through separately in The Marks on a Generated Image. This article does not re-explain that layer structure. It takes the second layer alone, signed provenance, and pushes the question of what it vouches for and what it does not all the way down. Attack procedures and equipment setups are outside its scope.

This article explains the general principles of provenance signing. It is not a security assessment or a legal judgment about any specific product, device or vendor.

1. What was reported, and only that far

The trigger was an analysis published by a security researcher. The subject was the family of provenance signing camera applications running on a widely used mobile platform. The finding compresses into one sentence. Where the protections of the device itself have fallen, an image that was never captured can receive a valid provenance signature through that device's normal signing path.

The researcher framed the result not as a defect in one application but as something arising from a structure shared by this class of applications on that platform. The write-up also records that the report submitted was triaged as not fixable and that a bounty was paid. That is the extent of the public record.

This article does not go into how any of it is done or which devices were involved. The argument stands intact without those details. What makes this class of problem interesting is not the elegance of the method but the ordinariness of the result. It is not a forged signature. It is not a broken one. It is a grammatically complete signature that any verifier is right to accept.

Nor has the point been raised only once. A university lab talk reviewed both specification and implementation and flagged missing version information on claims, unclear criteria for identifying which manifest is active, inconsistent validation behaviour, and inadequate certificate revocation management. The focus differs, but the direction converges: the context around the signature is weaker than the signature itself.

2. What the signature vouches for and what it does not

Start here. What a digital signature confirms are facts about the thing signed, never facts about the world that thing depicts. This is not a limitation discovered by the recent research; it is baked into the definition of the tool.

The C2PA explainer does not dance around it. The text states that provenance information alone cannot tell you whether the digital content is true, accurate or factual. The technical specification nails it down once more at the level of design goals: the specifications should not provide value judgments about whether a given set of provenance data is good or bad, merely whether the assertions included within can be validated.

What a passing validation saysWhat it does not say
A party holding this certificate assembled this manifestThat the party is honest
No byte has changed since the signatureHow those bytes came to be beforehand
The assertions in the manifest were declared under that nameThat the content of those assertions is true
The entries left by editing tools chain togetherThat no unrecorded edit took place
This file came out of that capture deviceWhat actually stood in front of the lens

The last row is the axis of this entire article. The first four rows generally hold, and when they do not, validation surfaces the failure as a failure. The last row behaves differently. That box was never subject to inspection, so when it is wrong the validation still comes back as a success. Problems that announce themselves as failures and problems that disguise themselves as successes call for completely different handling.

One more relationship sits on top of this. The specification describes trust in the contents of a Content Credential as resting on the trust relationship between the creator and the consumer. A signature confirms a name; whether that name deserves belief is left to the verifier. The design is sound, but on a user-facing screen that layer is usually folded away.

3. A chain of trust breaks at its weakest link

Lay out, at the level of principle, the stages a photo passes through before it is signed, and the location of the problem becomes visible. Light reaches the sensor, the values the sensor produced travel through an image processing pipeline and are shaped into what you see on screen, an application takes that result and makes a file of it, a claim generator gathers the assertions into a bundle, and a signing key finally seals the bundle.

The signature attests only the last stage. Everything after the seal is defended strongly. Everything before it is not something the signature inspects; it is a premise. Premises are not inspected.

Stage in the chainWhat holds this stage upIf this stage gives way
Sensor to raw dataPhysical device integrityEverything downstream runs normally
Raw data to processing pipelinePlatform execution isolationThe signature attaches normally
Pipeline to applicationOperating system process boundariesValidation passes normally too
Application to claim generatorCode integrity inside the appOnly the manifest grows plausible
Claim to signing keyKey storage and access controlThis alone surfaces as a validation failure

There is a reason only the last row is marked as surfacing. Stealing and reusing a key has established countermeasures - revocation, rotation, withdrawal from a trust list. When the first four rows give way, nothing needs to be stolen. A genuine key signs through a genuine procedure. So this class of problem never looks like a broken signature; it looks like a signature working perfectly.

Blaming the specification here points in the wrong direction. The C2PA security considerations document states that attacks relying on bypassing the conventional security models of non-C2PA technology are considered out of scope. The specification never promised to hold up what the platform undertook to hold up. The same document asks only that claim generators follow best practices for securing keys in their respective operating environments.

The trouble is that where this boundary line runs is invisible to the person looking at the result. The specification honestly limited its own remit, the product was built on top of it, and the user sees only a finished green check. It is not the specification that folded the boundary away, but the presentation layered over it.

4. The analog hole - photographing a screen

The previous section was about the inside of a device. This one is about the outside, and it cuts deeper.

Put an image on a display and photograph it with a camera. The capture that just occurred is a real capture. Light really did pass through the lens, the sensor really did produce values, the device really did sign. A verifier is right to pass it. The signature did not lie by a hair. It is only that the scene it attested was some image that happened to be on a screen.

This is commonly called the analog hole: no matter how tightly the digital domain is controlled, the moment output leaves as light and sound it can be recaptured outside that control. The reasons it resists a principled fix run as follows.

  • The thing to be judged is optical, not digital. A signing system has no instrument that inspects the space in front of a lens
  • Attempts to detect recapture are statistical inference over traces peculiar to screens, not cryptographic determination. A confidence value always rides along
  • Those traces thin out with shooting conditions. Adjusting angle, distance, focus and exposure alone weakens the signal
  • Screens are not the only medium. Add prints, exhibits, projection surfaces and reflective surfaces and the very character of the traces changes
  • The cost of defence and evasion is asymmetric. A detector must withstand every case; evasion needs one case to pass

Tighten detection and legitimate captures start getting caught. A work photo of a meeting room display or a photograph of an artwork on show carries the very features of a recapture.

So the analog hole is less a problem not yet solved than territory outside what signing set out to cover. Trying to close a problem that lives outside with a tool that works inside means the harder you push the tool, the more false positives you get and the less real defence.

5. The limits of a hardware root of trust

A common objection arrives here. The key lives in secure hardware inside the device, and that fact can even be attested remotely, so what exactly is missing? The objection mixes something true with something assumed.

Take the true part first. Placing a key in secure hardware makes extracting the key itself difficult. Android's official documentation describes key attestation as giving more confidence that the keys an app uses are stored in a device's hardware-backed keystore, and says that with a trusted root you know the key is in hardware believed to be secure and has the properties described in the attestation certificate.

Now the assumed part. The subject of that sentence is the key. It is a statement about where the key sits and what properties it has, not a statement that the software asking it to sign is honest. Attestation attests what it set out to attest, and nothing beyond.

  • Vouched for - where the key lives, what properties it was generated with, whether the attestation chain leads to a trusted root
  • Not vouched for - where the signing request came from, whether the data in that request originated at a real sensor, whether the execution environment is intact at this moment
  • A question of timing - attestation speaks of a state at some point. The document remains valid even after the environment around it has moved on
  • Assume physical access and another axis appears entirely. The specific shape of that axis is not covered here

Reduced to one sentence: a hardware root of trust is strong at protecting a key and weak at protecting the context in which that key is used. And questions about whether a photograph is authentic almost always belong to the latter.

6. What an assurance level tells you and what it does not

C2PA runs a conformance program. It evaluates generator products, validator products and certification authorities against the specification, the certificate policy and a set of security requirements. It began with two assurance levels, and the higher one demands more rigorous controls including hardware-backed key storage. The level a product passed is carried as a field inside its claim signing certificate.

Two symmetric misreadings attach to this program. One side reads a level as a certificate of authenticity; the other, on seeing a report like this one, concludes the whole scheme is meaningless. Both misread what a level does.

  • A level does say - that this product passed a defined degree of scrutiny against stated security requirements
  • A level does say - that a comparable axis now exists between different products, and that interoperability is established
  • A level does not say - whether the platform the product sits on is intact at this moment
  • A level does not say - whether it still holds in light of problems surfaced after the evaluation
  • A level does not say - and obviously so, what stood in front of the lens

In fairness, an assurance program is a device for raising the floor of implementation quality. The average is plainly better with scrutiny than without. But scrutiny is always conducted at some point in time and within a defined scope, and a level does not answer for what happens beyond that scope. Concluding that the scene must be real because the top level was awarded asks the level for a promise it never made.

7. What signing still leaves behind

Reading this far and filing it under signing is useless would be its own misreading. Signing continues to do the job it set out to do. The task is to be precise about where that job sits.

  • Edit history - a chained record of which tool changed what, in what order. The path from original to present can be walked
  • Publisher identity - whose name the content went out under is bound to the file itself. For newsrooms, institutions and archives this is effectively the core of it
  • Integrity in transit - if content changed somewhere along the distribution path, the hard binding exposes it
  • Non-repudiation - the fact that a name was staked persists. Denying it later becomes harder
  • Attribution-free by design - the core specification does not require attribution to individuals or organizations, so it can remain maximally privacy-preserving. Attaching a name is a choice

Flip the direction and the picture tidies up. Signing is strong for saying that what I made is mine. It is weak for declaring that what someone else made is fake. The first use has no ready substitute; the second was never designed for. Most of the current confusion comes from pressing it into the second.

8. What the verifying side gets wrong

Honest technology with dishonest presentation produces the same outcome as dishonest technology. How a validation result is displayed decides roughly half of what this system achieves in practice.

Wording on screenTechnically true sentenceSentence the reader actually takes
VerifiedThe signature and binding matchThis photo is real
Captured with a cameraA capture assertion is present in the manifestThis scene actually happened
No provenance informationNo manifest was found in this fileThis photo is suspicious
No AI edits recordedThere are no recorded edit entriesIt was not edited

That third column filling up wrongly is a product of design, not of reader carelessness. People shown a short label complete the blanks themselves, so whoever builds the label must not leave blanks. The principles worth holding to in practice run about like this.

  • Make the file the subject. A sentence that opens with this file rather than this photo cuts the room for overreach dramatically
  • Avoid the green-or-red binary. A validation result is a set of items, not a pass and a fail
  • Say what was verified. Integrity confirmed, signer confirmed, capture assertion present is far more accurate than the single word verified
  • Put the signer's name up front. The grounds for trust end up being that name, so hiding it removes the material for judgment
  • Do not render absence as a negative verdict. No information and suspected forgery are entirely different states
  • Separate assertion from confirmation visually. An entry in a manifest is a claim by that party, not a finding by the system

Where the criteria for deciding which manifest is active are blurry, or where verifiers behave differently, the same file returns a different colour depending on the tool. To the user both results look equally definitive.

9. Look at a bundle, not a chain

A chain breaks at its weakest link. Hang a judgment on a single chain and the weakest point of that chain becomes the strength of the judgment. The remedy is not a sturdier chain but several strands bound into a bundle. A bundle survives the loss of a few strands.

  • Multiple sources - does material exist from another position, by another person, of the same event? Independent witness to a scene is a stronger signal than a lone signature
  • Internal consistency of context - do time, season, weather, shadow direction and surrounding structures agree with one another? Manipulation usually fails here first
  • Reverse search - has the same image appeared earlier in a different context?
  • Marks in other layers - is there a signal that survives apart from the file, such as a pixel watermark or a fingerprint? That each layer disappears under different conditions was covered in the earlier article
  • Ask the holder of the original - can the photographer or issuing body be asked for the original directly? The oldest method, still the strongest
  • Distribution path - where did it first appear and through which accounts did it spread?

Taken one at a time, every strand is weak. Reverse search is powerless against a freshly generated image, context checking is slow, and asking for an original only works if the other side answers. They gain strength only by overlapping. How many strands to demand follows from the weight of the matter. For everyday sharing one strand is enough; for material used as workplace evidence two is about right. For journalism, legal proceedings or archival records, demand three or more and record which strands did the confirming.

What could not be confirmed

The items below could not be grounded. They are not stated as fact in the body.

  • How far the reported findings have been reproduced or verified by third parties does not appear in public material. The body treats them only as reported
  • No official position document from the platform provider could be located. The account of how the report was handled is the researcher's own record
  • The conformance program's requirements were not compared item by item across levels. The description here stays at the level of the published overview
  • Quantitative performance figures for recapture detection are too condition-dependent to carry over as a single number
  • How differently major verification tools actually behave on the same file could not be established from any published comparison

What to watch next

This topic will diverge at a few points.

  • Whether the wording changes - turning the single word verified into an itemized statement would produce the largest practical shift. It is faster than fixing the technology and more effective
  • Whether execution state travels with the signature - carrying information about the platform state at signing time as one of the assertions, and letting the verifier weigh it, is worth debating. Though that information sits on the same chain
  • Whether recapture detection settles in as a secondary signal - presented as a confidence rather than a verdict, and used alongside other strands
  • Trust list and revocation practice - which signers go on a list and when they come off matters more in daily use than the specification does

Three sentences, then. A signature is a statement about a file, not a statement about the world. Hand the whole chain to a tool that proves only its last link and the problem arrives dressed not as a validation failure but as a validation success. And knowing that, signing remains a good tool - in its own place, which is edit history, publisher identity and integrity.
