A verdict reached before reading

You open a document. The cover fades from violet to blue. The subtitle sits centred under the title, and every entry in the table of contents carries an emoji. Four cards share one corner radius and one shadow strength. Not a single line of the body has been read. The verdict is already in.

The problem is not that this verdict is often right. The problem is that there is no chance to reverse it when it is wrong. Two months of work classified as something knocked out in an afternoon, because of one cover — and however accurate the numbers inside, nobody reaches them.

The common reaction is to say we should stop using AI. That misreads the cause. The AI look does not come from AI being involved; it comes from slots nobody decided being left as they were. A human using a template untouched produces the same impression, and a screen generated under real constraints does not read as generated at all.

1. What the AI tell actually is — nine places the eye lands first

Tell is a poker word: the place where a hand is readable from habit rather than from the cards. Design tells work the same way. Taken alone none of them is a flaw — each is a reasonable choice. The issue is that these choices always arrive together. When all nine slots hold defaults at once, people read that not as taste but as an absence of decisions.

SlotCommon defaultWhy it registersWhat to do instead
TypeOne geometric sans across headings, body and captionsA single voice, so hierarchy rests on weight aloneDifferent classifications for heading and body (serif + sans)
ColorViolet-to-blue linear gradient, several accents at similar saturationThe refinement signal inverted the moment it became ubiquitousOne neutral ramp plus one accent, used once per screen
Corner radiusThe same large radius on buttons, cards, inputs and imagesElement weight stops being legible; everything reads as one tierTwo or three tiers assigned by role (container > control > tag)
SpacingEvery gap identical, or a mechanical multiple-of-eight scaleThe eye cannot tell where one group endsMake between-group space clearly larger than within-group space
ShadowThe same soft, wide shadow on every cardDepth becomes decoration rather than informationOnly on things that should float (modals, dropdowns)
AlignmentCentering pushed down into body paragraphsThe left entry point moves, so the reading rhythm breaksCenter only text of two lines or fewer
EmojiOne in front of every heading and list itemRepeated without meaning, it works neither as ornament nor as dataOnly where states must be distinguished; otherwise color, icon or weight
ImageryRounded 3D blobs, frosted glass, stock illustration in one toneA visual language unrelated to the product occupies half the screenReal screens, real data, real diagrams. Leave it empty otherwise
CopyThree three-word imperatives in a row, perfectly parallel subheadsRhythm this even erases the irregularity of human sentencesMix sentence lengths. One concrete noun beats three adjectives

This table is not a penalty sheet. A screen with a gradient is not a bad screen. There is only one test.

  • Can you justify the choice in one sentence? If you can, it is a decision, not a tell.
  • If the answer is that the tool produced it that way, that slot is the one to clear.
  • Two or three defaults out of nine is ordinary. The impression only hardens when all nine are defaults.

2. Why those defaults — convergence on the mode

A generative model fills an unspecified slot with the mode of its training distribution. A brief that says modern and clean contains no decisions at all. Color, type and spacing are unstated, so the model averages what those words denote. An average is by definition the most common value.

For a single screen this is a good property. Averages rarely fail badly. The trouble is that the same average lands on thousands of screens simultaneously. What is a safe choice individually becomes a fingerprint collectively.

Elements that read as refined because they were scarce flipping into the opposite signal once they are common is a pattern design keeps repeating. The heavy textures of the early 2010s did it, and so did the extreme flatness that reacted against them. Today's violet gradient sits on the same curve. The only difference is speed of diffusion — a trend used to take years to spread, and now the same default is reproduced tens of thousands of times a day.

  • The model is not incapable of originality; it returns to the centre when it is given no direction for originality.
  • Piling adjectives into the prompt changes little. Bold and refined are still mood, not decisions.
  • What changes things is numbers and proper nouns. Three colors, two families, a 6px spacing unit, two radius tiers — there is no blank left to fill.
  • For the same reason, organisations with a defined brand have less of this problem. They started with fewer blanks.

Logos converge the same way. Why circular outlines, hollow centres and radial curves keep recurring is covered separately in why AI company logos look alike. Here the subject is the defaults of a whole screen rather than a mark.

3. Form is read first — where impression eats trust

That appearance drives credibility judgements is measured, not felt. When the Stanford Persuasive Technology Lab had 2,684 people assess website credibility and classified their reasons, remarks about design look made up 46.1% of the total — more than any other category. Information design and information focus came next. People look at the screen before they examine the credentials of a source or the accuracy of a citation.

That study is from 2003 and concerns websites, so it cannot be transplanted wholesale onto a 2026 deck or app. The direction, though, has not moved. Form decides whether the content is worth judging before any judgement of the content begins.

One variable is new. Crude design used to read as an absence of resources, and scarcity earns some forgiveness. Mass-produced design now reads as an absence of care. With the tools free, leaving the defaults in translates as not having spent the time. The artefact is the same; the reading of it got worse.

  • This hurts most in proposals, research material and job postings — documents where trust in the content is the outcome.
  • Internal dashboards and throwaway prototypes lose almost nothing to defaults. When the reader is not a judge, there is no cost to pay.
  • So how far to polish is not an aesthetic question but a question of who is reading.

How state handling — loading, empty, error — affects trust is set out separately in every frame has to be right. This piece stays with the static first impression.

4. The problem is not AI, it is the untouched default

The moment «this looks AI-made» slides into «do not make things with AI», the discussion stops being useful. For two reasons.

First, a large share of work that carries the look has nothing to do with AI. Decks built from a free template with the colors swapped, blogs on a stock theme, services that never touched the framework's starter screen — all of that predates the current tools. The instrument changed; the phenomenon did not.

Second, work made with AI can avoid the look entirely. Given enough constraint, output arrives inside that constraint. Tools aimed squarely at this are appearing. hallmark, for instance, is a design rule-set for coding agents, published under the MIT licence, with 57 evaluation gates, 21 themes, and four verbs — build, audit, redesign and study. The approach is explicit: make the model refuse the on-distribution defaults it was trained into.

That is not to say such a tool is the answer. A rule-set carries the taste of whoever wrote it, and once widely adopted it becomes the next generation's default. The fashion moves one step; the structure is unchanged. Tool or no tool, the same question remains — which values on this screen did I decide myself?

  • Naming the decision points works better than banning the tool. A no-AI rule cannot be verified and is trivial to route around.
  • A workable team rule looks like a checkable sentence: when we use generated output, color, type and spacing are replaced with our tokens.
  • Disclosing that something was generated and securing its quality are separate problems. Merging them blurs both.

5. The order of the fix — settle constraints, then build

Order changes the result. Fix things after building and the defaults have already spread across the screen, so a trace survives wherever you touch. Pin the numbers down before building and there is no blank left for a generator, or a person, to fill.

DecisionValue to pin firstPass testHow to check
Color countThree neutrals + one accent + two status colors (six or fewer)An accent used twice on one screen is not an accentConvert to greyscale — hierarchy survives means pass
TypeTwo families, three weights (body, mid, emphasis)If heading and body share a voice, hierarchy rests on weight aloneCover the headings — is the structure still legible?
Spacing unitOne base unit (say 4 or 6) and four or five allowed multiplesBetween-group space > within-group space, at least 2×Squint and count how many blocks you see
Corner radiusTwo or three tiers, fixed to element rolesTwo elements sharing a radius must share a tierSet every radius to 0 — does the structure hold?
Depth (shadow)Two levels — flat, floatingNo reason to float means flatDelete all shadows, restore only where distinction is lost
AlignmentLeft by default; centre only for text of two lines or fewerNo more than three left entry points per screenHold a ruler against the left edge
Contrast4.5:1 body, 3:1 large text and controls (section 7)If it fails, change lightness rather than hueRun every foreground/background pair through a contrast calculator
Break pointOne or two only (asymmetric grid or a distinctive display face)Break three or more and coherence goes firstAsk someone what stands out most on the screen

The row skipped most often is the last one. Keep only the first seven and you get a screen with no faults and no memory. Constraints set a floor; they do not set a ceiling.

  • When you set a constraint, record why that number in one line. Without it the next person will loosen it.
  • Fixing an existing screen, start with spacing rather than color. Get spacing right and half the color problems disappear.
  • The granular rules — spacing, alignment, state handling — are collected in small UI design rules.
  • Hand the generator the values from this table instead of adjectives. Two radius tiers, one accent is a far stronger instruction than make it modern.
  • If you dislike the result, do not lengthen the prompt — settle one more constraint.

6. Brand tokens and the deliberate break

A token is a name attached to a value: color-accent instead of #4F46E5, space-4 instead of 16px. Two effects follow. Changing a value means editing one place, and — more relevant here — a named slot stops being a blank. Give a generator the token list and it picks from that list rather than from its own defaults.

That said, the better organised the token system, the more results tend to come out tidy but similar to each other. Screens that follow the rules well all follow the same rules. So it becomes necessary to choose one place to break, explicitly. Not breaking the rules, but deciding by rule where the deviation goes. Candidates are usually one of these.

  • Layout skeleton — an asymmetric split instead of a symmetric twelve-column grid, or one deliberately generous gutter
  • Display face — keep body text in a comfortable standard face and give headings one face with real character
  • Color anchor — fix one color derived from the brand instead of the usual violet and blue, and keep the rest neutral
  • Language of corners and lines — square by default, rounded only on specific elements, rather than rounding everything
  • Image rule — restrict imagery to real screens, measured charts and original photography instead of stock

One is enough. Two still reads as character; more than three reads as having no rules at all. This is the failure mode opposite to the AI look. Defaults untouched read as no decision; everything twisted reads as noise. Both arrive at the same conclusion — nobody can tell who took responsibility for this.

The case where every screen follows the rules yet the flow between them feels wrong is a different layer of problem, covered in coherence versus consistency.

7. Accessibility collapses alongside — the WCAG 2.2 floor

What appears almost without exception alongside untouched defaults is insufficient contrast. Not a coincidence: choices made to look clean cut contrast directly. Pale grey body text on white, grey captions on grey cards, white type over a gradient, borderless inputs barely distinguishable from the background — all cases of reaching for minimal and arriving at unreadable. There is a standard here already, so nothing needs inventing: W3C's WCAG 2.2.

SCNameLevelMinimum ratioApplies to
1.4.3Contrast (Minimum)AA4.5:1Text and images of text
1.4.3 (exception)Large textAA3:118pt and above, or 14pt bold, or an equivalent size for CJK fonts
1.4.11Non-text ContrastAA3:1Visual information needed to identify UI components and their states; graphical objects required to understand content
2.5.8Target Size (Minimum)AA—Pointer targets of at least 24 × 24 CSS pixels (spacing, equivalent, inline, user-agent and essential exceptions)

The important detail is that the large-text definition in 1.4.3 carries the qualifier that CJK fonts count at an equivalent size. Eighteen points of Latin and eighteen points of Hangul or Han characters do not deliver the same legibility, because stroke count and density differ. Judging by the Latin threshold that a text is large and therefore 3:1 suffices is exactly how a Korean screen ends up genuinely hard to read.

  • 1.4.11 targets borderless inputs head on. If the field boundary is under 3:1 against its background, nobody can tell where to click.
  • CSS that strips the focus ring (outline: none) usually goes in because the default is not pretty. If you remove it, you owe a replacement.
  • Charts that separate series by hue alone fail on graphical-object contrast and color vision deficiency at once. Add shape, pattern or direct labels.
  • Dark mode needs its own verification. A pair that passes in light mode is not guaranteed to pass after inversion.

Insufficient contrast is not a matter of taste but a matter of some users never reaching the content. Removing the AI look and meeting accessibility generally meet in the same place: get the lightness hierarchy right and both are handled.

8. Same settings, different impression — typography by script

Apply identical CSS across four languages and four different impressions come out. Generator defaults are largely tuned for Latin script, so laying them over Hangul, kana and Han characters exposes the awkwardness in a different way each time.

ScriptWhere the default goes wrongReference
HangulInter-character space is zero by default, so opening up tracking on Latin instincts blurs word boundaries. The same setting also looks different in proportional versus fixed-width Hangul facesW3C Requirements for Hangul Text Layout and Typography
JapaneseKana and kanji alternate, so density shifts across a line. Leave line spacing to a CSS default and the kanji runs feel cramped — Japanese composition treats line gap as a value set up front in the basic page frameW3C Requirements for Japanese Text Layout (based on JIS X 4051)
Chinese (Simplified)Bold tends to clog strokes, so it is a weak emphasis device. Chinese composition marks emphasis with emphasis dots (着重號) set below the character, or to its right when set vertically. Hei has traditionally belonged to headings and emphasis rather than body textW3C Requirements for Chinese Text Layout
LatinThe wider the pairing options, the narrower the default. Running everything through one geometric sans is where the tell shows most sharplyWCAG 2.2 1.4.3 (18pt / 14pt bold threshold)
  • The most common practical error is copying Latin-tuned tracking and leading straight into other languages. Right in one script means wrong in another.
  • Line-breaking rules differ too. Hangul admits both word-level and character-level division, and the choice changes the impression of the ragged edge.
  • Building hierarchy through weight works best in Latin and weakest in Han characters. Dividing hierarchy by size, color and space is the safer multilingual habit.
  • This is also why the WCAG large-text exception singles out CJK — the same point value reads differently in different scripts.

9. The limits of tools that claim to detect AI

Catalogue the tells and the next question follows — why not detect it mechanically? That experiment has already run its course on text, and the result is clear.

OpenAI released a classifier for AI-written text in January 2023 and withdrew it that July for low accuracy. The published figures were 26% true positives on AI text and 9% false positives on human writing, with an explicit note that it was unreliable below 1,000 characters.

More important is that the false positives are not evenly distributed. In a paper published in Patterns by a Stanford team, seven detectors wrongly flagged 61.3% of TOEFL essays written by non-native English speakers as AI-generated. The same error was near-absent on native writing. Detectors treat predictable word choice and simple sentence structure as an AI signal — which is also what naturally appears when writing in a second language.

  • The structure transfers directly to design. Using a default and being generated are indistinguishable. The person who used a template because there was no budget gets caught first.
  • Once the rules are known, evasion is cheap. Dropping the gradient and varying the radii clears the surface metrics. Evading costs less than detecting.
  • So it is better not to build procedures that penalise people on the strength of a verdict. The cost of a false positive is paid by the accused, not by the tool.
  • The usable direction is audit, not detection: not was this made by AI, but does this screen meet its contrast, hierarchy and spacing rules. That question has verifiable answers and obvious fixes.

The equivalent problem in writing is covered in the traces of AI-written text. The point here is only that the same conclusion transfers to visual output.

10. A review pass you can run today

Take one screen you have open and work down the list.

  1. Convert to greyscale. If the hierarchy disappears, color was holding up the structure, and that needs fixing.
  2. Squint at it. If the number of blocks you see differs from the number of groups you intended, the spacing is wrong.
  3. Set every radius to 0. If the structure collapses, radius was standing in for hierarchy.
  4. Delete every shadow. Anything still distinguishable never needed one.
  5. Hold a ruler to the left edge. More than three entry points means the alignment needs sorting.
  6. Calculate contrast. 4.5:1 for body, 3:1 for large text and controls. Adjust lightness if it fails.
  7. Remove every emoji. Restore only where information is lost.
  8. Count the values you decided. If none of the color, type, spacing or radius was set by you, nobody has made this screen yet.

Six of the eight are acts of taking something away, deliberately. Default problems generally arrive by addition and surface by subtraction.

What could not be verified

Items this piece could not put a source under are collected here rather than repeated through the text.

  • No public dataset was found measuring how frequently particular font or color combinations actually occur in generated work. The nine slots in section 1 are a qualitative classification, not frequency data.
  • The individual items among the 57 gates in the rule-set tool mentioned above are not enumerated at repository-documentation level, so that list could not be checked. The count, the licence and the four verbs are stated in the documentation.
  • The Japanese layout requirements document defines line gap as something to be set in the basic page frame but gives no recommended figure. No number is quoted here either.
  • No public study validating the accuracy of AI detectors built specifically for images or UI was found. Every figure in section 9 belongs to text classifiers; the direction is cited on structural grounds, not as a value that transfers to design.

Conclusion — what remains once the defaults are gone

«This looks AI-made» is not a complaint about a tool. It is a complaint that a decision is missing. Which is why the problem does not dissolve by avoiding AI, and does not dissolve by refining the prompt either. There is only one way through — the human sets the values before the tool fills them in.

There are not many values to set. A handful of colors, a couple of families, one spacing unit, two or three radius tiers, and one place where the rules are allowed to break. Write those six lines first and the generator moves only inside them. The time left over goes to the verifiable things: contrast and hierarchy. WCAG 2.2's 4.5:1 and 3:1 are not matters of taste — they are lines where passing is calculated.

And it is better to stay out of detection. As text has already shown, that road filters out the person who used a default and the person without resources first. The question worth asking is not whether AI made this, but who decided what on this screen. The second question comes with a fix attached.
