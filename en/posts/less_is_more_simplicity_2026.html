Less Is More, Mostly

Now that AI makes adding anything trivial, why is subtracting still usually the better call - and when is it not

Jul 7, 2026 Trends & Culture

Intro: Why We Need to Talk About Not Adding More, Right Now

As of July 2026, AI coding tools have pushed the cost of adding a single feature close to zero. Bolting on one more button, one more screen, one more setting no longer requires the meetings, the spec, and the days it used to. And yet, right as building has gotten easier than ever, an essay that circulated on GeekNews (hada) argues the opposite. Its title is "Less is more, mostly."

I agree with that piece almost entirely, but I want to linger on two words hiding inside that short sentence longer than the essay itself does. The first is "mostly" - not an absolute law but a probabilistic claim, one that quietly admits there will be exceptions. The second is an unspoken premise: that following this advice means going against instinct. If less is genuinely better, why do we keep adding anyway? This piece works through both questions - why we default to adding, and when adding is actually right - across software, products, and everyday life.

1. The Instinct and Bias Toward Adding - Why We Keep Reaching to Put Something On Top

1.1 Loss Aversion and Visible Effort

A study published in Nature in 2021 by a University of Virginia research team demonstrates this bias experimentally. Across tasks like reinforcing a Lego bridge, improving a piece of writing, or fixing a travel itinerary, most participants chose to add something rather than remove something. The reason the researchers point to is simple: adding produces a visible, easily explained result, while subtracting makes it harder to prove what you actually did. The core message is not that our brains are lazy, but that adding is the cognitively cheaper default option that comes to mind first.

1.2 Amplified Inside Organizations and Codebases

This individual-level bias gets amplified once it enters an organization. "What did we ship this sprint?" is a natural question in a review; "what did we remove this sprint?" almost never gets asked. To a product manager, adding a feature is a visible win, while removing one reads as a risk. Product research keeps turning up the same finding: the features users actually use routinely fall short of half of everything that was built. That is less a failure of any one team than a structural bias - backlogs and roadmaps are simply built in a way that favors adding.

2. Why Subtracting Is Usually Better - Maintenance, Cognitive Load, Clarity

2.1 Maintenance Cost Compounds

Adding one feature rarely adds just one unit of cost. It adds to the combinatorics of how that feature interacts with everything else, the surface area you now have to test, the documentation you owe, and the support tickets you will eventually field. The first few additions are manageable; as they pile up, the cost grows combinatorially rather than linearly. This is exactly why code quality should never be judged by line count, but by how much a system achieves with as little code as possible.

2.2 Cognitive Load and Processing Fluency

The same math applies on the user's side. A tiny appearance animation on a context menu you open hundreds of times a day costs a few hundred milliseconds each time - trivial on its own, but it quietly eats a non-trivial amount of everyone's attention and time once it accumulates across a whole user base, day after day. What cognitive psychology calls processing fluency - how effortlessly you understand something - is consistently higher for simpler interfaces. The more options a screen shows, the more mental energy it takes to figure out what to do next.

2.3 Clarity Builds Trust

A simple system is predictable. It is easy to trace what affects what, and easy to narrow down a cause when something breaks. A tangled, complex system, by contrast, can leave even the people responsible for it unable to hold the whole picture in their heads, touching only the part they understand. This clarity is not an aesthetic nicety - it is a working condition for how confidently the people who actually operate a system can make decisions.

3. The Word "Mostly" - When Adding Is the Right Call

3.1 Essential Complexity Cannot Be Cut Away

Regulatory compliance, multi-language support, accessibility - some complexity is demanded by the problem itself. Trimming the interface or the code does not make it disappear; it has to live somewhere, because it is a genuine requirement. In these cases, "subtracting" is not about eliminating complexity but about organizing it into a form people can actually handle.

3.2 Safety and Redundancy Are Deliberate Slack

An airplane has more than one engine, and a critical database keeps replicas, not out of waste but by deliberate design. Microsoft and plenty of other engineering organizations keep sounding the same warning: get too obsessed with removing redundancy, and you risk stripping out the safeguards along with it, trading simplicity for a single point of failure that is far more dangerous.

3.3 Early-Stage Discovery Sometimes Calls for Deliberate Expansion

When a product does not yet know what is actually valuable - the product-market-fit discovery phase - trying several things provisionally and cutting later can be the rational move. YAGNI itself is closer to "not confirmed necessary yet" than to "never necessary." Experimental additions during this phase are best read as a deliberate investment in information, made on the assumption that most of it will be cut later.

4. Simplicity Principles in Software and Product Design - YAGNI and Essential vs Accidental Complexity

4.1 YAGNI: Build It When You Need It

The YAGNI (You Aren't Gonna Need It) principle from Extreme Programming says not to build a feature just because you predict you might need it someday. Predictions are frequently wrong, and code built on a wrong prediction sits unused while still costing maintenance. The point is to build only what is confirmed necessary now, and build the rest when the need is actually confirmed.

4.2 Fred Brooks: Essential vs Accidental Complexity

Fred Brooks's 1986 classic "No Silver Bullet" splits complexity into two kinds. Essential complexity is inherent difficulty in the problem you are solving - it cannot be removed, only managed well. Accidental complexity is difficulty introduced by the tools, conventions, and design choices you made along the way, and can, in principle, be reduced. This distinction matters in practice because conflating the two lets teams excuse away reducible, accidental complexity under the cover of "the problem is just hard."

4.3 The 2026 Trap: AI Accumulates Accidental Complexity Faster

Here is the point I want to underline. In the age of AI coding tools, the cost of writing code has dropped sharply, but the cost of understanding and maintaining that code has stayed the same or gotten worse. As a result, accidental complexity now accumulates much faster than it used to. And once it tangles with dependencies elsewhere in the system, it frequently hardens into something nearly as hard to reverse as essential complexity. This shift is exactly why "less is more" reads with unusual urgency in 2026.

5. The Minimalism Trap - The Risks of Over-Simplifying

5.1 Lost Functionality and Worse Usability

Unlabeled icons and hamburger menus that bury even core functions look simple on the surface, but they make it genuinely harder for users to find what they came for. Simplicity and discoverability are different axes, and sacrificing one for the other tends to produce "simple and frustrating" rather than "simple and good."

5.2 Hidden Complexity Doesn't Vanish - It Relocates

Stripping options out of an interface does not make that complexity disappear from the world. Much of it simply relocates - into the user's head, or into the support team's workload when the tickets start coming in. If a product looks simple on the surface while its support team is drowning in repeat questions, that simplicity was probably a cost transfer rather than a real reduction.

5.3 The Single Point of Failure

The safety-and-redundancy discussion from Section 3 resurfaces here. Treat reduction itself as a virtue, and you risk cutting away the very safeguards and slack you should have kept. The minimalism trap tends to appear exactly when "cutting" becomes the goal, without ever asking what should be cut.

6. Applying This at Work and in Life - Questions That Find the Balance

6.1 At Work: Make the Subtraction Question a Standing Item

Simply adding "what happens if we cut this?" as a routine question in code reviews and planning meetings corrects a surprising amount of the bias on its own. A written codebase standard lets the same bar apply to both people and AI coding agents, which slows how fast accidental complexity accumulates. Giving feature removal its own explicit line on the roadmap, alongside feature addition, makes the intention concrete rather than aspirational.

6.2 In Life: The Same Bias Shows Up in Closets, Calendars, and Relationships

This bias is not unique to software. In our closets, our calendars, and our relationships, we habitually reach to add something new before we consider removing anything. Asking yourself, on a regular cadence, "what am I going to stop doing this quarter" is at least as important as deciding what to start.

6.3 The Deciding Question: Essential or Accidental?

Whether at work or in life, most decisions compress into one question: does this complexity come from the problem itself, or from a choice we made? If it's the latter, cutting it is, mostly, the better call.

Conclusion: Balance Is Not the Default - It's a Practiced Skill

If adding is the brain's default, subtracting is closer to a muscle you have to consciously and repeatedly train. In the sentence "less is more, mostly," the word doing the real work might not be "less" - it might be "mostly." Admitting exceptions is what keeps a principle from calcifying into dogma, and having a principle is what keeps an exception from becoming an excuse. Two things worth doing this week: add the question "what happens if we cut this?" to whatever spec or code review is already in front of you, and then be honest with yourself about whether the answer is "because it's genuinely necessary" or "because we just always add things out of habit."
