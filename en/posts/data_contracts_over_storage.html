Moving Data Is Easier Than Agreeing What It Means - The Semantic Drift Schemas Cannot Catch and Data Contracts

When a column disappears you know at once; when its meaning changes, nobody does

Aug 31, 2026 Dev & Infra

Opening: every pipeline ran green, and two teams brought different numbers

The Monday metrics meeting produces a familiar scene. Growth says last month had 4.12 million active users. The finance deck says 3.88 million. Nobody made a mistake. Every pipeline succeeded, schema validation passed, and both figures sit comfortably inside the normal range.

That meeting rarely ends by fixing data. It ends as an argument about whose number is right, and it reconvenes next month. The definition is written down nowhere, so whatever gets agreed survives in people's memory and never reaches the pipeline.

None of this comes from weak infrastructure. If anything it is the opposite. Moving, storing and scanning data quickly got largely solved over the past decade. What remains was not newly created; it was uncovered when the layer in front of it was cleared away.

A piece published on 19 August 2026 puts exactly this framing on the table: Data Architecture Patterns: Decisions for the AI Era. The author takes the six architecture patterns that usually appear as a menu and re-lays them as decisions along different dimensions rather than mutually exclusive choices. One is a decision about processing, another about transformation stages, another about ownership, and storage is only one of the cells. The conclusion overlaps with the starting point of this article: the author argues that when AI consumption becomes the dominant pressure, the things that gain value are not storage, processing and integration but meaning, contracts and governance.

This article takes that argument and drops one floor down. It is not about picking tools. It is about what happens when the same column name carries a different meaning on each team. Which product is good is not the question here. What counts as a promise, and where you make people keep it, is.

1. What was left behind when storage got cheap - same name, different meaning

Start by being honest about what actually got solved. Capacity and speed became problems that money answers. Pile data into a cheap, effectively unbounded file store, keep it in a column-oriented file format that compresses well, and separate the compute that reads it so it only runs when needed. Decisions that once needed months of capacity planning now arrive mostly as a pricing-tier choice.

The word lakehouse, which turns up constantly, points at this arrangement. Unpacked, it means laying a ledger of what changed, when and how over a cheap bulk file store, so that the store can be handled like a set of tables. With a ledger you can roll back, and concurrent writes become tractable. That is real progress. But it is a storage decision. A ledger does not tell you what any column means.

So the thing that eats time in practice now usually looks like this. Three teams use the identical column name and put different things inside it.

ColumnWhat the producer put thereWhat the consumer assumedSymptom when they diverge
countryBilling country of the payment methodCountry the user actually connected fromRegional growth appears concentrated in one market
revenueGross, tax included, refunds not deductedRecognizable net revenueFinance close and the dashboard permanently disagree
signup_dateWhen the account record was first createdWhen email verification completedTime from signup to first payment reads longer than it is
is_activeThe account is not suspendedThe account is actually being used latelyDormant accounts count as active and churn looks low
order_idIdentifier at cart levelIdentifier at shipment levelSplit shipments inflate order counts

Not one row in that table is a bug. On the producing side each value is correct. The problem is that the choice appears nowhere in the column name, and the consumer reads the name through their own common sense. The moment data crosses a team boundary, the name travels and the context falls off.

And this kind of divergence grows superlinearly with the organization. Five producers and twenty consumers give you a hundred possible readings. Those hundred readings live scattered across individual dashboards and individual queries, and nowhere are they collected in one place.

2. Nobody knows what an active user is

This is the most common example and the least resolved one. Almost every company has an active-user metric, and in almost every company nobody can state its definition in one sentence. Not because there is no definition, but because several exist at the same time.

Take the definition apart and the fault lines show. There are at least five.

Fault lineWhere the choice actually splitsDirection the number moves
What counts as activityLogin, app launch, reaching a specific screen, an API call, receiving a pushWidening it to app launch inflates sharply; narrowing to core-feature use collapses it
Who counts as a userAccount, person, device, organization (one contract covering 300 employees)Device basis inflates; organization basis shrinks dramatically
Over what windowCalendar month, rolling 30 days, last-activity basis, UTC versus local timeA single day at month end swings the figure by several percent
What gets excludedInternal accounts, test accounts, bots and crawlers, automatic retries, health-check trafficTurning the bot filter on or off alone shifts several percent
How it is countedDeduplication basis, churn and re-signup, treatment of history after account mergesMerge handling changes historical values themselves

Two or three branches at each of five fault lines gives dozens of combinations, and every one of them produces an honest number. So when two teams arrive with different figures, neither is wrong. They are calling two different metrics by the same name.

A second problem attaches here. Suppose the meeting settles the definition. That agreement lands in minutes. Next quarter a new analyst joins, never sees those minutes, looks at the table and writes a query. The column is named is_active. What they will put in it is not in doubt.

So the real issue is not knowledge but where the knowledge sits. A definition held in people's heads and meeting minutes leaves when the people leave. A definition sitting next to the code, which has to pass a check before merging, stays. That single difference is what the rest of this article is about.

3. A schema speaks only about form - the specs say so themselves

The obvious next question is whether using schemas properly would fix this. The answer is already written into the specifications. Schema specs explicitly separate what they check from what they do not.

The JSON Schema 2020-12 validation specification divides keywords into two kinds. One set is assertion keywords, which decide validity. The other is a vocabulary of basic meta-data annotations intended for documentation and interface display. title and description belong to the second set. The spec says only that they can be used to decorate a user interface with information about the data produced by this user interface. They take no part in validation.

Avro is blunter. Its schema resolution rules state that fields are matched by name, and separately that a schema's doc fields are ignored for the purposes of schema resolution. Field order can differ; matching names means compatible; and the sentence describing what a field means has no bearing on the verdict.

{
  "name": "is_active",
  "type": "boolean",
  "doc": "account not suspended (before 2026-08)"
}
// a release that changes only doc, to the line below
// → schema resolution reports full compatibility
{
  "name": "is_active",
  "type": "boolean",
  "doc": "account with a login in the last 30 days"
}

To a machine those two are the same schema. Yet what is_active counts has changed completely. That release trips no check anywhere, and the twenty dashboards built on the table start drawing different numbers without a single warning.

3.1 What a schema catches and what it does not

  • Catches — a field disappeared, a type went from string to number, a required field turned null, an unexpected value showed up in an enum
  • Catches — format broke. A date string left its pattern, or a number exceeded its declared range
  • Misses — meaning changed inside the same type. The currency of an amount changed, the time-zone basis of a timestamp changed, a boolean started counting something else
  • Misses — an aggregation rule changed. One filter condition was added, a join direction flipped, the deduplication basis moved
  • Misses — values are unchanged but the population is not. A table that held domestic rows yesterday quietly starts carrying international ones today, and a mismatch between a documented meaning and the actual query goes unnoticed because documents were never in scope for checking

So a schema is half of a contract, and the easier half at that. The machine-readable spec discussion in Why API Docs Always Lag Behind Code covers that half. The other half, meaning, sits outside it and has mostly gone unmanaged.

4. What a data contract promises and what it does not

Left as a buzzword, data contract says nothing. Better to narrow it in plain terms. It is a promise between the side producing data and the side consuming it, covering what will be sent, what is guaranteed, and how changes will be announced. And it earns the word contract only when it is checked by machine so that something fails when reality diverges, rather than resting on goodwill. A promise nobody checks is just a document.

A public standard for this shape already exists. The Open Data Contract Standard, maintained by Bitol, splits a contract document into eleven sections, and schema is one of them. The rest are data quality, support and communication channels, team, roles, service-level agreement, infrastructure and servers, and so on. The specification itself is saying that most of a contract lives outside the schema.

What a contract promisesWhat it does not promise
Field list, types, required or notThat the dataset is the business-correct metric
A sentence defining what each field meansThat consumers will read that definition correctly
Valid value ranges and null tolerance ceilingsThat the definition will never change
A freshness deadline for when data arrivesQuery performance and storage cost
Notice period and notification path for changesRetroactive correction of historical data
The team that answers for it and how to reach themThat the contract sentence matches the real query

The third item on the right causes the most confusion. A contract does not say the meaning is frozen. When the business changes, definitions have to change too. The one thing a contract promises is that the change will not be made quietly. Miss that distinction and you start treating a contract as a device for preventing change, which leads straight into the bureaucratic failure discussed later.

The bottom right deserves the same honesty. You can write an active user is an account that used a core feature at least once in the last 30 days into a contract file, and no machine can tell you whether the actual query is counting logins instead. Somebody has to make that comparison at some point. A contract narrows where that comparison is needed; it does not remove it.

5. It breaks quietly

This is the pivot of the whole article. Form breaking and meaning breaking are different in kind, and the difference is not severity but time to discovery.

Form breaks loudly. A column vanishes, the query fails on the spot, the scheduler goes red, an alert fires and somebody wakes up at 3 a.m. Unpleasant, but this is a well-designed failure. It is noticed within hours and fixed within hours.

Meaning breaks in silence. Pipelines succeed, checks pass, dashboards keep drawing. Only the numbers move, and the size of the move is usually indistinguishable from seasonality or a marketing campaign. Nobody suspects an incident because a figure went up three percent.

5.1 The usual routes to a silent break

  • A filter was added. Upstream starts excluding bot traffic and every downstream number drops at once. A good change, but without notification it is indistinguishable from an incident
  • The time-zone basis moved. Shifting aggregation from UTC to local time slides a few days between months. Annual totals stay identical, so cross-checking will not catch it either
  • Null handling changed. Missing values that used to stay null start being filled with zero, and only the side computing averages drifts down. The side reading sums sees nothing at all
  • The FX conversion point moved. Going from transaction-time rates to close-date rates shakes only the months with heavy international revenue
  • Upstream refilled history. After a reprocessing run, last month's figure in yesterday's report and today's report differ. Nobody knew the reprocessing happened
  • One side of a join went empty. An event stops arriving because of a missed deploy, and with a left join the rows survive while the values become zero. A row-count check will never catch it

A new enum value appearing in a status code with no matching branch in a downstream CASE statement, dropping those records into an other bucket, belongs to the same family. All six generate no failure alert. There is exactly one discovery path: somebody looks at a number and feels that it is off. That feeling generally arrives weeks later. By then the board report, the partner invoice and the training data have already gone out, and they cannot be pulled back.

An organizational asymmetry sits on top of this. The loud failures already have alerts, on-call rotations and budget. The quiet ones have none of the three. So investment keeps flowing toward what is already caught well. Building one more pipeline-success dashboard is always easier than getting two teams to agree on one definition.

Which narrows the response to one direction. Watch the distribution of values, not just their presence. Put null ratios, distinct counts, bucketed distributions and day-over-day row deltas into the contract and alert when a threshold moves. It is not complete. A three percent shift still slips through. But changes that move a distribution in a step, like adding a filter or filling nulls, are caught this way, and a large share of real silent incidents are of the step kind.

6. Where AI makes this problem bigger

Everything so far holds without AI in the picture. So why is this subject back now? The author of the triggering piece locates the reason in a change in who the consumer is. He offers the example that two domains can agree that customerId is a string and still fail if their definitions of customer differ. Schema compatibility is not enough; semantic consistency is required.

What makes that observation heavy is that human analysts and models behave differently.

  • People ask back. When a number looks unusual they message whoever built the table. That asking-back has been the real safety net catching semantic divergence so far
  • Models do not ask back. They use the column they were given and turn the result into fluent prose. The sentence reads smoothly on top of a false premise
  • Not verifying was the point of adoption. If a person were going to check every result by hand there would have been no reason to automate. And generated summaries carry hedging only as a formality, so readers cannot tell how confident the output actually is

6.1 Why tracing back gets harder

The more awkward part comes after the error. Working backwards to where it went wrong becomes structurally difficult.

  • The question was natural language, so the record of which column was chosen is thin. Without storing the generated query, the evidence is gone
  • Once several sources are merged into a single paragraph, the sentence does not separate which source was wrong
  • Data that went into training or fine-tuning cannot be pulled back out of the output. Labels built on a wrong definition spread across the whole model rather than one answer, and asking the same question again may not follow the same path, which makes narrowing the cause by reproduction difficult too

The prescription usually offered here is a semantic layer. Unpack that too. It is a layer above tables and columns where the formula and meaning of each metric are written in one place, with every query routed through that place. The point is not a particular product but turning definitions into code that lives in exactly one location. The language and tooling view of this approach is covered separately in ktx: An Executable Context Layer for Data and Analytics AI Agents and The 2026 Data Tooling Landscape.

It is not a cure-all either. If even one query bypasses that layer, the definitions split again, and in reality there almost always is one: the urgent analysis, the ad hoc extract, the private query in somebody's notebook. Gathering definitions into one place is engineering work. Making everything actually go through that place is organizational work.

7. Where to enforce a contract - document, test, or deploy gate

Leverage and cost run in opposite directions depending on where the contract lives. It is not a choice among three but a question of splitting what goes where.

7.1 Keep it in a document

  • Leverage — lowest. Nothing hurts when a document is wrong, which is why documents always lag the code
  • Cost — cheapest. A few meetings and one file
  • Still worth something — writing a definition down forces the negotiation to start. That three teams give three answers is invisible until somebody writes it out. Going from zero to one, this is the biggest single step

7.2 Check inside the pipeline

  • Leverage — middling. Whatever a machine can count gets caught reliably: null ratios, value ranges, arrival times, row-count deltas
  • Cost — it collapses if the failure decision is not made in advance. Halting delays everything downstream; passing through piles up warnings until they are noise. In practice teams lean toward passing through
  • Limit — the data has already landed. Catching a bad value after it entered the store leaves the cost of retroactive correction intact

7.3 Put a gate in the producer's deploy

  • Leverage — highest. If a change to a schema or a definition is blocked before it merges, the bad data never exists in the first place
  • Cost — highest. It requires the producing team to accept that its own deploy can be held up by somebody else's situation. This is where most adoptions fail
  • Failure shape — drop in the tooling without organizational agreement and an exception-approval path appears immediately; two months later every change ships as an exception

Arrangements that actually work usually split it this way: form at the deploy gate, distribution and freshness in the pipeline check, meaning in documents plus human review. That last cell is both the conclusion and the limit of this article. Because meaning cannot be machine-checked, no contract, however well built, removes the point where a person has to look. What a contract does is reduce that point from ten places to one.

8. Who owns it - where the engineering problem ends

A contract needs a signature. Without someone to sign, it is not a contract but a wish list. And on the data side, missing ownership is not an exception. It is the default.

The typical shape: a central data team manages every table. That team runs pipelines well but has no authority to decide meaning. What a payment means is known by the payments team; what a contract means is known by sales. The central team guesses from the middle, puts the guess in a column name, and that name becomes the company's shared vocabulary. Half of all divergence comes from this structure.

The term data mesh, which comes up often, aims at exactly this. Unpacked, it is the approach of saying that one central team cannot possibly know the meaning of all company data, so the domain teams that produce data should publish it in a form others can use and answer for its quality themselves. The triggering piece classifies this pattern as a decision about ownership and records its cost as requiring agreed standards across domains. Splitting ownership means more things that must be aligned.

For ownership to hold, four things are needed at minimum.

  • A named team, not an individual. One designated person means the contract is orphaned the day they leave
  • Consumer impact must appear in that team's goals. If nothing in their own numbers gets worse when downstream breaks, there is no reason to honor a notice period
  • A release process capable of honoring notice. Promising two weeks means nothing on a team that ships hotfixes weekly
  • The authority to say no. A team that must accept every incoming request is not an owner but a subcontractor, and a subcontractor cannot carry responsibility

No tool creates any of those four. Goal-setting and authority are decided by the org chart. What an Ontology Is That a Schema Is Not reached the same conclusion by a different route: the cost of agreement exceeds the cost of construction, and that invoice goes to the organization, not to engineering.

9. How to start small

Attempts that begin as a company-wide rollout plan mostly fail, for a simple reason. Attaching contracts to every table requires every team to agree at the same time, and that agreement does not happen in one pass. Fatigue also arrives before any result does.

Reverse the order instead. Build one visible save before worrying about coverage.

  1. Pick one metric that already hurt, and narrow to the one or two tables that produce it. The one where two teams brought different numbers is the candidate. Start from a problem nobody has felt and nobody gives it time; try to fix the whole upstream and you stall in week one
  2. Write the definition out as sentences. What is included, what is excluded, how wide the window is, how duplicates are removed. Three teams giving three answers here is normal, and that meeting is the first deliverable
  3. Move the agreed sentences into a contract file. One line of meaning per field, valid ranges, a freshness rule, the owning team and how to reach them. Borrow a public format instead of inventing one
  4. Automate what a machine can count first. Null ratios, value ranges, arrival times, day-over-day row deltas. What gets caught here is exactly the step-shaped change from the previous section
  5. Plant a notice rule in the producer repository. On any change that drops the field or alters its meaning, make the PR template show the list of consumers. It does not have to block. Visibility alone changes behavior
  6. Move to the second metric. From here you copy the format, and it takes half the time of the first

Step five tends to give the best return for its price. It is visibility rather than enforcement, so it draws little resistance, and it lets producers see for the first time what they are about to break. Most quiet incidents are not malice. They are people not knowing.

And when that first case actually catches something, write it down. One sentence saying this check caught that divergence measurably reduces resistance from the third metric onward.

10. What happens when you overdo it - contracts as bureaucracy

The opposite failure is just as real. Organizations that adopted contracts so heavily that they became inert are more common than ones that adopted them well.

  • Mandating every table. Force a contract file onto every table and you get hundreds of files nobody reads. The more documents there are, the less any of them is trusted, and the existence of a contract stops being a signal at all
  • Approval-stage sprawl. When adding one field takes two weeks, people move outside the contract. Temporary tables, extracts on personal laptops, spreadsheets. The divergence did not disappear; it moved somewhere invisible, which is the worst ending available
  • Alert flooding. Tight thresholds produce dozens of violation alerts a day. Trying to catch the quiet failures, you end up manufacturing loud failures that everybody ignores
  • Chasing a perfect definition. If the active-user definition meeting is in its sixth week, agreement is not hard: there were two metrics all along. Splitting the name in two is faster than forcing them into one

All four point one way. The heavier the process for changing a contract, the more definitions calcify while stale, and the contract turns from a device that announces change into one that prevents it. Keep a single test: when attaching a new contract, if you cannot name who gets hurt when it is violated, do not attach it. Contracts with nobody hurting only lengthen the inventory, and a long inventory hides the real ones. The point made about dashboards in Why the Observability Data You Collect Never Gets Read repeats verbatim here. Adding already has momentum; the side that needs to become a scheduled procedure is removing.

What could not be verified

  • No public survey measuring adoption rates or outcomes of data contracts was found. The judgments here are structural explanation, not measurement
  • There are also no public statistics on how far active-user definitions actually diverge between organizations. The fault lines in the table above come from decomposing the definition, not from a survey
  • No material quantifying how much semantic divergence contributes to AI output error rates could be confirmed
  • For the Open Data Contract Standard, only the section list in the specification was checked. Which sections get filled in and which stay empty in real deployments was not
  • The three cases in the triggering article are examples given by its author; the source or verification status of those cases is not stated in the original. Nor is there a public experiment comparing the three enforcement points, so the descriptions of leverage and cost are derived from when each point works and when it gets bypassed

Conclusion: the moving is done, the agreeing is not

When storage was hard, data problems looked like engineering problems. Add capacity, make processing faster, and you moved forward. With that layer resolved, what remains has a different character. It is choosing one meaning among people who use the same word for different things, and tooling does not finish that job.

So the conclusion here is not a product name but three sentences. A schema speaks only about form, and meaning lives outside it. Form breaks loudly and meaning breaks quietly, yet organizational investment attaches only to the loud side. A contract is not a promise that a definition will never change, but a promise that it will not be changed quietly.

What you can do today is correspondingly small. Pick one metric where the numbers diverged last quarter, write its definition as a single paragraph, and write down the name of whoever will keep it. That is where a contract starts. The format can be added later without penalty.
