Background and Problem — Strengths and Weaknesses of Ferromagnets and Antiferromagnets, and the Three Conditions the Blog Wants

The blog starts from the two kinds of magnet we are all used to. The common one, the fridge magnet, is ferromagnetic: its atomic magnets all point the same way (up or down), adding their magnetic effects. The less well known one, the antiferromagnet (AF), has neighbouring atomic magnets that point opposite ways and exactly cancel out magnetically. The author writes that for a long time there has been an intense drive in computer memory research to create materials in between these two extremes.

Each electron has a quantum mechanical property called spin, which is responsible for its magnetic moment, and the blog models the spin direction of each electron as either up or down. Spintronics uses that spin for storage. The most prominent example is the hard drive read head, the device that reads the magnetic bits on the disk, and MRAM uses the same principle to store binary information in a non-volatile way. In spintronics one wants to sort electrons by their spin orientation so that their information can be read and stored. Ferromagnets do this naturally, since the electrons that carry current are mostly of one spin, but ordinary antiferromagnets cannot distinguish up and down electrons.

A ferromagnet has a macroscopic magnetic field, a field that leaks out from the surface. The problem the blog names is that this field interferes with nearby materials and is difficult to control for storage purposes, and that switching magnets back and forth is relatively slow and consumes a lot of power. An antiferromagnet is the reverse: electrons of the same energy level have mixed spins, so it is hard to read and store information with spintronics techniques. In exchange, the lack of a macroscopic magnetic field allows these materials to be packed closer together, and the blog says AF materials are also about a thousand times faster to switch. That thousand-fold figure is a statement about antiferromagnets in general, not a measurement on the two materials discussed here.

The material the author wants combines the following three conditions.

  • Zero net magnetism: the net magnetic effect has to be zero, so that no macroscopic field leaks out.
  • A band gap: the material has to be a semiconductor, with a band gap.
  • A spin window: it must not lose the ability to separate spins by their energy levels.

Three Kinds of Magnet Seen in the Electron Energy Spectrum

The blog distinguishes the three kinds by the energy spectrum of the electrons. In a ferromagnet, near the edges of the spectrum the electrons all have the same spin. In an ordinary antiferromagnet, electrons at the same energy level have mixed spins. The third kind is the Luttinger compensated (LC) magnet, which has zero net magnetism and yet has up and down spins separated by energy. The next section deals with this third kind.

The Spin Window and Room-Temperature Thermal Jiggling (About 26 meV)

What matters for storage is the spin window. The blog defines it as the slice of energy at the edge of the band gap where every available electron state has the same spin. The larger this window is compared with the thermal jiggling at room temperature, about 26 meV, the better the electrons stay sorted. For that reason every window quoted later in this article, 1.0 eV, 1.4 eV, 2.6 eV, and 1.6 eV, has to be read against 26 meV.

Luttinger-Compensated Magnets and the Definition of the Spin Window

In the blog's definition, LC materials are antiferromagnets where the spin-up atoms and spin-down atoms have the same magnitude of magnetism, making the net spin moment zero. What differs from an ordinary antiferromagnet is that the up and down atoms sit in inequivalent environments: they can be different elements, with one element pointing up and another down, or the same element in two different kinds of site. Since the up and down atoms are not equivalent, the blog says, up and down spins can be separated, or sorted, by energy, just like in ferro materials.

The name comes from Luttinger's theorem: in an insulator, the net spin moment of each repeating unit of the crystal must be a whole number, so once it is zero it stays locked at zero. The blog adds that, strictly, this holds for a perfect crystal near absolute zero, and that smaller effects such as spin-orbit coupling, and heat, can leave a slight imbalance. The repository README makes a related point: the zero net moment is a spin-only, collinear result, spin-orbit coupling and orbital moments were not computed reliably, and at finite temperature two inequivalent sublattices need not stay exactly compensated.

Both candidates were evaluated the same way. The agents ran quantum-mechanical simulations of each crystal with the standard method for this, density functional theory, at two levels of approximation. The faster one is PBE+U, and the slower, usually more accurate one is HSE06. The band gaps and spin windows in the blog come from the more accurate HSE06.

The points to hold on to when reading about LC magnets are these.

  • The scope of zero net spin moment: the statement is about a perfect crystal near absolute zero. Spin-orbit coupling and heat can leave a slight imbalance.
  • Inequivalent environments: the up and down atoms are different elements, or the same element on two different kinds of site.
  • Spin separation by energy: because the up and down atoms are not equivalent, the spins can be sorted by energy as in a ferromagnet.
  • Where the numbers come from: the band gaps and spin windows are values from HSE06, the more accurate of the two levels of approximation used in the DFT calculations.

Candidate 1 — The 2.35 eV Band Gap of Designed YBaMnFeO₅ and the Atomic Ordering Problem

According to the blog, the first candidate, designed by the author and a team of AI agents, is YBaMnFeO₅, a new compound made of only five elements: yttrium, barium, Mn, Fe, and oxygen. The blog writes that, as far as they could find, it has never been made, nor proposed as this kind of magnet.

The predicted values for this compound are the following.

  • Semiconductor: the compound was predicted to be a semiconductor.
  • Band gap and spin sorting: the band gap is 2.35 eV, and spin sorting occurs on both sides of the band gap.
  • Spin windows: 1.0 eV for holes and 1.4 eV for electrons. The blog adds that thermal agitation at room temperature only causes around 26 meV of fluctuation.
  • Magnetic ordering temperature: about 420 K in the raw simulation, or about 490 K after calibrating the simulation against a known magnet.
  • Collapse of the atomic arrangement: the Mn and Fe checkerboard falls apart into a random mix at around 950 K.

The blog explains that this seems like a pretty promising candidate, but the design needs the Mn and Fe atoms to sit in a perfect checkerboard. When the agents simulated how the atoms arrange themselves at different temperatures, that checkerboard fell apart into a random mix at around 950 K. Making this kind of oxide takes about 900 to 1300 °C, and at lower temperatures the atoms barely move, so standard synthesis would likely give a scrambled crystal. A scrambled crystal loses the spin sorting, so the blog concludes that this design may be hard to make in its useful form.

The repository README puts this somewhat more concretely. The collapse temperature of about 950 K is below the temperatures at which atoms move quickly during synthesis, and a single swapped Mn and Fe pair closes the spin-selective gap. The status table in the README also carries the grade from the agents' own adversarial review: a design study, not a realizable discovery.

Candidate 2 — 376 K Magnetic Order and Site Locking in KV[Cr(CN)₆], First Made in 1999

The second material, KV[Cr(CN)₆], was found, according to the blog, by the author's own agent (my agent found). It was previously reported only in 1999 and matches many of the requirements above. Its zero net magnetism is not new, because the chemists who made it designed the magnetism of the two metals to cancel. Even its spin sorting was already on paper. A 2008 study using hybrid functionals like those in the blog plotted its electron states spin by spin, and both band edges in that plot carry the same spin. But that study was about magnetic coupling under pressure and never remarked on it. As far as the blog found, nobody had pointed out that this makes KV[Cr(CN)₆] a Luttinger-compensated semiconductor, put numbers on its spin-sorted windows, or tested how robust they are. In the blog's words, it had been hiding in plain sight.

The repository README gives the bibliography. The experimental compound was reported by S. M. Holmes and G. S. Girolami in J. Am. Chem. Soc. 121, 5593 (1999). The 2008 study is the hybrid-functional work of Middlemiss, Lawton, and Wilson, and the README says the agents' literature search missed it and that the novelty claim was narrowed accordingly. KV[Cr(CN)₆] belongs to the same family as Prussian blue, the 300-year-old pigment.

The blog summarizes the properties of KV[Cr(CN)₆] as follows.

  • Predicted band gap and spin windows: the band gap is about 2.1 eV, with both band edges sorted into the same spin. The window is 2.6 eV for holes and 1.6 eV for electrons.
  • Magnetic order of the 1999 sample: as measured by the chemists who made it, the sample stayed magnetically ordered up to 376 K (103 °C), above room temperature, and 365 K after the sample had been heated.
  • Locking into sites: the structure locks each metal into its own site. Chromium bonds to the carbon end of each cyanide and vanadium to the nitrogen end, which the blog calls exactly what YBaMnFeO₅ lacked.
  • Net magnetism, predicted and in the sample: it is predicted to be zero for a perfect crystal, and the net magnetic moment of the 1999 sample is near zero.

The Gap Between an Ideal Dry Crystal Calculation and a Powder Sample Containing Water

The blog states plainly that the predictions above are for a perfect, dry crystal. The only sample so far, from 1999, is a powder with water in its pores. It showed a small leftover magnetic moment of 0.125 Bohr magnetons per formula unit, where the perfect crystal would have zero.

The differences between the ideal crystal and the real sample can be summarized as follows.

  • Residual magnetic moment: a perfect crystal has zero, but the 1999 sample showed 0.125 Bohr magnetons per formula unit.
  • Water in the pores: the sample is a powder holding water in its pores.
  • Disagreement between the two methods: on how much the water weakens the effect, the more accurate HSE06 says the spin sorting survives, while the faster PBE+U says the hole window shrinks by more than half.
  • Gaps in measurement: neither the band gap nor the spin sorting has been measured yet. The README sums up that no spin polarisation, conductivity, or band gap measurement exists for either material.

The README lists three experiments that would settle the matter. The first is to re-make KV[Cr(CN)₆] and measure its composition and saturation magnetisation, which it calls the cheapest test. The second is element-specific X-ray magnetic circular dichroism (XMCD) at the V and Cr L-edges, together with magneto-optics, across a series of compositions. The third is spin-resolved photoemission of the top valence band of anhydrous KV[Cr(CN)₆], where the prediction is about 100 % one spin over about 2 eV, reversing with the magnetic order. The README also warns that DFT with a Hubbard U, and even HSE06, can misplace energy levels by tenths of an eV.

The blog's summary is short. YBaMnFeO₅ is a designed candidate that may be hard to make. KV[Cr(CN)₆] was first made in 1999, has a near-zero net magnetic moment in the 1999 sample, and is magnetic up to 376 K. The blog writes that, in the calculations for perfect crystals, both materials have zero net spin moment yet are semiconductors whose electrons are sorted by spin over windows far larger than the thermal jiggling at room temperature, and that with little stray magnetic field they should not disturb their neighbours much.

The blog invites the reader to go through all the computations that produced these predictions. What has been shared in the public GitHub repository spicylemonade/compensated-magnet-ledger, which the author created to document the research journey, is as follows.

  • What the blog says it shares: the input files, raw outputs, and analysis code behind the numbers in the post, along with a one-command checker, independent re-runs, and a list of known caveats.
  • Level 1, checking the arithmetic: according to the README, the DFT numbers are recomputed from the raw Quantum ESPRESSO outputs in the repository, and the rest are checked against recorded analysis files. It needs only numpy and takes seconds on a laptop.
  • Level 2, re-running the models: the exchange constants and Néel temperature of YBaMnFeO₅ are re-derived from the raw energies, and the cation-order simulation that rules the design out is redone. The README records that the recorded 417 K became 414 ± 4 K on re-run.
  • Level 3, re-running the quantum-mechanical calculations: the same pseudopotentials (checked by md5) are downloaded and the original input files run with Quantum ESPRESSO 7.5. The README says that in a fresh cloud container four sets of runs were repeated and all 16 checked values matched the originals.

The README also reports five corrections found while the repository was built. The hull distance of YBaMnFeO₅ was recorded by the agents as +2.6 meV/atom because they computed it before every competing phase had finished, and with all 33 completed competitors it is +13.7 meV/atom. The HSE06 run for water-containing KV[Cr(CN)₆] stopped before its exact-exchange loop converged, and carried to convergence it gives a hole window of 2.31 eV and an electron window of 1.40 eV, with both edges still spin-sorted. Some cells were computed on geometries whose relaxation had stopped early. One of the 96 cation-ordering outputs was saved without its last lines, so its recorded energy cannot be re-derived. The last is the missed 2008 prior work described above. The README says none of the five changes the main conclusions.

The repository also states how the work was done. Several Claude agents worked in parallel on 1 to 4 October 2026, pass or fail rules were pre-registered before the decisive calculations, and adversarial referee agents tried to kill each claim. The computing ran on Modal cloud CPUs with Quantum ESPRESSO 7.5, and the README describes the human role as setting goals, directing the search, and deciding what to publish.

Unverified Aspects

  • The GeekNews topic 34847 page returned an access denial and could not be opened, so its Korean title and summary are unconfirmed.
  • The 1999 paper by Holmes and Girolami and the 2008 paper by Middlemiss, Lawton, and Wilson could not be opened in full. The bibliography in this article leans on what the repository README and LEDGER state.
  • The 2025 earlier study the blog mentions, which predicted two Luttinger-compensated semiconductors that lose magnetic order below room temperature, has no bibliography in the blog text. Only what LEDGER states was confirmed: Guo et al., arXiv:2502.18136, with Néel temperatures of 210 K and 75 K.
  • The 61 claims of the repository were not recomputed while writing this article. What is reported comes from the README, not from running the verification tools.
  • The number of agents and the structure of their individual prompts are not given in the blog or the README. The README says only that several agents worked in parallel.
  • Whether anyone has re-made KV[Cr(CN)₆] and measured its spin sorting directly is not confirmed. The blog presents that as the next step.
