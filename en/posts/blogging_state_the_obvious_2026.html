Blogging Can Just Be Stating the Obvious — How to Lift the Writing Burden

Why your obvious insight is valuable to someone else, and how to write consistently without perfectionism in the AI era

2026-06-27 Trends & Culture

Introduction: The Trap of "Everyone Already Knows This"

Have you ever started a blog post, gotten halfway through, and then deleted everything? The reason usually collapses down to one thought: "This is too obvious. Everyone already knows this."

This is one of the most common and most mistaken beliefs that stop people from writing. As of June 2026, the conversation around this topic has moved well beyond simple writing advice. In an era where AI generates information at will, there is a genuine and somewhat ironic dynamic emerging: human experience-based writing is becoming more valuable, not less. The statement "obvious is fine" has shifted from being a comforting platitude into a verifiable claim about how content actually works today.

This post examines three questions. First, why do we misjudge our own writing as obvious? Second, through what mechanism does so-called obvious writing actually create value? And third, what specific role does experiential human writing play in the AI era? The analysis draws on research, external sources, and direct observation of how the content landscape has shifted through 2026.

1. The Structure of the "Obvious" Illusion

1.1 The Curse of Knowledge

There is a well-established cognitive bias known as the Curse of Knowledge. Once you understand something, it becomes nearly impossible to remember what it felt like not to understand it. This is the root of the "it's too obvious" feeling. The content feels obvious because you already know it — not because it is obvious to everyone.

Consider an example. For a developer who already knows the difference between Git rebase and merge, writing about this topic feels like stating the obvious. But there is always someone on their first week using version control for whom this is genuinely new. The day that person finally understands it, they will search for exactly that explanation. Is a post that answers their question "obvious"? No. It is the post that changes their day.

1.2 Perfectionism as Imposter Syndrome in Disguise

The second structure that stops writing is imposter syndrome. "I am not a real expert in this area. Someone who knows more has already written about this better." This pairs directly with perfectionism: an internal standard that says you must produce a perfect post, a uniquely original post, an expert-only post, or you have no right to publish at all.

The paradox is sharp. Perfectionism operates as a defense mechanism against the feeling of being an imposter, yet that defense ultimately cuts off the very activity — writing and publishing — that would allow you to grow beyond the imposter feeling. Perfectionism is not the solution to imposter syndrome. It is the feedback loop that sustains it.

1.3 The Illusion of Required Originality

There is also the belief that your writing must be original: if someone has already covered the subject, writing about it again adds no value. But total originality does not exist. Austin Kleon argues in Show Your Work that creativity resembles collaboration and recombination far more than it resembles invention from nothing. The same fact becomes an entirely different piece of writing depending on who tells it, from what position, with what examples, and with what personal judgment attached. "Is this original?" is far less useful a question than "Does this carry my experience and perspective?"

2. Three Reasons Why Obvious Writing Has Value

2.1 Your Readers Are Not All at the Same Stage

For any topic, someone is encountering it for the first time every single day. Even if a well-written post on the same subject already exists somewhere on the internet, the person who lands on your blog may never have read it. Or they may have read it but find your framing, your examples, or your specific emphasis a better fit for how their mind works. The same content, delivered differently, becomes a different post.

A blog's readership is never uniform. Someone learning what you consider basic, someone who knows it but wants to see it through your lens, someone who went through the same confusion and wants to feel less alone — all of these are potential readers for content that you have already dismissed as obvious.

2.2 Your Context Is the Differentiator

Even when you write about a well-known fact, only you know which mistake led you to learn it, which situation caused the principle to break down unexpectedly, and which approach worked when you actually applied it. That context is the substance of the post. It is not information — it is experience and judgment.

Take "code review is important" as an example. That is obvious. But "our team skipped code reviews for six months and discovered a system-wide bug one hour before a major deployment, and here is exactly what we changed afterward" — that is not obvious. The fact is the same. The experience transforms it into something irreplaceable.

2.3 Accumulation Builds Something Worth More Than Any Single Post

Kleon puts it directly: a single blog post may be nothing, but writing a thousand posts over ten years becomes your life's work. The value of accumulation exceeds the value of any individual post. A single obvious-seeming post may look small in isolation, but it makes the next post possible, and a hundred posts together form something no one perfect essay could replicate.

A consistently maintained blog is not just a collection of text. It is evidence of how you learn, how your thinking evolves, and what you actually know how to do. This is often a more credible signal of expertise than any credential.

3. Learning in Public — The Power of Sharing the Process

3.1 The Fantasy That Only Finished Experts Can Write

Many people believe that blogging is for experts — that it means transmitting fully formed knowledge from a position of complete mastery. But some of the most resonant blog posts begin with "I am learning this right now." According to Ness Labs' analysis of learning in public, the core of this practice is sharing the process, not just the finished result: where you get stuck, what you initially got wrong, what suddenly clicked.

This creates a distinct kind of trust. The reader thinks: "This person is going through the same thing I am." That empathy becomes credibility over time in a way that polished expert content often cannot match.

3.2 The Writer Learns the Most

The act of writing accelerates learning. Nearly everyone who has tried to write about something they thought they understood has experienced the moment when the explanation falls apart mid-sentence. That breakdown reveals where the understanding has gaps. Finding better examples, anticipating objections, and structuring an argument forces the understanding to become more solid. Learning in public appears to benefit the reader, but it produces some of the deepest learning gains for the writer.

3.3 Write for One Reader, Not One Thousand

One of the most practical ways to reduce the writing burden is to reframe who you are writing for. Instead of asking "Will this help a thousand people?", ask "Will this help the one person who is dealing with exactly this problem right now?" The second question is far easier to answer yes to. And a post that genuinely helps that one person tends to reach many others with the same situation — because they search for the same things, in the same words.

4. What Experiential Writing Offers in the AI Era

4.1 AI Generates Information; It Cannot Generate Experience

As of June 2026, AI can produce plausible-sounding text on virtually any subject within seconds. The scarcity of information has ended. This raises a direct question: what is the value of human-written content? The answer is clear. Perspective, context, and judgment that come from lived experience. AI learns from patterns in data, but it cannot possess the texture of having failed, adjusted, and tried again in a specific situation with real stakes.

The World Economic Forum's January 2026 analysis identifies the human competencies that become most valuable in the AI era: the ability to ask better questions, navigate ambiguity, and make judgments grounded in actual experience. These are exactly the capacities that experiential writing both demonstrates and develops.

4.2 The Human-Made Premium

MindStudio's 2026 research describes a "human-made premium" — a dynamic where readers assign higher trust to content that was clearly written by a person who actually went through the experience being described. As AI-generated content floods the internet, readers increasingly look for authentic voice and personal perspective. Substack and similar individual newsletter platforms saw subscriber counts rise in 2026, not fall, as readers actively sought content that could not have been generated by a model.

4.3 What AI Cannot Write: Why I Was Wrong Today

There is one category of writing that AI fundamentally cannot produce: "I thought this was true today, but I was wrong, and here is how I found out and what I am doing differently." AI has no today. It has no experience of being wrong about a specific thing in a real situation. It has no habits to change. Posts that document this kind of concrete, situated error and correction are among the most human and most resonant writing possible — and they are exactly what AI cannot replicate. What you got wrong today is your best available writing material.

5. Practical Methods for Dropping the Perfectionism

5.1 Make Publishing the Goal, Not Perfection

The most important mindset shift is this: replace "write a good post" with "publish." An 80-point post that is actually live is better than a 100-point post that stays in drafts forever. You can only learn how your ideas land by publishing them. You can only understand your own communication patterns by seeing them in the world. The writing muscle is built by writing and publishing, not by reading about writing.

5.2 Mine Your Daily Learning for Material

Running out of material is a common anxiety that is almost always unfounded for anyone who is actively learning. One concept encountered for the first time today, one problem solved, one thing you explained to a colleague — these are all post material. The "explained to a colleague" category is especially useful: you have already done the work of translating the idea into terms that someone else can follow, which is the core work of writing a post.

5.3 Lower the Format Bar

A blog is not an academic paper. It does not have to be 3,000 words with a complete argument arc. A 400-word summary of one thing you learned today, a single-sentence core insight from a concept you are working through, a short account of a mistake and how it was corrected — all of this is legitimate blog content. Lowering the format threshold makes starting easier, and sustained output naturally develops depth over time.

5.4 Fix the Writing Time

Psychology research consistently shows that perfectionism exploits the moments when decisions have to be made: when to write, what to write. If the writing time is fixed — 30 minutes each morning, one hour on Sunday — then the decision is already made and perfectionism has fewer entry points. The schedule replaces the decision. This single structural change makes sustained writing far more achievable than relying on motivation or inspiration.

6. The Actual State of the Blog Ecosystem in 2026

6.1 Personal Voice Rising Against the AI Content Flood

The largest paradox in the 2026 content landscape is that as AI makes generating text easier, the perceived value of text that was clearly written by a specific human from specific experience increases. When anyone can produce generic content, distinctly personal content — real failures, minority opinions, unusual interpretations grounded in actual situations — becomes the differentiator rather than a liability.

6.2 What Changes When AI Overviews Take the Informational Layer

There is evidence that AI-generated search overviews have reduced organic traffic to purely informational posts. But this trend points in the same direction: posts that just compile facts face headwinds, while posts that offer distinct perspective and experience-based judgment hold their ground. What you cannot get from an AI overview is what happened when someone actually tried a thing and encountered the specific friction that the documentation never mentions. That is where personal blog posts still win.

6.3 The Most Expensive Opportunity Cost: Not Writing

The most common regret among people who eventually build consistent writing practices is that they did not start earlier. The post you did not write because it felt too obvious is a post that no longer exists as an artifact of what you understood at a specific moment in time. Experiences and learning decay. Without the writing to fix them, even the most significant realizations fade. Writing is how learning becomes durable — for yourself first, and potentially for others second.

Conclusion: One Sentence You Can Write Right Now

The core claim of this post in one sentence: When you think "this is obvious," it is only obvious to you. For someone encountering it for the first time, it is new. For someone who went through the same confusion, it is recognition. With your specific context added, it is a document that exists nowhere else.

In an era when AI generates information without limit, the value of human writing lies not in information but in experience. The thing you understood for the first time today, the thing you got wrong and corrected, the thing you are explaining to yourself for the first time — this is your best writing material. It does not need to be perfect. It does not need to be original. It needs to be written.
