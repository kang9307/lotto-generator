Why Hidden LLM Reasoning Leaks

What the sealed intermediate output really is, and what a team calling the API should check today

Aug 16, 2026 Tech & IT

A design that hands the secret to the client

Anyone who has wired up a reasoning model over an API has seen the unreadable lump that comes back with the response. The name differs by provider. One calls it an encrypted reasoning item, another a thought signature, a third simply a thinking block. Two things are true of all of them. A human cannot read it, and the conversation only continues if you send it back untouched on the next request.

This is not laziness in the design. Request-and-response APIs treat statelessness as either the default or a first-class option. OpenAI documentation states that in stateless mode — when store is false, or when the organisation is on zero data retention — reasoning items in the response include an encrypted_content property by default. Google documentation defines a thought signature as an encrypted representation of the model internal reasoning state and, in stateless use, requires that every thought block be resent exactly as it was received. Anthropic documentation likewise instructs callers to pass thinking blocks back unchanged on the following turn.

Put plainly: if nothing is stored on the server and the next turn still has to build on earlier reasoning, the only option is to seal that intermediate output, hand it to the client, and take it back. A choice made to satisfy data-minimisation requirements ends up moving the secret physically outside the trust boundary. The oldest lesson in web security starts over here — why you cannot trust the client applies to LLM APIs as much as to anything else.

What follows treats that structure at the level of principle. It contains no reproducible procedure and no request shapes. There are three questions on the table: what property causes information to leak, what a provider can change, and what a team calling the API can check this week.

Why the reasoning is hidden at all — cost, safety, competition

Three distinct motives overlap in the decision to hide intermediate reasoning. Separating them makes the price of each mitigation easier to read later.

  • Competition. A raw chain of thought is premium teaching material for distillation. Final answers alone let a competitor imitate outcomes; the path to the answer transfers reasoning ability with far less data. This is the asset providers most want to protect.
  • Safety. Even when the final answer refuses a request, the intermediate process may contain text written while examining that request. Safety judgements usually apply to the final output, so exposing intermediate output raw routes around the checkpoint.
  • Product coherence. Unpolished reasoning misleads people. A hypothesis the model briefly entertained and discarded reads like a settled judgement, and that failure mode is common. Hence most providers surface a summary rather than the original.
  • Cost and latency. Reasoning tokens are billable while frequently being of no use on screen. Streaming only the summary saves bandwidth and rendering work.

Hiding has a cost on the consumer side too. Auditing, debugging and regulatory response all get harder, and in any workflow that has to explain why the model reached a conclusion, opacity becomes an adoption barrier in itself. That tension does not resolve anywhere in this article.

Narrowing hidden state from partial information, and the countermeasures

Side channels are an old subject in cryptographic implementation. If a smart card power draw or an operation timing is a function of the key, candidates can be eliminated without ever reading the key. The countermeasures are equally old — flatten the consumption, make the timing constant, add noise.

Two things make the principle sharper in the LLM setting.

  • Partial information is immediately usable. In cryptanalysis, key bits mean nothing until enough of them accumulate. A reasoning trace that leaks halfway is already readable and comprehensible by a person. The threshold for a useful recovery is low.
  • The observation channels are shipped as product features. Token-usage reporting, latency, summary display and cache-hit rates all exist to help developers. Removing them makes the product worse.

So the distinction that matters operationally is between full recovery and candidate narrowing. Most side channels only narrow candidates — which is nevertheless enough for competitive intelligence in some cases. A sealed block with no binding, by contrast, is not narrowing at all; it is transfer. Putting both in the same risk bucket produces the wrong priority order.

A seal needs three properties: confidentiality so the contents cannot be read, integrity so tampering is detected, and binding so the block is valid only for this organisation, this conversation, this model. The first two are textbook items and are generally implemented well. Real incidents come from the third.

What the research actually showed — scope and premises

Alexander Panfilov and seven co-authors published Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs (arXiv 2608.09867) on 10 August 2026. The architectural flaw described in their abstract reduces to a single sentence: the encrypted blocks are fully compatible and interchangeable across different sessions, users and models within one provider ecosystem. Because of that compatibility, a path exists to obtain a stronger model traces in plaintext without attacking that model directly.

The authors set out four consequences.

  • Circumvention of anti-distillation mechanisms. A defence that relied on concealment stops holding. The paper records demonstrating this axis across Anthropic, OpenAI and Google.
  • Large-scale private-data exposure. The premise is that developers publish session logs while unaware of what the sealed blocks contain. The authors report decoding 315,320 reasoning blocks scraped from public repositories and recovering 367 personally identifiable information artifacts and 182 credentials.
  • Inadvertent exposure of hazardous content. Even where the final visible output safely rejected a malicious request, material left inside the reasoning process can surface.
  • Invisible prompt injection. A payload placed entirely inside a sealed block can poison public agentic rollouts. Nothing is visible to a human reader.

Three framings are worth attaching. First, this is not a story about breaking a model; it is a story about the binding of a seal token. The cryptography is not weak — the gap is in never deciding what the seal is tied to. Second, the scale of the second consequence is the product of a provider design and a consumer habit of publishing logs. Fixing either side reduces it. Third, the demonstrated scope is exactly what the paper states, and this article ranks no provider above another. Any ecosystem using the same design pattern faces the same question.

The authors state that they followed a responsible-disclosure process and propose concrete cryptographic and system-level mitigations for securing client-side reasoning.

What the research did not show

Cutting the scope precisely is more useful than overstating it.

  • It is not a claim about obtaining model weights or training data. What is obtained is reasoning traces.
  • It is not a claim about reading arbitrary third-party conversations at will. The data-recovery axis targets logs that were already public.
  • When and what each provider changed is outside the abstract. Proposing a mitigation and having one deployed are separate events.
  • There are no attack cost or success-rate figures in the abstract, which is why none appear in this article.
  • Security findings are always a snapshot of one moment. Whether the same property persists today depends on each provider current implementation.

The question a practitioner should take away is therefore not whether something is broken right now. It is whether your own system has been designed so that it does not depend on the properties of a sealed block. That question stays valid no matter what any provider has already fixed.

The third row deserves attention. Server-side storage is the most reliable defence, but the client round trip exists in the first place precisely because of a requirement to leave nothing on the server. Increase retention for security and the privacy promise breaks; reduce retention for privacy and the secret goes out to the client. That trade does not disappear in principle, so realistic answers concentrate on the first two rows — bind the seal to context and keep its lifetime short.

Normalisation in the fifth row is routinely underrated. Rounding token usage into buckets and padding latency blurs the top three rows of Table 1 considerably. The catch is that developers then struggle to forecast spend, so a practical split is to expose coarse figures in real time and exact figures at reconciliation.

The table looks like it is for providers, but it is useful on the consumer side too, because these are cells you cannot change and can nonetheless choose between. Asking during procurement and contract review whether the first two rows exist — what scope the seal is bound to, whether it has a lifetime — surfaces real differences between candidate providers. If the documentation has no answer, that is the answer, and it should be read as a signal to lay the consumer-side measures below on more thickly.

Consumer side: the assumptions to hold when sending sensitive input

Provider mitigations are something you wait for. What the consumer controls, by contrast, is mostly changeable today and cheap. Start from four assumptions.

  • What you sent does not live only in the final answer. It is in the intermediate output too. Masking applied only to the final output covers half the surface.
  • The intermediate output passes through your process. That means your logs, your traces, your issue tracker, your screenshots. The exposed surface is your storage, not the provider infrastructure.
  • Unreadable does not mean absent. A seal removes neither retention obligations nor breach risk.
  • A safe-looking summary does not make the original safe. What appears on screen has been through a filter; the block you send back is from before that filter.

The actions that follow are mostly log hygiene.

  • Do not serialise whole response objects into application logs or your APM. Strip the sealed-block field before writing, or keep only its length.
  • Remove session dumps from the default attachments in support-ticket and bug-report templates. The place people make mistakes is nearly always a template default.
  • Keep conversation transcripts out of public repositories, public issues and demo notebooks. Secret scanners match plaintext patterns and will not catch credentials sitting inside a sealed blob.
  • When sharing agent run records outside the team, strip sealed blocks or replace them with placeholders.
  • Do not write code that reuses a sealed block in a different conversation. Check whether conversation fragments leak into global scope through your cache-key design.
  • Do not concatenate conversation history from untrusted sources as-is. If prompt-injection checks only inspect human-readable text, a sealed block sails straight through the net.
  • Keep the admissibility rules themselves in a separate policy. What may be sent at all is covered in can company data go into an LLM through classification and routing; this article assumes that policy already exists.

If only one implementation detail is worth insisting on, it is where the masking lives. Scatter the sealed-block removal across every call site and the next piece of new code will punch through it. Put it in one place every response must pass — an SDK wrapper or logging middleware — and block direct calls that bypass that point with a static check. If you already run an internal data classification table, assigning the sealed block an explicit tier there is cheap insurance. Data with no name in the table is data nobody handles, and data nobody handles ends up in the logs.

If it is already out there — detection and response

Running the checklist usually turns up a few hits, and this is where teams get the order wrong. The most common mistake is starting by deleting what was published. A public repository or issue has to be assumed copied, and deletion does not reverse exposure. The only thing that can be reversed is a credential.

  • Establish scope first. Which repositories, which time window, which workload the records came from. The point at which sealed blocks began appearing in responses is where the investigation window starts.
  • Rotation comes before deletion. If pinning down exactly what was inside is hard, taking every secret that workload could reach as the scope is faster in the end.
  • Put the recurrence fix in the same week. Cleaning up after the fact and leaving the pipeline untouched means the same thing happens next week. Adding the masking point is part of the same unit of work.
  • Record the basis for the decision. Whether a notification duty applies is for legal and privacy to judge, and that judgement needs a record of what was where, starting when.

Detection deserves a line too. Most organisations watch for plaintext secrets landing in a repository, but nobody watches for sealed blocks landing in a repository. Where the field has a consistent shape, that shape is itself usable as a detection rule. You can conclude that something is present which should not be, without reading it — and most of the response work only needs that conclusion to begin. The place to put the rule already exists too — a commit hook, the secret-scan stage in CI, or a repository scanner. Any one of the three is enough; all three is not required.

What to verify in contracts and terms of service

Some items are answered by contract documents before any technical control matters, and provider documentation tends to be reasonably explicit here. Google Gemini API additional terms separate the data handling of free and paid services and carve out regional exceptions. OpenAI data-controls documentation describes training exclusion for API inputs and outputs, retention periods, and the scope over which zero data retention applies, each as its own topic. Both are as of August 2026, and documents like these are revised often enough that reopening them at adoption time is the safer habit.

The questions worth putting to a contract review are these.

  • Is intermediate output — reasoning tokens, thinking blocks — inside the contractual definition of output? The reach of any training-exclusion clause depends on the answer.
  • How is intermediate output treated when zero retention is enabled? Remember that the client round trip is itself a consequence of the zero-retention requirement.
  • Retention period and access rights for reasoning summaries. Summaries are sometimes treated differently from originals.
  • The clause prohibiting reverse engineering of reasoning traces. It sits in most terms and is the starting point for the disputes discussed below.
  • Who is obliged to notify, and within what window, if intermediate output is exposed. If the leak comes from your own logs, the notifying party is not the provider.
  • How accumulated conversation history transfers when a model is deprecated or swapped. That axis continues directly into the design problem covered in escaping AI vendor lock-in.

Run contract review separately from the engineering team and all six of these usually drop out wholesale, because they sit where legal cannot judge alone and an engineer cannot read alone. Adding a single line labelled intermediate output to the adoption review document, so that both sides are looking at the same line, removes most of the omissions on its own. Put the re-check interval on that line as well — documents like these are revised quietly, and there is rarely anything that tells you it happened.

Distillation and model extraction — what is actually contested

From here on this is not legal advice, and this article asserts no conclusion. What can be laid out is the axes along which the argument runs.

  • Breach of contract. Major provider terms widely prohibit using outputs to train a competing model. In practice this is the axis addressed first.
  • Copyright. Whether model output attracts copyright at all is disputed across jurisdictions, so there is no single construction. Whether a trace counts as part of the output is contested alongside it.
  • Trade secrets. The dividing line is whether the provider is recognised as having taken reasonable measures to keep the material secret. The existence of the concealment measures can itself be cited as evidence of those efforts.
  • Computer-access statutes. Whether a terms violation amounts to unauthorised access is decided differently in different jurisdictions. The same facts can be assessed differently country by country.
  • Personal data. If a trace contains personal data, controller obligations attach regardless of whether it was hidden. Unlike the four axes above, this one leaves little room for argument.

Only one thing on that list is operationally certain. The reasoning that says it is protected because it was hidden is weak both technically and legally. On the technical side the claim holds only if the seal is bound to context; on the legal side the real line of defence is what the contract document says. Concealment substitutes for neither.

What could not be confirmed

Collected in one place: the items this article does not assert as fact or figure.

  • Timing and content of provider-side fixes. The paper states that responsible disclosure took place and proposes mitigations, but which provider changed what, and when, is not established in the abstract.
  • Breakdown of credential types. Counts by category — keys, passwords, tokens — appear in secondary summaries but sit outside the abstract, so they are not carried over here.
  • Current reproducibility. Whether the same property still holds depends on each provider present implementation, and this article did not test it.
  • Attack cost and success rate. No figures appear in the abstract, so none were written down.
  • Cases in Korea. No reported instance of this exposure class in a Korean corporate environment was confirmed.
  • Quantitative contribution of each side channel. How much each row of Table 1 narrows candidates varies by system, and no generalised measurement was confirmed.

Frequently asked questions

Why does the client hold the encrypted reasoning block instead of the server?

Most reasoning-model APIs offer a stateless mode, either by default or as an option, in which no conversation state is kept on the server. If nothing is stored server-side and the next turn still has to build on earlier reasoning, the intermediate output has to be handed to the client and taken back. A choice made to satisfy zero-retention and data-minimisation requirements ends up moving the secret physically outside the trust boundary.

If the block is sealed, is it not unreadable by definition?

A seal needs three properties: confidentiality, integrity, and binding that makes it valid only for this conversation, this user and this model. With the first two present and the third missing, the block stops being context and becomes portable data. That missing binding is what arXiv 2608.09867 identifies. Nothing about the cryptography is weak.

What did the paper actually demonstrate?

Four things, per the abstract: circumvention of anti-distillation mechanisms, recovery of private data from published session logs, exposure of hazardous content left in the reasoning even when the visible answer safely refused, and prompt injections hidden entirely inside sealed blocks. The authors report decoding 315,320 reasoning blocks scraped from public repositories and recovering 367 personally identifiable information artifacts and 182 credentials. It is not a claim about obtaining model weights or reading arbitrary third-party conversations.

What can a team calling the API do today?

Log hygiene is the cheapest fix. Stop serialising whole response objects into logs, and strip or mask the sealed block before anything is written. Remove session dumps from the default attachments in support and bug-report templates, and keep transcripts out of public repositories and demo notebooks. Secret scanners look for plaintext patterns, so credentials inside a sealed blob will not be caught.

What is the legal position on training another model on extracted reasoning traces?

This is not legal advice and no conclusion is asserted. The contested axes are clear enough: breach of the contractual prohibition on training competing models, whether model output attracts copyright, whether trade-secret protection is recognised, whether a terms violation constitutes unauthorised access, and controller obligations when a trace contains personal data. Apart from the last item, none of these resolve the same way across jurisdictions.

Conclusion: hiding is not the same as protecting

Three things to carry away from this episode.

  1. The problem sits at the binding layer, not the cryptography. Handing sealed state to the client is a design with legitimate reasons behind it. The failure appears when nobody decides which context the seal is tied to. Ask the same question of your own session tokens and signed cookies.
  2. The blast radius is set by consumer log habits. Bulk recovery from public repositories was possible because sealed blocks were published without review, on the grounds that they were unreadable. That half can be fixed today without waiting for anyone, and the cost is reworking one point in the logging pipeline.
  3. Do not place inspection points only on human-readable text. Whether the control is prompt-injection defence or secret scanning, an opaque blob left outside the inspected set becomes the channel.

The common mistake when reading security research is to look only at what broke and miss what was being relied upon. What this work unsettled is not a specific product but the assumption that concealment equals protection. Counting how many defences in your own system rest on that assumption is a better first move than waiting for a provider notice.
