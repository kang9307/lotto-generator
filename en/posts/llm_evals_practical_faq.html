An Eval Without a Definition of Failure Only Produces Numbers - Building Your Own LLM Eval

Where eval data comes from, what grades it, how you validate the grader, and how it hangs on CI

Aug 16, 2026 Dev & Infra
What this article covers
  • Why the definition of failure is written before the metric, and what that sentence looks like
  • Six sampling strategies for pulling eval data out of production traces
  • Where synthetic data earns its place and where it collapses
  • Cost and trust conditions for rule, model and human grading, plus a routing rule
  • Three kinds of judge bias, and how to validate a judge against human labels
  • How to set CI thresholds on non-deterministic output
  • Three routes by which scores rise while the product gets worse

Introduction: the dashboard says 0.82. Did the product get better?

The eval script is wired up, the pipeline runs, a number appears on the dashboard. 0.82 this week, 0.79 last week. Somebody in the meeting asks what those three points mean.

The reason the answer stalls is not a missing tool. The order is inverted. A score is the sentence what counts as failure translated into a number. Without that sentence, 0.82 does not mean a particular failure dropped from so many cases to so many - it is just 0.82. When it rises nobody can say what improved, and when it falls nobody knows what to fix.

What follows is about an instrument that measures how your prompt, in your product, breaks on your users' input. Leaderboard scores and the trustworthiness of public benchmarks are a separate problem, covered in what knowledge benchmarks will not tell you and why a company built its own benchmark. Who in the organisation owns this work and on what review cadence is handled in how eval-driven development is run. This piece sits in between, on the engineering: data, grading, validation, gates.

1. What counts as failure - the sentence that precedes the score

1.1 Failure definitions come from observation, not imagination

The most common opening move is to draft a list of metrics. Accuracy, relevance, harmfulness, concision. That list attaches to any product and fits none of them, because it leaves out how your users actually get hurt.

Invert the order. Read the outputs first and pull the metrics out of them. The work has names borrowed from qualitative research: reading traces and jotting free-form notes on what looks wrong is open coding, and grouping those notes into failure types is axial coding. Categories do not come first with notes filed into them - the notes create the categories. Automation can help with the grouping at the back; the reading at the front has to be human or no information enters.

A trace here means everything the system did for one user query. Not just the final answer, but the prompt as actually assembled, tool calls and their return values, and any retrieved documents. That distinction pays later. Store only the final answer and you cannot reconstruct why it was wrong, and the failure type collapses into one line: sometimes says weird things.

Fix a stopping point too. It goes by theoretical saturation, and the usable form is this: if twenty consecutive traces surface no new failure category, that round is done. A hundred traces is a commonly cited starting volume. A plan to read a thousand usually stalls around three hundred, and those three hundred sit unprocessed.

1.2 Land the judgement on pass or fail

A one-to-five scale looks appealing because it seems to carry more information. The problem is whether you can write down the difference between a three and a four. If you cannot, that scale becomes a different axis for every annotator, and averaging it averages different axes.

Primary sources split here. The practitioner FAQ argues for binary judgements; Anthropic's guide to writing evaluations ships a Likert-scale grading example as-is. Neither is wrong - the premises differ. If the property really is continuous enough to warrant grades, a scale fits; if you cannot write the rubric as a sentence, the scale manufactures noise.

When you do need grades, splitting into several binary items is easier to handle than a scale. The sum becomes the grade, and you also learn where it failed.

{
  "trace_id": "t-10482",
  "checks": {
    "grounded":      0,   // cited a policy clause absent from retrieved docs
    "no_jargon":     1,
    "format_valid":  1,
    "answered":      1    // did not refuse an answerable question
  },
  "label_source": "human",
  "notes": "Stated a 14-day refund window. Source document says 7."
}

1.3 How to test a failure definition

There is one test for whether a failure definition is usable. Do two different people looking at the same output reach the same conclusion?

  • Bad - is the answer sufficiently helpful? People differ
  • Bad - does the answer match our brand tone? The tone is not written down anywhere to begin with
  • Good - citing a figure or clause absent from the retrieved documents is a failure
  • Good - leaving any one of the user's asked items unanswered is a failure
  • Good - exposing an internal system name to the user is a failure

Attach a model judge while two humans still disagree and the judge picks a side at random and records agreement around half. What needs fixing then is not the grading prompt but the definition. Automate on top of unresolved human disagreement and what you have automated is not judgement but confusion.

2. Where eval data comes from

2.1 The first source is production traces

This is the part most often skipped. Metric names get settled in one meeting, but what you measure them against is always a data problem - and if the logs are not there you stop right here. At minimum, keep the following.

  • The raw user input and its timestamp
  • The full prompt as actually assembled and sent to the model
  • Model identifier, version and sampling parameters such as temperature
  • Tool call names, arguments and return values
  • If there is a retrieval step, the retrieved document identifiers and scores
  • The final output and the response latency
  • User behaviour signals - immediate re-ask, conversation abandonment, copy, explicit feedback

That last line pays the most later. The fact that a user asked the same question again is a more accurate failure signal than any automatic grader.

2.2 Random sampling alone will not surface failures

In a system with a five per cent failure rate, a random hundred contains five failures. One or two of them belong to any given type, and one or two cases are not a type. So you mix your sampling.

  • Random - the baseline that shows the distribution. Some of your sample must come from here
  • Stratified - build cells by user type, request category, input length and language, then fill each cell
  • Outlier - the longest and shortest responses, the slowest, the ones with the most tool calls
  • Signal-driven - traces carrying negative feedback, an immediate re-ask or mid-conversation abandonment
  • Clustered - embed the inputs, cluster them, and draw from each cluster. You get a hundred different questions instead of one question a hundred times
  • Existing graders - whatever the rule checks already running have flagged
Always record where each sample came from. Random samples tell you about the distribution; biased samples tell you about failures. Mix them and compute one overall pass rate and that number represents no population at all. Attach a field such as sample_source to every case and you can pull out the random portion later and compute a real pass rate.

2.3 Collect failures first, but not only failures

On a set of nothing but good outputs, a judge that always says pass records high agreement. That is an illusion, not performance. Collect only failures and the pass rate loses meaning and you cannot measure the judge's false positive tendency.

What has to be separated here is not a ratio but the sets themselves. Build two and keep them apart.

  • Calibration set - its purpose is to test whether the judge discriminates. Roughly balance pass and fail, and value boundary cases over obvious ones
  • Tracking set - its purpose is the pass-rate trend. Preserve the production distribution as it is

Merge them and you get neither. The extra failures in the calibration portion push the pass rate below reality, and preserving the tracking distribution means the judge is being tested on skewed data.

2.4 Personal data when logs become an eval set

Logs are sentences your users wrote. The moment they enter an eval set, several people read them, they travel to an external grading API, they get committed to a repository and they stay for months. That needs a control separate from your retention policy.

  • Apply masking to the eval-set copy, never the original log. Destroy the original and incident investigation becomes impossible
  • Masking changes the distribution. Replace every name with the same placeholder and model behaviour itself shifts. Confirm the same failure still reproduces after masking before you admit the case
  • Keep eval-set access rights separate from production log access rights
  • Set a retention period, and design it so expired cases drop out of the pass-rate computation too

3. Synthetic data - where it earns its place and where it collapses

3.1 An open prompt produces the same thing repeatedly

Ask for a hundred customer enquiries and you get the same question a hundred times in different words. Given an open instruction, a generative model converges on what it considers most plausible. The pass rate of a set built that way is the pass rate for that one kind of question.

The remedy is defining the dimensions by hand first. Set up axes such as user type, request category, difficulty and input form, build the combinations, and generate per combination. Expanding the full cross product and then filtering out the nonsensical ones works too. Either way the point is the same: dimension design is the declaration of what you claim to cover, and without it the word coverage does not mean anything.

3.2 Four conditions where synthetic data breaks

  • Complex specialist domains - the generating model does not know the real query distribution
  • Low-resource languages - expression variety and error types come out unlike the real thing
  • High-stakes applications - one wrong synthetic case cements a wrong standard
  • Underrepresented groups - groups that were scarce in the original data get scarcer in generation

All four share a structure. If the generating model does not know the distribution, the synthetic set is the distribution the model imagined, not the one your users have. Grade it with a model from the same family and the grading happens entirely inside that imagination. English is hardly low-resource, yet the same trap appears in business documents where internal jargon, register and institution-name conventions are mixed.

3.3 Where synthetic data clearly earns its place

  • An early prototype with no users - the stage where you want the shape of the failures, not a pass rate
  • Rare inputs you must handle anyway - injection attempts, empty input, extreme length, mixed languages, broken encodings
  • Boundary cases for judge calibration - obvious passes and obvious failures are graded correctly by any judge
  • Fixed regression cases - production data keeps moving, but a regression test has to stay still
Never report a synthetic pass rate as a product quality metric. Synthetic cases are an instrument for finding out what breaks, not for finding out how good things are. Put the two on the same dashboard and they will get conflated.

4. Three kinds of grader - cost and trust conditions

There are only three graders - rules, models and humans - and they are not alternatives but instruments with different properties. Choosing exactly one usually ends up either expensive or inaccurate.

AxisRule-basedModel gradingHuman grading
What it catchesFormat, schema, length, banned terms, numeric matchFactual grounding, tone, completeness, refusalSafety, legal exposure, subtle context

The most important row is how it fails. Rule-based grading passes things silently, model grading confidently reports a wrong number, and human grading gets adopted but never actually runs. A design that uses all three is arranging for those three failures to cover one another.

5. The nature of the output decides which grader gets it

Fix the routing rule in advance and the arguments disappear.

  • Is there exactly one right answer? → Rules. Exact match or a numeric tolerance
  • Is it a structural or format requirement? → Rules. Schema validation and parsing finish the job
  • Is it something that must never appear? → Rules first, model as backup. Blocking has to be fast and deterministic
  • Is it factual agreement against a reference document? → Model grading, but the reference has to go into the grading prompt
  • Is it tone, completeness or usefulness? → Model grading plus a human sample
  • Is it safety, regulation or legal exposure? → Humans. Money saved here gets paid back with interest

Do not ask a model what a rule can decide. Asking an LLM whether the JSON failed to parse buys cost and latency in exchange for lower accuracy. If it is unclear which items are decidable by rule at all, the method for drawing that line is set out in the boundary a verification harness draws.

Order is part of the cost design too. Re-measuring in an expensive layer what a cheap layer already filtered spends money without adding information. Send a malformed output to a model judge and the judge reacts to the malformation and marks the content down as well.

6. The trap in model grading - what the judge is drawn to

A model judge is not a proxy for a human but another model, and it responds to different things. The three below have been measured in published work, so you can design around them up front.

BiasDrawn toWhat was measuredMitigation
Position biasWhatever is placed first in the promptMeasured over 15 judges, 22 tasks and more than 150,000 instances. Grows as the quality gap between candidates narrows; only weakly related to prompt lengthGrade twice with the order swapped and void the case if the verdict flips
Length biasThe longer answerA preference for length was confirmed in pairwise automatic evaluation. After a length-controlled regression correction, rank correlation with human preference rose from 0.94 to 0.98Put length in the rubric as its own item, or control for length in the correction
Familiarity biasText that does not read as unfamiliar to itModels score lower-perplexity text more generously than humans do. The explanation offered is familiarity rather than authorshipUse a different model for grading than for generation, and split the family too

The third row is the one most often ignored in practice. Generate and grade with the same model and the pipeline gets simpler while the judge starts rewarding its own style. Anthropic's evaluation guide also lists using a different model for grading as general best practice. If budget forces the same model, at least measure agreement with human labels and confirm the gap is inside your tolerance first.

The condition on position bias is worth a second look. That it grows as the quality gap narrows means the bias peaks when comparing prompt A against A with one line changed - which is precisely what we use evals for.

7. Validating the judge against human labels

7.1 Accuracy does not measure a judge

The most common mistake in judge validation is reading accuracy. On data with a low failure rate, accuracy reflects the skew of the data rather than the discrimination of the judge. Below are values computed from confusion matrices for four judges run against a hundred human-labelled cases.

function stats(tp, fn, fp, tn) {          // tp = human pass & judge pass
  const n = tp + fn + fp + tn;
  const po = (tp + tn) / n;                // raw agreement
  const pe = ((tp+fn)/n)*((tp+fp)/n) + ((fp+tn)/n)*((fn+tn)/n);
  return {
    acc:   po,
    kappa: (po - pe) / (1 - pe),           // Cohen's kappa
    tpr:   tp / (tp + fn),                 // pass judged as pass
    tnr:   tn / (tn + fp)                  // fail judged as fail
  };
}

Three of the four judges have the same 0.880 accuracy. Their kappas spread from 0.189 to 0.760. And A, which does nothing at all, has the highest accuracy with a kappa of exactly zero. Put A on the dashboard on the strength of accuracy and it prints 90 per cent pass rate every day, a number that responds to no prompt change whatsoever.

Kappa subtracts the share that agreement would reach by chance. With three or more annotators, or with ordered grade labels, Krippendorff's alpha is easier to work with.

7.2 Read the true negative rate separately

The TNR column is the crux. B catches two of ten failures. Attach that judge and the pass rate looks lovely while the failures ship. The opposite risk exists too: tighten the rubric to raise TNR and normal outputs start being marked as failures, and after a few weeks of such alerts the team stops looking at the dashboard.

  • Items where failures must not escape - prioritise TNR and let humans filter the false alarms
  • Items used as blocking gates - prioritise TPR, since one false alarm stops a release
  • If both demands exist at once, split the item. Trying to satisfy both with one judge usually misses both

7.3 The judge is a versioned artefact too

If you edited the rubric to raise agreement, that rubric is now fitted to that set. Split the human-labelled set in two from the start, tune against one half only, and produce the final number from an untouched holdout. Skip this and agreement climbs while nothing changes on new data.

  • Version the grading prompt, model and temperature. Change the judge and past scores are no longer comparable
  • On the day you swap judges, run the old one once more and overlap the two series
  • Refresh the human-labelled set when failure types change. That is event-driven, not calendar-driven

Version control and rollback for the prompt itself are covered in change, verification and rollback design for production prompts. A judge needs one more layer of that discipline: when the measuring instrument wobbles, you cannot see change in the thing being measured.

8. Putting evals in CI - non-determinism, thresholds, regression calls

8.1 Do not decide on a single run

The starting condition of this problem is that identical input does not return identical output. Dropping temperature to zero does not fully remove it; batch composition, hardware and provider-side updates remain.

So the first job is not setting a threshold but measuring the width of the wobble. Run the same case set several times with no code change and look at the spread of pass rates across rounds. Without that width, every drop reads as a regression or every drop reads as noise. If the width is large, add cases or quarantine the ones that swing most.

8.2 Thresholds relative to a baseline, not absolute

An absolute line like a 0.85 pass rate gets picked without grounds, and every time it blocks someone a human lowers it. A few months on it sits where it guarantees a pass.

  • Run the previous release on the same data for the same number of rounds
  • Compare the two distributions and ask whether the drop exceeds the run-to-run wobble
  • Look per item. A flat headline number while one failure type doubles is common
  • Emit the list of individual cases that newly turned into failures. That list finds the cause faster than the number

8.3 CI evals and monitoring evals are different objects

AxisCI gateProduction monitoring
DataCurated fixed casesA sample of live traffic
GradingRule-centric, deterministicModel-centric, asynchronous
Time budgetMinutesUnconstrained
On failureBlock the mergeAlert and review samples
Change cadenceRarely, deliberatelyWhenever a new failure type appears

Attempts to merge the two into one pipeline fail often. Put two hundred model-graded cases in CI and the pipeline gets slow, expensive and occasionally red for no reason. A few weeks later somebody makes that step conditional, and the gate is gone. The combination that survives has two layers: rule-decidable items block if a single case breaks, and model-graded metrics warn rather than block while a human looks.

9. When the score rises and the product gets worse

9.1 Goodhart's law - the moment the measure becomes the target

Once a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure. In LLM evals this law bites unusually fast. Let it become known that the judge prefers longer answers and prompts quietly grow, scores rise, and users stop reading. The length bias from earlier turns directly into cost here.

Rule-based items are no safer. Impose passes if it includes a citation from the source documents and the model will cite an irrelevant document. A rule looks at form, not intent.

9.2 Eval set ageing - the team learns to pass it

Look at the same hundred cases for six months and the team learns how to pass those hundred cases. Exception handling accumulates in the prompt, and each exception targets a specific case in the set. At that point the eval set is not an exam paper but training data. Structurally it is the same as public benchmark contamination, and being your own set makes it harder to notice.

The same problem has another face: the lifespan of the eval format itself. As tasks lengthen and steps multiply, grading a single output loses resolution. That transition is set out separately in evaluation moving to long-horizon work.

9.3 Distribution shift - the population you were measuring is gone

  • The user base changes - moving from an internal pilot to public launch changes the register of the input itself
  • The acquisition channel changes - users arriving from search ask different things than users arriving in-app
  • A feature ships - and the eval set contains not a single input for it
  • Seasons and events - tax season, term start, an outage notice, and the question distribution shifts wholesale
  • An upstream system changes - refresh the search index and the same question pulls different documents

9.4 Defences - three are enough

  • Rotation - swap a fixed proportion of the set into fresh logs each round, and score the rotated and fixed portions separately
  • Holdout - keep a set the team cannot see. If the score fails to move only there, the optimisation was aimed at the eval set
  • Parallel product metrics - watch signals independent of the eval, such as re-ask rate, abandonment rate and escalation-to-human rate. When those move opposite to the eval score, suspect the eval

11. What could not be confirmed

  • There is no general figure for how often a model judge agrees with humans. What is published are individual case values for a specific task, rubric and dataset. No basis was found for transplanting another organisation's agreement figure as your own target
  • Position bias was measured on two public benchmarks. Whether it appears at the same magnitude on non-English business data or an internal domain could not be confirmed
  • The length-control method was designed for pairwise ranking. A procedure for transferring it directly to pointwise grading does not appear in that paper
  • The observation that error analysis takes 60 to 80 per cent of development time, and that teams with custom review tools iterate roughly ten times faster, are practitioner reports. No study with a disclosed sample and measurement method could be confirmed
  • Whether binary judgements or Likert scales are superior remains unsettled. Two primary sources give different recommendations, and no comparative study separating the conditions could be confirmed
  • No numeric basis was found for what proportion of an eval set must rotate to prevent ageing. That rotation is needed and how much is needed are different questions

Conclusion: evals are not a tool-selection problem

Eval adoption fails in nearly the same place every time. The tool gets chosen first, the metric names get fixed first, and the data is fitted afterwards. You end up with a dashboard whose numbers nobody can say are a proxy for anything.

Keep the order and the tool becomes a later question. Read outputs, write failures as sentences, test whether two people read those sentences the same way, assign the cheapest adequate grader per item, validate the judge against human labels, measure the wobble, then hang the gate. The 0.82 that comes out of that order translates into this failure type dropped from this many cases to this many.

If you start today, it is three things. Turn on trace capture, read a hundred sampled traces, and split the human-labelled set in two so one half stays untouched. Everything else needs those three to begin, and with those three you can begin without any tool at all.
