This article summarizes the wording, tables and figure captions of "Introducing Beam: Reflection's 501B open-weight model", published on Reflection's official blog on October 5, 2026, checked against the original.

Announcement Overview: The 501B Open-Weight Model Beam and the Scope of the Planned Release

On October 5, 2026, Reflection announced Beam, its first open-weight model. The weights are to be released later this month, and at the time of the announcement they could not yet be downloaded. Beam is a sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model built for coding, reasoning and agentic workloads. It has 501 billion (501B) total parameters, of which 23 billion (23B) are active per token.

The original explains that Beam's capabilities come from major investments in both pretraining and reinforcement learning (RL). The model was pretrained on 23.8 trillion tokens selected from the web and proprietary licensed datasets, and a high-compute RL run generated over 100 million rollouts on 10.5K NVIDIA GB300 GPUs over four weeks. The original sums these up as competitive open-weight performance with frontier inference compute efficiency.

  • Release schedule: the original says the weights, technical report, model card and developer artifacts will be released later this month.
  • License: the weights are to come out under an Apache 2.0 license this month, together with documentation and the full stack for running, evaluating and fine-tuning the model. The launch is also to include an ecosystem of distribution partners and integration with a range of open-source libraries.
  • Current access: the model is undergoing final red-teaming and evaluations, and the original points to an early access sign-up link, offers the early version to a select group of users, and takes a waitlist.

Beam is therefore not yet released open-source software. It is a model whose specifications and benchmarks have been announced and whose weight release has been promised. This article keeps the figures the original states apart from the denominators behind them, and collects what the original does not say in a separate section. A reader who only needs the status can stop at the three bullets above: the weights are promised but not yet shipped, and the license is announced but not yet attached to any downloadable file.

Architectural Specifications: A Sparse MoE with 23B Active of 501B Total

Beam is a sparse MoE model with 23B of its 501B total parameters active per token. The active share is about 4.6% (23 divided by 501, calculated). The original says the architecture and optimization recipe emphasize a numerically healthy foundation for sustained downstream RL, and it describes the validation of pretraining as follows. A series of iteratively bigger models was pretrained to establish and verify the scaling recipe, the final Beam Base matches its predicted performance, and it matches or outperforms accessible, similar-sized open base models.

  • Components: the original summarizes the design as interleaved local and global attention, fine-grained routed experts, a controlled residual stream, and multiple forms of load balancing.
  • Expert load balancing: building on auxiliary-loss-free load balancing (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2024), the team introduced a cosine decay of expert-bias updates to reduce routing perturbations later in training.
  • Sequence-level balancing: sequence-level balancing was added to encourage balanced expert utilization on data outside the pretraining distribution, preparing the model for the changing distribution of downstream RL.
  • Result of balancing: the pretrained base showed almost-perfect uniform utilization, and the busiest expert's load, averaged across MoE layers, was 1.04 times at pretraining completion (Figure 7).
  • Residual stream: a depth-based scaling approach counteracts activation growth as sublayer outputs accumulate, combined with SandwichNorm, elementwise attention gating, and FP32 residual accumulation, which reduces rounding error when adding small updates to the stream.
  • Scope of stability: the residual stream RMS stayed bounded throughout pretraining across all 52 layers (Figure 8), and the original says this stability persisted through pretraining, reinforcement learning and alignment.

The original does not translate the model's light active footprint into hosting requirements. The memory or GPU configuration needed to serve the full weights is not in the original, so this article does not invent such figures.

Pretraining and Infrastructure: 23.8T Tokens and 6,144 GB300 GPUs

The original says Beam was pretrained on 23.8 trillion diverse, high-quality tokens from the web, public sources and proprietary licensed datasets. The data pipeline was designed to give Beam a foundation for downstream agentic coding, preserving source code, technical explanations, and mathematical and scientific knowledge through every stage of curation. The team says it trains on almost all publicly accessible and unrestrictively licensed code and code documentation on the web.

  • Filtering: about 95% of raw Internet tokens are eliminated through parsing, deduplication and curation. On the other hand, conventional techniques would have missed roughly 1.8 trillion high-quality tokens that the team retains, including 87% of its curated web-code tokens. The 1.8 trillion is therefore not the total of the final selection but the amount that conventional filters would have missed.
  • Quality classification: the team trained its own quality classifiers for web, code and STEM content, divided the data into fine-grained quality tiers, and weighted training toward stronger material.
  • PDF processing: a high-throughput pipeline combining a vision-language OCR model with in-house quality classifiers and artifact detectors that catch malformed reconstructions, distributed across thousands of GPUs, processed petabytes of technical data.
  • Compute infrastructure: pretraining finished in under four weeks on a cluster of 6,144 NVIDIA GB300 NVL72 GPUs. The team built in-house a topology-aware, Kubernetes-based scheduler, a node lifecycle system, and a silent data corruption (SDC) detection system capable of semi-autonomous rewinds and restarts.
  • Training stability: nine semi-automatic rewinds were executed, attributed to non-deterministic gradient norm spikes or suspected SDCs, and the loss curve showed no instabilities or large irrecoverable spikes (Figure 9).
  • Goodput: it reached 92.3% towards the end. By the original's definition it is the share of wall-clock time spent on training steps retained in the final model.

The midtraining stage after pretraining was designed as a foundation for high-compute RL. Training data was built with multi-stage curation pipelines that capture the structure and complexity of real-world tasks, and the effective context length was extended to 1 million tokens. The original says structured code repositories, long-horizon tasks and high-quality long-form documents are combined to teach the model to identify, retain and connect relevant information across long sequences.

High-Compute Reinforcement Learning: 10.5K GPUs and Over 100M Rollouts

The original describes high-compute RL as a central scaling axis for Beam. 10.5K NVIDIA GB300 GPUs were deployed for four weeks, generating more than 100 million rollouts with a maximum context length of 256K tokens, and training and grading used approximately 1.3 billion sandboxes. The original says the team believes this is one of the largest scale RL runs conducted by any open lab to date. Across the evaluation suite, capabilities continued to improve as RL compute increased, with no sign of a plateau.

  • Environments: a pool of nearly one million environments was built, primarily through synthetic data pipelines and supplemented by proprietary vendor data and open-source sources. The domains are software engineering, terminal use, competitive coding, STEM, web search, tool use and general knowledge work.
  • Environment curation: tasks were synthesized or sourced, filtered for difficulty (neither consistently solvable nor impossible) and quality (excluding underspecified, guessable or hackable tasks), and tested through RL to inform the next round of sourcing and filtering.
  • Sandboxes: up to 170K ran concurrently, more than one billion sandbox creation requests were processed across over 20 clusters, two clouds and four regions, and 90% of new sandboxes were ready in under 10 seconds.
  • Asynchronous execution: an average of 110K rollouts ran concurrently. Each token was tagged with the model version that produced it so the training algorithm could account for policy staleness, and the numerics stayed stable even with samples 107 weight versions, about a day, behind the current policy (Figure 4).
  • Compute allocation and weight updates: the inference-to-training GPU ratio was adjusted between 3.9:1 and 5.4:1. New weights reached the inference fleet in a median of about 12 seconds, and hierarchical transfer over RoCE across racks and NVLink within them cut cross-rack traffic by 75% and made adoption 2.2 times faster.
  • Failure recovery and packing: 71 inference incidents were handled without terminating the training job, capacity recovered in a median of eight minutes, and lost capacity was 0.02% of elapsed serving GPU-minutes. Dynamic packing kept training batches 99.99% full on average.

For the reward design, the team used a controllable length penalty that rewards successful solutions and discourages unnecessary tokens. Early in RL, performance improved even as completion lengths fell, and later, as agentic capabilities grew stronger, completion lengths grew again with those additional tokens supporting further gains. Users can control this tradeoff through a reasoning effort parameter. Figure 3 shows 80 million of the over 100 million rollouts, those used for the reasoning expert, and for comparison the original says Inkling was trained on 30 million rollouts and MiMo on 753 thousand.

The Benchmark Table and Positioning Against Comparison Models

The original says Beam advances the Western open-weight frontier, is competitive with larger open models like GLM 5.2, and is approaching Qwen 3.8-Max on coding and agentic tasks. At the same time it says that frontier open models like Kimi K3 remain ahead on raw capability, and that Beam's advantage is efficiency at inference time.

  • Coding: 65.5 on SWE Bench Pro v1, above GLM 5.2 (62.1) and below Qwen 3.8 Max (67.7). On Terminal Bench v2.1, 80.1 is close to GLM 5.2 (81.0).
  • Reasoning and STEM: AIME 2026 at 97.8 trails GLM 5.2 (99.2), GPQA Diamond is 90.5, and HLE without tools is 36.2.
  • Tool calling and search: MCP Atlas 78.7 and AutomationBench public 37.0 exceed GLM 5.2's 77.8 and 26.2.
  • Gap to leading models: in the table Kimi K3 scores higher, with 88.2 on SWE Bench Pro v2-Hard and 46.9 on HLE, and so does DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, with 74.2 on DeepSWE and 90.6 on Terminal Bench v2.1.

The Formula Behind the 3-4x Inference Compute Claim and the Excluded Costs

The original says that on advanced reasoning benchmarks Beam achieves scores comparable to GLM-5.2 while using 3-4 times less inference compute. It adds that the efficiency gains are even more pronounced against models in the 2T+ parameter family such as Qwen 3.8-Max, which require significantly more inference compute per token. The denominator of this claim is in the caption of Figure 2. Below is the full caption, unchanged from the original except that each sentence is placed on its own line for readability.

Figure 2: Beam demonstrates frontier-level inference efficiency, both when measured in terms of FLOPS and token count across DeepSWE, Humanity’s Last Exam (HLE), and Terminal Bench 2.1.
                    We used data from Artificial Analysis and DataCurve, estimating generation forward-pass compute as FLOPs ≈ 2 × active parameter count × mean generated tokens per attempt, counting each multiply-add as two operations.
                    Generated tokens include both reasoning and the final answer.
                    For mixture-of-experts models, we used the parameters activated per token rather than the total model size.
                    These estimates exclude prompt prefill, context-dependent attention operations, and serving overhead, so they represent an approximate compute comparison rather than measured inference cost.
                    We use Artificial Analysis and DataCurve as sources for other model’s evals.

Reading the caption reveals the premises of this comparison.

  • Estimation formula: generation compute is twice the active parameter count times the mean generated tokens per attempt, counting each multiply-add as two operations.
  • Scope of generated tokens: generated tokens include both the reasoning and the final answer.
  • MoE parameter basis: for MoE models the parameters activated per token were used rather than the total model size.
  • Excluded costs: prompt prefill, context-dependent attention operations and serving overhead are left out, so this is an approximate compute comparison and not a measured inference cost.

So the 3-4 times is not a result of measuring GPU cluster power or hosting cost. It is a comparison of compute estimated from active parameters and mean generated tokens per attempt. By the formula, a model that generates more tokens per attempt gets a larger estimated compute. The comparison also rests on the three benchmarks named in the caption, DeepSWE, HLE and Terminal Bench 2.1, so it is a claim about those three and not about every kind of task.

Agentic Transfer Examples and What to Check Before the Weights Arrive

The original says Beam's RL was designed to develop capabilities that generalize beyond its training tasks. During a phase of training on reasoning, software engineering and terminal tasks, browsing performance improved consistently even though the RL mixture contained no browsing tasks, and when given web access the model organically learned to search for and query other large language models and to use OCR APIs to read documents. The original adds that Beam is text-only but can work with information from other modalities when it is represented as text.

  • Demonstrations: a 3D astronaut game in p5.js, a live NYC subway map built on public MTA data, and a fine-tuning notebook for the small Gemma-4 model on a Text2SQL task are shown. Fine-tuning Gemma with the notebook is said to have raised its accuracy on the held-out test set by 66.5%.
  • Land and water experiment: on a 180×90 grid of 16,200 points, Beam gets 95.5% right, between Opus 5 (92.5%) and Fable 5 (97.8%), according to the original. This value is the original's own demonstration result.
  • Safety and alignment: a second model was trained from the pretrained checkpoint with a separate SFT and RL pipeline, and the capabilities of the two teachers were merged through multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD). The original says safety evaluation results will appear in the technical report.
  • Checks after the weights are out: how the Apache 2.0 terms apply to the actual distributed artifacts is best confirmed from the license text that is published with them.

In summary, what the original lets us confirm is the specification, the training scale, the published benchmark values and the way compute is estimated. Until the weights can be downloaded and served, the figures should be read as Reflection's own report. Practical information such as serving framework support is not in the original, so it is left to the "Unconfirmed Details" section below.

Unconfirmed Details

  • Whether the weights have actually been released, and the size of the released checkpoints and any plan for quantized versions. The original says only that they will come out this month.
  • The hardware needed to host the 501B weights. The original gives no serving requirements.
  • Whether and when open-source inference engines such as vLLM and SGLang support the model. The original announces integration with a range of open-source libraries but gives no concrete list.
  • The composition ratio of web, public and proprietary licensed data within the 23.8 trillion pretraining tokens.
  • Independent third-party reproduction of the values in the benchmark table. The table is what Reflection published.
  • The raw data on the mean generated tokens used per model in Figure 2.
