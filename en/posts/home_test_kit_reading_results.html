Reading At-Home Test Results - Why Two Lines Are Not a Diagnosis

What sensitivity, specificity and predictive value each actually answer; how changing prevalence alone drops the same positive result from 84% to 4.5%; and what to do with a positive, a negative and an ambiguous line

Aug 17, 2026 Health & Wellness
Before we start: This article is for general information and does not substitute for medical diagnosis or treatment. Have test results interpreted, and any next steps decided, at a medical facility. The numbers used below are hypothetical values chosen to show how the metrics behave, not the performance of any product. Which test to take, when to take it, and what to do with the result belong to a clinician who can see your history alongside the result.
If you take one thing from this article, take this: A self-test is not a diagnosis. A negative does not mean the condition is absent. It means the probability moved down a little. If you have symptoms and the test is negative, that is not a reason to postpone care, it is a reason to seek it. A delayed clinic visit is very nearly the only route by which these tools cause real harm.

Introduction: what two lines actually tell you

You open the pouch, wait fifteen minutes, and see two lines. One sentence arrives immediately: I have it. One line, and the opposite sentence arrives just as fast. I do not.

Both are wrong, or more precisely both are intermediate steps mistaken for conclusions. A test does not settle whether something is true or false. It shifts a probability you already carried before you opened the box. How far it shifts is set by the performance of the test. Where the shift starts from is set by your situation. That is why identical lines from an identical product can be close to conclusive for one person and mostly noise for another.

Miss that structure and a self-test becomes a reassurance dispenser. A home device that grinds up a removed tick and checks it for the Lyme bacterium in about fifteen minutes drew attention in the US recently. That product is the occasion for this piece, but the subject is the thing that applies to any self-test you happen to be holding: how to read the lines on the strip.

1. Why self-testing spread, and what is genuinely good about it

Home testing became an everyday object during the COVID-19 pandemic. Pregnancy tests and glucose meters had been household items long before that, but rapid antigen kits on convenience store shelves belong to that period. The catalogue has kept growing since.

The spread brings real benefits. Stated without inflation, there are three.

  • Access - it lowers the thresholds of appointments, waiting, travel and cost. Lower thresholds mean more people get tested at all
  • Earlier awareness - it adds one piece of evidence to the moment when someone is undecided about seeking care. Where time matters clinically, the value is greatest when the result pulls the clinic visit forward
  • Avoiding stigma - in areas such as sexually transmitted infections, where face-to-face testing is often avoided, it lowers the psychological cost of a first test. In those areas, people not testing at all is itself the public health problem

All three benefits point in the same direction, which is toward more contact with care. The moment a self-test starts replacing that contact, the benefit flips straight into a loss.

2. The numbers that describe test performance

There are effectively five numbers in play. One of them should not be used at all, and the other four answer different questions. Taking them in order.

2.1 Why 95% accuracy is not information

Accuracy is the figure that appears most often on packaging, and on its own it settles nothing. The reason is in the arithmetic.

Accuracy is the number of correct calls divided by everyone tested. It lumps together patients correctly called positive and non-patients correctly called negative. So when the tested group is overwhelmingly made up of non-patients, getting the non-patients right is enough to produce a high score.

An extreme case makes the trap visible. Take 10,000 people with 1% prevalence and apply a broken device that simply prints negative for everyone. All 100 patients are missed. All 9,900 non-patients are called correctly. Accuracy comes out at 99%.

A real test at 95% sensitivity and 98% specificity scores about 98% in that same group. The device that finds nobody scores higher. That is why accuracy is dangerous as a performance claim.

US Food and Drug Administration statistical guidance for diagnostic test studies addresses this head on. It asks for sensitivity and specificity to be presented as a pair, and for each estimate to carry a 95% confidence interval. A test strong in only one of them is half a test, and with a small sample the value itself is unstable.

Three things to check on a label: first, which metric the word accuracy refers to. Second, the separate values for sensitivity and specificity. Third, which population they were measured in (symptomatic people only, or including asymptomatic ones). Without all three, the number cannot be compared to any other product.

2.2 Sensitivity and specificity - properties of the test

Sensitivity is the proportion of people who have the condition that the test calls positive. It describes how rarely real patients are missed. Specificity is the proportion of people without the condition that the test calls negative. It describes how rarely healthy people are wrongly flagged.

Both are calculated knowing in advance who has the condition. That makes them relatively stable across populations and close to being properties of the product. Which also means they do not directly answer the question you actually have, because you do not know which group you are in.

2.3 Predictive values - what your result means

Positive predictive value is the proportion of people with a positive result who really have the condition. Negative predictive value is the proportion of people with a negative result who really do not. Anyone holding a strip cares about these two and essentially nothing else.

The difficulty is that these two are not determined by test performance alone. With sensitivity and specificity held fixed, predictive values move sharply depending on how many patients are mixed into the tested group. Clinical reference material hosted by the US National Library of Medicine makes the same point: where a disease is highly prevalent a test is better at ruling in and worse at ruling out, and where it is rare the relationship reverses.

3. How pretest probability flips the result

The probability that a person has the condition before testing is their pretest probability. At the group level that is prevalence; at the individual level it is prevalence with exposure history and symptoms layered on top. A test shifts that value. It does not conjure one out of nothing.

What follows is a hypothetical test. Sensitivity is fixed at 95% and specificity at 98%, and only the prevalence of the tested group changes, applied to 10,000 people each time. These are not product figures; they exist to show the structure.

Same product, same two lines, and the meaning ranges from 4.5% to 96.9%. Three things have to be read together.

  • The count of false positives barely changes. 200, 198, 180, 120. The error produced by 98% specificity scales with the number of non-patients, so where non-patients dominate it stays near 200 throughout
  • Only the count of true positives moves sharply, from 10 to 4,000. The ratio between those two counts is positive predictive value, which is why it turns over completely
  • As positive predictive value falls, negative predictive value rises. For rare conditions, negatives are trustworthy and positives are not. For common ones it is the reverse. This trade cannot be eliminated

What the table means in practice is this. The strip does not know which row you are in. That is settled by local transmission, season, exposure history and the presence and severity of symptoms, and none of that information reaches a piece of paper. So interpretation has to happen, in principle, outside the device.

3.1 How low pretest probability gets for a rare condition

Korean Lyme disease surveillance illustrates the point. A summary published in the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency weekly bulletin counted 116 reported cases in total from 2019 through 2023, with yearly figures of 23, 18, 8, 22 and 45. The trend is upward but the absolute count is tiny. Of those, 84 (72.4%) were domestically acquired, and South Chungcheong Province accounted for the largest share of estimated exposure locations at 22.6%.

Scaled against population, the pretest probability sits far below even the first row of the table. Testing without exposure history against that background can produce positives that are mostly false, even from a good test. What raises pretest probability is exposure and symptoms, not test performance.

4. The two ways a test is wrong, and what each one costs

There are only two failure modes. Saying absent when present, or saying present when absent. They produce entirely different harms.

4.1 False negatives - when timing produces the negative

A large share of false negatives are not caused by a bad test but by testing too early. Antibody tests especially.

An antibody test looks at the immune response rather than at the pathogen itself, and immediately after infection that response does not yet exist. For Lyme disease, the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention notes that serologic assays can be falsely negative during the first four to six weeks after infection, a window that usually overlaps with the appearance of the characteristic skin lesion. When recent infection is suspected, it recommends repeat testing on a new sample after seven to fourteen days. For the same reason the standard approach is run in two tiers rather than settled in one pass.

The trap for a self-test user follows directly. The moment you most want to test is the moment the test is least able to answer. People test right after the bite, right as symptoms begin. The antibodies are not there yet.

Beyond timing, several conditions produce false negatives.

  • Insufficient sample - South Korean regulator guidance on self-testing states plainly that an accurate result cannot be expected if the specimen is inadequate. A half-hearted swab or a short urine sample belongs here
  • Storage outside spec, or an expired kit - a kit left in a car in summer or on a balcony in winter, or one whose reagent has degraded past its date, produces faint lines or none at all
  • Reading outside the window - reading earlier than the interval the instructions specify
  • Low pathogen load - antigen tests need a certain quantity in the sample before they respond
The route from a false negative to real harm is nearly always the same one: seeing the negative and postponing the clinic visit. The more time-sensitive the condition, the higher the price of that delay. If you have symptoms and the test is negative, the strip is one piece of evidence and the symptoms are the heavier one.

4.2 False positives - a wrong positive has costs too

False positives are treated as the safer error, and they are not. As the table showed, for a rare condition most positives can be false, and those people really do pay the following.

  • Unnecessary anxiety - the days until confirmation are long, and that burden never appears in any statistic
  • Further testing and cost - where the confirmatory test is invasive, the follow-up carries its own risk
  • Stigma and damaged relationships - a false positive on a sexually transmitted infection self-test has consequences well outside medicine
  • Improvised self-treatment - taking leftover medication makes any later real diagnosis harder to reach
  • Missing the actual cause - once a single positive fixes the explanation, the search for other causes stops

That last item matters most. Settling on one condition because one test was positive removes other infections carried by the same vector, and entirely unrelated causes, from view. One test result answers exactly one question.

5. What to do with each result - positive, negative, ambiguous

In practice there are four outcomes: positive, negative, ambiguous and invalid. Each needs different handling.

Invalid
(no control line)		 The test itself failed A negative. It is not information of any kind Do not count it. Run a fresh kit. If it repeats, check the product and how it was stored

A few things apply to all four.

  • Write down the date and time you tested. Timing is half of interpretation, and the interval since suspected exposure matters most
  • Photograph the result inside the reading window. Lines change after it closes
  • Keep the product name and approval number. Which kind of test it was carries meaning during confirmatory testing
  • If symptoms are emergent, do not wait for a test. Difficulty breathing, reduced consciousness or severe chest pain are emergencies regardless of any strip
  • Do not make treatment decisions. Taking leftover antibiotics is outside what this article can advise, and whether any medication is needed, and which, is entirely a physician judgement

6. First ask what the test is measuring

The first question in interpretation is not performance, it is target. What is this test measuring?

The recent tick testing device makes the distinction unusually clear. It checks whether the tick that bit you carries the Lyme bacterium. It does not check whether a person is infected. Reporting on it notes that only about 1 to 5 percent of tick bites actually result in Lyme disease. So even a positive tick leaves the probability of human infection on the low side.

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention does not recommend testing ticks removed from people or animals, and states that such results should not be used to make treatment decisions. Two reasons: a tick carrying a pathogen does not mean the pathogen was transmitted, and that testing produces false negatives and false positives of its own.

Generalised, the same question yields three to ask of any self-test.

  • What is the specimen? Blood, saliva, urine, stool, or something that did not come from a person at all
  • What is the target? A fragment of the pathogen (antigen), the immune response (antibody), genetic material, or a metabolite
  • Does that target speak about now or about the past? Antigen and nucleic acid generally indicate present presence; antibodies indicate a history of exposure. A positive antibody result may not mean current illness, and can persist long after treatment

The third causes the most confusion in practice. Having antibodies and being ill right now are different statements, and the distinction is often absent from the result card.

7. What to check when choosing a product

When performance figures are hard to compare, start with what can be verified. The following are visible on the box and in the instructions.

  • Medical device marking and approval number - a domestically distributed product should carry them, and the number can be looked up in the national regulator database
  • Approved for self-testing? - the same underlying assay can be approved for professional use only. A professional-use product bought by an individual may not match the conditions of use
  • Timing conditions - the instructions state how many days after symptom onset, or after suspected exposure, the test is valid. This connects directly to the false negative problem above
  • The reading window - the interval within which the result must be read. Read late, and lines can appear that were not there
  • Storage temperature and expiry - some products have a narrow room-temperature range, and separate limits often apply before opening and after. Summer shipping conditions count here
  • Language of the instructions - products bought directly from overseas may fall outside domestic approval, and misreading the conditions of use becomes far more likely
  • How performance is reported - sensitivity and specificity together, with confidence intervals, and with the population they were measured in

If you need an order, the approval marking comes first and the timing conditions second. The first tells you the product passed some minimum verification; the second decides whether this is even the right moment to use it.

8. Where self-test devices sit in South Korean regulation

Rules differ by country, and what follows is the South Korean arrangement as one worked example. These products fall under the In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Devices Act. Article 2 paragraph 1 defines an in vitro diagnostic medical device as reagents, control and calibration materials, instruments, machinery, devices and software used alone or in combination to examine specimens of human or animal origin outside the body. Subsequent paragraphs define specimen (paragraph 2), clinical performance study (paragraph 3) and performance evaluation (paragraph 4).

Not every such device may be sold for self-testing. The Ministry of Food and Drug Safety applies four criteria to the self-test category.

  1. There is a genuine need for a non-expert to examine their own physiological state
  2. The device uses a specimen that is easy to collect, such as saliva or urine
  3. A layperson can understand the procedure without specialist knowledge
  4. The potential harm to the individual or to public health from an erroneous result is not large

The fourth criterion is exactly where this article lands. Approval for self-testing presupposes a judgement that being wrong here is not catastrophic. That premise holds only if users do not treat the result as a confirmed diagnosis. Regulation does not guarantee safety on its own; how users handle results is what keeps the premise standing.

The category is widening. On 25 March 2026, under Notice 2026-159, the ministry gave administrative notice of a partial amendment to the regulation on in vitro diagnostic device categories and grades. It creates self-test categories for influenza virus, sexually transmitted pathogens and drug metabolites, and moves COVID-19 self-test reagents from a middle to a lower classification tier. The stated rationale was that self-testing products have become widespread, demand for more categories has grown, and access to testing during infectious disease events needs broadening.

In short, self-testing is positioned in this framework as an access tool rather than a substitute for diagnosis. Approval confirms that a product performs as specified under specified conditions. It is not an endorsement that a result is a diagnosis.

Frequently asked questions

Q. If I bring a self-test result to a clinic, will they just test again?

Usually yes, because confirmatory testing runs under conditions designed for diagnosis. Retesting does not mean the self-test was pointless. The date tested, the product name and a photo of the result carry real value as timing information.

Q. Two negatives in a row - does that settle it?

What matters is when the two were done. Two on the same day are close to one piece of information, and if that moment was before antibodies formed, two negatives are exactly what you would expect. Repeat testing earns its value from the interval. Retesting a fresh sample seven to fourteen days later in Lyme serology is one such example.

Q. Does running several products at once cross-check the answer?

Less than you would hope. Several tests of the same design share their failure modes, so they fail together. If the sample collection was inadequate, three kits give three negatives. Combining different underlying methods does help, but choosing that combination and its order is itself clinical judgement.

Q. What about testing regularly as a precaution while asymptomatic?

Repeated testing at low pretest probability accumulates false positives. As the table showed, in a group dominated by non-patients false positives arrive in proportion to how often you test. Screening programmes have target ages, risk groups and intervals designed against evidence, which is a different thing from an individual raising their own frequency.

Q. What should I ask first when a result worries me?

In a form you can use verbatim: what does this test measure? Was the moment I tested a valid moment for it? Given my situation, how much weight does this result carry? If confirmatory testing is needed, when and what? Is there anything I need to do right now, or not? The last one gets skipped most often, and nothing to do is also information.

Conclusion: the strip only moves a probability

More self-testing is a good thing. More people get tested, and the undecided interval before seeking care gets shorter. But the direction the benefit comes from is fixed: toward the clinic. Used the other way, treating a single negative line as grounds to postpone care, the tool becomes a hazard.

The structure is simple. A test does not create probability, it moves it. How far is set by sensitivity and specificity; where it starts is set by the person's situation. Which is why the same two lines are worth 96.9% to one person and 4.5% to another. The strip does not know the difference.

Who does know is you, holding your own exposure history and symptoms, together with a clinician who can put those beside the result. The practical point of learning to read results is not to diagnose yourself. It is to not miss the moment when help is worth asking for.

To restate: This article is for general information and does not substitute for medical diagnosis or treatment. Have test results interpreted, and any next steps decided, at a medical facility. The calculations above are entirely hypothetical and do not represent the performance of any product. A negative self-test does not exclude disease, and if you have symptoms, seek care regardless of the result. Every treatment decision, including whether and which medication to use, belongs to a clinician who can see your history and results.
