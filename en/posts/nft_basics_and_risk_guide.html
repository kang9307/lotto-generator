NFT Investing Basics and Risk Guide

From blockchain and ERC-721 mechanics to digital art, membership, and gaming use cases, to high-risk dangers like rug pulls and wash trading — what you need to know before investing

Introduction: The Hype Has Faded, But the Market Hasn't Disappeared

In 2021-2022, NFTs (non-fungible tokens) sat at the center of a frenzy in which a single piece of digital art sold for millions of dollars and cartoon-ape profile pictures became status symbols. Since then, the market has cooled sharply, and as of 2026 trading volume remains far below its peak. Yet NFTs have not disappeared. Analysts note the market is reshaping itself around more practical use cases — gaming items, memberships, and real-world-asset tokenization — into a smaller but arguably more substantive market.

The problem is that much of the information surrounding NFTs still leans toward either exaggerated profit expectations or vague, undifferentiated fear. This article lays out, as of July 2026, what an NFT technically is and how it works, where it is used, and above all how risky it can be. It contains no recommendation to invest in any specific NFT or project.

1. What Is an NFT — Concept and Mechanics

1.1 What "non-fungible" means

NFT stands for Non-Fungible Token. "Fungible" describes an asset like one bitcoin that can be swapped for another bitcoin with identical value. NFTs are the opposite: each one carries a unique identifier and cannot be substituted for another. In simple terms, an NFT functions like a unique proof-of-ownership record inscribed on a blockchain. It's important to understand that while the record itself lives on-chain, the actual content it points to — an image, video, or in-game item — is typically stored elsewhere, off-chain, on a centralized server or a distributed store such as IPFS.

1.2 Blockchain and smart contracts

NFTs are issued and traded through smart contracts (self-executing code that runs when certain conditions are met) on blockchains such as Ethereum. The smart contract records the owner address for each token, and every time ownership changes hands, that update is logged publicly and irreversibly. This means anyone can use a block explorer to verify which wallet held a given token at a given time.

1.3 The ERC-721 token standard

ERC-721 is the technical standard for NFTs, finalized in January 2018 as Ethereum Improvement Proposal EIP-721. The standard assigns each token a unique token ID (a non-repeating identifier) and defines a consistent way to manage information such as the contract address, metadata, and owner address. Standardization is what lets different wallets, marketplaces, and games recognize and transfer NFTs under the same rules. Beyond ERC-721, extended standards such as ERC-1155, which lets multiple copies of the same token be issued together, are also widely used.

1.4 The minting process

Creating a new NFT is called minting. When a creator issues a token through a smart contract, that token receives a unique ID and metadata, and the first record is written to the blockchain. Minting incurs a gas fee — the cost of processing the transaction on the network — which can fluctuate sharply depending on network congestion.

2. NFT Use Cases — Where They're Actually Used

2.1 Digital art and collectibles

The best-known use case is digital art and collectibles. Creators tokenize digital works — images, animations, and more — for sale, and buyers receive an ownership record for that work. Profile-picture (PFP) collections drove the 2021-2022 frenzy.

2.2 Membership and community access

An application gaining prominence in 2026 is membership and access. Holding a specific NFT can function as an "admission ticket" granting access to an online community, event, or brand perk. Analysts note that more brands are experimenting with programmable, transferable membership assets instead of static point systems.

2.3 Gaming items and virtual assets

Efforts continue to issue in-game characters, weapons, and skins as NFTs so players can genuinely own them and trade them across games or marketplaces. Whether such in-game economies actually hold sustainable value, however, varies enormously from project to project.

3. Market Volatility and Liquidity Risk

3.1 The 2021-2022 bubble and its collapse

The NFT market's annualized trading volume reportedly surged to roughly $17.6 billion in January 2022 — an approximately 21,000% year-over-year increase — and OpenSea, the largest marketplace at the time, reportedly saw daily volume peak near $2.7 billion in May 2021. The collapse that followed was just as dramatic. Between January and September 2022, OpenSea's volume reportedly plunged around 97% to roughly $470 million, with some analyses putting the decline from the peak at nearly 99%. The crash is generally attributed to a combination of oversupply, thin liquidity, the collapse of FTX, and fading community engagement.

3.2 Where the market stands in 2026

As of early 2026, total NFT market capitalization is reported to sit roughly 95% below its 2021 peak (in the neighborhood of $1.5 billion), with monthly trading volume reportedly around $100 million. That said, modest signs of recovery are being observed around practical use cases — gaming, real-world-asset (RWA) tokenization, and brand memberships. Some in the industry describe this as a "K-shaped" reshaping in which strong projects survive while weak ones fade. This remains an industry outlook, not a settled fact, and should be treated with caution.

3.3 The liquidity problem

One of the fundamental risks of NFTs is a lack of liquidity. Unlike stocks or large-cap cryptocurrencies, there isn't always a buyer waiting for any given NFT. Without a bid, a listed price is meaningless — you simply cannot sell. This risk grows larger for thinly-traded collections, and it is common for holders who need cash quickly to find they cannot sell even at a steep discount.

4. High-Risk, Speculative Nature and Fraud — Threats You Must Know

This is the risk that deserves the strongest emphasis. What follows is not exaggeration — it reflects patterns that have recurred repeatedly in practice.

4.1 Rug pulls

A rug pull is a scam in which developers raise funds from investors and then abandon the project or withdraw the funds and disappear. Academic research has identified roughly 7,487 NFT projects as rug pulls, more than 250 of which were explicitly classified as "pull-the-rug" scams; the same research documented one creator who attempted 37 rug pulls in just three months. Warning signs include an anonymous team, no verifiable track record, token concentration in a small number of wallets, an unpublished smart-contract source, and exaggerated promises such as "guaranteed 100x returns."

4.2 Wash trading

Wash trading is when the same person — or multiple colluding wallets — repeatedly buys and sells an NFT among themselves to artificially inflate trading volume and apparent demand. Apparent popularity and trading activity may not reflect real demand at all, and buying in near the top after being fooled by this illusion is a common pattern behind losses. Multiple studies and security-firm reports name wash trading as one of the most common manipulation types in the NFT market.

4.3 The risk of going to zero, and other scams

When liquidity dries up, a given NFT can become effectively unsellable — its value converging toward zero. This is not a theoretical possibility; it has actually happened to a large number of collections since 2022. Beyond this, phishing (stealing wallet credentials via fake sites or apps) reportedly accounts for more than half of NFT-related scams, and fake or counterfeit NFTs (tokenizing someone else's work without the creator's consent) are estimated to account for roughly 30% of reported scam cases. More than $100 million worth of NFTs were publicly reported stolen between July 2021 and July 2022 alone, and 2023 saw more than $100 million in additional losses attributed to NFT-related fraud.

5. Wallets, Gas Fees, and Custody — Practical Precautions

5.1 Types of wallets

Holding NFTs requires a crypto wallet. Internet-connected hot wallets (software wallets such as MetaMask) are convenient but more exposed to hacking and phishing. Cold wallets (hardware devices disconnected from the internet) offer stronger security but carry the risk of loss or physical damage. If you plan to hold high-value NFTs long term, using a cold wallet is generally recommended.

5.2 Gas fees

Minting, buying, or selling an NFT incurs a separate gas fee — the cost of processing the transaction on the blockchain network. Gas fees are typically paid in the base coin of the relevant chain (such as ETH on Ethereum) and can spike sharply during network congestion. It is not uncommon for the gas fee to exceed the price of the NFT itself, so always check the total cost before transacting.

5.3 Protecting your private key and seed phrase

A wallet's private key and seed phrase (recovery phrase) are the master keys to all of your assets. Never store them as a photo or text file on a digital device, and never keep them in cloud storage or email. The standard practice is to write them down on paper and store that paper somewhere safe; legitimate services will never ask you for your seed phrase. Treat any request for your seed phrase as a scam, without exception.

6. Copyright and Legal Gray Areas

6.1 An ownership record is not copyright

This is the single most common misunderstanding. Buying an NFT gives you an on-chain ownership record (the token), but absent a separate, explicit agreement, it does not give you the copyright to the underlying work. The same principle applies as buying an original painting: owning the physical canvas doesn't automatically transfer the copyright in the artwork. Unless the creator expressly assigns reproduction, distribution, or derivative-work rights, a buyer typically holds no rights beyond personal enjoyment of the piece.

6.2 Practical considerations

Many NFT sales don't clearly document copyright terms, and marketplace terms of service frequently fail to spell this out either. Cases of someone else's work being tokenized and sold without the original creator's consent continue to be reported. Before buying an NFT, check the sale terms directly to see what rights the project actually grants, and if you intend any commercial use, be sure to review the written license terms.

7. Who Should Avoid NFTs

NFTs are likely a poor fit if any of the following apply to you: you would be investing money earmarked for living expenses or an emergency fund rather than true discretionary funds; you expect a guaranteed return within a short timeframe; you plan to fund the purchase with a loan or leverage; you're not confident in your ability to secure a wallet and private key; or you may need to convert the funds back to cash on short notice. Because NFTs are illiquid, high-risk assets whose value can converge toward zero, it is reasonable to avoid or minimize exposure if any of these describe your situation.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

  • Do I get the copyright when I buy an NFT? No. Absent an explicit separate agreement, you only get the ownership record; copyright remains with the creator.
  • How do I spot a rug pull? Watch for an anonymous team, no liquidity lock, concentration in a few wallets, unpublished contract code, and exaggerated profit promises.
  • What is wash trading? The same party trading among their own wallets to manipulate apparent popularity and volume.
  • Are NFTs still worth investing in? This article does not make that determination. The market has shrunk substantially and remains high-risk and speculative.
  • How much should I invest? This article does not recommend an amount. Only invest what you can afford to lose, and consult a professional for personal guidance.
