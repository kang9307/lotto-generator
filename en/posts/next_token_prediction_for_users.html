What Picking the Next Word Actually Means - The Traces You Can See on Your Own Screen

Enough structural explanation. Let us look at what that structure does to your screen

2026-08-01 Tech & IT

Opening: I asked the same question as yesterday and got a different answer

You needed the answer you got yesterday, so you typed the same sentence again. The reply came back different. The content seems similar, and also subtly off somewhere. You have no way of knowing which one to trust. And in moments like this, one explanation always shows up.

「AI is just predicting the next word.」

That statement is true. The problem is that it is only true, and completely unhelpful. Nothing changes for you after hearing it. It tells you nothing about which answer to trust, how to phrase things so the tool wanders less, or when you need to verify. It is like saying 「a car just burns fuel and rolls forward.」 Accurate, and useless for driving.

This article is not another attempt to explain that sentence. It stitches together what that structure looks like on your screen. Text arriving one piece at a time, the same question returning different answers, long chats behaving as if they forgot the beginning — these three look like unrelated quirks, but they come out of one and the same structure. Once you see that connection, the way you read results and the way you use the tool genuinely change.

Two rules for this article. First, no claims about what happens inside the model. Not 「it works like this」 but 「this is what is observed, and it fits with a structure that picks the next piece.」 Nobody reading this has opened the box, and the goal here is knowledge that works without opening it.

Second, no claims about any specific product's numbers. Anything taken from vendor documentation is marked as such, and anything computed here is shown with its working. Where a figure is a rough approximation, it is labelled as one.

For context, this is not the only way text can be produced. Instead of choosing left to right, some approaches start from a blurred whole and sharpen it step by step, which is covered separately in diffusion-based text generation. This article deals with what the tools we actually use today put on the screen.

1. Why text arrives one piece at a time - the answer is not sitting there finished

Start with the most visible thing. Send a question and the answer pushes out from the left, a bit at a time. Many people assume this is a 「made to look like typing」 animation. Some services may well do exactly that. But open the developer documentation and the picture shifts a little.

One vendor's public documentation splits receiving a response into two distinct modes. One returns the answer as a single lump once it is finished. The other hands over fragments as they are produced. And for the second mode, the names and the ordering of the signals are published. One signal announces the start, then content fragments arrive repeatedly, then a final signal says it is done.

What matters is the worked example printed in that document. It shows exactly what order one sentence arrives in.

The decisive rows are 3 and 4. let and 's arrived as two separate fragments. If the cutting were for human readability, there would be no reason to slice it that way. Splitting a single word in half buys nothing at all as an animation.

Notice too that pieces like " the" arrive with the leading space attached. People do not chunk sentences with the space stuck on the front. The natural reading is that these fragments are not a display unit carved up for viewing, but something much closer to a unit of production.

1.1 So how does this change the way you read results

Once you accept that the answer is built in fragments, front to back, several things explain themselves.

  • The conclusion may not exist in advance. At the moment the first sentence is being made, the last sentence does not yet exist. That makes it a different kind of text from an essay whose introduction was written knowing the ending
  • Cutting off mid-way is a thing that happens. Since it is not completed and then shipped, stopping partway leaves an unfinished sentence. That is not a malfunction, it is what the structure implies
  • The thinking time often clusters at the front. You frequently see a long wait for the first character, then everything flowing smoothly after that
  • Turning streaming off changes the felt speed entirely. At identical production speed, a blank screen until completion feels far slower
  • You are allowed to judge halfway through. If the direction is already wrong, there is no reason to wait for the end. Section 5 returns to this

This is the point where words like 「transformer」 and 「attention」 normally appear. In one line: the structure that automatically weighs which parts of what came before to consult and how heavily is called a transformer, and that weighing is called attention. Those two words do not appear again in this article, because what we can verify is the screen, not the interior.

2. Not words but fragments - character count is not the bill

The fragments from section 1 are usually called tokens. The definition is simple: the smallest unit used when handling text. The core point is that it does not match the units people use, characters and words, and that mismatch is where the misunderstandings come from.

One vendor's documentation states, for its own models, that one token is roughly 4 characters and that 100 tokens is about 60 to 80 English words. Those are rough figures for English. Which raises the question that matters for anyone not writing in English. What about other languages?

Counting beats guessing. The last two columns are the whole point of this section. The Korean sentences have less than half the characters of the English ones (0.46x), yet more bytes (1.13x). What looks shorter on screen runs in the opposite direction from what is actually processed.

The reason is plain. One Hangul syllable costs 3 bytes to store while one Latin letter costs 1. Korean packs meaning densely so the character count drops, but each character is worth three times as much, which cancels the saving and then some.

The Japanese and Chinese rows, included for comparison, mostly tell the same story. Chinese, though, has a different texture. One third the characters of English, but almost identical bytes (0.98x). So a tidy conclusion like 「anything in a CJK script is automatically worse off」 does not hold. Only one thing is certain: the character count on your screen tells you nothing.

2.2 How far can we honestly go on token counts

Here is where honesty is required. Token counts differ by product, and you cannot know them without a proper counter. Which cluster of characters becomes a single token depends on a dictionary that differs from product to product. One vendor's documentation states outright that its newer model counts the same text as roughly 30 percent more tokens than the earlier one, and instructs readers not to reuse figures measured on the older model. Same company, same sentence, different model, different number.

The width of that range is itself information. It means if you want the actual number, stop estimating and run a counter. And there are exactly three places where this number bites.

  • Billing. Paid services generally price by token count, not character count. Short on screen does not mean cheap
  • How much you can paste in at once. The threshold that triggers a 「too long」 response is not measured in characters either
  • How long a conversation stays coherent. The cause of the problem in section 6 lives right here

The practical conclusion collapses to one line. Do not gauge volume by character count. When a limit or a bill is in play, checking the real number with a token counter is the accurate move.

3. The structural reason 「I do not know」 rarely appears

A common complaint: you wish it would say it does not know, but instead it manufactures something plausible. The phenomenon itself is widely observed. But explaining the cause as 「it was built to lie」 or 「it is dishonest」 overshoots. Seen through a structure that picks the next piece, there is a sense in which it could hardly be otherwise.

Think of it this way. At the point where the next piece is chosen, the candidates are all fragments that can actually be written. There is no separately provisioned option labelled 「stop here and write nothing.」 Of course the fragments that spell out 「I do not know」 are also among the candidates, so such an answer is perfectly possible. But that is not silence, it is writing one more sentence.

Several consequences follow.

  • Empty and filled do not cost differently. Writing an answer and writing a disclaimer are the same act of picking fragments and joining them. The latter is not sitting there as a specially safe default
  • Fluency and factuality are unrelated. A sentence that flows well is well-formed as a sentence, not verified as a claim. That is exactly why smoothness must never be used as grounds for trust
  • Confidence level does not show up in the prose. People trail off when unsure, but joining fragments does not attach that signal automatically. A confident tone is not evidence of confidence
  • Numbers and proper nouns are the danger zone. These are places where a plausible shape is enough to continue, so a combination that does not exist can be produced while the sentence still reads fine

This section stops here. The topic alone deserves far more room, and the aim of this article is to stitch several phenomena into one structure. The takeaway is one line: write on the assumption that 「I do not know」 rarely arrives, and do the verifying yourself.

4. Why the same question gives a different answer every time

Back to the opening scene. The reason yesterday's question gives a different answer today is that randomness sits at the point of choosing.

When the next fragment is picked, there is more than one candidate. It would be possible to always take the top one. Then the same question yields the same answer. But building it that way produces stiff text that keeps reusing the same phrasing. So most products pick among candidates according to probability.

The important part is that differences propagate. When a different choice happens at one fragment, that fragment becomes the material for choosing the next one. The front changed, so the back changes too. Which means a small difference in the first few fragments can drag an entire passage down a different road. That is the bridge into section 5.

There is one more layer. One vendor's documentation states that the model decides for itself how much thinking to spend per request, so usage varies from request to request. In other words, beyond the randomness at the point of choosing, there are further per-request variables.

So what can a user control? This is a place to avoid asserting and to state only what is checkable.

  • Where it is adjustable. Developer screens and some advanced settings often expose an item governing how much randomness is permitted. The name varies by product
  • Where it is not. Ordinary chat windows mostly have no such item. If you cannot see it on your screen, assume you are on the side that cannot adjust it
  • Reducing it indirectly. Even without a dial, a narrower question narrows the range of answers. Specifying the format and the required items leaves less room to wobble
  • You cannot eliminate it. If a task requires a guaranteed identical answer, it was never a task for this tool. That judgement is baked into the table in section 8

This section also stops here. The problem of varying answers is worth digging into on its own, but what this article needs is the link showing that this phenomenon connects back to the structure of picking the next fragment.

5. The opening determines the rest

Turn section 4's 「differences propagate」 around and you get this section. The first few lines set the direction of the whole answer. This is not only inference, it is a property visible in the documentation.

One vendor's documentation carries a procedure for recovering a response that was cut off mid-way. The method is telling. You save whatever you received so far, then on the retry you put that content back in and instruct the model to continue from there. The document even prints a sample phrasing, along the lines of 「your previous response was interrupted and ended here, continue from where you left off.」

The mere fact that this method works says a great deal. It means the raw material for continuing is whatever was written before. What was already written serves as the basis for what comes next, which is why supplying only the opening is enough to have the rest follow on.

5.1 So sometimes starting over beats correcting in place

When we dislike an answer, we normally follow up with 「no, do it like this instead.」 That approach has a trap. The bad opening does not disappear. It stays in the transcript, and one correction request is simply stacked on top of it. You are asking for a change of direction while the wrong direction remains in play as material.

Setting the decision rule this way is right more often than not.

  • You like the direction of the first two or three sentences, so correct in place. Local fixes such as wording, length, tone, or adding an example are faster continued
  • It went wrong from the first two or three sentences, so rewrite the question and start over. Wrong topic, misread premise, or an entirely different format all belong here
  • You have repeated the same request three times, so start over unconditionally. If three did not work, the fourth will not either. The longer the chat, the larger the share held by the bad opening
  • When you start over, change the question. Throwing the same question again is likely to land in the same place. Add the condition that was missing the first time
  • Judging from the first paragraph alone is fine. If the direction is wrong there is no reason to read to the end. Stopping early while the text streams saves real time

To add one thing: the opening of your own question works by the same principle. Conditions written at the very front of a prompt stay in play as material for longer. Placing them up front generally beats piling them at the end of the paragraph.

6. Why long conversations seem to forget the beginning

As a chat grows, rules set earlier start getting ignored. You said not to use casual register and casual register comes back. You fixed a format and at some point the format falls apart. This phenomenon has a name, and vendor documentation explains it.

One vendor's documentation calls the total range that can be consulted at once the working memory of the model. It then nails down that this is different from the whole body of training data. And the decisive explanation follows: as the conversation advances, your messages and the replies accumulate inside it, and earlier turns are preserved completely.

Unpacked, that means this. You are not just sending one new question. The entire conversation up to that point goes back in every time. The same document states that what goes in is not only the conversation but also system instructions, attached documents and images, and tool descriptions, all of it. So the longer the chat, the less room is left.

The same document adds one more line. Accuracy and recall degrade as the token count grows. In other words, performance can drop from sheer length even while room remains. Curating what sits in there, that document notes, matters as much as how much space is available.

6.1 Where summarizing and trimming happen

So what happens on hitting the ceiling? Two branches are visible in the documentation.

  • Rejection. On developer connections, if the input alone already exceeds the ceiling, the request itself is refused. The document names an error to the effect of 「the prompt is too long」
  • Stopping during generation. If the input fits but the ceiling is reached while writing, the response ends with a distinct reason attached saying it stopped there
  • Pushing out. A footnote in the same document states that ordinary chat interfaces may manage this on a rolling oldest-out basis. That is the route by which the beginning quietly disappears
  • Replacing with a summary. A feature that automatically summarizes earlier material server-side to carry a conversation past the ceiling also appears in the documentation. The original becomes a compressed version, so fine-grained conditions can blur
  • Reporting the remaining room. Some models, the same document notes, are given an update on remaining capacity each request. It is automatic, not something a user toggles

Put together, 「forgets」 is the wrong word. Nothing faded. It is closer to never having been resent, or having been swapped for a summary, or having been buried by sheer length. That distinction changes what you do about it.

  • Do not write an important condition once at the top and call it done. Restate it when it matters
  • Rather than dragging one long chat forever, tidy up and move to a new one. Copy across only the conclusions you need and the earlier noise does not follow
  • If you attached several reference documents, removing the ones you are not using can help. Everything you put in takes up room
  • Restating the content beats pointers like 「that thing I mentioned earlier」

7. Three traces you can verify yourself

Everything so far was explanation. Now the part you run yourself and confirm with your own eyes. All three work on whatever screen you normally use, with no special tooling.

7.1 Check 1: ask the same question three times

  1. Open a new chat. Do not do this inside an existing one. The earlier content stays as material and contaminates the result
  2. Pick a question likely to produce a long answer. Ones without a single correct answer, like 「recommend me something」 or 「explain this」, work best
  3. Type it in without changing a single character. Take the answer
  4. Open another new chat and type the same question. Do this three times in total
  5. Put the first sentence only of each of the three answers side by side

Two things to look for. First, are the answers different? If so, that is a trace of randomness at the point of choosing. Second, and this matters more, where does the divergence begin? If they split at the first sentence, everything after probably went down a different road. If the first sentence matches and later parts differ, the fork was somewhere in the middle. This observation makes section 5's 「the opening determines the rest」 visible.

7.2 Check 2: cut off and continue

  1. Open a new chat
  2. Type a sentence that is deliberately cut off mid-way and ask for it to be completed. For example, 「complete this sentence: the reason I would not recommend this product is」
  3. Look at the result
  4. Repeat the same procedure, changing only the cut-off point. Try 「the reason I would recommend this product is」 once more

Comparing the two makes it certain. The opening you supplied determines the rest. A one-fragment difference between 「would not recommend」 and 「would recommend」 flips an entire paragraph. This is not a research finding reversing itself, it is the back being built to fit what was placed in front. This experiment meets section 3's 「fluency is not grounds for trust」 exactly, because both versions come out smooth.

7.3 Check 3: behaviour near the length limit

  1. Open a new chat and give one rule that is easy to follow and easy to notice right at the start. For example, 「from now on put (checked) at the very end of every answer」
  2. Keep asking questions unrelated to that rule. Use questions with long answers rather than short ones, to get there faster
  3. Mark whether the rule held on each answer
  4. Record the point where the rule first drops out
  5. At that point, restate the rule once. See whether it recovers

This yields the most practical result of the three. You learn roughly where earlier instructions start blurring on the tool you actually use. And if restating it brings the rule back, that is evidence it is closer to 「was not resent」 than to 「forgot」. The remedies collected in section 6 get verified right here.

A caution on reading these three experiments. If a result contradicts your expectation, do not immediately conclude 「my model of this was wrong.」 Chat interfaces handle things differently per product, and the same product changes over time. What these experiments confirm is how the tool you are using right now behaves. They are not proof about the interior of every tool.

8. What changes once you know the structure

There was one reason for coming this far: to change how you read results and how you use the thing. Not 「so AI is dumb」 and not 「still, it is amazing.」 The goal is to derive from the structure which tasks it suits and which it does not.

8.1 Fitness table by task

Two criteria are enough. Is it acceptable for the answer to differ every time, and would you notice if it were wrong. Two yeses means you can delegate it. Two noes means you must not.

Drafting, expanding ideasYesYesGood fitVariety is a bonus and you read and pick
Polishing your own text, changing toneYesYesGood fitYou hold the original, so comparison is easy
Summarizing long textMostlyAgainst the sourceFits, with comparisonOmissions only show against the original

One rule runs through the table. It is strong on things you can verify and weak on things you cannot. That is why 「explaining an unfamiliar field」 sits in the caution row. It is the use you most want, and precisely because the field is unfamiliar, you cannot catch it being wrong. That combination is the most dangerous one there is.

8.2 Checklist

Items to run past yourself in actual use.

  • Important answers were asked once more in a fresh chat. Where two answers diverge is the shaky spot
  • An answer was not trusted for being fluent. Prose style is not evidence of confidence
  • Numbers, proper nouns and quotations were checked separately. That is where plausible-shaped combinations are easiest to produce
  • After three failed correction requests, I started over instead of continuing
  • When starting over I did not reuse the same question but added the condition that had been missing
  • Important conditions were placed at the front of the conversation
  • In long chats the conditions were restated partway through
  • When a chat got too long I carried only the conclusions into a new one
  • Reference documents that were no longer in use got removed
  • I restated the content instead of writing 「that thing from before」
  • Volume was not gauged by character count. When a limit or a bill applied, I checked a counter
  • I judged direction from the first paragraph and stopped if it was wrong
  • Tasks I could not verify were not delegated in the first place
  • I asked without expecting an 「I do not know」 and came prepared to verify
  • It was not used for automation requiring an identical result

One last thing. None of the items above amounts to telling you not to use this tool. Equally, this article contains no expectation that 「it will handle it well on its own.」 Knowing the structure lets you draw your own line between how far you delegate and where you start checking. Drawing that line is what this article is for.

## 5. What not to do

- Accepting a fluent sentence as a verified fact
- Stacking more correction requests onto an answer already off-course
- Writing an important condition once at the top and calling it done
- Estimating volume from character count
- Skipping verification because you expect to be told I do not know
- Using it where an identical answer must be guaranteed
