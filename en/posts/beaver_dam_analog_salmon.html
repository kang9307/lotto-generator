What Follows When You Slow the Water - Beaver Dam Analogues and a Coho Winter

The conditions that sit between 8 and 60 percent, and how a single structure builds a habitat

2026-09-06 Trends & Culture

Restoring a river usually means taking something away. Concrete comes out, structures spanning the channel are opened, and a straightened course is allowed to bend again. On a small tributary in northern California the direction was reversed. Posts were driven into the bed, branches were woven between them to hold the water back, and juvenile salmon made it through the winter.

What follows is not an animal story. It is about what comes trailing behind a single structure that slows the water, and about the conditions a summary number needs before it can be read. Those two questions are really one. Habitat is not scenery but the product of structure, and a survival rate is a compressed statement about what that structure did for the fish in one particular season.

The figures here come from the primary study as published in a peer-reviewed journal and from public documents released by government agencies. Anything not settled is marked as such in the text, and the second-to-last section gathers those items together with the reason. Questions of policy merit are left alone.

The trigger - a number compressed into one sentence

The line going around was that artificial beaver dams lifted juvenile coho salmon survival from 8 percent to 60 percent. That summary began in a popular magazine article, and behind it sits a study in a peer-reviewed journal. It was written by Michael Pollock, Stuart Munsch and Erich Yokel and appeared in Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution on 8 July 2026. The first two authors are with the Northwest Fisheries Science Center of the United States National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration; the third is with the Scott River Watershed Council.

Detach the numbers and the sentence becomes a claim that survival rose more than sevenfold, and a sentence like that travels far. But a multiple is the fastest way to erase the conditions. The phrase survival rate points at completely different things depending on the season, the population and the denominator. Egg-to-adult survival and survival across a single winter differ by orders of magnitude. So the order here is inverted. Instead of starting with the number and interpreting it, the piece first establishes what a juvenile salmon needs, and only then puts the number in its place.

Why winter is the bottleneck for juvenile coho

Coho salmon carries the scientific name Oncorhynchus kisutch and is one of the Pacific salmon species. It is called coho salmon in English and is not a species that occurs naturally in the rivers of the Korean peninsula.

Its life history has a feature that sets it apart from other Pacific salmon. The fry do not head straight for the ocean but spend roughly a year in fresh water. A winter falls inside that period, and the winter is the bottleneck. Drying channels in the summer low-flow season are a problem of their own, but winter brings a different kind. Rain pushes discharge up sharply and the current picks up speed. A fish less than ten centimetres long has to swim continuously to hold position in that current, and once it can no longer pay that cost, it is swept downstream.

Winter habitat for a juvenile coho therefore means somewhere the water is slow. Pools, side channels branching off the main stem, ponded water left on the floodplain, dead zones under accumulations of wood. The difficulty is that over the past century these were the first places to disappear. Straighten a channel and confine it between levees and the velocity rises, the bed incises, and the water no longer has occasion to spill onto the floodplain. A river with nowhere to overflow in a flood is a river with nowhere for a small fish to hold on.

What a juvenile coho needs in winterWhat a straightened, confined channel actually offers
Refuge where the velocity is lowA uniform cross section with velocity high and even throughout
Water spreading into floodplain and side channelsWater locked into the main stem by levees and an incised bed
Deep pools and coverA shallow, flat bed with the wood cleared out
Cool water and base flow through the summer low-flow seasonReaches that dry early and warm because groundwater storage has shrunk
Food organisms produced in slack waterFlow with residence time too short to produce much

Nothing in the left column of that table is exotic. All of it appears automatically once the water slows. And the right column is what results from removing every reason for the water to slow. With that set out, the purpose of the structure in the next section becomes clear.

What follows once the water slows down

What a structure across the flow does to water is simple. It lifts the water surface upstream of itself. But the moment the surface rises, a chain begins. The order of that chain is what matters.

A higher surface pushes water sideways

An incised channel holds water in a single line. When the surface rises, the water reaches the elevation of the old floodplain above the bed, and from there it spreads laterally. Side channels take water again, and shallow water stands on the floodplain. More wetted area means, in practice, more area where the velocity is low. The places a juvenile fish can hold during a winter flood come out of exactly that area.

Standing water moves into the ground

As the surface rises and the wetted area grows, more water infiltrates the alluvium beside the channel and the water table rises. That water is not lost; it returns to the stream over time. Base flow in the dry season holds up as a result, and water that has spent time underground follows air temperature less closely, so it emerges colder in summer. This is the path by which a structure installed as a winter measure ends up touching summer temperature.

Slower water sets down what it was carrying

When velocity drops, the sand and gravel the water was transporting settle out. As sediment accumulates in an incised channel the bed elevation recovers a little at a time, and each increment makes it easier for water to reach the floodplain the next time. This is where the chain begins reinforcing itself. At the same time the structure of posts and branches is itself cover, and production of the aquatic insects that serve as food increases in the slack water.

To put it plainly: slowing the water is one action, but its consequences are not one thing. Refuge area, groundwater storage, summer temperature, bed elevation, cover and food production all branch off the same cause. What makes this case interesting is that chain rather than the salmon.

What a beaver dam analogue actually is

The animal that used to produce this chain is the beaver. Beavers dam water as a matter of habit, and pools, wetlands and side channels follow. A great many streams in the western part of North America took their form under conditions that assumed beavers were present. When those populations collapsed during the fur trade era, the structure went with them. The Scott River valley, the setting for this case, was once called Beaver Valley, a detail quoted in the magazine coverage.

The human imitation of that structure is the beaver dam analogue, shortened to BDA. The construction method described in the study runs as follows.

  • A line of small-diameter wooden posts is pounded in, roughly perpendicular to the flow
  • Branches are woven between the posts, typically willow or conifer
  • No concrete, no rebar and no machined footing are used
  • Complete impoundment is not the goal; the structure assumes water will overtop it and leak through

Read as a materials list, it looks flimsy. That flimsiness is part of the design. What is needed is to raise the surface a little, not to hold the water. A leaky structure does not cut off downstream flow, and when it does fail it does not fail the way concrete fails. The price is that it does not repair itself, and that point comes back in section 12.

What was built on the two creeks

The setting is the Scott River watershed in Siskiyou County, in the northwest corner of California. The Scott River drains into the Klamath River. The study covers two of its tributaries, Sugar Creek and French Creek.

The first thing the table shows is the scale. The two sites together come to roughly 8,938 square metres, a little more than one football pitch. The structures themselves amount to a handful of hand-built dams. And at French Creek the work was not confined to structures; an off-channel pond was created alongside them. That detail connects directly to the temperature discussion further down.

Tracking ran across six brood years, from 2015 through 2020. Across the two creeks, 8,256 juvenile coho were tagged in total. Adult returns were monitored separately at four tributaries, Bogus, Shasta, Trinity and Scott, from 2007 through 2021, giving 59 replicate years of observation.

Eight percent and 60 percent are rates of what

Now the number can be set in its place. What the study reports is overwinter survival of tagged juvenile coho salmon. It is the proportion of fish that made it through one winter. It is not egg-to-adult survival, and it is not the rate at which fish return from the ocean.

The most important row is the third. Sugar Creek at 55 percent lands in essentially the same place as French Creek at 60 percent. That two separate creeks produced comparable values says more than the before-and-after gap at a single creek does. The 8 percent, by contrast, comes only from French Creek in its pre-work condition; it is not a control value measured on a neighbouring untreated stream in the same winter.

The authors state this as an interpretive condition themselves. Reference streams and restored streams differ together in land use, hydrology and human impact, so results from any observational study need care in reading. They also record that the monitoring period happened to coincide with severe drought and with some of the worst ocean conditions on record. Their phrasing is that a restoration signal was still visible through that amount of noise.

Six things to establish before reading the figure

Numbers shaped like this one keep coming back. Establishing the six items below each time separates a claim that stands from one that does not. The same habit of taking apart the inputs and assumptions behind an estimate is applied in the piece on where the figure of 100 billion humans comes from.

  1. What is the denominator. Here it is tagged juveniles, not eggs deposited and not fish passing the estuary
  2. Which interval. One winter, not the whole life history
  3. Compared against what. The pre-work mean at the same creek, not a neighbouring control measured at the same time
  4. How large is the sample. Two creeks, six brood years, 8,256 tagged fish
  5. What were the background conditions. A period combining severe drought and poor ocean conditions
  6. Is there another instance of the same treatment. Yes, the 55 percent at Sugar Creek

Establish all six and a different sentence comes out of the same data. At two creeks, overwinter survival of tagged juvenile coho was observed in the range of 55 to 60 percent after the structures were installed, and at one of those creeks the pre-work mean had been 8 percent. It is longer, but it does not shed its conditions. The word sevenfold does not appear in it.

The part where the water got colder

That impounding water raises its temperature is close to common sense. Water pooled wide and shallow presents a large surface to the sun, and heat does not leave water that is not moving. The paper cataloguing BDA risks does place warming of ponded water on its list.

At Sugar Creek the measurements ran the other way. Readings taken at 15-minute intervals from April to December of 2017, summarised as a moving average of weekly maxima, showed that temperatures in the habitats created by the structures were lower than in unrestored reaches. The study is more specific than that. In the unrestored reaches temperatures climbed into the range that suppresses growth or induces sublethal stress; in the structure-built habitats they did not. The off-channel pond was especially cool and stayed inside the optimal range for juveniles. The band the study marks as optimal runs roughly from 10 to 15 degrees Celsius.

The explanation for that reversal sits inside the chain in section 3. Water connected to groundwater does not warm. The French Creek pond was built on a side channel fed by groundwater, and a rising surface pushes water into the alluvium and increases storage. Water that has passed through the ground tracks air temperature less closely. The equation of standing water with warming water is an equation drawn without asking where the water comes from.

A caveat left by the same authors supports that reading. They note that the cooling effect of a BDA may be limited over bedrock substrate. Bedrock is a bed the water cannot infiltrate. In other words, whether temperature falls or rises is settled by the bed and the source of the water, not by the structure.

Area created and carrying capacity

The second value to set beside survival is area. Roughly 8,938 square metres of habitat were created across the two creeks, and the study estimates capacity there at about 8,514 juvenile coho, the sum of 6,744 at Sugar Creek and 1,770 at French Creek.

Capacity and survival sit at different levels. Survival is how many of the fish that arrive remain; capacity is how many can arrive at all. If one improves while the other stays put, population size does not move much. That both values shifted together in this case is the backbone of the observation. For reference, a mark-recapture estimate in the French Creek pond in autumn 2017 put the population at 2,517 fish with a margin of 1,173. The size of that margin is itself part of what the value says.

  • Roughly 8,938 square metres is a little more than one football pitch
  • The capacity estimates carry standard errors of their own: 537 fish at Sugar Creek and 127 at French Creek
  • The margin on the pond population estimate approaches half of the estimate itself
  • Area increasing and a population increasing are separate propositions, and it is more accurate not to write them as one

Do juvenile fish get past the structures

Put a structure across a stream and one question follows immediately: does it block juvenile fish from moving? That question was tested on its own in the same watershed, in a field experiment published in PLOS ONE in 2022 and carried out at Sugar Creek under low-flow conditions.

  • The subjects were juvenile coho salmon and steelhead of 65 to 80 millimetres, tagged in the peritoneal cavity
  • Of the juvenile coho displaced below the structures, 91 percent of 155 fish were detected upstream within 36 hours
  • Among steelhead, 54 percent of 39 fish reached upstream locations by the close of the experiment
  • Some 49 percent of the coho and 43 percent of the steelhead used routes that meant clearing drops of 38 to 40 centimetres
  • Sixty percent used side channels with slopes of 8 to 11 percent as their passage route
  • Among fish left in place below the structures rather than displaced, only 18 percent of coho and 0.7 percent of steelhead were detected upstream

The last line is the core of the experiment. Being able to pass and wanting to pass are different things. Displaced fish had a reason to return to where they had been, and most of them did. Fish in their natural position did not bother going upstream. The result therefore answers a question about passage capability, not about demand for movement. The authors concluded that juveniles possess the behavioural and physical adaptations passage requires, and that beaver dams are candidates for preservation and restoration rather than removal.

These values were nonetheless obtained under low-flow conditions, at those particular structures on that particular creek. They do not transfer unchanged to periods of high discharge or to structures with larger drops.

Documented risks and the conditions that magnify them

In November 2025 the same journal published a paper setting out the benefits and risks of BDAs together, written by Meyer and co-authors. The risks enumerated there do not stand in flat contradiction to the observations in sections 8 and 10. What that list actually says is that the same structure produces different outcomes under different conditions.

Movement routes

Whether passage is blocked depends on the form of the structure and on flow conditions. The fork is whether the drop is one a fish can clear and whether a low-gradient lateral bypass exists. Those two routes are precisely what the experiment in section 10 measured.

Temperature and dissolved oxygen

As section 8 showed, the direction is set by where the water comes from. A wide shallow pool of surface water alone and a pool with groundwater continuously entering it produce different outcomes from the same standing condition. Dissolved oxygen tends to move in the same direction as temperature, so where temperature stays stable the risk falls with it.

Durability of the structure

The condition the authors state is explicit. A BDA is less durable in steep or larger streams and lasts longest in low-gradient small channels with a floodplain. They also write in the paper that effectiveness is highly location-specific and that choosing the site well is decisive. No sentence describing this as a solution that can go anywhere comes out of this study.

The structures are not a substitute for beavers

This is the point deferred in section 4. Between a human-built structure and a beaver-built dam there is a difference that materials do not explain. A beaver dam is a maintained structure. Leaks are packed each night, height is added when the water drops, and the whole thing is relocated when the site turns bad. When a flood tears part of it away, it is rebuilt within days. A bundle of posts and branches put in by people has none of that. Once it fails, it stays failed.

A BDA therefore sits closer to a primer than a replacement. What the structure produces is pools, wetland and ground wet enough for willow to grow. Those are conditions a juvenile salmon needs, and at the same time conditions in which a beaver can settle. Comments from the watershed council side point the same way. The magazine coverage quotes a remark to the effect that when beavers do take up a structure and begin working on it, the result is far better than anything people can build.

The distinction is not wordplay. Where the goal sits changes how the work is managed. Treated as a replacement, the structure demands an endless maintenance budget; treated as a primer, the performance measure becomes whether beavers return to the site rather than how long the structure holds. What happens when only outcomes are managed while the incentive structure is left untouched has been examined through a different case in the piece on why reef-wrecking fishing persists.

What transfers to Korean rivers and what does not

Several conditions make this method hard to move to Korean rivers as it stands. Coho salmon does not occur naturally in the rivers of the Korean peninsula, and neither do beavers. The second objective, creating conditions for beavers to return, does not transfer at all. What remains is the first objective, changing the structure in order to build habitat.

One Korean document makes an interesting contrast here. The Ministry of Environment released monitoring results on weir openings on 13 April 2021, produced by the investigation and assessment body for the natural recovery of the four major rivers. Starting in June 2017, eight institutions monitored 14 fields across 11 weirs. The release states that opening the weirs made the flow faster and produced clean sandbars and gravel beds, improving the river environment, with health indices for fish and benthic invertebrates trending upward. Residence time is reported as shortening by up to 88 percent and current speed as increasing by up to 813 percent.

The directions are exactly opposite. On the Californian tributaries, slowing the water was the improvement; on the Korean weir reaches, speeding it up registered as the improvement signal. That is not a contradiction but a difference in starting point. One is a river where water left too quickly and the winter holding places disappeared; the other is a reach where water stayed too long and sandbars and gravel beds were buried. The two cases share exactly one sentence. What needs attention is the structure the water flows through rather than a water quality figure, and what disappeared from that particular river sets the direction of the work.

No conclusion about which policy is right can be attached to that contrast. The two documents differ in purpose, in subject and in what they measured. What transfers is not the conclusion but the form of the question: establish what structure was lost from a reach and what would come back with it, and only then choose a method.

The reading that remains - restore one thing and others follow

The most portable part of this case is not the survival figure. It is the way a single intervention branches into several outcomes. Raising the water surface by twenty centimetres or so with posts and branches means nothing in itself. The meaning arrives afterwards. Wetted area grows, groundwater is stored, summer temperature falls, the bed recovers, cover and food appear.

The sentence holds in the opposite direction too. Straightening and confining the channel over the past century was also a single intervention. As that one branched outward, winter refuge, summer cold water and groundwater storage disappeared together. Things that vanished together come back together. What lies between 8 percent and 60 percent is not the determination of a fish but that chain.

And verifying the chain means returning to conditions in the end. Which season, what denominator, compared against what, bedrock or alluvium underneath, what gradient. A performance number with no conditions attached does not move to another river. The study says as much about itself, in the sentence stating that effectiveness is highly location-specific and that site selection decides the outcome.

What could not be confirmed

  • Per-brood-year survival values and confidence intervals could not be confirmed. The 8, 60 and 55 percent figures are reported as means, and the year-to-year variance behind them is not written into this piece
  • Exactly which brood years count as before the work could not be settled. Only the facts that the French Creek work was in 2018 and that tracking ran from 2015 to 2020 were used
  • Whether a designated reference stream existed for French Creek could not be confirmed. The 8 percent is therefore described only as a before-and-after comparison and is not used as a simultaneous control value
  • The exact count of small structures at French Creek could not be confirmed. The study says several, and that is how it is written here
  • Quantitative evidence for each risk item could not be confirmed. The list in section 11 enumerates categories and does not express the frequency or magnitude of any of them
  • Dissolved oxygen measurements for these creeks could not be confirmed, which is why that cell in the table is left empty
  • The quantitative link between the adult-return analysis and overwinter survival could not be confirmed. Only the scale of that design, four tributaries and 59 replicate years, is stated in the text
  • Individual method-level cases from Korean ecological stream restoration projects could not be pinned to a primary document. The relevant press release address no longer resolves after an agency website reorganisation, so the Korean material rests on the weir-opening monitoring release alone
