Flu Shots in Korea - Who Gets One Free, When to Get It, and What to Check First

Whether you fall inside the funded groups, how late is too late, what was actually removed when the vaccine went trivalent, and who to contact if something goes wrong afterwards - with the season each figure belongs to spelled out

2026-08-11 Health & Wellness
Before anything else: this article is general health information about how influenza vaccination is organised and delivered, and it does not replace medical diagnosis, prescription or treatment. Whether to be vaccinated, when, and with which product is a judgement for a clinician who knows your health status and history. Nothing here recommends or discourages vaccination for any individual, and it cannot be used to decide for yourself whether you fall into a contraindicated group. The eligibility rules, dates and vaccine type described below are values that are set anew every season, and the season each one belongs to is stated. This article describes the Korean system; readers elsewhere should see the comparison section near the end.

Introduction: the first question is whether it costs anything

Come autumn, notices about flu vaccination go up on clinic doors across Korea. The question that stops most people in front of them is a single one. Am I in the free group or not?

The answer turns on three categories. Korea's National Immunization Program funds influenza vaccine for children, pregnant women and older adults, and everyone else pays. That structure has held for several years.

The trouble starts after that. The exact age brackets, the opening dates and the vaccine type are decided fresh for each season. Copy last year's notice, or a blog post with no date on it, and you will be off. Eligibility is published as a range of birth dates rather than an age in years, so the same age can fall inside the bracket one season and outside it the next.

And the timing of this article is awkward. As of 11 August 2026, the plan for the 2026-2027 influenza season has not been announced. Everything below is therefore based on the 2025-2026 season, the most recent confirmed one. The structure and the procedures generally carry over from season to season, but the dates and birth-year brackets must not be copied forward. Confirmed figures come from the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency notice and its vaccination portal.

This article does not cover antiviral treatment or how to tell influenza from a common cold. What it covers is what you actually need when you go to be vaccinated: eligibility, timing, vaccine type, where to go, what to disclose beforehand, adverse events and how they are reported, co-administration, and cost.

1. The funded groups - three categories and how the age line is drawn

The National Immunization Program funds influenza vaccine for three groups. All three are populations at relatively higher risk of severe outcomes if infected.

1.1 Children - the line is drawn by date of birth

  • In the 2025-2026 season the group was children from 6 months through 13 years of age, published as the bracket born 1 January 2012 to 31 August 2025.
  • The reason the bracket closes on 31 August is that infants under 6 months are not eligible for vaccination; the cut-off is set so that a child reaches 6 months by the autumn campaign.
  • Children split into one-dose and two-dose groups depending on their vaccination history, and the program gave the two groups different opening dates.
  • Which group a given child falls into depends on past records, so the vaccinating clinic checking the immunisation history is the accurate way to settle it.

1.2 Pregnant women - gestational week does not matter

  • Pregnant women are eligible regardless of gestational week. Early or late in pregnancy makes no difference to eligibility.
  • Putting pregnant women in a priority group is not a Korean peculiarity. The World Health Organization also lists pregnant women first among its annual-vaccination priority groups.
  • Some clinics ask for documentation confirming the pregnancy, so checking with the clinic before you travel there saves a wasted trip.

1.3 Older adults - opening dates are staggered by age band

  • In the 2025-2026 season the group was adults aged 65 and over, published as those born on or before 31 December 1960.
  • They do not all start on the same day. The oldest band opens first and the rest follow at intervals of a few days. The design spreads out crowding at clinics.
  • For the 2025-2026 season, people aged 65 and over could receive influenza and COVID-19 vaccines at the same visit.

2. Timing - the two-week window and the season calendar

One fact sets the timing. Protective antibodies take about two weeks to develop after vaccination. Get the shot after the wave has started and those two weeks pass with protection still climbing. That lag is why the national program opens in autumn.

The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency describes preventive effectiveness in the range of 70 to 90 per cent in healthy adults. That figure should not be read as a personal guarantee rate. As the next section covers, effectiveness varies by season and by age group.

2.1 What to do if you missed the opening

  • Passing November is not a reason to skip it. Influenza can circulate into the following spring, which is exactly why the program stays open until late April.
  • That said, the later you go, the more of the wave has already passed. What you buy is protection over the remainder of the season.
  • If you are in a funded group, the program closing date is the real deadline. After that the same shot becomes a paid one.
  • When several people in a household are eligible, going together on the schedule of whoever opens last cuts the number of trips - at the cost of delaying protection for those who became eligible earlier.

3. Trivalent and quadrivalent - what was removed, and why

For the 2025-2026 season the national program switched from the quadrivalent vaccine it had been using to a trivalent one. People hearing this often ask whether the product was downgraded. Look at the composition and it reads differently.

TypeAntigens includedNote
QuadrivalentTwo influenza A (H1N1 · H3N2) + two influenza B (Victoria · Yamagata)Used in the national program until then
TrivalentTwo influenza A (H1N1 · H3N2) + one influenza B (Victoria)Used in the 2025-2026 national program
  • What was removed is one antigen, the B/Yamagata lineage. The stated basis is that this virus has not been detected anywhere in the world for an extended period.
  • The change followed World Health Organization guidance and review by the national immunization advisory committee. It was a response to circulation patterns, not a procurement cut.
  • Influenza vaccines are reformulated every year to match the viruses predicted to circulate in that season.
  • Which is why effectiveness differs from season to season. It depends on how well the prediction matched what actually spread. That is not a defect in the vaccine; it follows from how quickly influenza viruses drift.

WHO acknowledges that effectiveness may be lower in older people while adding that vaccination still makes illness less severe and reduces the chance of complications and death. There is a wide gap between the sentence effectiveness varies by season and the sentence therefore it is not worth getting. The first is true; the second does not follow from it.

4. Finding a clinic - the vaccination portal and public health centres

National program influenza vaccination is available at any participating clinic or public health centre regardless of your registered address. There is no catchment area to worry about, so a clinic near your workplace is fine.

  • The list of participating clinics is searchable by region on the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency vaccination portal.
  • Even for a clinic that appears in the results, vaccine stock is managed clinic by clinic. Being on the list and having doses that day are two different things.
  • In practice, narrowing the list and then phoning to confirm stock and booking policy is what avoids a wasted trip.
  • Public health centres differ by local government in how they run the campaign and whom they serve, so checking your own district's notice is more reliable than a general rule.
  • Children should be accompanied by a guardian, and bringing the immunisation record speeds up the one-dose or two-dose decision.

5. Before the shot - contraindications are a clinician's call

Vaccination runs as a screening form, then a clinician's pre-vaccination assessment, then the injection. One thing needs saying plainly here. Whether you are in a state where the vaccine should not be given, or should be postponed, is decided by the clinician at that assessment. It is not something to adjudicate for yourself from a list online, and this article does not fill that list in for you.

What can be laid out is the information you should not leave out of that assessment. The judgement belongs to the clinician, but the material for it has to come from you.

  • Any reaction after a previous influenza vaccination - which symptoms, when, and how long they lasted
  • Any history of severe allergic reaction to a vaccine or vaccine component - in particular whether you have ever needed emergency treatment for anaphylaxis
  • Whether you currently have a fever or an acute illness - with a fever, postponing until recovery is a common decision
  • Chronic conditions, immunocompromised status, and medications you take - immunosuppressants and anticoagulants in particular must be mentioned
  • Pregnancy or plans to become pregnant
  • Any recent vaccination - the date and which vaccine
What happens right after the injection

Rather than leaving immediately, you are asked to stay at the clinic for a short period so your condition can be observed. Severe allergic reactions can appear within a short time of the injection, and being where clinical staff are is the safer place to be. For the rest of the day the usual advice is to pay attention to how you feel and avoid strenuous activity if anything seems off.

6. Adverse events - the common ones and the rare ones

Reactions are mostly mild and short-lived. The problem is that the common ones and the rare severe ones circulate together in news coverage and online discussion. They differ in frequency and in what you should do.

With these signs, emergency care comes before any paperwork
  • Hives spreading across the body
  • Swelling of the lips, tongue or face
  • Difficulty breathing or wheezing
  • Severe dizziness, pallor or fainting
  • Severe vomiting or clouding consciousness

In these situations, call emergency services or go to an emergency department immediately. Reporting comes afterwards.

One more thing worth separating out. Every vaccination season brings coverage of deaths reported after a shot. The distinction to hold on to is that reporting and causality assessment are different stages. A report records that a symptom followed a vaccination in time; whether there is a causal link between the two is decided by a separate review. Coming later in time does not by itself establish a cause.

7. Reporting an adverse event and claiming compensation

There are two reporting routes, one for the person vaccinated and one for clinicians. Either alone is sufficient.

7.1 The person vaccinated

  • A report can be filed on the web through the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency vaccination portal.
  • Alternatively, a report can be made by telephone to the public health centre for your registered address.

7.2 Clinicians

  • The reporters are doctors, dentists and oriental medicine doctors who diagnose or examine the adverse event.
  • The report goes to the head of the public health centre covering the affected person's location, filed through the integrated disease and health management system (is.kdca.go.kr) online or by fax to the centre.

7.3 Filing a compensation claim

Harm caused by vaccination is covered by a state compensation scheme under the Infectious Disease Control and Prevention Act. The process has three stages.

  1. The person vaccinated, or a guardian, files a compensation claim.
  2. The public health centre forwards the claimant's documents to the provincial government.
  3. The provincial government reviews causality and notifies the outcome.

The public health centre will explain which documents are required. Regardless of whether you end up claiming, keeping your own record of the vaccination makes everything downstream easier.

  • The date and time of vaccination
  • The name of the clinic
  • Product details of the vaccine (the clinic holds the product name and lot number)
  • When symptoms started and how they progressed - photographs where the symptom can be photographed
  • Medical records and prescriptions if you sought care

8. Getting it alongside COVID-19 and other vaccines

For the 2025-2026 season, people aged 65 and over could receive influenza and COVID-19 vaccines in a single visit. The design cuts the number of trips in order to lift coverage.

  • Even given together, the two are injected at different sites. They do not go into the same spot.
  • The COVID-19 vaccination in the 2025-2026 season used an LP.8.1-based vaccine.
  • Eligibility for free COVID-19 vaccination is defined differently from influenza. They run on separate criteria even within the same seasonal campaign; immunocompromised people and residents or inpatients of high-risk facilities opened earlier regardless of age.
  • Whether other vaccines such as shingles or pneumococcal can be given at the same visit depends on the vaccine and is decided at the clinic. The shingles vaccine itself is covered separately in the complete guide to shingles (herpes zoster).

9. When you are not in a funded group - cost and how to decide

Outside the three groups, vaccination is a paid, non-reimbursed service. Non-reimbursed items are priced at each provider's discretion, so the amount differs from clinic to clinic. That is why no figure appears in this article. Asking the clinic you plan to visit is the only accurate method.

  • Some local governments fund additional coverage from their own budgets. The eligible groups and dates differ from the national program, so check your district's public health centre notice.
  • Some employers run group vaccination sessions. You give up the choice of provider and get a simpler process in return.
  • The World Health Organization lists pregnant women, children aged 6 months to 5 years, people over 65, people with chronic medical conditions and health workers as priority groups for annual vaccination. Falling outside Korea's funded groups while sitting on that list is a reason to consider paying for it.
  • There are also situations that cost alone cannot settle. With chronic lung or heart disease, or an immunocompromised state, whether to be vaccinated at all is a conversation to have with your treating clinician.

Worth adding: in influenza prevention the intervention with established evidence is vaccination, not lifestyle measures. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health notes in its material on colds, flu and complementary approaches that no complementary approach has been proven to prevent or treat colds or influenza, and that the evidence for most studied folk remedies is limited or conflicting. The lifestyle side is covered in the complete guide to immune system management, and the evidence level behind folk remedies in green onion root tea and cold home remedies.

10. How other countries do it - what matches and what does not

Who gets prioritised for influenza vaccine does not vary much across borders; the World Health Organization's priority list looks similar everywhere. What differs is who pays for the shot - the delivery structure.

  • Korea - the National Immunization Program provides free vaccination for children, pregnant women and older adults, with everyone else paying out of pocket. Participating clinics and public health centres nationwide sit inside the same program, so your registered address does not restrict where you go.
  • United States - recommendations are set through a federal advisory process, but delivery is split across private insurance, pharmacies, workplace programs and public safety-net channels. There is no single free national program for adults in the way Korea has one, so whether you pay anything depends on your coverage and the route you use. Confirm the current season's recommendation with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and confirm cost with your plan or the pharmacy.
  • Japan - immunisation is split between routine vaccination aimed at older adults and others, and voluntary vaccination for everyone else. Eligibility conditions and whether any subsidy applies are set at the municipal level, so the local municipality's notice is the source that governs.
  • China - influenza vaccine generally sits outside the national immunisation programme, meaning voluntary vaccination paid for by the recipient, with some localities adding their own support for particular groups. Policy is set locally.

In short, who it is recommended for is broadly similar and who pays is not. That is where most guidance imported from another country stops matching the situation on the ground.

  • The exact age and vaccination-history criteria for the two-dose children's group - only the fact that one-dose and two-dose groups had different opening dates was confirmed. Which group a child belongs to is settled at the clinic from the immunisation record.
  • The price of paid vaccination - a non-reimbursed item set by each provider. There is no official representative figure to quote.
  • Season-by-season vaccine effectiveness figures - a value that moves with the season and with how well the strains matched, so no single season's number was generalised here.
  • Local government top-up programs - they differ by district and cannot be summarised in one place. The district public health centre notice is the primary source.
  • The recommended observation time after injection - only the principle of staying briefly for observation is stated. Follow the clinic's instruction on the exact duration.

12. Frequently asked questions

Q1. Will the funded groups be the same for the 2026-2027 season?

As of August 2026 the plan for the 2026-2027 season has not been announced. The broad shape of eligibility - children, pregnant women, older adults - has held across several seasons, but the birth-date brackets that draw the age line and the opening dates are published fresh every season. The children's bracket in particular has its closing edge set to a date in that year and its opening edge moves forward a year at a time, so dropping last season's bracket into this season can flip eligibility. Confirm against the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency notice and its vaccination portal.

Q2. It is August. Can I get the shot now?

The national program begins on the season's opening dates, so free vaccination does not run before the campaign opens. In the most recent season it started in late September for children and pregnant women, and from mid-October for older adults by age band. Paid vaccination depends on when supply reaches each provider, so the clinic is who to ask. Note also that vaccinating extremely early is not automatically better. Immunity from vaccination wanes over time, so the timing relative to the expected wave is worth discussing with a clinician.

Q3. It switched to trivalent. Is protection weaker now?

Trivalent is the quadrivalent formulation minus the B/Yamagata lineage antigen. It was removed because that virus has not been detected worldwide for an extended period, and the change was applied following World Health Organization guidance and review by the national immunization advisory committee. Since what was dropped is an antigen that is not currently circulating, reading it as a downgrade is hard to sustain. Effectiveness itself does vary by season, though: influenza vaccine is reformulated each year against the predicted strains, and the outcome depends on how closely prediction matched circulation.

Q4. I had one last year. Do I need another?

It is a vaccine built around annual repetition, for two reasons. The virus composition of the vaccine is updated every year, and immunity acquired from vaccination wanes over time. The World Health Organization cites both in recommending annual vaccination. Counting last season's dose as this season's protection is therefore hard to justify.

Q5. I have a fever after the shot. Do I see a doctor or file a report?

They are separate tracks. Systemic reactions such as fever, muscle aches and headache are relatively common and usually improve within a few days, but if symptoms persist or worsen, care comes first. Reporting can be done independently of that, through the vaccination portal or the public health centre for your registered address. If a severe allergic reaction is suspected - breathing difficulty, swelling of the face or lips, hives across the body - then it is neither a clinic visit nor a report but emergency services. And filing a report does not amount to a finding of causation; causality is decided by a separate review.

Guidance without a season attached cannot be used as is

In influenza vaccination, eligibility, opening dates and vaccine type are values reset every season. A notice or post that does not say which season it describes may not apply to the current one. Everything in this article is 2025-2026 season material, and should be re-checked against the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency announcement before you go.

Conclusion

Three things need settling first: whether you are in a funded group, when you can start, and where to go. The funded groups are children, pregnant women and older adults, and in every case the age line is drawn by date of birth rather than by years. Change the season and the group changes with it.

Timing follows from the roughly two weeks it takes for antibodies to develop. Getting vaccinated ahead of the wave is what buys you that lag. Being late is not a reason to give up, though - the program stays open until late April precisely because influenza can run into spring.

The shift to trivalent removed a B/Yamagata antigen that is not currently circulating, and the fact that effectiveness varies by season is a property of influenza rather than a flaw in the vaccine. Whether you are in a state where the shot should not be given is decided by a clinician at the pre-vaccination assessment, and no notice, this one included, can stand in for that. What you can do is prepare the information that assessment needs - previous reactions, severe allergy history, current fever, chronic conditions and medications, pregnancy, recent vaccinations.

If something goes wrong afterwards, the routes are defined. Common local and systemic reactions usually pass quickly, and if a severe allergic reaction is suspected, emergency services come before any report. Reports go to the vaccination portal or your local public health centre, and compensation is a separate claim under the Infectious Disease Control and Prevention Act. One last time: every date and birth-year bracket above is a 2025-2026 value, and the 2026-2027 season has not been announced. Opening that season's notice before you go is more reliable than reading this article.
