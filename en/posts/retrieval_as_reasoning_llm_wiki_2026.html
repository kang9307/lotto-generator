Retrieval as Reasoning: What the LLM-Wiki vs RAG Benchmarks Actually Show

It wins on multi-hop and loses on single-document lookup — drawing the line, in numbers, where this result transfers to your RAG pipeline

2026-08-06 IT/Tech

Change the Shape of the Knowledge, Not the Retriever

When a RAG pipeline starts missing answers, the order of operations is almost ritual. Resize the chunks. Bolt on a reranker. Swap the embedding model for whatever shipped most recently. Underneath that ritual sits a single unexamined assumption: retrieval is one lookup, and if you make that lookup more accurate the answers get better.

The paper "Retrieval as Reasoning: Self-Evolving Agent-Native Retrieval via LLM-Wiki" (arXiv 2605.25480), by Haoliang Ming, Feifei Li, Xiaoqing Wu, and Wenhui Que, goes straight at that assumption. Their argument runs like this: what an LLM agent needs is not a single context fetch but something closer to an act of reasoning — searching, reading, following a link, and judging for itself whether the evidence in hand is sufficient yet. So instead of treating external knowledge as a static retrieval index, they rebuild it as something compilable, composable, and self-evolving.

The claim itself is not new. What makes this paper worth the time is that the authors put it on four benchmarks and ran it head-to-head against the established RAG families, and that the results do not tilt cleanly in one direction. On multi-hop queries the margin is large. On single-document queries it loses. That dividing line is what this article digs into. The similarly named debate in personal knowledge management — what happens to human learning when an AI writes the wiki for you — is a separate matter covered in LLM Wiki and Germane Load. Here we are looking only at the model's accuracy, not the human's.

What LLM-Wiki Actually Does: Compile, Traverse, Self-Correct

Split the design into three axes and you can predict in advance where the gains come from.

Compilation — Pages, Not Chunks

At ingest time, source paragraphs are not sliced and dropped into an index as-is. An LLM runs two steps. First SelectPages picks up to five existing pages (k=5) related to the incoming paragraph; then CompileWikiPages takes those pages plus whatever constraints are currently active and produces the page body, the links, and the index updates. The output is a hierarchical Markdown wiki.

  • A directory index (_index.md) serves as the wiki's table of contents.
  • Entity pages carry metadata alongside the discrete facts attached to that entity.
  • Pages are joined by bidirectional wikilinks — which is what makes traversal possible later.
  • When the same entity appears scattered across many source documents, those mentions converge onto one page.

Traversal — Reading Several Times Through Tool Calls

At query time the agent has exactly two tools. wiki_search(query) looks first at structural signals — page names, aliases, tags, descriptions — and only drops down into body content when those come up empty. wiki_read(paths) reads a directory index or full page bodies in a batch and returns the links each page holds alongside the text. The agent uses those returned links to decide where to read next.

  • The tool-call budget is capped at fifteen calls per query (Tmax=15).
  • A patience threshold (P=3) halts the loop after three consecutive empty searches.
  • The agent is forced to call wiki_read at least once before answering — a guard against answering off search-result snippets alone.

Self-Correction — An Error Book That Rewrites the Next Compile Prompt

This is the least glamorous part of the paper and the most operationally useful. Any wiki an LLM writes will contain broken links and semantic inconsistencies. Rather than treating those as defects to be patched after the fact, the authors feed them back as input to the next compilation. The loop has five stages.

  1. Discover — a validator catches structural and semantic errors.
  2. Attribute — the root cause behind each error is traced back.
  3. Constrain — that cause is rewritten as a natural-language rule.
  4. Inject — the rule is spliced into subsequent compilation prompts.
  5. Verify & Close — the fix is re-validated and the entry is closed out.

Repair runs on two tiers. Tier one is code-based automatic correction that fires on every compilation batch and handles structural problems; tier two is a periodic LLM pass triggered every N documents that catches semantic and content-level errors. The authors classify seven error types, and of those, dangling links account for 29.1 to 63.8 percent of all errors. Put plainly: ask an LLM to create links and it points at pages that do not exist roughly that often.

What It Was Compared Against: The Baseline Lineup

The comparison set covers the major branches of the RAG family fairly evenly.

  • None (closed-book) — answers from model parameters alone, with no retrieval at all. The floor.
  • Vanilla RAG (BM25) — classic lexical sparse retrieval.
  • Vanilla RAG (Dense) — embedding-based dense retrieval using the top five passages.
  • RAPTOR — builds a tree by hierarchically summarizing documents.
  • GraphRAG — extracts a knowledge graph and attaches community summaries on top.
  • LightRAG — a lightweight family combining graph and vector retrieval.
  • HippoRAG 2 — a graph-based long-term memory approach inspired by hippocampal memory structures.

The backbone was GLM-5.1 from the GLM-5 family for both index construction and answer generation, and Qwen3-Embedding-8B was applied identically to every method requiring text representations. In other words, the baselines did not lose because they were handed a weaker embedding model. The multi-hop benchmarks use the first 500 questions in original dataset order, and the 2WikiMultiHopQA corpus comes to 3,440 passages.

The authors summarize this margin as 2.0 to 8.1 F1 points over HippoRAG 2, LightRAG, and GraphRAG. Read the table down its columns rather than across, and you can see where each end of that range comes from.

  • HotpotQA yields only +2.0. That is 0.839 against LightRAG's 0.819. This benchmark is mostly two-hop, so the top of the field is already bunched tightly together. There is little headroom left to take.
  • MuSiQue is where the gap is widest. 0.739 against 0.659. MuSiQue has more hops and deliberately seeds distractor passages, which makes it the least forgiving terrain for any method that pulls top-k once and commits.
  • On 2WikiMHQA the EM gap exceeds the F1 gap. F1 is 0.911 versus 0.847 (+6.4), but EM is 0.854 versus 0.764 (+9.0). The gain is concentrated in landing the answer exactly, not in producing strings that partially overlap it.

  • GraphRAG underperforms across the board. It fails to beat Dense RAG on all three benchmarks. Building a graph is not, by itself, a guarantee of anything.

Table 2: AuthTrace — Where LLM-Wiki Loses

AuthTrace is a diagnostic benchmark the authors introduce alongside the method, and it has a different character from the previous three. It tests the ability to select and assemble evidence from a thematically dense single-author corpus. Its composition is as follows.

  • The corpus is 860 publicly available works by five modern Chinese prose writers.
  • It comprises 2,099 QA instances after filtering.
  • Each instance ships with the query, the cited gold evidence units, atomic answer claim units, a reference answer, and an evidence fan-in label.
  • The fan-in label records how many documents must be pulled together to build the answer, as Single, Low, or High.

Here are the points worth reading, in order.

  • LLM-Wiki loses the Single column. HippoRAG 2 takes 78.3 to LLM-Wiki's 76.0, a 2.3 point deficit. The authors' explanation is unambiguous: many single-document questions are better answered by retrieving the original article verbatim, and because LLM-Wiki reorganizes content into structured pages, it occasionally drops local detail.
  • The ordering inverts as fan-in grows. +5.0 on Low (64.6 versus 59.6), +8.9 on High (55.4 versus 46.5). Overall it is +2.1 (70.4 versus 68.3). The overall margin looks thin for a simple reason — single-document queries make up a large share of the dataset.
  • LightRAG's collapse is dramatic. It falls from 73.4 on Single to 14.5 on High, dropping below closed-book (16.2). There is a regime in which retrieval is not helping the answer but actively obstructing it.
  • BM25 crushes GraphRAG. 75.4 versus 55.8 on Single — a twenty-point gap. Elaborate graph pipelines coming in behind plain lexical search happens more often than you would think.
  • The Single column has the highest absolute numbers overall. Whatever method you pick, single-document queries are already largely solved. The room left for improvement lives in the High column.

The ranking of the drops is the conclusion.

  • Removing progressive traversal hurts most. MuSiQue goes from 0.739 to 0.601 — 13.8 points gone. HotpotQA drops 11.7 points and 2Wiki drops 12.2.
  • Removing the wiki structure is next. A fall of 6.1 to 7.0 points. With no structure at all, keeping traversal still preserves more than half the gain.
  • Removing the error book is the smallest drop, but not negligible. 3.4 to 4.0 points. That is what quality control on an auto-generated wiki is worth.

Translated into engineering terms: investing in making the agent read repeatedly and follow links pays better than investing in making the index beautiful. However well you compile the wiki, if the query path still does one lookup and stops, more than half the benefit evaporates. Read that in reverse and it says something practical — you may be able to recover a substantial share of the gain by layering a search-and-read loop over the index you already have, without rebuilding anything into a wiki.

The Gap Widens With Hops, and Latency Actually Falls

That the margin scales with hop count is visible outside the main table too. On 2WikiMultiHopQA, the F1 gap over LightRAG grows from 5.7 points on two-hop questions to 8.3 points on four-hop questions. Each additional hop multiplies the probability that a one-shot top-k retrieval misses a required passage, whereas a traversal approach can build the next search term out of what the previous step returned.

And here a counterintuitive result shows up. It calls tools multiple times and is nevertheless faster.

  • LLM-Wiki's per-query latency runs 14.9 to 27.1 seconds depending on the benchmark.
  • LightRAG comes in at 39.7 to 51.3 seconds and HippoRAG 2 at 32.4 to 38.2 seconds.
  • The authors report LLM-Wiki as comparable to or faster than BM25 and Dense RAG.
  • The agent actually reads an average of 2.5 to 3.9 pages per query — fewer than the fixed five passages the RAG baselines always inject.

The reason is not exotic. The cost of repeated calls was outweighed by the savings from shrinking how much context gets pushed in at once. The graph-based methods are slow because community summarization and graph traversal each burn additional LLM calls. Where context tokens are simultaneously the cost and the latency, reading exactly the three pages you need beats stapling on five passages regardless of relevance, and that shows up in per-query price too.

The Hidden Invoice: Compilation Cost and Freshness

The two limitations the authors state about their own work are, in practice, the whole adoption decision.

First, Compilation Cost

Every source paragraph passes through SelectPages and CompileWikiPages. Both are LLM calls. The authors state plainly that initial construction is more expensive than chunking and embedding, and add that the cost amortizes across subsequent queries. That amortization argument is conditional.

  • It only holds if the expected number of queries per document is large enough.
  • It only holds if the corpus survives long enough — a corpus you will discard in a month has no amortization window at all.
  • The per-query savings (2.5 to 3.9 pages read versus a fixed five passages) help the amortization along, but whether they cover the ingest bill depends entirely on corpus size.

Second, Scalability

The authors write that once the wiki grows to tens of thousands of pages, the directory index becomes unwieldy and page selection quality may degrade. Their diagnosis is that web-scale or frequently changing corpora would require hierarchical indexing, sharding, stale-fact handling, and maintenance of the global directory. Large-scale dynamic maintenance, multimodal wikis, and cross-corpus transfer are left as future work.

If you are evaluating this for adoption, the questions that decide the outcome are not about benchmark scores. They are these.

  • When one document is edited, how far does the recompilation blast radius extend across pages?
  • What guarantees that recompilation does not break the bidirectional links that already exist?
  • If dangling links account for up to 63.8 percent of errors, does your validator catch them automatically?
  • Does a fifteen-call-per-query tool budget fit inside your latency SLO?
  • When an answer requires quoting the source, is there a path back from the wiki page to the original text?

What I Could Not Verify

Leaving this on the record. The items below could not be confirmed from this paper, and so nothing in this article makes numerical claims about them.

  • The absolute compilation cost. The authors state it is more expensive than chunk-and-embed, but token counts, dollar figures, or a multiplier could not be verified.
  • Reproducibility under a different backbone. The experiments are all on GLM-5.1. Cross-validation on other model families could not be verified.
  • Variance and statistical significance. Whether the table values are single runs or averages over several seeds, and whether confidence intervals exist, could not be verified.
  • Incremental update cost. An experiment covering the scope and cost of recompilation when part of the corpus changes could not be verified.
  • Code and data availability. Whether the artifacts needed for reproduction, AuthTrace included, are released could not be verified.
  • Behavior beyond English and Chinese. AuthTrace is a Chinese corpus and the other three are English. Compilation quality on a Korean corpus could not be verified.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is LLM-Wiki different from conventional RAG?

Conventional RAG slices source text into chunks, loads them into a vector index, and pulls the top k in a single shot per query. LLM-Wiki builds structure instead of an index. At ingest time it compiles the source into Markdown wiki pages and wires bidirectional links between them; at query time the agent repeatedly calls wiki_search and wiki_read to search, read, and follow links. Evidence is gathered through several rounds of judgement rather than one lookup.

How large was the performance gap in the benchmarks?

In the multi-hop QA table, LLM-Wiki scored HotpotQA F1 0.839, MuSiQue 0.739, and 2WikiMultiHopQA 0.911. LightRAG, the strongest baseline in the same table, scored 0.819, 0.659, and 0.847 respectively. The authors summarize the margin as 2.0 to 8.1 F1 points over HippoRAG 2, LightRAG, and GraphRAG. All figures are over 500 questions per benchmark.

Are there situations where LLM-Wiki loses?

Yes. On AuthTrace's single-document queries, HippoRAG 2 scored 78.3 and LLM-Wiki 76.0, a 2.3 point deficit. The authors explain that many single-document questions are better served by pulling the original passage verbatim, while LLM-Wiki reorganizes content into structured pages and can drop local detail in the process. On the high-density multi-document queries of the same benchmark, it leads by 8.9 points instead.

Is the wiki structure or the traversal the real driver?

The ablation points at traversal. Removing progressive traversal drops MuSiQue F1 from 0.739 to 0.601, the steepest fall among the three components. Removing the wiki structure lands at 0.669 and removing the error book at 0.699. Compiling good pages buys roughly half the gain, and the rest only appears when the agent actually follows links and reads several times.

Should I rebuild my RAG pipeline this way?

It depends on your query mix and the nature of your corpus. If many answers require crossing several documents, the corpus is reasonably stable, and queries hit the same corpus repeatedly, the approach is worth adopting. If most traffic is local fact lookup inside a single document, if answers must quote the source verbatim, if the corpus changes daily, or if there are too few queries per document to recover the compilation cost, conventional chunking is the better fit.

Conclusion: What Needs Changing Is the Definition of Retrieval, Not the Retriever

Reduce the paper to three things worth carrying out of it and they are these.

  1. Most of the gain comes from traversal. Pulling traversal out of the ablation cost 13.8 F1 points on MuSiQue. Before building any new structure, the first thing to check is whether your current pipeline still ends after a single lookup.
  2. The gain scales with hop count. On two-hop HotpotQA it was only +2.0; at four hops it widened to +8.3. If your query logs are overwhelmingly single-hop, this paper is not about your system.
  3. For single-document lookup, the raw source still wins. On AuthTrace Single, LLM-Wiki trailed HippoRAG 2 by 2.3 points. Structuring is not free — it is traded against local detail.

The most practical conclusion is not pick one of the two. Split your query log by fan-in, route multi-document queries down a traversal path and single-document queries down a direct source-retrieval path, and you have the design most faithful to what the tables say. That different methods top different columns of Table 2 is itself the argument for routing.

Treating retrieval as reasoning ultimately means relocating a retriever-accuracy problem into a systems-design problem. Before agonizing over one more reranker, the question that comes first is whether your agent can notice, on its own, that the first set of search results was not enough.
