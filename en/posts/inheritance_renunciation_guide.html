Inheritance Renunciation vs Limited Acceptance in Korea: Complete Guide

Differences, 3-Month Deadline, Court Procedure, Documents, and Special Limited Acceptance — Based on Korean Civil Act, June 2026

Introduction: Inheritance Is Not Just About Assets

When a family member passes away, grief is quickly joined by legal obligations. Many people are surprised to learn that under Korean law, debts are also inherited along with assets. If no action is taken within the statutory period, the heir is deemed to have accepted the inheritance unconditionally — known as simple (unconditional) acceptance — meaning all assets and all debts of the deceased pass to the heir. When the deceased owed more than they owned, this can leave surviving family responsible for debts out of their own pockets.

Two legal mechanisms exist to protect heirs from this outcome: inheritance renunciation (sangsok pogi) and limited acceptance (hanjeong seung-in). Both serve to shield heirs from excessive debt liability, but they work differently and have distinct downstream effects. This guide explains both options, the critical 3-month deadline, the family court procedure, required documents, and the nuances of multiple heirs and the special limited acceptance remedy — all based on the Korean Civil Act.

1. Three Options Under Korean Inheritance Law

Article 1019(1) of the Korean Civil Act gives an heir three choices, all of which must be exercised within 3 months of learning of the commencement of inheritance.

1.1 Simple (Unconditional) Acceptance

Simple acceptance means the heir takes on the decedent's entire estate — assets and debts alike — without any limit. This happens automatically if no declaration is filed within 3 months, or if the heir disposes of inherited assets (Article 1026: statutory acceptance). It is appropriate when assets clearly exceed debts, but devastating when the reverse is true.

1.2 Limited Acceptance

Limited acceptance (Article 1028) means the heir accepts the inheritance but agrees to pay debts only to the extent of the inherited assets. If the estate has assets worth 100 million KRW but debts of 300 million KRW, only the 100 million in assets is used to repay debts — the heir's personal wealth is not at risk. After filing, the heir must follow a statutory liquidation procedure: publishing notices to creditors, and distributing assets in priority order.

1.3 Inheritance Renunciation

Renunciation (Article 1041) is an absolute refusal of the inheritance. The renouncing heir is treated as if they were never an heir — inheriting neither assets nor debts. However, the crucial consequence is that the inheritance passes to next-in-line heirs, which may catch relatives off-guard if the estate is debt-heavy.

3. When to Choose Which Option

3.1 When Debts Clearly Exceed Assets

Both mechanisms protect the heir's personal assets. The deciding factor is what happens to subsequent heirs. Renunciation by children passes the inheritance to the decedent's parents; if parents are elderly, they may face the burden unexpectedly. A cleaner solution is often for the immediate-priority heir to choose limited acceptance and liquidate the estate themselves, terminating the debt chain at that point.

3.2 When the Asset/Debt Balance Is Uncertain

If hidden debts may exist — undisclosed loans, personal guarantees, tax debts — limited acceptance is the safer choice. Even if unexpected debts surface later, the heir's liability remains capped at the inherited estate's value. Unconditional acceptance followed by discovery of excess debts requires going through the more difficult special limited acceptance procedure.

3.3 When Assets Clearly Exceed Debts

Simple acceptance is the natural path. There is no need for court filings. However, always verify whether the decedent had joint liability debts (co-signed loans, personal guarantees) before deciding.

4. The 3-Month Deadline

Article 1019(1) of the Civil Act sets the window at within 3 months from the date the heir learns of the commencement of inheritance. Understanding this correctly is critical.

4.1 The Starting Point: When Did You Learn?

The clock starts not from the date of death, but from the date the heir learns of the death. If news of the passing reached a distant family member weeks later, their 3-month window begins from when they found out. Korean courts interpret this as the date the heir learned both that the decedent died and that they themselves are an heir.

4.2 Filing (Not Decision) Within 3 Months

The heir must submit (file) the declaration to the Family Court within 3 months. The court's ruling may come after the 3-month mark without issue — what matters is that the filing is received in time.

4.3 Extending the Period

Under Article 1019(2), the Family Court may extend the period upon request by an interested party or a prosecutor. The extension request must be filed before the 3-month period expires. This is worth considering when the full scope of the estate is difficult to determine quickly.

4.4 Consequences of Missing the Deadline

Failure to act within 3 months results in simple acceptance by operation of law (Article 1026). Standard renunciation and limited acceptance are no longer available. The only remaining remedy is special limited acceptance, discussed below.

5. Special Limited Acceptance: The Missed-Deadline Remedy

Article 1019(3) provides an exceptional pathway for heirs who have been deemed to have accepted unconditionally.

5.1 Requirements

Special limited acceptance requires proving two things simultaneously: (1) the heir did not know, without gross negligence, that inherited debts exceeded assets; and (2) the heir files within 3 months of discovering that fact. Gross negligence means a failure to exercise even minimal care that would have revealed the debt excess. The burden of proof lies with the applicant.

5.2 Special Rule for Minors

The proviso of Article 1019(3) gives minors a second chance. If a minor's inheritance was accepted unconditionally before they reached adulthood (whether by the minor or their guardian), the now-adult heir may file for limited acceptance within 3 months of discovering the debt excess after turning 18.

5.3 Practical Difficulty

While special limited acceptance is a safety net, successfully obtaining court approval is demanding. The applicant must affirmatively demonstrate both unawareness and the absence of gross negligence — ideally with documentary evidence. Professional legal assistance is almost always advisable.

6. Family Court Filing Procedure

Both renunciation and limited acceptance require a formal declaration filed with the Family Court. A private statement of intent has no legal effect; the court filing is essential.

6.1 Jurisdiction

File with the Family Court (or, where no Family Court exists, a District Court or its branch) having jurisdiction over the decedent's last place of residence. Seoul residents who have passed away fall under the Seoul Family Court.

6.2 Contents of the Declaration

The declaration must state: the parties' identifying information; the relationship between heir and decedent; the date on which the heir learned of the inheritance; the specific intent (to renounce or to accept with limitation); and, for limited acceptance, an attached inventory of the estate.

6.3 Required Documents (Typical Cases)

  • Regarding the decedent: Cancelled resident registration certificate; closed family register or closed basic certificate of family relations
  • Regarding the heir: Resident registration certificate; certificate of family relations; certified seal (ingan) certificate or signature verification
  • For limited acceptance, additionally: Estate inventory (listing real property, bank accounts, debts, etc.)
  • When minors are involved: Legal guardian documentation; special representative appointment if there is a conflict of interest

Confirm exact requirements with the relevant Family Court or via the Supreme Court's electronic family registration system (efamily.scourt.go.kr) or e-court portal (ecfs.scourt.go.kr).

6.4 Online Filing

Declarations may be submitted online through the Supreme Court's electronic court system (ecfs.scourt.go.kr), provided the applicant has a recognized digital certificate (public certificate) for electronic signature and can attach all documents in electronic form.

7. Multiple Heirs and the Cascade Effect

7.1 Co-Heirs and Limited Acceptance

When multiple heirs exist, each may independently file for limited acceptance of their own share. One heir's limited acceptance does not bind others. If some heirs choose limited acceptance and others accept unconditionally, each bears their own share of liability accordingly.

7.2 The Renunciation Cascade

When an heir renounces, their share passes to co-heirs of the same priority, or — if all same-priority heirs have also renounced — to the next-priority heirs (Article 1043). Priority order under Korean law:

  • 1st priority: Direct descendants (children, grandchildren) and spouse
  • 2nd priority: Direct ascendants (parents, grandparents) and spouse
  • 3rd priority: Siblings
  • 4th priority: Collateral relatives within the 4th degree

If all children renounce, the debt passes to the decedent's living parents, who may face unexpected liability. Coordinating a family-wide renunciation strategy — or having the first-priority heir use limited acceptance instead — prevents this cascade from harming unsuspecting relatives.

7.3 Spouse

The surviving spouse is always a co-heir alongside the highest-priority class present. The spouse may renounce or file limited acceptance independently. A spouse's renunciation does not affect children's shares — it simply reduces the spouse's own inherited liability.

8. After Limited Acceptance: The Liquidation Process

Once limited acceptance is granted by the court, the heir must carry out a formal liquidation procedure toward creditors.

8.1 Publishing Notice to Creditors

Within 5 days of the limited acceptance filing, the heir must publish a notice in a daily newspaper inviting creditors and legatees to submit their claims (Civil Act Article 1032). The notice period must be at least 2 months.

8.2 Priority of Repayment

After the notice period, secured claims (mortgages, pledges) are satisfied first, followed by general claims on a pro-rata basis. Any excess debts beyond the estate's value are extinguished — the heir is not personally liable.

8.3 Prohibition on Asset Disposal During Liquidation

The heir must not dispose of estate assets during the notice period. Preferential repayment to specific creditors or concealment of assets can result in legal consequences.

9. Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Does the Family Court have to issue its ruling within 3 months?

No. The heir only needs to file within 3 months. The court's ruling may be issued after the deadline has passed without affecting the validity of the filing.

Q: What if I already received some of the inheritance before filing?

Disposing of or consuming inherited assets before renunciation is complete may trigger statutory simple acceptance (Article 1026), making renunciation or limited acceptance impossible. Act before touching any inherited property.

Q: Can the Family Court reject my renunciation filing?

A properly completed renunciation declaration filed within the deadline is generally accepted. However, filings that are late, that appear to follow acts constituting simple acceptance, or that are deficient in documents may be rejected.

Q: Can a parent file on behalf of a minor child?

Yes, the legal guardian (parent or court-appointed guardian) files on the minor's behalf. If the parent and child have a conflict of interest — for example, the parent is keeping the inheritance while filing renunciation for the child only — a special representative must be appointed by the court.

10. Practical Checklist

  1. Record the exact date you learned of the decedent's death
  2. Mark the 3-month deadline on a calendar; aim to decide within 1-2 months
  3. Inventory all known assets (real estate, bank accounts, insurance, securities) and debts (loans, guarantees, taxes)
  4. Compare assets vs debts to choose renunciation, limited acceptance, or simple acceptance
  5. Consult all co-heirs and downstream relatives if renouncing — ensure they understand the cascade effect
  6. Confirm jurisdiction and gather required documents for the relevant Family Court
  7. Seek professional legal advice if the timeline is tight or the estate is complex
  8. If filing limited acceptance, complete the creditor notice and liquidation steps
