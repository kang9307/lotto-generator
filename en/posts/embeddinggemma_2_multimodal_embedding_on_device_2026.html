This article rests on the sentences and tables in three documents: the EmbeddingGemma 2 announcement published on the official Google blog on October 6, 2026, the developer guide published on the Google Developers Blog on the same day, and the model card. Figures are carried over together with their measurement conditions, and any value that does not appear in the sources is marked as a calculation.

Background — How a Text Embedder Came to Place Five Kinds of Input in One Space

Google published the EmbeddingGemma 2 announcement on its official blog on October 6, 2026. The authors are Sahil Dua and Henrique Schechter Vera, both research engineers at Google DeepMind. According to the announcement, the first-generation EmbeddingGemma was introduced last year as a lightweight text embedding model that runs on consumer hardware, it has been downloaded more than 20 million times, and builders have used it to power on-device search tools and privacy-first retrieval augmented generation (RAG) pipelines.

The second generation widens the scope beyond text. The announcement says the model unifies code, images, video and audio in a shared embedding space, and the model card specifies that space as a single 768-dimensional vector space. The model is built on the Gemma 4 architecture, has 740 million parameters (740M) in total, and is released, in the words of the announcement, under a commercially permissive Apache 2.0 license. The usage scenes the announcement offers are finding a specific video clip from a voice memo and searching through hours of audio recordings with a text query, all handled by a single natively multimodal model.

The subtitle of the announcement calls the model the most capable model for on-device multimodal embeddings, and the body describes it as best-in-class for its size. Both phrases are Google describing its own model, and the original text limits the comparison to multimodal embedders under one billion parameters (sub-1B). The values that actually matter when choosing an on-device embedding model sit underneath those phrases, and there are four of them: how the model is put together, how far its output vectors can be shortened, how much memory it occupies on a device, and how much input it accepts in a single call.

  • Configuration: Text-only workloads need as little as 270 million (270M) parameters, and the vision encoder of 170 million (170M) and the audio encoder of 300 million (300M) are optional.
  • Dimension truncation: With Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL) the 768-dimensional output can be truncated to 512, 256 or 128 dimensions, and the storage reduction is stated as up to 6x.
  • Memory: With quantization, on a Google Pixel 11 Pro, the text-only weights need about 191MB of active RAM and the full multimodal model about 567MB. The original wording is as little as, which presents these as lower bounds.
  • Context: The 8K-token context window is four times that of the first generation, and it accepts up to 5.5 minutes of audio, 29 images, 58 video frames, or interleaved combinations of them.

Configurations — A 270M Text Backbone and Selectively Loaded Encoders

The model card splits the 740 million parameters into three blocks. The 270 million text backbone is further divided into a 130 million transformer and a 140 million embedder, and a 170 million vision encoder and a 300 million audio encoder are attached to it. As the developer guide explains it, the text and code side is an adapted Gemma 4 decoder with an 8,192-token context window, the vision encoder processes images, visual documents (PDFs, slides and charts) and video frames, and the audio encoder is a dedicated speech and sound encoder that ingests raw audio directly. Every input passes through its modality-specific encoder and then through the shared backbone, and the resulting embeddings land in the same 768-dimensional space.

The vision and audio encoders are independent components. In sentence-transformers, setting vision_config or audio_config to None in config_kwargs means that encoder is not loaded. The guide notes that disabled encoders are never loaded into memory, so the savings apply to both the weights and the peak allocation.

Configuration config_kwargs (model card) Effective size Active RAM (quantized, Pixel 11 Pro, announcement)
Text only {"vision_config": None, "audio_config": None} 270M about 191MB
Text and image {"audio_config": None} 440M —
Text and audio {"vision_config": None} 570M —
Full multimodal {} 740M about 567MB

The following is the selective loading code printed in the guide. MODEL_ID is defined as "google/embeddinggemma-2" in the preceding block of the guide. Each of the three calls builds its own model object from that same model ID, and the comment line above each call states the parameter count of the configuration it produces.

# Text only (270M parameters)
                    text_only_model = SentenceTransformer(
                        MODEL_ID,
                        config_kwargs={"vision_config": None, "audio_config": None},
                    )
                    
                    # Text, images, and video (440M parameters)
                    text_image_model = SentenceTransformer(
                        MODEL_ID,
                        config_kwargs={"audio_config": None},
                    )
                    
                    # Text and audio (570M parameters)
                    text_audio_model = SentenceTransformer(
                        MODEL_ID,
                        config_kwargs={"vision_config": None},
                    )

According to the guide, in sentence-transformers all four encoder setups load from the same checkpoint, so they share one vector space. A query embedded with the 270M text-only setup can be matched directly against documents embedded with the full model. If a project starts with a text-only index and later adds image or audio embeddings, it is enough to reload the model with the additional encoder enabled, and embeddings that have already been computed do not need to be computed again. The model card adds one caveat: the way to configure the model so that some encoders are omitted differs among model libraries, and the documentation of each library should be consulted.

Only two cells in the RAM column of the table carry a value. The announcement gives figures for the text-only weights and for the full multimodal model, and it gives none for the 440M and 570M configurations, so the two intermediate rows are left with a dash rather than an estimate. The general guidance the guide offers on choosing a configuration is to load only the encoders the data needs, and to truncate dimensions only when storage or search speed require it.

Dimension Truncation — From 768 Down to 128, and What It Costs

Because the model is trained with MRL, the 768-dimensional output vector can be shortened by keeping only its leading dimensions. The supported dimensions are 768, 512, 256 and 128, that is, the native output length and three shorter lengths. The up to 6x storage reduction in the announcement is the ratio obtained when 768 dimensions are truncated to 128 (768 divided by 128 is 6, a calculation), and it matches the 1:6 compression ratio in the model card table. The guide gives an example: in bfloat16 precision, storing one million 768-dimensional vectors takes roughly 1.5GB of memory, while truncating them to 128 dimensions requires just 250MB.

Quality falls as the vector gets shorter, and the table shows that the size of the fall differs from one benchmark column to the next. The model card prints the scores for each dimension in a table and states that all of these results use the full-precision checkpoint.

Output dimension Compression ratio MTEB multilingual v2 MTEB English v2 MTEB code v1 MIEB lite MMEB v2 overall MSEB retrieval MAEB
768d (full) 1:1 61.36 68.46 78.68 64.64 59.01 69.54 49.39
512d 1:1.5 61.17 68.41 77.24 64.32 58.38 69.18 49.21
256d 1:3 60.41 67.78 76.18 63.13 56.24 66.76 48.91
128d 1:6 57.89 65.68 71.41 59.06 45.65 56.71 46.92

The model card says the quality impact is minimal down to 256 dimensions, and that 128 dimensions degrades multimodal quality substantially and should be validated against your own workload before adoption. The drop of the MMEB v2 overall score from 59.01 at 768 dimensions to 45.65 at 128 dimensions in the table is where that statement shows up. In the same column the score at 256 dimensions is 56.24, and the MTEB multilingual v2 column moves only from 61.36 to 60.41 over the same step. The guide gives the following criteria for choosing a dimension.

  • 768 or 512 dimensions: Use these for multimodal retrieval and visual document retrieval, or whenever recall matters more than storage.
  • 256 dimensions (3x storage reduction): Use this for storage-constrained indexes. It keeps most of the full quality of the original embedding on text and code, and about 95% on image, video and speech retrieval.
  • 128 dimensions (6x storage reduction): Use this for large text-only indexes and for first-stage shortlisting before re-ranking. Text and code retain around 90% of quality, but image, video and speech retrieval quality drops to around 75%. The guide attaches a sentence telling readers to validate on their target data before deploying 128 dimensions for multimodal queries.

The model card attaches two runtime rules to truncation.

  • Re-normalize after truncating. Slicing a unit-length vector does not preserve unit length, so the shortened vector has to be L2-normalized before it is used for cosine similarity. Skipping this step does not raise an error; it produces plausible-looking scores while silently degrading ranking quality.
  • Queries and documents share one dimension. A 768-dimensional query cannot be scored against a 128-dimensional corpus.

In sentence-transformers, passing truncate_dim and normalize_embeddings to encode() handles both steps together. The example in the guide is as follows.

# Truncate the query
                    query_emb = model.encode(
                        query,
                        prompt_name="SearchQuery",
                        truncate_dim=256,
                        normalize_embeddings=True,
                    )

To use one dimension for every call, it can be set at load time instead, as in SentenceTransformer(MODEL_ID, truncate_dim=256).

Input Limits — The Rules for Sharing 8,192 Tokens

The context window is 8,192 tokens, and all modalities draw from this single budget. The announcement describes it as four times larger than that of the first generation (working backwards, the first generation had 2,048 tokens; this is a calculation). Each modality consumes the budget at a fixed rate, and the figures in the announcement of 5.5 minutes of audio, 29 images and 58 video frames follow from those rates. The table below sets the rate and the maximum given in the model card next to the wording used in the announcement, one row per modality.

Modality Token cost (model card) Max input (model card) Wording in the announcement
Text 1 token per subword 8,192 tokens 8K tokens
Image 280 tokens per image (default) about 29 images 29 images
Video 140 tokens per frame (default) about 58 frames 58 video frames
Audio 25 tokens per second about 327 seconds up to 5.5 minutes
  • Single-modality assumption: The maximums assume a single modality with no accompanying text. Interleaved inputs draw from the same budget, so mixing modalities reduces the amount of each that fits.
  • Video sampling: By default, video is processed as frames sampled at 1 frame per second through the vision encoder, and the sampling rate is configurable. At the default rate, 58 frames correspond to about 58 seconds of footage (a calculation).
  • Audio format: Audio should be supplied as mono at 16 kHz.
  • Vision token budget: The number of soft tokens that represent an image or a video frame can be chosen in the range from 70 to 1120. With a lower budget, as many as about 114 images or frames fit; raising the budget trades latency and token count for quality.
  • How media is passed: Media is passed as file paths (MP4 for video), URLs (for images and audio), or in-memory PIL images, arrays and tensors.

When text and media are mixed in one input, the placeholder tokens <|image|>, <|video|> and <|audio|> are placed in the text to mark the position of each media item. Each placeholder is filled from the corresponding key, in order, and the call returns a single embedding that represents the text and the media together. The interleaved example in the usage section further down relies on exactly this mechanism.

Benchmarks — The Numbers Google Published and Their Scope

The announcement makes exactly one performance claim with numbers attached. The MTEB Code score rose by 9.92 points, from 68.76 for the first generation to 78.68, and the guide and the model card describe this as an improvement of about 14%. On the strength of that figure, the announcement judges the model well-suited for local codebase indexing, semantic code search and coding agent retrieval. For multilingual text, the wording of the announcement is that the model matches the performance of the first generation, and the MTEB multilingual v2 scores in the model card are 61.36 against 61.15.

The scores for the remaining modalities are in the model card. The values below are for the full-precision checkpoint at 768 dimensions, and because the first generation is a text model, its cells for image, video and audio are empty.

This table contains no scores for models from other companies. The announcement says the model achieves leading scores among sub-1B multimodal embedders, matches or outperforms many larger models, and even outperforms some specialist models more than twice its size, but it does not name the models it was measured against. For that reason the numbers are read together with the following conditions, which cover what the comparison is limited to, who produced the scores, under what setting they were taken, and how evenly they can be expected to hold across languages.

  • Scope of comparison: The phrase best-in-class is used together with the qualifiers for its size and among sub-1B multimodal embedders. The wording in the model card is that the model is among the strongest multimodal embedding models under 1B parameters.
  • Who measured: Every score in the table is a value Google published in the model card.
  • Measurement condition: The scores are for the full-precision checkpoint, while the figures of about 191MB and 567MB are for a quantized model. The two sets of numbers come from different conditions.
  • Languages: The model card says the model supports more than 100 languages, and alongside that it lists the limitation that the model may not exhibit equal performance across languages.

Usage — sentence-transformers, Task Prefixes, and Numerical Precision

The guide says the model can be run across text, code, images, video and audio with the sentence-transformers library (v6.1.0 or later), and gives pip install -U sentence-transformers[image,audio,video] transformers as the install command. The model ID is google/embeddinggemma-2, the same identifier under which the weights are published on Hugging Face.

Text inputs take a task instruction prefix. According to the model card, the model is trained with short task instruction prefixes prepended to text inputs; using the right prefix improves quality, and omitting it still works but reduces precision. Giving a name to prompt_name in encode() adds the prefix automatically.

  • Asymmetric tasks (such as retrieval): Use a query prefix for queries and a document prefix for corpus items. The prompt names are SearchQuery, QuestionAnswering, FactChecking and CodeRetrieval.
  • Symmetric tasks (such as classification and similarity): Apply the same task prefix to all inputs being compared. Classification, Clustering and SentenceSimilarity belong here.
  • Document format: Documents with a real title are formatted as title: {title} | text: {content}, and title: none is used when no title is available. Because prompt_name="Document" applies title: none, titled documents still have to be formatted manually.
  • Media: Prefixes apply to text only. Images, video and audio are passed without any prefix.
  • Numerical precision: Run inference in bfloat16 or float32, and do not use float16. The model card explains that the activation range of the model exceeds the dynamic range of float16, so in float16 the model returns NaN or silently degraded embeddings rather than raising an error. bfloat16 is the recommended default on hardware with native support and halves memory use relative to float32; float32 is used elsewhere, including on most CPUs.

Media is passed as a dictionary keyed by modality. The cross-modal search and interleaved input example in the guide is as follows. Here model is the full model loaded with SentenceTransformer(MODEL_ID). The first half of the block embeds a photo and a sound recording and scores one text query against each of them, and the second half embeds a product listing made of text, a photo and a video as a single vector and scores a second text query against it.

# Cross-modal search: one text query against a photo and a sound recording
                    image_emb = model.encode({"image": "sunset_beach.jpg"})
                    audio_emb = model.encode({"audio": "ocean_waves.wav"})
                    query_emb = model.encode("ocean waves at sunset", prompt_name="SearchQuery")
                    
                    print(model.similarity(query_emb, image_emb))
                    print(model.similarity(query_emb, audio_emb))
                    
                    # Interleaved: one embedding for a product listing with text, photo, and video
                    listing_emb = model.encode({
                        "text": "Waterproof trail shoe. <|image|> Grip test on wet rock: <|video|>",
                        "image": "trail_shoe.jpg",
                        "video": "grip_test.mp4",
                    })
                    query_emb = model.encode("waterproof trail shoes", prompt_name="SearchQuery")
                    
                    print(model.similarity(query_emb, listing_emb))

The guide notes that, despite coming from different modalities, the embeddings occupy the same dimensional space and can be compared on their semantic similarity. In the example above, a single text query is matched in turn against the embeddings of a photo, a sound recording and a product listing, which is exactly that use.

Pairing with Gemma 4, Where to Download, and Supported Tools

The announcement says that pairing the model with a generative model such as Gemma 4 enables on-device RAG pipelines that understand complex multimodal data. Because EmbeddingGemma 2 is built on Gemma 4 and shares its text tokenizer and audio encoder, the two models can run together in a unified pipeline with a lower combined total memory footprint. The guide words the same point as sharing the same text tokenizer and audio encoder architecture. The conditions for running Gemma 4 on an older CPU are covered in Run Gemma 4 on a 10-Year-Old Xeon, diffusion-based text generation is covered in the DiffusionGemma article, and on-device tool calling with a model of a few tens of megabytes is covered in Needle 3 Technical Analysis.

The announcement, the guide and the model card all state the license as Apache 2.0; the announcement adds the description commercially permissive, and the guide says the weights on Hugging Face are released under the Apache 2.0 license. On top of that, the model card says that deployments must adhere to the Gemma Prohibited Use Policy. It also states that this is a pre-trained embedding model which, unlike generative models, does not undergo post-training alignment, safety tuning or output-level moderation, and that developers and deployers are responsible for application-level safeguards such as retrieval filtering and fairness testing.

The announcement says Google worked closely with partners so that the model works immediately where developers build, and it lists the following routes.

  • Weights: The weights are on Hugging Face and Kaggle. Availability in Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform Model Garden is described as coming soon. Models optimized for on-device use are in the LiteRT Community on Hugging Face.
  • On-device deployment: Google AI Edge MediaPipe is listed for turnkey embedding, retrieval and decision tasks, and LiteRT for custom model integration.
  • Browser: Builds for the browser use transformers.js or WebGPU.
  • Development tools: The list reads transformers, sentence-transformers, MLX, vLLM, llama.cpp, SGLang, Ollama and LMStudio.
  • Vector storage: Qdrant is listed for storing embedding vectors.
  • Fine-tuning: The announcement points to guidance by Unsloth.

The hands-on routes the announcement names are Instant Media Search in Google AI Edge Gallery (using text or an image to find the top semantic matches in a media library), Video Moments Finder in the same gallery (locating specific moments in video with text or audio queries), the Google AI Edge Foresight app, which pairs the model for local file retrieval with Gemma 4, and the MediaPipe Decision Task API for classification, routing and predictive capabilities.

What Could Not Be Confirmed

  • Quantization level and runtime. The announcement does not state the quantization bit width or the runtime under which the figures of about 191MB and 567MB were measured. It gives no values for other devices or for the 440M and 570M configurations.
  • Quality of the quantized model. The scores in the model card are for the full-precision checkpoint, and no scores after quantization were found in the three documents.
  • Models used for comparison. The names and scores of the models behind the claims of leading scores among sub-1B multimodal embedders and of outperforming specialist models more than twice its size do not appear in the text of the announcement, the guide or the model card. The benchmark figure in the guide is an image, and the values inside it were not used as evidence.
  • Processing speed. None of the three documents gives a latency per embedding or a throughput figure, on the Pixel 11 Pro or on any other hardware, so how fast the model indexes a collection on a given device is not something these sources answer.
  • Details of the license documents. The Apache 2.0 label in the model card links to a Gemma 4 license page, and the requirement to follow the prohibited use policy is stated separately. How the two documents fit together could not be confirmed, because those pages were not among the sources used here.
  • Size of the saving when run with Gemma 4. There is only the statement that the combined memory footprint is lower, with no figure attached.
  • Context window of the first generation. The 2,048-token value is worked backwards from the four-times statement and was not checked against first-generation documentation.
