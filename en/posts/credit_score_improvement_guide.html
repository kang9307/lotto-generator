Introduction: Why You Should Understand Your Credit Score Precisely, Not Vaguely Fear It

Your credit score in Korea can influence loan interest rates, card limits, and even some rental screening decisions, yet surprisingly few people know exactly what determines it. Claims like "using too many cards hurts your score" or "checking your own score lowers it" circulate widely with little basis.

This guide walks through what goes into a Korean credit score, which habits help, why submitting non-financial data matters, where the credit-check myth came from, and which programs to look into if you fall behind on payments. The scoring factors and weights follow what NICE Information Service and KCB publish on their own websites, and the programs follow materials from the Financial Services Commission and the Credit Counseling and Recovery Service, all rechecked on September 29, 2026. Credit-scoring criteria and related systems can change with the bureaus' and financial authorities' policies, so always verify your exact score and its basis through official channels like NICE and KCB. It is informational and does not recommend any specific financial product.

1. What Is a Credit Score? NICE and KCB

In Korea, personal credit scores are calculated independently by two credit bureaus: NICE Information Service and KCB (Korea Credit Bureau). It is common for the same person to receive different scores from the two, because the bureaus put different weights on categories such as repayment history, debt level, and credit-transaction type. Their published average weights show that NICE (based on its RK0600 score) weights repayment history (28.4%) and credit type (27.5%) most heavily, while KCB, averaged over general customers, weights credit-transaction type (38%) and debt level (24%) most heavily (see the table in section 2). Which bureau's score a given lender references can also vary, so it is safer not to judge your overall credit standing from a single bureau's number alone.

These are the average weights each bureau publishes (as of 2026-09-29). KCB notes that the figures are averages for each customer group and can differ depending on an individual's credit situation. Because factors with similar names contain different information and weights at each bureau, it is normal for NICE and KCB scores to differ. If you want to compare these with the US FICO score weights as well, see the table in section 3 of How to Improve Your Credit Score 2026 - A Complete Guide to Factors and Strategies. Here is what each factor covers.

  • Repayment history: Whether loan and card payments were made on time and whether you have been delinquent before. It carries the largest weight at NICE (28.4%), and KCB weights it most heavily (32%) for customers with past long-term delinquency. NICE states that longer, larger, and more frequent delinquencies are more negative.
  • Debt level: The size of debts you must repay, such as loans and guarantees. NICE states that new loans or guarantees are reflected negatively and repayment positively. KCB grades loans by lender sector, product attributes, and interest level, and states that the higher a loan's risk level, the more negatively it is used.
  • Credit type: How you have used debit and credit cards. NICE treats using a credit card without delinquency as a positive factor and persistent, habitual heavy use of installments and cash advances as a negative one. KCB lists continued debit-card use as positive and frequent use of short-term card loans (cash advances) as negative.
  • Length of credit history: How long it has been since your first and most recent credit accounts were opened. NICE classifies a longer history as positive and having no credit history at all as negative.
  • Non-financial and MyData information: Payment records for national pension, health insurance, telecom bills, and apartment maintenance fees, proof of income, and savings-type financial asset data registered via MyData. The weight is 7.3% at NICE and 8% for KCB general customers.

3. Practical Habits That Help: Avoiding Delinquency, Autopay, and Managing Card Balances

The most reliably helpful habits are simple. First, avoid delinquency in the first place. Both NICE and KCB start using a delinquency of KRW 100,000 or more lasting 5 business days or more as a short-term delinquency, so setting card and loan payments to automatic transfer to prevent missed payments caused by simply forgetting is the first step. Second, manage card balances and cash advances. The often-cited "keep utilization under 30% of your limit" is a rule of thumb used mainly for US credit scores, and NICE and KCB do not publish a utilization threshold. Instead, KCB treats increases in credit card balances and short-term card loan balances as a rising repayment burden and uses them negatively, while NICE treats an appropriate card spending amount as positive and habitual installments and heavy cash-advance use as negative. Not letting balances build up before the payment date and not relying on cash advances is the habit that matches the published criteria most directly. Third, avoid unnecessary new loans. A new loan, once disbursed, is itself reflected in your debt level, and the higher its risk by lender sector, product, and interest rate, the more negatively it is evaluated. It is worth asking whether a loan is truly necessary, and if it is, looking at lower-risk products first.

4. Raising Your Score by Submitting Non-Financial Data

A method especially meaningful for young adults or so-called thin-file consumers with little loan and card history is submitting non-financial data. NICE states that submitting non-financial information such as payment records for national pension, health insurance, telecom bills, and apartment maintenance fees, or proof of income (used only to confirm that income exists), is reflected as a positive factor in the credit score, and that these payment records are used for up to one year after submission (the period varies with the length of the payment record). KCB likewise explains that it judges a consumer's diligence using records of steady payments registered through non-financial data and MyData, such as national pension, health insurance, telecom, and insurance premium payments. Both bureaus accept submissions through their websites and MyData, so it is worth checking if you feel your financial transaction history is thin. Keep in mind that the usage period is limited, so it is not a one-time task. That said, this is one contributing factor and does not guarantee the same increase for everyone.

5. Debunking the Credit-Check Myth

The claim that "checking your credit score often lowers it" is wrong by today's standards. The myth does have roots, though. When Korea's Financial Services Commission announced its plan to strengthen the financial foundation for ordinary citizens in April 2011, it said credit inquiry records would no longer be reflected in credit evaluations, citing the problem that the many inquiry records created while people sounded out several lenders about loans had been acting as a factor that lowered their credit ratings. There really was a time when inquiry records counted against your rating, and the memory of it has lingered.

Today, NICE states that inquiry information is not used in credit evaluation, and KCB states that credit inquiry information, as information about new credit activity, is not used in evaluation regardless of customer group. Not only checking your own score, but also the inquiries lenders make when you apply for a loan or card, are not scoring factors. What affects your score is not the inquiry but the actual transaction. If loans you applied for at several places are actually disbursed, they are reflected in your debt level, and a high-risk loan is evaluated more negatively. In short, "checking your own score" or "comparing loan terms" is entirely different from "actually taking out several loans." For reference, the US FICO score gives a 10% weight to new credit and treats opening several accounts in a short period as a greater risk, so it is better not to carry advice from overseas sources straight over to Korean scores.

6. What to Do If You Fall Behind on Payments

If a delinquency has already occurred, the first priority is to resolve the overdue amount as quickly as possible. The published criteria show why speed matters. NICE and KCB start using delinquencies of KRW 100,000 or more lasting 5 business days or more, and classify those of 90 days or more as long-term delinquencies that weigh more heavily. KCB states that it uses 5 business days while a delinquency is ongoing and 8 business days at release, so the history of a delinquency repaid before 8 business days is not used.

Even after repayment, the record stays for a while. KCB states that it uses delinquency experience for up to 5 years if it lasted 90 days or more and up to 3 years if shorter, and NICE states that it uses credit-standing information, such as delinquencies over KRW 1 million lasting 3 months or more, for 5 years after repayment, and short-term delinquency information registered by lenders for 3 years after repayment. NICE also explains that repaying a delinquency does not immediately restore your score to where it was, and that it recovers gradually over time. If a lump-sum repayment is difficult, contacting the lender first to ask about adjusting the repayment schedule is better than letting it sit. If you already have multiple delinquencies or an amount too large to resolve on your own, it is worth looking into the credit-recovery programs covered next.

7. An Overview of Credit Recovery Programs

Korea's Credit Counseling and Recovery Service divides its debt-adjustment programs by how long you have been delinquent. If you are still paying normally or are 30 days or less behind, the program is fast-track debt adjustment; if you are 31 to 89 days behind, it is pre-workout; and debtors 90 days or more behind fall under personal workout. The adjustment methods it lists include debt reduction, interest-rate adjustment, installment repayment, extension of the repayment period, and repayment deferral. When debt is large and even these adjustments cannot realistically make repayment feasible, court-based individual rehabilitation (repaying within your disposable income for a set period, with the remaining debt discharged) or personal bankruptcy (release from repayment liability when you cannot repay) can be considered. The record left on your credit also differs by program. In February 2024, the Financial Services Commission announced that for people using debt adjustment through the Credit Counseling and Recovery Service or the New Start Fund, the debt-adjustment record, which had been cleared after two years of faithful repayment, would be cleared after one year (at the time, implementation was planned for March). It is advisable to consult an official channel such as the Credit Counseling and Recovery Service or the Korea Legal Aid Corporation about eligibility and procedure for your situation.

There were also temporary measures for people who had already repaid past delinquencies. The Financial Services Commission twice ran a credit recovery measure that stopped financial institutions from sharing and using the delinquency history of individuals and sole proprietors who had fully repaid small delinquencies incurred in a set period. The first covered delinquencies of KRW 20 million or less incurred between September 1, 2021 and January 31, 2024 and fully repaid by May 31, 2024, and was announced for implementation on March 12, 2024. The second covered delinquencies of KRW 50 million or less incurred between January 1, 2020 and August 31, 2025 and fully repaid by December 31, 2025; it was announced for implementation on September 30, 2025, and according to the government's policy briefing of 2026-01-22 it ended at the end of 2025. Both were time-limited measures, so whether a similar one returns has to be checked against Financial Services Commission announcements.

8. Common Myths, Corrected

  • "Checking your score lowers it": NICE and KCB state that they do not use inquiry information in credit evaluation, whether it is your own check or a lender's screening inquiry. Inquiry records counting against you belongs to the period before the Financial Services Commission said in 2011 that it would stop reflecting them.
  • "Never borrowing and never using a card gives you the highest score": Not necessarily. NICE classifies having no credit history as negative and a growing history as positive. Too little transaction data to evaluate can work against you, so building a modest, responsibly managed credit history is more favorable in the long run.
  • "A day or two late doesn't matter": NICE states that delinquency information under KRW 100,000 or under 5 business days is not reflected. But that threshold is easy to cross, and once crossed the record remains even after repayment (NICE keeps short-term delinquency information for 3 years after repayment). If a payment might slip even by a few days, contacting the lender in advance is the safer route.
  • "Only debit-card use helps your score": KCB lists continued debit-card use, and NICE lists using a credit card without delinquency, as positive factors. It is hard to say one is always better; what both bureaus treat as negative is frequent use of cash advances (short-term card loans).
  • "You must stick with one card issuer long-term": The number of institutions you deal with is not itself a published scoring factor. What is reflected negatively is new loans and guarantees, rising balances, and high-risk loans. The problem is opening several products at once and actually increasing your debt, not the number of institutions as such.

9. Conclusion: A Credit Score Management Checklist

Managing your credit score ultimately comes down to a handful of habits. First, set card and loan payments to autopay so you never cross the KRW 100,000 and 5-business-day delinquency threshold. Second, do not let card balances build up, and do not lean on cash advances or habitual installments. Third, avoid new loans you do not genuinely need, and if you do need one, look at lower-risk products first. Fourth, if your financial transaction history is thin, consider submitting non-financial data such as national pension, health insurance, and telecom payment records, and check whether to resubmit once the usage period (up to one year after submission at NICE) has passed. Fifth, since neither your own checks nor lender inquiries are reflected in your score, check your score periodically. Sixth, if a delinquency has occurred or seems likely, do not let it sit - talk to the lender or the Credit Counseling and Recovery Service first.
