Slop Is Not the Name of a Tool - How to Tell Unverified Filler From Writing That Earns Attention

Judging by style stacks up false positives, while the honest signals sit on the evidence side

2026-09-05 Trends & Culture

The word slop has become newsroom vocabulary in the space of about two years. It began as a term for the thin gruel poured into a livestock trough, and it now names writing that occupies a page without earning the space. The difficulty is that the word spread faster than its definition, and it is hardening into a synonym for something a machine typed.

Those are not the same claim. When the question is why a particular piece of writing is not worth reading, the identity of the author explains almost nothing. Recycled press releases, guides whose only real asset is a table of contents, and statistics that cite themselves through three intermediaries all predate the current generation of tools by decades. Run the argument the other way and it still holds: a draft that came out of a model, but which has been traced back to primary sources and signed by someone willing to attach a name to it, is not slop under any definition worth using.

Get this distinction wrong and everything downstream goes wrong with it. The test slides down to style, and the moment it lands on style it starts catching writers whose first language is not English and manuscripts that have been through heavy copy-editing. Meanwhile the thing that actually needed catching - the unsupported assertion and the number nobody can trace - hides better with every round of polish.

The starting point for what follows is a post published on 28 August 2026 by Ahrefs. The publisher sells search-visibility software, earns its revenue from search traffic, and the post is itself a piece of its own content marketing. That is an interested party, so its claims are attributed rather than adopted, kept inside the boundaries of what the post actually says, and marked as absent where the post says nothing. Search policy language and detector figures come from separate primary sources and carry their measurement conditions with them.

The trigger - what a post about avoiding slop actually argued

Si Quan Ong of Ahrefs published an account of the company workflow under the title How We Use AI for Every Article Without Making AI Slop. The editor named on the piece is Ryan Law, Director of Content Marketing, and the publication date is 28 August 2026.

From the headline alone it reads like another collection of workflow tips. The content is a different shape. The centre of gravity is not tooling but definition. The post nails down what slop is in a single sentence and then derives the rest of its process from that sentence. That is why it holds up better than a list of tips, which decays as soon as the tools change.

The position of the publisher still has to be read alongside it. Ahrefs sells a backlink index and rank tracking. Any narrative about how search evaluates content bears directly on demand for that product. The definition and the process are worth quoting on their own merits, but the implicit promise that following the process produces results is a vendor account rather than a verified claim. The post carries no traffic figures, no ranking data, and no quality metrics from before and after adopting the workflow.

Fix the definition first - slop is a state, not an authorship claim

The definition in the post reads: AI slop is content published without enough human understanding, judgement, evidence, or original contribution to justify the reader's attention. The summary that follows is shorter still: In short, slop transfers effort from the creator to the reader. The effort the creator declined to spend does not disappear. It lands on whoever reads the result.

What deserves attention in that definition is the vocabulary it leaves out. There is no tool. There is no model. There is no percentage of generated text. The conditions number four, and all four attach to the human side: understanding, judgement, evidence, original contribution. Slop, on this reading, is not a statement about whose hands the text passed through but about the state the text was in at the moment it was published.

Defining it as a state is what makes adjudication possible in the first place. Authorship cannot be established from the artefact; state can. Whether an assertion carries a source, whether that source is primary, whether a figure carries a denominator and a time window, whether a counter-example is addressed anywhere - all of this is visible on the surface of the page. Whether a given paragraph was typed by a person is not, and no amount of staring at the paragraph changes that.

Who produced the draftVerification and ownership attachedVerdictWhat this looks like in practice
A personYesNot slopReported pieces with checked sources, written records of real experiments
A personNoSlopRewritten press releases, rearranged encyclopedia summaries, guides that are only a table of contents
A machineYesNot slopGenerated drafts checked against primary sources and put through accountable editing
A machineNoSlopKeyword lists unfolded directly into pages at volume

The second row is the one that matters most for this argument. Slop written entirely by hand was the majority case long before any of this. What is new is not the phenomenon but the rate at which it can be produced.

Slop written by hand, non-slop written by machine

With the definition framed as a state, the examples follow. Every item below is written by a human being, and every one of them leaves at least two of the four conditions empty. None of these forms is recent, and all of them are being produced today.

  • The rewritten announcement - the word order of the press release changes and nothing is checked. The evidence is the announcing party itself, so what the reader receives is a summary of the announcement and nothing more
  • The rearranged ranking - ten other list articles are blended into a new ranking. There are no selection criteria, so there is no judgement, and tracing the sources back collapses them into the same two or three originals
  • The table-of-contents guide - every subheading a reader could want is present, and each section is filled with definitions and generalities. What decision the reader is supposed to make never appears
  • The orphan statistic - a sentence opens with seventy percent of the industry, the citation leads to another citation, and there is no original survey at the end of the chain
  • The case study with no counter-example - only successes are listed. How many attempts at the same method failed is never given, which makes the base rate impossible to estimate
  • The translated digest - a foreign-language original is rendered into a new language while the conditional clauses and stated limitations fall off in transit. What survives is a conclusion stripped of the conditions that made it true

The reverse direction holds just as firmly. If a model produced the draft but the topic was chosen by human judgement, every figure carries a primary source, the passages that could not be settled say so, and a named person is prepared to stand behind the publication, then all four conditions are satisfied. That piece is not slop, by definition.

The same distinction has already been worked through on the code side. How a repository handles a flood of unverified change requests is covered in the SlopGuard case, and the way comments accumulate while drifting away from what the code does is covered in comment slop, so neither is repeated here. The axis is identical: did the artefact pass verification.

Surface signals - the wall that style-based detection hits

In practice the first instinct is always style. Count the em dashes, add certain verbs to a banned-phrase list, measure the variance in paragraph length. Which expressions recur often enough to be noticeable is already catalogued in the inventory of AI smells, so the list is not reproduced here.

The source post rejects this direction outright: Remove every em dash and banned phrase and congratulations: you may now have slop with cleaner punctuation. Strip the punctuation tics and the banned vocabulary, and what remains is slop with tidier typography. Surface correction satisfies none of the four conditions in the definition, because none of the four conditions is about the surface.

Three reasons surface signals fail

  • They measure something else - what a style signal actually responds to is the predictability of word choice and the uniformity of sentence length. Both rise in machine output, and both rise equally in second-language writing, in corporate house style, and in manuscripts that have been through several editors
  • The costs are asymmetric - erasing a surface signal takes minutes and doing the verification takes hours. Put the test on style and the cheap side is the only side that gets optimised, which is exactly the wrong incentive
  • The false positives land on one group - the writers who get caught are disproportionately those who do not write in their first language, those following a rigid internal template, and those keeping sentences short and even to meet accessibility guidance

The size of the false positive problem, with conditions attached

The third point is not a matter of impression. It has been measured. Weixin Liang, Mert Yuksekgonul, Yining Mao, Eric Wu and James Zou published GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers in 2023. The conclusion in the abstract is brief: the detectors consistently misclassify writing samples from non-native English speakers as machine generated, while samples from native speakers are identified accurately.

The figures have to travel with their conditions. Detached from them, they create the false impression that the same numbers apply to every detector in service today.

ItemValueMeasurement conditions
Detectors evaluatedSevenCommercial and free services in public operation as of 2023. The individual product names are listed in the methods section of the paper

The direction of the last two rows is easy to invert by accident. Those values are not false positives; they are detection rates. A light rewrite of machine-generated text was enough to make the detectors miss most of it. Combine them with the four rows above and the conclusion converges: these tools flag honest human writing and let machine writing slip past. As a classifier, that is the worst possible pairing of errors.

One more condition belongs on the record. That measurement used the detectors of 2023 against the model output of that period. There is no basis for asserting that the same percentages hold for services in 2026. What does carry across is the structure of what is being measured, which is still the statistical texture of prose, so the direction of the bias - harsh on non-native writers, weak against light evasion - follows from the design rather than from a particular vintage. Using any generation of these tools to accuse an individual of misconduct is not something the evidence supports.

Honest signals - the weight of judgement sits on evidence

Set style aside and what remains are the signals attached to verifiability. Three properties make them worth using. The checking procedure is explicit. The result does not vary with the writer's first language or house style. And the same test applies without modification to writing produced entirely by hand.

Why the honest signals are honest

  • Assertions with no source - a factual claim that opens with generally or in most cases and carries nothing behind it. The check is trivial: ask for the material that makes the sentence true
  • Figures that cannot be traced - a percentage or an amount appears with no surveying body, no sample, and no period. Remove any one of the three and the next person cannot reproduce it
  • Multipliers with no denominator - three times faster, with nothing to compare against. Without knowing what the baseline was and under what conditions it was measured, a multiplier carries no information at all
  • Citation chains - the link leads to another summary article, which points to a third. If there is no primary material at the end of the chain, the existence of the figure has not been established
  • Absence of counter-examples - every case supports the argument and not one cuts against it. On any real-world subject a count of zero counter-examples is improbable on its face
  • Concealed failures to confirm - nowhere in the piece is there a document that would not open, an enquiry that went unanswered, or a point where the evidence divides. Honest work always leaves those marks somewhere

Kept as a list, these six are easy to skim past. Moved into a ledger, review becomes mechanical. The trick is to split the text into claims rather than sentences, because a single sentence often carries two claims of very different quality.

[CLAIM LEDGER]
claim     : half of domestic workplaces have adopted the scheme
type      : factual (statistic)
source    : ? none given
primary   : ? not established
conditions: surveying body / sample / period  ->  all three missing
verdict   : hold - remove from the draft until the original survey is located

claim     : processing time was reduced threefold
type      : factual (measurement)
source    : vendor announcement (primary, interested party)
primary   : yes
conditions: against what / under what load / how many runs  ->  second and third missing
verdict   : cite with conditions - mark as vendor measurement, load conditions undisclosed

Where the boundary falls between what a machine can adjudicate and what it cannot is treated separately in the line a verification harness draws. The short version: form and existence can be checked automatically, while interpretation and fitness for purpose cannot.

When drafting gets cheap, the bottleneck moves to verification

From definition to process. The cost of a published article divides roughly into four blocks: choosing the subject, gathering material, producing the draft, and verifying and editing. Over the past few years the unit cost of the third block has fallen sharply. The other three have barely moved.

When exactly one stage of a pipeline gets cheaper, what changes is not the throughput but the location of the bottleneck. If drafting yields ten pieces a day and verification clears one, the output of that team is still one piece a day. Ship the other nine unverified and, by the definition set out above, nine pieces of slop have just been created. The same structural shift, with comprehension as the constraint, is worked through on the engineering side in understanding as the new bottleneck.

The prescription the source post offers at this point is unambiguous: Don't just ask how many more articles AI lets you publish. Ask what you can now put inside an article that you previously could not afford to do. Do not ask how many more pieces are now possible; ask what can now go inside one piece that was previously unaffordable. Convert the saved hours into a higher article count and the bottleneck is untouched, so the only thing that grows is the slop.

The reinvestments the post names are data analysis, prototypes of free tools, interactive elements, interface improvements, and automation of the research process itself. What they have in common is that each of them is something a reader cannot get elsewhere. That is where the fourth condition in the definition, original contribution, actually gets satisfied.

Three seats a human keeps - topic, fact, accountability

If the bottleneck has moved to verification, where do the people go? Three seats.

StageWhat a machine can carryWhy a person remains
Choosing the subjectEnumerating candidates, aggregating search demand, detecting overlap with existing piecesDeciding what not to write is an organisational judgement. Subjects with demand and subjects you are entitled to write about are different sets
Setting the angleListing the arguments that could be madeStanding on one side while accepting the objections is a matter of accountability. An enumeration is not a position
Gathering materialSearching, summarising, proposing candidate sourcesTelling which item is primary material and which comes from an interested party is a judgement call
Producing the draftStructuring, generating sentences, controlling lengthThe least is left here, provided the raw material has already been supplied
Checking the factsFormat checks, link liveness, assisted comparison of quotationsWhether a source actually supports the claim requires reading it. Citations that are correctly formed and substantively wrong are the ones that get through most often
Owning the publicationNothingAttaching a name is not a delegable act

The source post puts its strongest language on that last seat: The person running the workflow should understand the topic well enough to validate its claims, correct misinformation, explain where the evidence came from, and decide whether they are happy attaching their name to it. The person operating the process has to know the subject well enough to validate the claims, correct the errors, explain where the evidence came from, and decide for themselves whether they are content to put their name on it. The conclusion is one line: If nobody truly owns the result, it's slop.

A second person attaches alongside. In the workflow described, every article gets one owner and one editor. The editor's job is not to smooth sentences but to challenge the assumptions, find the sections that could have appeared anywhere, and rule on whether the promise in the headline was kept. Why two people using identical tools end up with different results, and how much of that gap is verification skill, is treated in what actually separates the outcomes.

Six practices that lower the cost of verification

Blocking a bottleneck with additional headcount only raises the wage bill. Lowering the unit cost of the bottleneck is what changes the arithmetic. The six practices below need no new tooling, can be introduced today, and cut review time noticeably.

  • Make primary sources the default - if the citation lands on an institutional document, a statute, a paper, or an original announcement, the reviewer confirms it once. Cite a secondary digest and the reviewer has to walk the whole chain, which doubles the cost of every check
  • Force three fields onto every figure - surveying body, sample, period, left as blanks that must be filled before the number passes. A figure whose blanks cannot be filled is usually a figure that should not be cited
  • Attach a denominator to every multiplier - whenever a number of times appears, the baseline and the measurement conditions go in the same sentence. That single rule strains out a large share of the exaggeration that arrives with trigger headlines
  • Collect the failures to confirm in one section - documents that would not open and questions that stayed open go in a dedicated section near the end. A reviewer can read that section alone and see the risk surface, and the next revision inherits a list of what to revisit
  • Require one counter-example - asking for a single case that cuts against each claim makes the generic sections disappear by themselves. If no counter-example can be found, the claim has not yet been verified
  • Put names on the artefact - the owner and the editor go at the top of the document. The cost is close to zero and the effect on review density is larger than any other item on this list

Installing the six lines in a single day

No new software is required. Fix a six-line preamble at the top of the draft template and adopt one rule: review does not begin while any of the six lines is empty. Rules that live only in a policy document are not followed, so the move that works is changing the template itself.

[PRE-PUBLICATION PREAMBLE - SIX LINES]
owner       :
editor      :
reader      :            (who arrives here, trying to do what)
promise     :            (what they can do after reading)
info gain   :            (one line absent from the current top results)
open items  :            (documents that would not open / questions still open)

The full procedure for splitting text into claims and verifying them one at a time is laid out stage by stage in filtering hallucination through process. The six lines above are the entrance to that pipeline.

Where the gates go - the four points the post proposes

The workflow in the source post reduces to four large moves: finish the human work before drafting, build places where the process stops for a judgement, spend the reclaimed time on making the article better, and give every piece an owner plus a second human.

The second move is the concrete one. Five things get decided before any drafting begins: who the reader is and what they are trying to do, what the piece promises, which point of view it takes and which objections it accepts in return, what evidence it needs, and what its information gain is relative to the results that already rank. Letting all five be settled at once in a single instruction is the failure mode the post names most directly.

The gates sit at four positions: the idea gate, the outline gate, the evidence gate, and the draft gate. The work done at each is not polishing prose but deciding whether this proceeds or stops here. The failure the post warns against is seizing on the first output and trying to improve it. The question is not how to make this better but why this should exist at all.

The raw-material list is equally concrete: interviews, internal knowledge, proprietary data, real demonstrations, failed experiments, and specific examples drawn from your own work. What unites them is that they are things a model cannot produce on its own. Add gates without raw material and the pass criterion quietly slides down to style, because there is nothing else at the gate to examine. Gates only function when there is material to gate.

The platform baseline - value, not method of production

Whether writing with AI carries a penalty in search is the question that comes up most often in practice. The answer falls out of reading the conditions in the official document as written.

The relevant clause in the Google Search spam policies is scaled content abuse. The definition reads: Scaled content abuse is when many pages are generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users. Many pages generated for the primary purpose of manipulating rankings, and not helping users. What matters is that there are two conditions. Scale and absence of value have to arrive together.

A tool does appear, in the list of examples under that same clause: Using generative AI tools or other similar tools to generate many pages without adding value for users. Using generative AI tools or similar tools to produce many pages that add no value for users. The weight of the sentence sits on without adding value, not on the tool. No sentence in that policy makes the act of using a tool a violation by itself.

The conditions that fall off when policy language is repeated

Policy tends to be misquoted the same way every time: the conditions drop off one at a time. Written with AI, therefore spam discards one of the two conditions outright, and produced at volume, therefore spam discards the other. What the policy targets is the intersection of ranking manipulation and absence of value, and either condition alone falls outside it.

What changes and what stays the same when results are rendered as generated answers is treated in the confirmed boundaries of answer engine optimisation. One point carries over here. However the surface is presented, what the policy documents put in the condition slot is still value to the user.

Is disclosure required - what the official documents actually say

Disclosure is the next question. The Google guidance document on generative AI content carries a last-updated stamp of 10 December 2025 and offers two sentences on the subject: Sharing information about how a piece of content was created can help give your readers more context. And: Consider adding information on how your content was created in a way that makes sense for your audience.

Both sentences are advisory. Can help, and consider. Nothing in that document mandates an AI label on an ordinary article. There is, however, one obligation stated in mandatory terms, and it sits in commerce: where product data has been generated by AI, it must be specified separately and labelled as AI generated.

One more observation. That document does not mention E-E-A-T at all. It points instead to Search Essentials and to the spam policies. The sentence asserting that Google requires E-E-A-T of AI-assisted content circulates widely in practitioner writing, and at minimum it is not in this guidance document. The concept belongs to the quality rater guidelines, which is a different source and should not be blended with this one.

Taken together, disclosure is less a compliance question than a question about the relationship with the reader. How readers form judgements about machine-assisted text is set out in how readers respond to AI-written text, and the same problem in visual work is covered in reading the tells in design.

An editorial checklist in twelve lines

Everything above compresses into a form usable immediately before publication. The left column is the item, the middle is the pass condition, the right is the action when it fails.

Reader and promiseEach is written out in one sentenceReturn the piece to the drafting stage
Information gainOne line exists that the current top results do not haveChange the subject or add raw material
Raw materialAt least one of interview, proprietary data, direct measurement, or a failure recordHold publication and secure the material first

Denominators on multipliersWhat it is measured against sits in the same sentenceAdd the conditions or drop the multiplier

None of the twelve lines examines style. That omission is deliberate. Style admits no pass condition, and an item with no pass condition, once placed on a checklist, becomes noise that each reviewer resolves differently.
