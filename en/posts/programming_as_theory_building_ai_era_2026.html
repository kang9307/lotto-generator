Why is a 1985 Paper Back on Top in 2026?

On May 14, 2026, a 41-year-old paper was reposted on GeekNews (news.hada.io), a Korean developer news community, under a Korean title that translates as "Programming as Theory Formation (1985)". The paper is Peter Naur's "Programming as Theory Building", originally published in Microprocessing and Microprogramming, Vol. 15, No. 5 (May 1985), pp. 253-261. At the time of its publication, object-oriented programming had not yet conquered the industry, and the entire software field was openly worrying about the so-called "software crisis". The common question of that era was painfully simple: why does software keep falling apart the more we build of it? Naur's answer opens the paper. Programming, he wrote, "properly should be regarded as an activity by which the programmers form or achieve a certain kind of insight, a theory, of the matters at hand", in contrast to the more common notion "that programming should be regarded as a production of a program and certain other texts."

The reason this essay is being reread alongside today's tooling debates is unmistakable. It is the explosion of AI coding tools: GitHub Copilot, Cursor, Claude Code, OpenAI Codex, Windsurf, and the rest. These tools arrived with the expectation of a large jump in code production. At the same time, an increasingly common complaint from senior engineers has emerged: "Code is faster, but systems are breaking faster too. Why?" There is also evidence that perception and measurement can diverge. In METR's July 2025 randomized controlled trial, 16 experienced open-source developers completed 246 tasks; tasks where AI tools were allowed took 19% longer, and afterwards the developers still believed AI had sped them up by 20%. METR explicitly cautions against generalizing the result to software development as a whole. In a follow-up notice on February 24, 2026 (We are Changing our Developer Productivity Experiment Design), METR said that 30-50% of the developers it invited to a follow-up experiment declined to take part in the no-AI condition, and that this selection effect biased the sample. METR thinks developers in early 2026 are likely faster with AI, but calls that evidence weak, and says it is changing its experiment design. In July 2025, developer John Whiles wrote a post explaining that result through Naur's theory building view, and it was picked up by GeekNews as well. The most precise answer to that question already existed, written four decades ago, by a man who edited the ALGOL 60 report in his early thirties and published this essay in his mid-fifties.

This article is not a translation of Naur's paper. It is an opinionated analysis that reads the concept against the realities of Korean SI contracts, startups, large-enterprise legacy modernization, and AI tooling rollouts: how the 1985 concept of theory building applies, concretely, to programming in 2026. Sentences taken from Naur are quoted as such; the rest is this article's interpretation. The short version is this: Naur's framework is the single sharpest tool we have to understand which parts of programming AI can automate and which parts it cannot. The faster AI generates code, the more valuable the parts that are not code become.

Who Was Peter Naur, and Why Should You Care?

Many developers know the name only obliquely. If you ever used Backus-Naur Form (BNF) to describe a grammar in a compilers course, the "Naur" is this man. Peter Naur was a Danish computer scientist and the editor of the highly influential ALGOL 60 report. According to ACM's biography, Backus's 1959 paper on syntax notation initially received little attention; several months later Naur applied it to ALGOL and demonstrated its practicality. He received the ACM Turing Award in 2005 - often called the Nobel Prize of computing - "for fundamental contributions to programming language design and the definition of Algol 60, to compiler design, and to the art and practice of computer programming."

What makes Naur particularly interesting is the direction of his thinking. The master of formal notation increasingly treated programming as human knowledge and activity. ACM's biography notes that he studied William James's psychology for decades and, in the 1990s and 2000s, wrote several books scrutinizing assumptions of western philosophy that many computing researchers take for granted. In this essay he already compares learning the theory of an existing program to learning writing or playing a musical instrument, where the most important education is doing the relevant things under suitable supervision and guidance. "Programming as Theory Building" is the most condensed expression of this view. It is short, written in plain prose, and contains almost no formal notation - which is striking, coming from the man whose name sits inside BNF.

Naur died on January 3, 2016. He could not have foreseen that within ten years, the explosion of AI coding tools would push his 1985 essay back into the center of industry conversation. But his core insight was the kind of thing that does not age. It just waits, quietly, for the moment when it becomes obvious. That moment is now.

What is Programming as Theory Building?

Naur's central claim, condensed into a single sentence: programming is the construction of a theory about a system in the programmer's mind, and code is only a partial expression of that theory. The word "theory" here is not used in the everyday English sense of a vague guess. It means something closer to a structured understanding: why this system behaves the way it does, what alternatives were considered and discarded, what constraints were assumed, what failure modes were anticipated. That structured understanding lives in full form only inside the heads of the people who built the system.

An analogy helps. An architectural blueprint is not a building. The blueprint is a partial expression of the building. The building itself is the blueprint plus the architect's complete decision history, the rejected alternatives, the on-site adjustments, the negotiations with the client, and the constraints of the surrounding environment. Hand the same blueprint to a different architect and you get a different building, because too much of the decision context never made it onto the page. Programming follows the same structure exactly: code is the blueprint, the real system is code plus the theory in the head of whoever wrote it.

Naur's most consequential insight is what he says next: "an essential part of any program, the theory of it, is something that could not conceivably be expressed, but is inextricably bound to human beings." A programmer who has the theory can, in his words, "explain how the solution relates to the affairs of the world that it helps to handle", "explain why each part of the program is what it is", and "respond constructively to any demand for a modification of the program". If the original authors leave and only the code remains, the system is, in a deep sense, dead, even if it continues to run. A successor team can rediscover parts of the theory by reading the code, but never all of it. The reasons specific alternatives were rejected, the context in which a particular trade-off was made, the failure modes that were anticipated and pre-empted, the deliberate simplifications and their boundaries - all of these belong to the theory and rarely survive a clean code handover.

Why Can't You Just Read the Code to Understand the System?

Anyone who has been on a team for more than a couple of years has lived through this. You inherit a codebase. You read it carefully. The naming is reasonable, the comments are present, the function decomposition is sensible. But every few hundred lines you find yourself asking: why is this module sitting here? why does this caching layer get bypassed only in this specific path? why is async used in only some of the request handlers and not others? No matter how long you read, the code does not answer those questions, because that information was never the kind of thing that could live in code in the first place.

Here is this article's taxonomy of what kinds of information actually go missing. First, rejected alternatives: "we tried B three times and discovered it broke transactional consistency under load" never appears in the committed source. Second, external constraints: "this API still mirrors a 2018 vendor contract that we are obliged to support" is not a comment anyone writes. Third, anticipated futures: "this module is intentionally loosely coupled because we plan to split it into a separate service in 12 months" stays in someone's head. Fourth, non-functional priorities: "this section was deliberately optimized for readability over performance because the CTO made that call" disappears with the people who were in the room.

The downstream effect is brutal and predictable: once the original authors are gone, maintenance cost explodes. The successor team has to re-derive every non-obvious decision from scratch, and along the way is heavily tempted to throw out the existing code and rewrite it, often reintroducing the very mistakes the originals had already paid to learn. This is the pattern that produces the recurring Korean SI experience: the system that was outsourced, delivered, and then quietly rewritten by a successor team three years later. It is not exclusively a Korean problem - the same dynamic is widely described elsewhere - but it is especially acute in markets where vendor handovers and team turnover are frequent. Naur put it this way in 1985: "The death of a program happens when the programmer team possessing its theory is dissolved." A dead program may keep running and producing useful results; its death "becomes visible when demands for modifications of the program cannot be intelligently answered." He called reestablishing a program's theory merely from the documentation "strictly impossible", and went as far as suggesting that the existing program text be discarded and a new team be given the opportunity to solve the problem afresh, at no higher, and possibly lower, cost. The urge to rewrite is not necessarily the mistake; it is the signal that the theory is already gone.

How Does AI Coding Affect Theory Transfer?

This is where the 2026 picture gets really interesting. With the explosion of AI coding tools through 2024 and 2025, a new dynamic has appeared: AI generates code rapidly and capably, but AI does not generate theory. To be precise, AI assistants do leave behind a residue of context - chat transcripts, scratch notes - but that residue is not the same thing as the structured understanding that emerges when a human has personally validated every assumption baked into a design. It is the byproduct of statistical pattern matching, not the byproduct of conviction.

As of September 2026, no public study was found that measures how much more AI-led code costs to change months later than equivalent human-authored code. So this article argues from structure rather than numbers. AI-generated code can look fine on day one - tests pass, linters are happy, behavior matches the spec - and still enter the repository with no one in the team holding the theory that produced it. As the METR trial showed, perceived speed and measured time can diverge, so teams are safer measuring how long changes to AI-generated code actually take.

This article uses a phrase for this: theory debt. It is not the same as classical technical debt. Classical technical debt is "the code is messy and we know where the mess is". Theory debt is "the code is fine, but no human in this organization can reliably tell you why it is shaped the way it is, and so every meaningful change requires re-deriving the original design from scratch". Theory debt is invisible on day one and devastating in month nine. In organizations that skip theory work, an AI tooling rollout can become an uncontrolled experiment in accumulating theory debt at unprecedented speed.

What Are the 5 Symptoms of a System Whose Theory Has Broken?

In this article's reading, a system whose theory has effectively decayed tends to show a recognizable cluster of symptoms. If three or more of the following are true for a system you work on, the theory is likely already in serious trouble. This is a rule of thumb, not a measured threshold.

Symptom 1. Adding a new feature breaks something unrelated

You touch module A to add a feature and module B, which appears unrelated, breaks in production. This means implicit dependencies exist between A and B, and no one in the team holds the mental model that would explain them. The theory has decayed to the point where the modules can no longer be reasoned about in isolation.

Symptom 2. No one in code review can answer "why was it done this way?"

The most reliable single test. If you ask the question in a code review and the answer is "that's how it has always been" or "the person who built it left", theory transfer has already failed. The code keeps working, but the system has effectively gone dark.

Symptom 3. The same bug class keeps reappearing in different modules

You fix a bug in module A. A month later, the same kind of bug shows up in modules B, C, and D. The cause is structural: when the first bug was fixed, the underlying lesson was not preserved as part of the team's living theory, so the same mistake propagates wherever the original author did not personally write the code.

Symptom 4. Documentation has drifted from code

You open the wiki, the README, or the ADR folder and find content frozen six or twelve months ago. The documentation is not lying; it is just stale. This is the signal that documentation is being treated as a "deliverable" rather than as a living expression of the team's current theory.

Symptom 5. New hires take 6+ months to contribute meaningfully

If a competent engineer joins your team and still cannot ship meaningful changes after six months, it is almost certainly not their fault. It is a sign that the team's theory lives almost entirely inside a small number of senior heads, and there is no scalable channel for transferring it. The day those senior people leave, you have a crisis.

How Can Developers Preserve Theory in Practice?

Theory does not naturally end up in source files, but that does not mean it cannot be preserved. The patterns below are this article's recommendations.

Architecture Decision Records (ADRs)

The single highest-leverage practice. Every non-obvious decision gets a short document - usually one page - covering the context that forced the decision, the alternatives that were considered, the choice that was made, and the expected trade-offs. There is no accepted rule for how many are enough, so start with the decisions that are hardest to reverse. The non-negotiable part is the "alternatives considered" section. The reasons a path was rejected are exactly the information that never appears in code and never survives a handover.

PR descriptions that explain why, not what

If your team's pull request descriptions are routinely a single line ("adds feature X"), the team is already accumulating theory debt at full speed. Mandate at least three to five sentences of "why" in every PR. The reviewer should not have to ask. This habit, alone, recovers an enormous amount of context six months later, when the original author has rotated off the team.

Pair and mob programming, for the right reason

The standard argument for pair programming is "faster development". That is the weakest possible reason. The real reason is real-time theory sharing: two people end up with the same structured understanding of a piece of the system at the same time. When one of them leaves, the theory survives. Pair programming often meets resistance because it looks inefficient by the wrong metric. By the right metric - long-term theory preservation - it is one of the cheapest insurance policies a team can buy.

The human role with AI tools is theory curation

When you delegate code generation to an AI assistant, your job shifts. You stop being a typist and start being a theory curator: reading the generated code, asking explicitly "what theory does this code assume? does that theory match our system? what alternatives were silently discarded?", and writing the answers down. Skip this step and AI tooling becomes a short-term productivity gain bought with massive long-term theory debt.

What Can Senior Developers Do About It in the AI Era?

One of the strongest arguments people made in 2023 was "AI will hollow out senior engineering jobs". Naur's framework suggests why that argument is at best half right: split the work that produces code from the work that produces theory, and the picture changes.

Let us be honest about what AI is genuinely good at: code generation, pattern matching, syntax conversion, boilerplate, known-algorithm implementation, standard library usage, format-to-format translation. In all of these, modern AI assistants outperform the average junior engineer on speed and often on correctness. But the things AI is structurally bad at look like a different list entirely: business context interpretation, trade-off judgment, anticipation of future change, evaluation of rejected alternatives, maintenance of system-wide theoretical coherence, and the design of theory transfer between people. Every item on this second list is, in Naur's vocabulary, theory.

Put differently: AI is eating the work that produces code, but it is not eating the work that produces theory. As the supply of code goes up, the relative scarcity of theory rises with it. That is why this article does not expect the value of senior engineers to fall easily: what is scarce is not experience as such, but people who can run theory.

What Should Junior, Mid, and Senior Developers Do Differently?

Juniors (1-3 years experience)

Use AI tools aggressively, but lock in two habits early. First, after every AI-generated block of code, rewrite it yourself from scratch. The point is not the code, which the AI already gave you; the point is to force the theory into your own head. Second, train yourself to articulate, out loud or in writing, why you chose this approach over alternatives. The fastest path from junior to senior in the AI era is not code throughput. It is the conscious formation of theory.

Mid-level (4-8 years)

Start writing theory down in public. Internal wiki, personal blog, ADRs, PR descriptions, conference talks - any channel will do, but at least one channel is mandatory. If you reach senior level without ever forming the habit of writing decision context, you will spend the rest of your career trapped inside your own head, with disproportionate influence loss. The mid-level years are when the writing muscle is built; if you skip them, it usually never comes back.

Seniors (9+ years)

Spend less time writing code and more time building theory transfer infrastructure. ADR processes. PR templates. Onboarding curricula. AI tooling guidelines. Pair programming rituals. Code review norms that enforce "why" questions. The difference between senior engineers in this dimension determines, far more than language choice or architecture style, the five-year fate of the systems they own.

How Should You Use AI Coding Tools in a Theory-Friendly Way?

The following seven patterns are this article's recommendations for working with Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Copilot, Windsurf, and similar tools. Their effect has not been measured; if you adopt only two, start with patterns 1 and 3, the two smallest habits for recording the why.

  1. State the "why" explicitly in every prompt. Not "write a JWT decoder" but "we are migrating from session auth to JWT and need backward compatibility for six months; write a JWT decoder that satisfies the following constraints". The prompt itself becomes a record of theory.
  2. Analyze AI-generated code, then rewrite it once by hand. Do not paste it in raw. The rewrite forces the theory into your head; without it, the code lives in the repo while the theory lives nowhere.
  3. Document the rationale within five minutes of the AI conversation ending. If you delay, the theory evaporates. Write the ADR or the PR description while the chat is still warm.
  4. Attach AI chat transcripts to the PR. The transcript is evidence of how the theory was formed. One option is an "AI conversation summary" section at the bottom of every PR description.
  5. Treat the AI as a pair programmer, not a code printer. Ask "how does this approach compare to alternative X?" and "what assumptions does this design make?". The dialogue itself is theory work.
  6. Write tests first as a way of stating theory. A test suite encodes the claim "this system must behave in these specific ways under these conditions". Write the tests, then let the AI implement to the tests. The tests are an explicit, machine-checkable expression of theory.
  7. Challenge the assumptions the AI silently embedded. For every block of AI-generated code, explicitly ask "what is this code assuming? what happens if those assumptions break?". The act of surfacing assumptions is the act of theorizing.

What Will Programming Look Like in 2030?

Naur's 1985 insight is more relevant in 2026 than it was in 1985, and it will be even more relevant in 2030. As code production becomes nearly free, the relative scarcity of theory rises to dominate everything else. The 2030 picture, as this article projected it at publication (May 2026) - a forecast, not a measurement: AI will write 90+ percent of code by volume; humans will increasingly work as theory curators, system architects, and theory transfer designers; the definition of "a good developer" will quietly shift from "writes a lot of correct code fast" to "forms clear system theories and transfers them reliably"; ADR-style theory preservation will become a baseline industry practice rather than a niche habit; and AI tools themselves will begin to ship features that interview the developer ("why did you make that decision?") and store the answer alongside the code.

What Is the Practical Takeaway?

The practical conclusion is simple. Stop optimizing only for code-writing speed; AI has already won that race. Start optimizing for theory formation and transfer. The single highest-leverage habit you can adopt today is also the smallest: write down, in plain prose, the "why" behind every non-trivial decision you make this week. That habit, compounded over a few years, is what separates the engineers whose value rises in the AI era from those whose value falls. Naur said it forty-one years ago. The industry has finally been forced to hear it.

# Programming as Theory Building - Theory Preservation Checklist

## 1. What Naur actually said - check before quoting

|---|---|

| "Rewriting is failure" | He suggested a new team solving the problem afresh may beat revival, "at no higher, and possibly lower, cost" |

- [ ] Explain how the solution relates to the affairs of the world it helps to handle
- [ ] Explain why each part of the program is what it is
- [ ] Respond constructively to any demand for a modification

## 2. Has the theory broken? - five symptoms

Three or more means the theory is likely in serious trouble (rule of thumb).

- [ ] Adding a feature breaks an unrelated module
- [ ] No one in code review can answer "why was it done this way?"
- [ ] The same bug class keeps reappearing in different modules
- [ ] Wiki, README, or ADRs have drifted from the code
- [ ] A competent new hire still cannot contribute meaningfully after six months

## 3. What to record, and where - decision table

| Situation | Record | Do not skip |
|---|---|---|
| A design decision that is hard to reverse | One ADR | Context, alternatives considered, choice and reason, expected trade-offs, **why the alternatives were rejected** |
| Every PR | 3-5 sentences in the PR description | The why, not the what |
| Only one person knows a core module | Pair or mob programming | Two people holding the same theory at the same time |
| Code came out of an AI conversation | ADR or PR description within five minutes | Why this approach, plus an AI conversation summary |
| An outsourced project ends | Theory handover | ADR bundle, decision-context documents, recorded interviews |
| Someone changes departments | 2-4 weeks of theory transfer | Guaranteed as a formal policy |
| Early-stage startup (around six people) | The ADR habit | Adopting it later is too late |

---

## 4. Working with AI coding tools - seven checks

- [ ] The prompt states the why and the constraints (e.g., six months of backward compatibility)
- [ ] The AI-generated code was analyzed and rewritten by hand once
- [ ] The decision rationale went into an ADR or PR description within five minutes
- [ ] An AI conversation summary is attached to the PR
- [ ] Alternatives were requested ("how does this compare to option X?")
- [ ] Expected behavior was written as tests before the AI implemented it
- [ ] The code's assumptions, and what happens if they break, were questioned

If you pick only two, start with 1 (the why in the prompt) and 3 (record within five minutes).
