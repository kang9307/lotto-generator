How to Choose a Power Bank - Capacity, PD/PPS, Airline Rules, and Safety

A complete 2026 buying guide: what the mAh label really means, which fast-charging standard matters, and how to fly with one legally and safely

2026-07-23 Life & Practical

Introduction: Why the mAh Number Alone Isn't Enough

It's hard to get through a day on a single phone charge anymore. Screens keep getting bigger and batteries keep draining faster, yet most people still pick a power bank by glancing at one number: mAh. The trouble is that the mAh printed on the box doesn't match what actually reaches your phone, and a "fast charging" label is meaningless if the standard - PD, PPS, or QC - doesn't match your device. If you're about to travel, airline carry-on rules add another layer, and safety issues like overcharging, swelling, and fire risk are not something to shrug off either.

This guide walks through what actually matters when buying a power bank, in order: understanding capacity, fast-charging standards, the portability trade-off, airline carry-on rules, safety, picks by use case, and maintenance. It reflects the latest standards and regulations as of July 2026.

1. Understanding Capacity - Rated mAh vs. Actual Charges

1.1 What the Printed mAh Actually Means

A number like 20,000mAh printed on a power bank is the rated capacity of its internal lithium-ion cells, measured at roughly 3.6-3.7V. The catch is that what you actually use is the 5V (or 9V, 12V with PD) power coming out of the USB port. In other words, the mAh on the label describes "how much charge is stored in the cell," not "how much charge your phone actually receives."

1.2 Voltage Conversion Loss - Why You Get Less Than Advertised

Stepping the 3.7V cell voltage up to a 5V USB output requires a boost circuit, and that conversion loses energy - typically around 20-30%. Add cable resistance and heat loss in the charging controller, and the effective capacity actually delivered over USB often lands at only about 55-65% of the labeled figure. For a 20,000mAh unit, that means a realistic usable capacity of roughly 11,000-13,000mAh. Comparing products in watt-hours (Wh) instead of mAh gives a more honest, voltage-independent comparison.

1.3 How to Estimate Real-World Charge Count

To figure out how many times a power bank can actually charge your phone, use the realistic usable capacity, not the label. The formula is (usable power bank capacity) ÷ (phone battery capacity). For a phone with a 4,500mAh battery charged from a 20,000mAh power bank with roughly 12,000mAh of usable capacity, that works out to about 2.6 full charges. Since the phone's own charging losses shave off a bit more, it's safer to assume you'll get somewhat less than half of the label's mAh value in actual charges.

2. Fast-Charging Standards - PD, PPS, QC, and Wattage

2.1 What Is USB PD

USB PD (Power Delivery) is the universal fast-charging standard built by USB-IF. PD 3.0 supports up to 100W (20V x 5A), and the newer PD 3.1 extends that ceiling to 240W (48V x 5A). It spans everything from phones to tablets to laptops under one standard, and it's the default protocol on nearly every recent device from Apple, Samsung, Google, and others.

2.2 What Is PPS - An Extension of PD

PPS (Programmable Power Supply) is an extension protocol added on top of PD 3.0 that adjusts voltage very precisely across a 3.3-21V range, typically in 20mV steps. Compared to standard PD's fixed voltage steps, PPS can match a device's exact charging state more precisely, which reduces heat and improves efficiency. This is the technology behind "super fast charging" on many Samsung Galaxy phones and other Android devices, typically operating in the 25-45W range.

2.3 What Is QC (Quick Charge) - The Legacy Standard

QC (Quick Charge) is Qualcomm's proprietary fast-charging standard. Several versions existed - QC 2.0, 3.0, 4+ - but most new devices have shifted their weight toward PD. You'll still find QC on older Android phones and some car chargers, but for a new purchase, checking PD/PPS support is the more sensible priority.

2.4 Checking Device Compatibility

Fast charging only works when both the power bank and your device support the same standard. Look for a power bank that lists both the protocol and wattage together - something like "PD 20W" - and compare it against the maximum input your phone or laptop actually accepts. If you plan to charge a laptop, aim for PD 60W or higher, ideally a unit that supports the higher wattage tiers of PD 3.1. Keep in mind the cable matters too; a cheap cable can bottleneck fast charging even with capable hardware on both ends.

3. The Portability Trade-Off - Capacity vs. Weight

3.1 Why More Capacity Means More Weight

Lithium-ion cells have a roughly fixed energy density, so as rated capacity (mAh) climbs, the physical volume and weight of the cells climb with it. As a rough sense of scale, 5,000mAh units often weigh around 100-130g, 10,000mAh units around 180-220g, and 20,000mAh units around 350-450g. Add protection circuitry, a case, and a display, and the weight goes up further.

3.2 Matching Weight to Real Usage

If you're carrying it in a bag or pocket every day, the weight adds up, so a 5,000-10,000mAh unit is usually the more comfortable choice. But for a trip or camping stretch where outlets are scarce for days at a time, total charge capacity matters more than weight, and 20,000mAh or above becomes worth considering. The sensible order is to first decide how many charges you actually need for your usage pattern, then pick the weight class that delivers it - not the other way around.

4. Airline Carry-On Rules - The 100Wh and 160Wh Thresholds

4.1 How to Calculate Watt-Hours

Airline rules are set by watt-hours (Wh), not mAh. You can convert with the formula Wh = (mAh x V) / 1000, where V is the battery's rated voltage printed on the product (usually 3.6-3.7V). A 20,000mAh, 3.7V power bank works out to roughly 74Wh. Many products sold today print the Wh figure directly on the unit to comply with international rules, so checking the label is the most reliable way to know.

4.2 Rules by Capacity Tier

Under IATA international guidelines and common domestic airline policy, the rules break down as follows. 100Wh or under is allowed in carry-on baggage (some airlines cap the number of units, so it's worth checking in advance). Over 100Wh up to 160Wh requires advance airline approval and is typically capped at two units per passenger. Over 160Wh is prohibited in both carry-on and checked baggage on passenger flights. And regardless of capacity, spare lithium batteries - including power banks - are never allowed in checked baggage; carry-on is mandatory.

4.3 In-Cabin Storage Rules

Starting March 2025, domestic Korean airlines tightened their rules to prohibit storing power banks in the overhead bin, requiring passengers to keep them on their person or in the seat-back pocket instead - even inside a plastic bag, overhead storage is no longer allowed. Some airlines also restrict or require crew supervision for charging devices from a power bank during flight, so it's worth checking your specific airline's current policy before you fly.

5. Safety - Certification and Fire Prevention

5.1 Checking Safety Certification

Power banks (lithium-ion secondary battery packs) sold in Korea are classified as a mandatory safety-verification item under electrical and consumer product safety law, meaning manufacturers and importers must pass safety testing and display a certification mark and number on the product. Before buying, check that the certification mark, certification number, model name, and manufacturer information are printed on the product, and you can look up the certification number on Korea's official product safety information center to confirm it's actually registered. Wherever you're buying, favor products from established brands that publish safety-testing documentation.

5.2 Overcharge Protection and Swelling

A unit with a genuine protection circuit board (PCB) automatically cuts current in the event of overcharging, over-discharging, overcurrent, or a short circuit, preventing accidents before they start. Formal safety testing typically includes an overcurrent charging test for exactly this reason. Cheap, uncertified products, by contrast, sometimes have a weak protection circuit or none at all, which raises the risk of swelling - a bulge caused by internal chemical reactions producing gas inside the cell. Left unaddressed, swelling can progress to rupture or fire, and it should always be treated as a warning sign.

5.3 What to Do If It Swells

Stop using and charging it the moment you notice swelling. Never press, puncture, or bend it, and move it away from heat and humidity into a cool, isolated spot. Don't throw it in regular household trash - take it to a designated battery collection point, or contact the retailer or manufacturer for safe disposal. If it feels warm or the swelling is severe, isolating it in a non-flammable container with sand or salt and contacting your local fire department or product safety authority is the recommended precaution.

5.4 Fire Prevention Habits

Don't leave a power bank in a hot environment for extended periods - a sun-exposed car dashboard or a sealed bag in summer heat are common culprits. Avoid dropping or impacting it, and use certified cables and adapters rather than unbranded ones. If a unit has been soaked in water, discard it rather than reusing it even after it dries out completely. Cheap, uncertified products - especially those from unclear parallel-import or overseas direct-purchase sources - are hard to verify for protection-circuit quality, so choose carefully.

6. Picks by Use Case

6.1 Everyday Carry

For daily use that tops up your phone once or twice, a 5,000-10,000mAh unit strikes the best balance of weight and capacity. PD 18-20W or higher will charge your phone quickly without adding much bulk.

6.2 Travel

For long trips, staying around 20,000mAh (about 74Wh) - comfortably under the 100Wh threshold - is the safer overall choice given airline rules. Keep in mind that very large units approaching 100Wh can draw extra scrutiny at airport security.

6.3 Camping and Outdoors

Where outlets are scarce, a 20,000-30,000mAh unit with multiple ports for charging several devices at once, water/dust resistance, and optional solar top-up is the more practical choice, despite the added weight and bulk - just weigh that against how much you'll actually be carrying it.

6.4 Charging a Laptop

To charge a laptop you'll need PD 60W or higher (45-65W for lightweight laptops, 65-100W for higher-performance ones), paired with generous capacity of 20,000mAh or more. Check whether your laptop's USB-C port actually supports PD charging and how many watts it needs, then match the power bank to that requirement.

7. Maintenance Tips - Lifespan and Storage

Lithium-ion batteries degrade faster when left fully drained or fully charged for extended periods. Keeping it roughly in the 20-80% range during normal use is better for the battery's chemistry. If you won't use it for three months or more, charge it to around 50% and store it somewhere cool and dry. Avoid hot, humid environments, direct sunlight, and leaving it inside a car. As a general rule, lithium-ion batteries show a noticeable capacity drop after 300-500 charge cycles, so if you use yours heavily, planning to replace it every 1-2 years is a reasonable approach.

Conclusion: Buy for Your Usage Pattern, Not the Marketing Copy

A power bank has more to check than a single mAh number suggests. Understand the gap between rated and usable capacity, confirm the fast-charging standard actually matches your device, and if you're traveling, check the airline carry-on thresholds (100Wh and 160Wh) ahead of time. Above all, safety certification and protection-circuit quality are the baseline you shouldn't compromise on. Work through these checks in order, and you'll end up choosing a power bank that fits how you actually use it - not just what the packaging claims.
