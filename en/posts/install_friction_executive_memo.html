Why Friction Is Invisible From the Inside - How One 2003 Internal Memo Became a Record

The complaints happened every day and never became documents. The conditions that produce a record, and how to manufacture those conditions on purpose

2026-09-08 Trends & Culture

An internal email sent at 10:05 in the morning on Wednesday, 15 January 2003 is still circulating on the internet more than twenty years later. The subject line reads Windows Usability Systematic degradation flame. The sender is Bill Gates, the single addressee is Jim Allchin, and six more names sit on the copy line. It opens with the sentence 「I am quite disappointed at how Windows Usability has been going backwards and the program management groups don't drive usability issues.」 and then moves immediately to 「Let me give you my experience from yesterday.」 Everything after that is a chronological account of one day spent trying to download a single piece of software.

The document is usually introduced as 「the email where the chief executive lost his temper.」 That framing picks out the least interesting thing in it. People lose their temper every day. Nobody knows how many other people got stuck on exactly the same path on exactly the same day that year, because nobody was counting. What makes the memo unusual is not the size of the frustration. It is the single fact that the frustration acquired the form of a document.

So the axis here is neither a person nor a company. There is one axis. Why is friction invisible to the people who build the thing, and how do you convert what is invisible into a record? Twenty years on, every product name in that story has changed, but the categories of place where a person gets stuck in the first five minutes of installing or signing up have barely moved. If what survives is a set of categories, then the countermeasures have to be categories too.

The 2003 document discussed below is published as an internal record made public through Comes v. Microsoft, the Iowa consumer class action of 2007. Direct quotation here is limited to four short passages, and the wording follows the archive that publishes the document together with contemporaneous press transcriptions. It has not been collated word by word against the original court filing, and that limitation is set out with the rest of them in a later section. A twenty-year-old document is also not used here as an assessment of any product today, and no current product is singled out anywhere in this piece.

Why that email is still around

Start by pinning down the identity of the document. The more widely a document is quoted, the more variant versions of it circulate, and variants are almost always polished into something stronger than the original. That memo routinely travels as a one-line summary along the lines of 「Gates could not install his own company's software.」 The range that the actual wording supports is narrower than that.

The send time is Wednesday, 15 January 2003, at 10:05 in the morning. There is one addressee and six people on copy. The word flame in the subject line was, at the time, a piece of internal shorthand announcing that a heated tone was about to follow. The body is written as an account of the previous day, which places the events themselves on 14 January. The publication route is the Iowa class action of 2007. That case ended in a settlement while the trial was under way, and the exhibit archive the plaintiffs had been publishing became restricted afterwards. The wording in circulation today comes through transcriptions made during that window and through press coverage in 2008.

The point of that table is that the right-hand column is not empty. As a document becomes famous, the right-hand column gets filled in from memory, and what memory supplies is usually wrong. That is the reason to keep quotation short and attribution attached.

Not the discovery of friction but the birth of a record

Two passages from the body get quoted over and over. One is the search scene: 「I typed in moviemaker. Nothing. I typed in movie maker. Nothing.」 The same word entered twice with the spacing changed, and no results either time. The other comes later, relaying the answer that came back from the people responsible: 「So they told me that using the download page to download something was not something they anticipated.」

That second sentence compresses the whole argument of this article into one line. Using the download page to download something was not on the list of anticipated uses. That is not a confession of error. It is a statement about a field of view. The people who built that screen were using it for a different purpose, and for that purpose nothing was wrong with it. The problem did not live in the screen. It lived in the list of anticipated uses.

And this is where the interesting split happens. That hole in the list of anticipated uses did not open that morning. Neither the property that a single space in a search term changes the result, nor the structure in which the page where you find a thing and the page where you download it are different pages, is something that can be created in a day. So friction was not discovered that day. Friction acquired the form of a record that day. Three conditions made the difference.

  • The person who hit it held decision rights. Others lost the same time on the same path that week, almost certainly. Their time was never written down anywhere, because there was nowhere for it to be written.
  • The path was traversed without being cut. Search, download, install, restart, all of it, without detouring to an internal tool or asking a colleague halfway through. That is what turned it into a single narrative rather than five disconnected annoyances.
  • The record entered a channel that does not discard. Internal mail is retained, and what is retained can be opened later. A verbal complaint at the end of the day would have evaporated by that evening.

Those three conditions coincided by accident. What an organisation should take from the story is not the accident but the method for producing the same three conditions routinely. Do that, and you no longer have to wait for the person with the most decision rights to happen to sit in a user's chair.

The builder machine is not the user machine

The first reason friction is invisible is not attitude. It is state. The machine belonging to the people who build a product is a machine that has already been prepared for that product to run well on it. Prepared means, precisely, that the conditions of a first run have been erased.

What makes this particularly unkind is that the first-run path is the path builders travel least. During development you relaunch something already installed and open a screen you are already signed in to. A screen may be opened a hundred times a day, but if all hundred are second-or-later visits, the first visit is never observed at all. And because confidence grows with repetition, the places nobody has observed are precisely the places people declare fine with the most conviction.

The last row of that table differs in kind from the seven above it. The first seven are restored by changing the machine. The last one is not restored by changing the machine, because you can provision a fresh device but you cannot reset the head of the person holding it. That last row is what the next section is about.

What knowing hides

The inability to reproduce a state of not-knowing is not a question of will. It is a repeatedly observed property, and the name for it came out of economics. The paper Colin Camerer, George Loewenstein and Martin Weber published in the Journal of Political Economy, volume 97, issue 5, in 1989 showed experimentally that better-informed agents fail to ignore their own private information even when ignoring it is in their own interest. The finding that matters most for practitioners comes from the market side of that work. Market pressure reduced the bias by roughly half but did not eliminate it. Incentives shrink the effect and leave a remainder.

The same property has a more intuitive demonstration in the experiment reported in Elizabeth Newton's 1990 Stanford doctoral thesis The rocky road from actions to intentions. One person taps the rhythm of a widely known song on a table and another listens and tries to name it. The widely repeated figures are that 2.5 percent of 120 tapped songs were identified, against a 50 percent success rate predicted in advance by the tappers themselves. The melody is playing inside the tapper's head, so the fact that what actually arrives on the other side is nothing but the beat cannot be reproduced from the inside.

In a product the same structure looks like this. In the builder's head the name of the feature, the position of the screen and the order of the steps are already playing. What arrives at the user is only the words actually printed on the screen. Which is why the instruction to think like a user is not a method. It is the equivalent of telling the tapper to forget the melody.

The alternative is not an attitude but the physical restoration of conditions. A fresh account, a device being switched on for the first time or at least a new browser profile, an empty cache, the default locale, and a person who did not build the thing. The first four can be prepared. The last one has to be borrowed.

How friction disappears inside an organisation

The second reason is not cognitive. It is organisational. Several stages usually sit between the person who hit the friction and the person able to fix it, and information falls out of the pipe at every stage.

  • Most people who hit it never say anything. When filing a report costs more time than simply giving up, people give up. The tickets that do arrive are therefore not a sample of friction. They are a sample of patience.
  • Tickets that do arrive get summarised. Summaries lose the reproduction steps. Once ten different failures land in a single bucket labelled how-to question, that bucket becomes a low-priority line item in the next planning meeting.
  • The path gets cut along the org chart. Search belongs to the search team, distribution to the distribution team, installation to the product team, payment to the payments team. Every team's metrics look healthy within its own segment. The user, however, does not travel in segments.
  • The authority to fix and the vantage point to observe sit in different places. The organisations that see friction best are usually support and operations, and the organisation that can fix it is usually product. That mismatch is the same structure as gains leaking at the boundaries rather than inside the work itself.

The third item is what explains the 2003 document all over again. What made that email exceptional was not its tone. It was its span. One person wrote search, download, installation and restart as a single story. No metric inside the organisation covers that span. However well each segment's numbers are watched, the total elapsed time and the total drop-off across the joined-up path appear on nobody's dashboard.

Complaints evaporate, records do not

How common friction actually is only becomes visible where somebody counted. The public aggregate that shows this best comes from e-commerce. The Baymard Institute pools 50 studies published between 2006 and 2025 and puts the documented average cart abandonment rate at 70.22 percent. Among people who abandoned for a reason other than simply browsing, the site wanting me to create an account accounts for 18 percent. In the same survey, extra costs account for 40 percent, slow delivery for 20 percent, distrust of the site with card details for 19 percent, and a checkout that was too long or complicated for 17 percent.

What matters there is not the size of the number 18 but its form. It is not a statement that somebody got annoyed. It is a counted value. If nobody had counted, that 18 percent would not exist, and a value that does not exist cannot win an argument in a planning meeting. There is no separate mechanism by which dissatisfaction converts into churn. Uncounted dissatisfaction is simply settled as churn, quietly.

Kind of frictionWhat remains when nobody countsWhat remains when somebody does
Abandonment on the signup screenAn impression that signups are lower than expectedCompletion against entries, and the field abandoned on
Failed installationA rumour that it does not work in some environmentsCounts per failure code, distribution by OS and edition
Cannot find the featureA sense that there are a lot of how-to questionsZero-result rate per query, and what people did next
Time to first valueA belief that people love it once they startThe distribution of time from signup to first success
RetriesAn awareness that errors happen occasionallyRepeat count on the same step and the drop-off that follows

Everything in the middle column is a sentence and everything in the right column is a value. Sentences are rebutted by other sentences. Values are rebutted only by other values. Half the work of reducing friction is therefore not design work at all but the work of moving the middle column into the right one, because only after that move does a piece of friction become eligible for a priority discussion. Counting has an order to it, though. What gets counted has to be decided before the events happen, since a number assembled afterwards by digging through logs shows only what happened to be retained at the time, and whatever was not retained is counted as zero forever.

Four instruments that turn friction into a record

Four instruments are enough, and none of them is a new tool. Each one is a rule for slicing something that already exists differently.

Step-level completion for the first run

Treating installation and signup as a single completion rate makes it impossible to say where people stopped. Count entry and completion separately for every step. The criterion for splitting steps is not screen transitions but the points at which the user has to decide something. If one screen carries three decisions, that is three steps.

step_enter  { flow: "signup", step: "email_verify", session, ts, platform, locale, is_first_run }
step_exit   { flow, step, outcome: "done|abandon|error", ms_in_step, retries, error_code }
first_value { flow, ms_since_signup, path_taken }

The drop-off point and the behaviour just before it

A drop-off rate on its own generates no candidate causes. It has to carry the last interaction before the exit, the error text that was on screen, the number of retries on that same step, and the dwell time in it. Retry count in particular is cheap to collect and carries a strong signal. A session that repeated the same step three times is a session in which the wording of that step failed to communicate what it was asking for.

A taxonomy for support contacts

Splitting contacts into defects and how-to questions sends all friction into the second bucket, and that bucket never gets opened again. Keep two independent axes at minimum: which screen and what the person was trying to do. With both axes present the top items sort themselves, and that ordering is the table of contents for your first-run documentation. The structure in which recurring questions never get promoted into documents and keep recurring instead is what happens when those axes are missing.

Scheduled walkthroughs on a fresh account and device

Fix a cadence and walk the whole path from the beginning under genuinely fresh conditions. The usual objection here is sample size, and the required headcount is smaller than people assume. The paper Jakob Nielsen and Thomas Landauer published on pages 206 to 213 of the INTERCHI 1993 proceedings modelled the discovery of usability problems as a Poisson process and expressed the proportion found with n participants as one minus the quantity one minus L raised to the power n. L is the probability that a single participant finds a given problem, and a typical value of about 31 percent is cited as an average across the projects studied. Substituting that gives roughly 85 percent at five participants. The 31 percent is a cross-project average rather than a constant valid for every product, so the practical implication is that regularity matters more than headcount.

What happens once the record exists

Friction does not disappear because a record exists. The 2003 document has a sequel attached to it. Tim Jarrett, who describes himself as having been in the marketing organisation responsible for that company's website at the time, wrote a recollection in 2008 saying that roughly eighteen months of work followed: improvements to search indexing and search algorithms, an editorial policy overhaul grounded in user behaviour data, and better internal analytics tooling. In the same piece he wrote that his team had no power to change or override decisions made by the product organisations.

That recollection is one individual's self-report published five years after the fact, not a company record. The structure it describes is nonetheless familiar. When the organisation that observes and the organisation that fixes are different organisations, records accumulate and changes turn into negotiations. Negotiations generally lose priority contests, because friction is nobody's target. Signup completion is a growth organisation's target. Installation success rate is nobody's.

So the final condition on any of these instruments is not collection. It is attribution. Every friction item needs the name of the party that will fix it, and that name should be a goal rather than a team. A record without attribution ends in one of three ways.

  • It becomes a report. Beautifully produced and circulated each quarter, with no item ever closed.
  • It becomes a tooling argument. Objections about the accuracy of the metrics recur, and work on accuracy substitutes for work on the friction.
  • It becomes a people problem. It gets translated into one team's diligence, and at the next cycle that team defends its own segment and nothing else.

The friction categories still standing twenty years later

All the names changed and the categories stayed. What follows singles out no product on the market today, and lists only the categories for which public research produces a signal.

On the scale of manipulative interface design, the survey Arunesh Mathur and colleagues presented at CSCW in 2019 crawled roughly 53,000 product pages across roughly 11,000 shopping sites, identified 1,818 instances, and organised them into 15 types across 7 categories. On consent interfaces, the study Midas Nouwens and colleagues presented at CHI in 2020 scraped the designs used by the 680 sites among the top 10,000 in the United Kingdom that ran the five most popular consent management platforms, and found that 11.8 percent met minimal legal requirements. The same paper ran a field experiment with 40 participants on how the eight most common designs shift the choice people actually make.

The bottom three rows have no public aggregate. That does not mean those categories are rare. It means nobody counts those places as a matter of standard practice — which is exactly the position everything was in back in 2003. Categories in which the issue crosses over from friction into rights, such as software installed without consent, belong on a different axis from this table entirely.

A procedure for finding friction in your own product

Three steps are enough, and one cycle fits into half a day.

Step one: restore the conditions

Create a new account. Signing out of an existing one is not sufficient. Use a new browser profile or a reset device, empty the cache, leave the locale at its default, and connect over something other than the corporate network. Put one low-specification device and one narrow screen on the list. That is as far as the machine-restorable part goes.

Step two: traverse without cutting

Define the goal as the moment of first value, which is neither installation complete nor signup complete, but the point at which the reason for using the product is realised for the first time. Between here and there, do not reach for an internal tool and do not ask a colleague. When you get stuck, stay stuck and write down the clock. The moment you route around it, that cycle's value is gone. This is the part that reproduces the second of the three conditions described earlier.

Step three: attribute the record

Fill four columns for every item: which screen, what was being attempted, how long it took, and whose goal it belongs to. When the last column is blank, that item will still be there at the next cycle. Then keep the table rather than deleting it, because the next cycle's baseline is that table. Without a baseline, better or worse is only ever an impression.

  • What the procedure catches is friction along a path. A missing feature, or a refusal on price, is not caught by it.
  • What it does not catch is long-tenure friction. Problems that only appear once data has accumulated are invisible during a first run.
  • When the same person repeats it, the numbers improve each cycle. That improvement belongs to that person's learning rather than to the product. Rotating the person each cycle works better.

Where this method fails

There are cases where all the instruments are in place and nothing comes out. The failures usually arrive from four directions.

  • Observation changes the conditions. Somebody running a walkthrough knows they are running a walkthrough, so they tolerate more than usual and read a screen to the end that they would normally have closed. Total elapsed time comes out shorter than reality, and abandonment is undercounted.
  • Metrics count only the people who stayed. Whoever left on the first screen often disappeared before the instrumentation ran at all. The first row of any completion table is therefore permanently more optimistic than the truth.
  • Removing friction is not always right. In front of actions that are hard to undo — payment, deletion, granting permissions, sharing outside the organisation — friction is a safety device. The single test separating friction to delete from friction to keep is reversibility.
  • Measurement manufactures new friction. More instrumentation means more consent notices and more choice screens, which lands straight back on the second row of the category table above. The safe rule is to keep only as many measured items as there are questions you are able to close.

To put it together: what the 2003 memo left behind is not an assessment of a company. It is a specimen of the conditions under which friction becomes a record. That specimen tells you two things. One is that friction is invisible for reasons of state and structure rather than indifference. The other is that state and structure are restorable. A fresh account, a fresh device, and a table that does not get deleted. That is roughly all it takes. The rest of the work is putting names on the rows of that table.

What could not be confirmed

The following items were either left out of the body or written in a deliberately narrow form because the primary wording could not be pinned down. The reasons are attached.

  • Collation against the original court filing - the plaintiffs' public exhibit archive from the Iowa case became restricted after the settlement. The wording in circulation today passes through transcriptions from that window and 2008 press coverage, and has not been matched word by word against a filing scan. The exhibit number is likewise unavailable.
  • A settled text of the full body - several variants circulate, so direct quotation was held to four passages and the remainder is given only as substance. Popular summaries sometimes carry emphasis that is not in the original.
  • The software version and distribution channel - the document carries only the name Moviemaker. Which edition was being sought, and from where, is not settled by the wording.
  • Replies and follow-up internal documents - neither a reply to the memo nor whatever internal instructions followed it appears in the public material.
  • The sample composition in Camerer and colleagues, 1989 - the abstract and secondary literature support the comparison between individual-level and market experiments and the finding that markets cut the bias by roughly half. Participant counts and composition require the full text.
  • The figures from Newton, 1990 - the values of 120 songs, 2.5 percent and 50 percent recur through secondary literature reporting the thesis rather than through the thesis itself. The original conditions and exact sample were not collated.
  • The basis for the 31 percent in Nielsen and Landauer - the summary literature supports the description of a typical value averaged across projects, and does not settle how many projects or of what kind.
  • The internal composition of the Baymard aggregate - the list of the 50 individual studies, and the mix of countries and sectors surveyed, is not on the public summary page. The figures are therefore used as averages only.
  • Verification of the sequel - the recollection in section eight is a self-report on a personal blog and is not corroborated by company documents. The period and scope of the work described are attested only within that piece.
  • Public aggregates for three categories - no standard measurement was located for split download paths, update timing or invisible prerequisites. The table says so in place of a figure.
