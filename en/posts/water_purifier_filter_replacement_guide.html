Water Purifier Filter Replacement Cycles and How to Do It Yourself

How to judge when a filter is actually due, and whether you should be the one changing it

Jul 27, 2026 Lifestyle
Regional note: This guide is written around the South Korean market, where household water purifiers are dominated by rental and subscription contracts with scheduled technician visits, and where tap water is generally soft with municipal quality data published by local waterworks. Filter types and replacement logic are universal, but contract rules, service pricing and water hardness will differ in your country.

Introduction: a purifier is not an appliance you buy once and forget

Once a water purifier is installed, it feels like the water question is settled. But a purifier behaves differently from a refrigerator or a washing machine. The cabinet stays the same while the consumable inside is steadily used up. Filters work by trapping what is in the water, so as time passes there is less room left to trap anything, and eventually the unit stops performing the way it did on day one. Replacement is not an optional extra. It is the minimum condition for a purifier to keep being a purifier.

The awkward part is that the decision is rarely obvious. The manual prints an interval in months, but you have no idea whether it fits your household. Booking a technician feels like paying for something simple. Doing it yourself sounds fine until you remember that the machine sits between a water line and a power outlet. And if the unit is on a rental contract, the first question is not how but whether you are even allowed to touch it. Online you will find plenty of filter price lists, but very little that helps you answer the two questions that actually matter: is this filter due right now, and should I be the person changing it.

This article is written for people who already own or rent a purifier. Choosing a model or comparing rental plans is covered in separate articles, so those topics appear here only where they are needed. Instead we work through what each filter stage does and how long it lasts, the variables that shift those intervals, the warning signs and why signs alone are a poor basis for decisions, the step-by-step DIY procedure with its safety rules, the real cost structure of DIY versus service visits versus rental, the risks of third-party cartridges, and the mistakes that keep repeating. No brand recommendations, only criteria you can apply to whatever unit you own.

1. Why filters need replacing - what each stage does

A household purifier normally routes water through several filters in sequence. Each stage handles a different size of contaminant, and the important structural point is that the earlier stages protect the later ones. That is why front-end filters clog fastest and carry the shortest intervals, and why neglecting them quietly destroys the expensive membrane sitting behind.

1.1 Sediment filter - the first stage and the fastest to clog

The sediment filter is the gateway. It captures visible-scale particles such as rust flakes from old pipework, sand and suspended solids. The job is simple, and precisely because of that it fills up fastest. In buildings with aged plumbing, or in areas where a supply interruption has ever produced cloudy water, faster consumption than the printed interval is normal rather than a defect. When this stage stops working, the load passes straight through to the carbon and the membrane.

1.2 Pre-carbon filter - it protects the membrane, not just the taste

Pre-carbon uses activated carbon to adsorb residual chlorine and off-odours. Removing the chlorine smell is the visible benefit, but the more consequential job is shielding the membrane downstream from chlorine. Reverse osmosis membranes are especially vulnerable, so a dead pre-carbon stage shortens membrane life sharply. The catch is that once carbon is saturated it simply stops adsorbing while water keeps flowing at the same rate. This is the stage whose end of life is hardest to notice.

1.3 UF and RO membranes - the core filtration stage

A UF hollow fiber membrane is a bundle of very fine straws perforated with microscopic pores that hold back bacteria-sized particles while letting minerals through. An RO membrane uses pressure to push water through a far tighter barrier, removing dissolved substances broadly, but it produces reject water and works more slowly. Either way this is the heart of the machine and the most expensive part. Managing the stages in front of it is, in practice, managing its lifespan.

1.4 Post-carbon filter - the finishing stage

Post-carbon polishes whatever taste and odour remain at the end of the chain. This is the stage users notice first when it tires: water starts tasting flat or faintly odd. As the next section explains, though, judging replacement time by taste alone means you are already late.

2. Why the printed interval may not fit your home

The months in a manual assume standard usage conditions. What actually consumes a filter is not elapsed time but the volume of water that has passed through it and the state of that water. That is why identical models wear out at different rates in different homes.

2.1 Usage - cumulative throughput, not the calendar

  • A single-person household drinking a litre or two a day and a family of four using the purifier for cooking, tea and ice differ in throughput by several times over.
  • Periods when people are home more, such as remote work stretches or school holidays, accelerate consumption for the same household.
  • Long absences do the opposite, but stagnant water sits inside the unit, so run plenty to waste before drinking when you return.
  • Some units count throughput and raise a replacement alert on that basis. A volume-based alert is more accurate than a calendar-based one.

2.2 Water quality and hardness - it varies by area and building

  • Korean tap water is generally on the soft side, but hardness varies with the source, and harder water consumes filters faster.
  • Buildings with aged pipes, rooftop storage tanks, or recent plumbing work push more particulate into the line, so the sediment stage clogs quickly.
  • If your supply ever ran cloudy after a shutoff and restart, treat that date as a reason to bring the front-end interval forward.
  • Local waterworks in Korea publish periodic quality test results, which is a reasonable starting point for judging your area.

2.3 Direct-flow and tank models need different attention

A tank model stores filtered water, so residence time exists and the tank and internal pathway become their own hygiene task. A direct-flow model filters on demand with no storage, which shifts the focus to the dispensing nozzle and the short internal path. What both share is a point worth repeating: replacing filters is not the same thing as keeping the whole machine clean. The next section returns to this.

3.2 Signs are lagging indicators

This deserves emphasis. Almost every sign above appears only after performance has already degraded. Saturated activated carbon has essentially no effect on flow rate, so there is a window in which water pours perfectly while the filtration you are paying for has quietly stopped. Nothing about the experience tells you. The inverse trap is just as expensive: reduced flow is not always the filter. A partly closed inlet valve or a drop in building pressure produces the same symptom, and people routinely replace a perfectly healthy cartridge because of it.

How to frame the decision — make the manual interval plus your written history the primary basis, and treat signs as a secondary input for pulling the date forward. Sticking a dated label on the back of the unit does more for your maintenance quality than any sensor.

4. What actually goes wrong if you postpone

This is the part marketing exaggerates most, so it is worth separating what can be stated confidently from what cannot.

4.1 Certain: filtration performance declines

  • A clogged sediment filter reduces flow and shifts load downstream, which means you are shortening the life of the expensive membrane yourself.
  • Saturated carbon stops adsorbing chlorine and odours, and its membrane-protection role disappears at the same time.
  • Sustained pressure on a clogged medium encourages channelling, where water finds the path of least resistance instead of using the whole bed evenly.
  • On RO systems a tired membrane produces less permeate for the same input, raising the reject ratio and wasting water you pay for.

4.2 Handle with care: bacteria are not purely a filter problem

The claim that skipping a filter change breeds bacteria is usually stated too simply. In a survey by the Korea Consumer Agency, no clear correlation was found between filter replacement intervals and general bacteria detection; the finding pointed instead to the cleanliness of the dispensing nozzle and the internal pathway that water touches after filtration. In other words, the belief that timely filter changes complete your hygiene routine does not match the evidence.

For reference, Korea's drinking water standard limits general bacteria to 100 CFU per millilitre, and that limit applies to water passing through a purifier as well as to tap water. So filter replacement is a necessary measure for maintaining filtration performance, while microbial control is a separate axis covering the nozzle, the internal path and any storage tank. They run in parallel; neither substitutes for the other.

On health effects it is more accurate not to overstate. Korean media have reported cases where water from a poorly maintained purifier tested worse than tap water, but generalising individual health risk from that is not sound. If taste, colour or smell is persistently abnormal, ask the manufacturer to inspect the unit or use a municipal water testing service rather than diagnosing it yourself.

5. Should you be the one doing it?

Before the procedure comes a prior question: are you allowed to open this machine. Skipping that check can cost far more than the service fee you were trying to avoid.

⚠️ Rental and subscription users: read the contract first
In Korea, rented or subscribed purifiers usually remain the property of the provider, and the monthly fee already includes filters and scheduled visits. Opening the unit yourself or fitting a non-genuine cartridge can be read as breach of contract or user fault, which may void free repair and expose you to restoration charges when the unit is returned. Paying for a service you then decline to use is also a straightforward loss. If you want to do it yourself, the correct order is to check the service scope and any self-maintenance clause in the contract, then ask the provider.

5.1 When DIY is reasonably open to you

  • A self-managed unit bought outright - many are designed for owner replacement in the first place.
  • A rental whose mandatory term has ended and whose ownership has transferred to you.
  • Countertop or desktop designs where only a cartridge changes and no plumbing is opened.
  • Models for which the maker publishes a user replacement procedure in the manual or on an official support page.

5.2 When to call a technician instead

  • Under-sink and RO systems - storage tank, reject line and supply plumbing all interact.
  • Designs where the filter is buried inside the cabinet behind an outer panel.
  • Units with ice or sparkling functions where refrigerant or gas lines run nearby.
  • Any unit already showing leak marks or mould - a new filter will not fix that.
  • Anything where the contract forbids self-service or the warranty is still running.

6. The DIY procedure, step by step

⚠️ Before you start - electric shock and leak risk
A purifier handles water and electricity at the same time. Before touching anything, ① unplug the power cord from the outlet (switching the unit off is not enough) and ② fully close the raw water inlet valve under the sink. If the valve is left open, line pressure not only makes the cartridge hard to turn, it can send water spraying over nearby outlets and wiring the moment the filter comes free. Never handle the power section with wet hands, and if water splashed during the work, let everything dry completely before reconnecting. On hot and cold models, wait until the hot side has cooled.

6.1 What to have ready

  • Genuine filters matching your model - stages differ, so check part numbers and order
  • Towels and a wide basin, plus dry tissue for leak checking
  • A flashlight - the space under a sink is dark and the valve is hard to find
  • The manual or the maker's official replacement page
  • A label and marker for writing the date

6.2 The eight steps

  1. Cut the power - unplug it. On hot and cold models, wait for the hot side to cool.
  2. Close the inlet valve - shut the supply valve under the sink. Photograph its position so you can restore it.
  3. Release residual pressure - press the dispenser to drain water and pressure from the line. Skip this and you will get sprayed.
  4. Remove the old filter - open the cover and turn the cartridge in the specified direction, usually counter-clockwise on twist types, with a basin underneath for the water still inside.
  5. Fit the new filter - keep the stage order exactly right. Confirm visually that the O-ring is seated, not twisted or missing, then turn to lock in the specified direction. Do not overtighten with tools.
  6. Restore supply and check for leaks - open the valve slowly, then wipe the housing joints and valve area with dry tissue and see whether it comes away damp. If it does, close the valve and reseat.
  7. Flush - reconnect power and run the volume the manual specifies to waste. Makers commonly indicate several litres; this washes out fine carbon dust and residue from the new cartridge.
  8. Reset and record - clear the replacement alert if the model has one, and write the date and which stages you changed on a label stuck to the unit.

A cartridge-type job takes roughly 20 to 30 minutes. If it is your first time, allow extra for the flushing, and check where outlets or power strips sit under the sink so you can move them out of the water's way beforehand.

8. Are third-party filters acceptable?

Cheaper compatible cartridges are easy to find. They are not automatically bad, but three distinct risks need checking.

  • Contract and warranty - on a rented unit or one still under warranty, non-genuine parts can be classified as user fault and free repair may be refused. That exposure dwarfs the price difference.
  • Dimensional mismatch - similar looks are not the same as identical thread pitch, O-ring thickness and seating depth, and small differences cause leaks or housing damage. Under-sink leaks are found late, which makes them costly.
  • Unverifiable performance - filtration cannot be inspected by eye. If a product cannot show drinking-water certification or test data, you have no way to know what it removes.

On balance, they are hard to recommend outside a self-managed unit you own outright. If you do use one, confirm the exact model fit and any certification, and check the housing for leaks more frequently than usual for the first few days.

9. Mistakes people keep making

  1. Skipping the flush - the usual cause of black dust or a strange taste right after a change. Many people assume the new filter is defective and return it.
  2. Wrong stage order - swapping sediment and carbon positions collapses the protective sequence. Check part numbers and flow arrows.
  3. Missing or pinched O-ring - the old ring often stays stuck in the housing, and fitting a new filter on top of it is the classic cause of leaks.
  4. Leaving the inlet valve open - water can spray over the outlet under the sink. Pressure release is part of the same step.
  5. Overtightening - using tools where hand tight is enough cracks threads and plastic housings, which cannot be undone.
  6. Not recording the date - without a record you are back to guessing, and interval-based judgement becomes impossible.
  7. Changing filters but ignoring the nozzle and drip tray - microbial control is about the surfaces water touches, not the cartridge.
  8. Drinking straight after a long absence - run the stagnant water out first when you return from a trip.
  9. Using badly stored stock - filters left in sunlight or heat for long periods cannot be relied on for performance or hygiene.

10. Turning it into a routine - the 3, 6 and 12 month checks

Maintenance is a routine problem rather than a memory problem. The following works for most household units.

  • Weekly - wipe the dispensing nozzle and drip tray with a dry cloth and look for scale.
  • Monthly - shine a flashlight on the housing joints and check for moisture or white residue.
  • Every 3-6 months - inspect and replace the sediment and other front-end filters, update the date label.
  • Every 6-12 months - replace carbon stages; on tank models consider a professional tank and pathway sanitising service.
  • Beyond 12 months - check whether the UF or RO membrane has reached its replacement point.
  • After a move, supply shutoff or plumbing work - inspect the front-end filter regardless of the interval.

Add a repeating calendar reminder on your phone and the job is essentially handled. If your unit has an alert, just confirm whether it counts days or litres. A day-based alert will fire too late in a heavy-use household.

11. Frequently asked questions

Q1. I changed the filter and the taste has not improved. Why?

Often only the post-carbon was replaced while the front-end stages were left, or the problem is hygiene at the nozzle, internal path or tank rather than the filter. Clean the tray and nozzle, and if the issue persists, book an inspection.

Q2. The alert is on but water still flows well. Can I wait?

Flow rate has almost nothing to do with carbon saturation, so good flow is not evidence that the filter is alive. That said, if the alert is purely date-based and your usage has been very light, a few days of slack is not a serious problem.

Q3. Can I buy several filters in advance?

Yes, but store them sealed, cool and out of direct sunlight, and respect any expiry or recommended storage period printed on the pack. Stockpiling too much invites the mistake of fitting an old cartridge believing it is new.

Q4. What if a leak starts during a DIY change?

Close the inlet valve and unplug the unit first, then check the seating and the O-ring. If it still leaks after reassembly, stop repeating the attempt and book a service call. Under-sink leaks can spread to the floor below, so this is not something to sit on.

Q5. What should I do if the purifier will be unused for a long time?

Where possible close the inlet valve and switch off the power, and when you return, run the stagnant water out thoroughly before drinking. After a month or more, clean the nozzle and flush generously regardless of remaining filter life.

Conclusion: the interval is a baseline, the judgement is yours

Filter maintenance comes down to two sentences. First, use the manual interval as a baseline and pull it forward to match your household's usage and water quality. Changes in taste and flow are reference signals, not a sound basis for the decision, because they arrive late. Second, whether you do it yourself is settled by your contract and the unit's design before it is settled by your skill. On a rental, read the contract first; on under-sink or RO systems, a technician usually works out cheaper in the end.

The job itself is not difficult on a cartridge-type unit. What protects both safety and result is never skipping five things: cutting power, closing the inlet valve, releasing pressure, checking the O-ring, and flushing. And a filter change does not complete your hygiene routine. Given evidence that microbial issues track the surfaces and pathways water touches more closely than the cartridge itself, put nozzle and tray cleaning into the same routine. A well-maintained purifier is not an expensive one. It is the one with a date written on it, managed by records rather than by symptoms.
