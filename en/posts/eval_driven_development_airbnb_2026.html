Introduction: the place where every test is green and the product is still wrong

Unit tests all passed. Integration tests were clean. The deploy went out without incident. And on the user's screen sits a refund policy that does not exist. Any team that has shipped an LLM feature knows this scene. The code did exactly what it was told, and the defect lives inside a sentence the model produced.

Conventional testing has nothing to say here, for two reasons. First, the same input does not return the same output. Second, what counts as correct varies from one reviewer to the next. An assertion cannot absorb either condition, let alone both at once.

Airbnb Engineering gave their answer to this a name: eval-driven development (EDD). The name borrows from test-driven development, but the working order is different. In their own framing, EDD does not try to predict every failure mode upfront. It builds the infrastructure and habits to discover, encode, and continuously test for failure modes as they appear.

This article runs in two layers. The first eight sections are what Airbnb actually described: how the eval set gets built, who labels it, how the judge is calibrated, and where regressions get caught. The last three sections answer a different question, what your team should do first if you start today. That part carries judgment the article does not make, and the boundary is marked every time it appears.

Where the numbers come from. 100, 50 to 100, 60, 78%, 88%, 5,000, 5%, 3 to 5, and 20 to 30 are all values Airbnb wrote down. The suggestion to start with a 20-row eval set is not in the article; it is my own recommendation, and section 9 gives the reasoning separately. Blur those two layers and you end up treating another organization's numbers as your own targets.

1. How Airbnb defines eval-driven development

It is tempting to file this under TDD for LLMs and move on. That summary drops the property that matters most.

1.1 It does not try to predict failure upfront

TDD assumes you can write the desired behavior down first. What a function should return is settled before the function exists. That assumption does not survive contact with a generative model. Nobody can enumerate the ways a model will be wrong before they have looked at its outputs.

So the center of gravity in EDD is not completing the criteria in advance. It is the procedure that converts a failure, once observed, into a standing check. Airbnb notes a side effect of this approach: it forces stakeholders to externalize what good actually means, and that act reshapes the product roadmap.

1.2 Five anchoring principles

The article lists five. Read in order, they map cleanly onto the order in which an organization has to do the work.

  • Define goals and gates upfront. Set before launch, they are criteria; set afterward, they are a negotiation
  • Let real errors guide your metrics. Metrics are co-developed with cross-functional partners, not written by an engineer alone
  • Keep your evaluator set small and sharp. In their words, 3 to 5 well-calibrated LLM-as-judge evaluators beat 20 to 30 noisy ones
  • Appoint a human decision-maker for disagreements. What counts as correct is a team discussion, but one named person makes the final call
  • Collaborate continuously with product partners. This is not an artifact you build once and file away

The third principle is the one most often violated in practice. Twenty metrics on a dashboard produce a feeling of control, but if half of them come from uncalibrated judges, that dashboard is a noise generator wearing the costume of a measurement tool. The fourth deserves more weight than it usually gets, too. The gap between an organization that schedules another meeting when labels disagree and one that decides on the spot compounds into a multiple, not a margin.

1.3 What it shares with TDD and where it splits

AxisTest-driven developmentEval-driven development
When criteria are writtenBefore the codeAfter reading a batch of real outputs
Form of judgmentPass or failScores and labels against a rubric
Who judgesAn assertion in codeThree layers: code, LLM judge, human
ReproducibilitySame input, same resultSame input, results that wobble
Lifespan of the criteriaUntil the spec changesRefreshed every time failure modes shift
What is under testThe codeCode plus prompt plus model plus serving config
How failure announces itselfA red build, immediatelyA score that quietly sags

That last row is the contrast this whole article turns on. Tests make noise when they break. Evaluations degrade in silence if nobody is looking. Which is why an eval program needs a standing human reader attached to it, not just automation.

2. Building and maintaining the eval set

This is the part most evaluation discussions skip. Naming a metric is easy. Deciding what you measure it against is always a data problem.

2.1 Step one is running 100 examples and reading them

Airbnb's process starts with manual review, not tool selection. Build the prototype and run it through 100 examples. The article explicitly says synthetic data is fine at this stage. What matters is not the provenance of the data but that a human sees the shapes the failures cluster into.

In the worked example in the article, reading 100 responses split the failures like this.

  • 15 generated policy details that were not in the source documents (faithfulness)
  • 8 were correct but too verbose (conciseness)
  • 5 refused questions they could have answered (over-refusal)
  • 3 came back with broken JSON (format)

Those four lines are the first draft of the eval suite. The data points at the metrics; the metrics are not chosen first and then fitted to data. That ordering is what the second principle looks like when it is actually followed.

2.2 A golden set of 50 to 100, and bad examples are mandatory

Once manual review has named the dimensions, the golden set comes next. Airbnb puts it at 50 to 100 examples. Because this set becomes the yardstick for calibrating the judge, its composition has to be deliberate.

  • Include bad examples. In the article's phrasing, you cannot test discernment without them
  • A set of only good outputs lets a judge pass everything and still post a high agreement rate. That is not performance, it is an artifact
  • Coverage should follow the observed failure types. A 15-case faithfulness problem and a 3-case format problem do not deserve equal weight
  • Borderline cases carry the most information. Obvious successes and obvious failures are graded correctly by any judge

2.3 Labeling belongs to PMs and subject-matter experts

Here the discussion turns organizational. Airbnb has subject-matter experts do the initial labeling, and in the worked example a PM or subject-matter expert labels 60 examples. They also pin down when to hand this to a scaled annotation workforce: only once the rubric is rock-solid and volume has become the bottleneck.

And a condition sits in front of all of it. This is one of the sharpest lines in the article.

If your experts disagree on a label, stop. Solve human disagreement before automating anything.

Why does this matter so much? If two people look at the same output and split between pass and fail, and you attach an LLM judge on top of that, the judge picks a side arbitrarily and posts 50 percent accuracy. Then the team misreads that number as a judge quality problem and starts rewriting the judge prompt. The thing that needed rewriting was never the prompt. It was the sentence in the rubric.

2.4 Refresh is event-driven, not calendar-driven

The article gives two clues about cadence. One is to recalibrate periodically as failure modes evolve. The other is the flow where new failure modes surfaced by production monitoring pass through a weekly PM review and become new evals. In other words the trigger is an event, not a date on a calendar.

  • When the model changes — the shape of failure changes with it, so revisit the difficulty distribution of the golden set first
  • When a new user segment arrives — the existing golden set may contain zero inputs that look like theirs
  • When production surfaces a new failure type — add that case to the golden set and recalibrate the judge
  • When agreement drifts downward on its own — that is the signal that your data has moved

3. The three layers of defense

Airbnb's evaluation stack has three layers. They are not alternatives to one another. They are tools with different costs and different reliability, and the article's recommendation is to use all three.

3.1 Layer one — checks that finish in code

Deterministic checks. Does the JSON validate against the schema, is the length inside its bounds, does the format meet spec, did a forbidden term appear, does a regex fire, and for classification problems, what do the classical ML metrics say. Failures caught here are the cheapest failures you will ever catch.

The three broken-JSON cases in the worked example land precisely in this layer. Asking an LLM judge about them would be spending money and latency to learn something a regular expression already knew.

3.2 Layer two — the virtual judge

A stronger LLM grades outputs against a rubric. This is where the qualities that cannot be written as code go: tone, faithfulness, and similar nuance. The article stresses that eliminating ambiguity in the rubric is the whole game, and states flatly that a rubric phrased like is the provided explanation readable and up to our standards will not work. Its counter-example instead enumerates tone, internal terminology, formatting, grammar, and complexity issues, and resolves to a 1 or a 0.

3.3 Layer three — humans

The ground truth. Safety-critical domains, and any case where the layers below disagree, end up in front of a person. It is the slowest and most expensive layer, but the yardstick that measures the accuracy of the other two originates here.

LayerWhat it catchesCost per caseWhen you can trust it
Programmatic checksFormat, length, schema, forbidden termsEffectively zeroWhen the rules match the real requirement
Virtual judgeFaithfulness, tone, conciseness, refusalsInference billingWhen golden-set agreement hits target
Human evaluationSafety, subtle context, final callsSalary and calendar timeWhen your raters agree with each other

The ordering is the cost design. Re-measuring at an upper layer what a lower layer already filtered spends money without adding information.

4. Calibration before you trust the judge

This is the step most teams skip when they adopt an LLM judge. It is also where the article's most uncompromising sentence lives.

A virtual judge that hasn't been calibrated is worse than no judge at all, because it gives you false confidence.

4.1 The loop that moved 78 percent to 88 percent

Calibration is a five-step loop. Build a golden dataset of 50 to 100 examples, run the judge against it, measure agreement with the human labels, analyze the disagreements and refine the rubric and few-shot examples, then re-run until you hit the target. In the worked example, the faithfulness judge started out agreeing with the PM 78 percent of the time. After the disagreements were dissected and the rubric was tightened, agreement jumped to 88 percent.

The number 78 is not the interesting part. What matters is what happens if you put a 78 percent judge straight onto a dashboard. More than one case in five is wrong while scores accumulate daily, and every prompt decision you make afterward is a decision made inside that noise.

4.2 What you measure agreement with

The target the article gives is the high 80s to 90s percent, measured with Cohen kappa or Krippendorff alpha. The reason for using agreement coefficients instead of raw accuracy is straightforward.

  • On skewed label distributions, agreement that happened by chance gets counted as capability
  • If 90 percent of your data is a pass, a judge that always says pass posts 90 percent accuracy
  • Kappa subtracts that chance agreement and reports only what is left
  • When you have three or more raters, or ordinal labels, alpha is the more comfortable instrument

And this calibration has to finish before you scale up. The article's own ordering puts calibration ahead of scale for a reason. Discovering that your judge was wrong after running it across 5,000 examples means throwing away all 5,000 results.

5. Where an LLM judge gets it wrong

This section unpacks the article's uncalibrated-judge warning into operational terms. The five-item breakdown below is my own organization, not a list Airbnb published; only the underlying principles come from the article.

  • When the rubric is vague. A phrase like is this readable invites the judge to invent a fresh standard on every call. The article names this failure directly
  • When it splits where humans never split. If the golden set labels themselves wobble, your agreement rate is measuring the quality of the rubric document, not the judge
  • When it rewards length. This is why conciseness earns its own dimension; verbosity showed up as a distinct failure type in the worked example too
  • When judge and generator are the same model. Grading your own prose converges on your own taste, which is exactly why the article puts a stronger LLM in the judge seat
  • When it leans toward passing. Without bad examples in the golden set, this bias never becomes visible

The practical test for all of this is simple. Plant deliberately broken outputs in the golden set and see whether the judge filters them out. If it does not, that judge is not yet a metric.

6. Where regressions actually get caught

One thing needs to be stated precisely first. The Airbnb article does not describe CI pipeline integration in any detail. What it describes explicitly is production monitoring.

  • Sample 5 percent of live de-identified traffic, daily
  • Run programmatic checks and virtual judges over that sample
  • Escalate flagged outputs to human review
  • A weekly PM review turns newly observed failure modes into new evals
  • Pre-production metrics are not a one-time validation; they must be measured again in production the same way

That last line is the hinge between offline and online. The metrics you used before launch keep getting measured after launch under the same definition. Put a metric with the same name and a different definition on each side and the comparison stops meaning anything. The article does not, however, publish correlation figures between offline scores and online outcomes.

CI is absent from the source, so everything from here is my recommendation. Runtime and cost differ enough that a two-stage split is the sane default.

  • Pull request stage — all programmatic checks plus a judge run at golden-set scale. It has to finish in minutes
  • Nightly batch — regression runs on a large sample, alerting when a score drops below threshold
  • Keep prompts and rubrics under version control in the same repository as the code. Regression detection is meaningless if you cannot say which commit bent the curve
  • Pin the judge model version too. If the judge updates itself quietly, you cannot tell a product change from a referee change

7. Deciding when to swap a prompt or a model

The article's guidance here is short and unambiguous. Fix one variable at a time.

  1. Fix the model and vary the prompt
  2. Fix the prompt and vary the model
  3. Fix both and vary the serving configuration

At each stage, virtual judge results narrow the candidate pool. The ordering matters because of attribution. Change the prompt and the model together, watch the score rise three points, and nobody knows where those three points came from. The next time you need to roll the model back, the prompt goes with it.

What you changeWhat stays fixedWhat you watchWhat not to do at this stage
PromptModel and serving configPer-dimension judge scoresEditing the rubric in the same pass
ModelPrompt and serving configPer-dimension scores plus format pass rateTuning the prompt to suit the new model
Serving configPrompt and modelLatency plus whether scores holdChanging the sample size at the same time
RubricAll three of the aboveAgreement with human labelsJudging success by product metrics

That last row gets missed constantly. Editing a rubric is not a product improvement; it is a change to the ruler. Once the ruler changes, both the old values and the new ones have to be re-measured with it. The moment you report a score increase that followed a rubric edit, the report has stopped carrying information.

8. Agents cannot be judged on the final answer alone

In a multi-step system the final output can be right while the path was wrong, and the path can be right while the final step goes astray. Airbnb splits agent evaluation into three layers.

  • Step level — is each individual tool call correct, both the call itself and its arguments
  • Trajectory level — is the overall path reasonable, and is the sequencing of subagent invocations sound
  • Session level — did the user's goal actually get accomplished

What makes this possible is traces and spans. Agent type, inputs and outputs, and the tools invoked get pushed to an observability platform, and that record is walked back with depth-first search or another tree traversal to reconstruct the agent's decision path. Subagent invocation order and parameter correctness are then verified on top of that reconstructed tree.

There is a practical fork buried in here. Build an agent without instrumentation and you cannot bolt evaluation on afterward. If only the final answer survives, trajectory-level evaluation was never possible in the first place. So when you build an agent, trace collection is not an observability nicety. It is a precondition for being able to evaluate at all.

9. Starting today - a 20-row eval set

From here on this is my recommendation, not a summary of the source. Airbnb's number is a 50 to 100 example golden set, and the 20 below is smaller. The reasons for going smaller come first.

9.1 Why twenty

  • One person finishes it in an hour. 50 to 100 requires scheduling a labeling session, and scheduling a session pushes it into next week
  • The goal for week one is that something actually runs. Twenty rows cannot calibrate a judge, but they are more than enough to reach agreement on what the failure types are
  • Twenty is a starting line, not a destination. Every time production surfaces a new type, that case goes in and the set grows
  • The moment you intend to trust a judge and put it on a dashboard, raise it to the 50 to 100 the article specifies. An agreement rate computed on twenty rows carries a confidence interval too wide to act on

9.2 What fills the twenty

The composition matters more than the count. Twenty good examples measure nothing at all.

BucketCountWhat goes inWhat this bucket exposes
Obvious success5Outputs nobody argues aboutWhether the judge is too strict
Obvious failure5Factual errors, broken formatA judge biased toward passing
Borderline5Cases a human pauses onAmbiguity in the rubric wording
Over-refusal3Answerable questions that got refusedSafety rules tuned too aggressively
Real traffic2Verbatim samples from live logsThe gap between synthetic and real

Three pieces of machinery attach to that: two programmatic checks (format validity, length bounds), one judge covering the single dimension that hurts most in your product, and one named human decision-maker. Not building several judges from the start is the whole point. That is the third principle, applied literally.

9.3 What not to do in week one

  • Choosing an eval platform first. A 20-row table is a spreadsheet. Tool selection comes after the dimensions stabilize
  • Defining more than ten metrics. The line about 3 to 5 calibrated evaluators beating 20 to 30 noisy ones applies with particular force in week one
  • Putting an uncalibrated judge on a dashboard. An uncalibrated judge is worse than no judge at all
  • Moving forward while labels disagree. Automate on top of two people's unresolved disagreement and the disagreement gets engraved into the metric
  • Stopping at synthetic data. Fine for exploration, but with zero real samples you will not see the failure types that exist in production
  • Treating an absolute score as the goal. Week one's value is not in the score; it is in the list of failure types

10. What to measure and what to leave alone

A failure the article names directly is the starting point for this section. A generic helpfulness metric scores well, you ship, and it turns out the metric never captured the failure mode people actually hit.

Which means the test for a metric is not whether it can be measured. It is what you will do when it goes down. If there is no answer, the metric is occupying dashboard real estate and nothing else.

Measure thisWhyNot thisWhy not
Faithfulness to source documentsA drop sends you straight to retrieval or the promptA generic helpfulness scoreA drop tells you nothing about what to fix
Format validityDownstream parsing breaks immediatelyThe model's self-reported confidenceWeakly correlated with being right
Over-refusal ratePoints directly at over-tuned safety rulesToken count on its ownLength is not a proxy for quality
ConcisenessVerbosity exists as a distinct failure typeA composite of several dimensionsIt erases which dimension degraded
Tool call accuracyCatches step-level agent failuresAny output of an uncalibrated judgeIt supplies false confidence

Among the right-hand entries, the composite score is the seductive one. It reports cleanly and it graphs beautifully. The problem is that when a composite drops, finding the cause means splitting it back into dimensions. You have bundled up precisely the thing you will need to unbundle.

The article also concludes that the set of things worth measuring differs per product. Import someone else's metric suite and you will be measuring someone else's failure modes.

11. What this costs as it scales

What Airbnb says about cost is about allocation rather than dollars. Expect to spend a meaningful share of total project effort on evaluation, they write, adding that this is not unnecessary overhead but how you build products that actually work.

The breakdown in the table below is my own organization; the article contains no dollar figures or rate cards.

Cost lineWhere it growsHow to hold it down
Judge inference billingSample size × dimensions × run frequencyNever send layer-one catches to layer two
Human labeling hoursGolden set expansion and recalibrationAdd annotators only after the rubric is solid
Recalibration effortEvery model or prompt changeChange one variable at a time to narrow scope
Trace storageExplodes as agent step counts riseFix retention and sampling rates upfront
Weekly review timeScales with dimensions and flagged volumeHold the dimension count at 3 to 5

The third row is the one that stings in practice. Change the model and, even with the product code untouched, you may have to recalibrate the judge. Plenty of teams leave that line out when they estimate the cost of a model swap. And the fifth row shows why the third principle is a budget clause as much as a quality clause. Twenty dimensions turn the weekly review into a full-day meeting, and a full-day meeting disappears within two months.

13. Frequently asked questions

How is eval-driven development different from test-driven development?

The judgment criteria live outside the code. Test-driven development writes the desired behavior as an assertion first, then bends the code to match. Pass and fail are binary, and the same input always yields the same output. Generative models break both assumptions. The output shifts every run, and what counts as correct varies from person to person. So Airbnb chose to build the infrastructure and habits to discover, encode, and continuously test for failure modes as they appear, rather than predicting every failure upfront. The practical difference is ordering. A test can be written before the code exists, but an eval rubric only gets written properly after a human has read roughly 100 real outputs.

How large should an eval set be, and who labels it?

Airbnb puts the golden dataset at 50 to 100 examples. In the worked example, a PM or subject-matter expert labels 60 of them. The article also pins down when to hand labeling off to a scaled annotation workforce: only once the rubric is rock-solid and volume has become the bottleneck. One condition matters more than size. Bad examples must be included, because you cannot test discernment without them. And there is a prerequisite on top of that: if your experts disagree on a label, stop. Automating on top of unresolved human disagreement bakes that disagreement straight into your metrics.

How do you validate an LLM judge?

Run the judge against a human-labeled golden set, measure agreement, dig into the disagreements, refine the rubric and few-shot examples, and repeat until you hit the target. The target sits in the high 80s to 90s percent, measured with agreement coefficients such as Cohen kappa or Krippendorff alpha. Raw accuracy alone is misleading, because on skewed label distributions it credits lucky guesses as skill. In the worked example a faithfulness judge started at 78 percent agreement with the PM and reached 88 percent after the rubric was rewritten. Airbnb is blunt about the stakes: an uncalibrated virtual judge is worse than no judge at all, because it hands you false confidence rather than a wrong signal you would notice.

How do you catch regressions? Does this go in CI?

What Airbnb describes explicitly is production monitoring, not a CI pipeline. They sample 5 percent of live de-identified traffic daily, run programmatic checks and virtual judges over it, escalate flagged outputs to human review, and let a weekly PM review turn newly observed failure modes into new evals. The article does not spell out how evaluation is wired into a deployment pipeline. What it is clear about is the principle that pre-production metrics are not a one-time validation and must be measured again in production the same way. In practice, splitting the work is sensible: golden-set checks on pull requests, larger-sample regression runs as a nightly batch. That split is a recommendation of mine, not something the article states.

If I am starting today, what comes first?

Reading outputs with your own eyes. Airbnb recommends the same starting point to teams: manually review your data and build an intuition for what counts as success. Push 100 inputs through the prototype, read every result, and the failures cluster into types on their own. Metrics come next. Attach a generic helpfulness score first and it will look healthy while missing the failure mode people actually hit. As a minimum setup I would use a 20-row eval set, two programmatic checks, and one judge. Twenty is smaller than the 50 to 100 in the article, but one person can label it in an hour, which means it actually runs in week one, and you grow it as failure types surface. That number is my recommendation, not Airbnb's.

Conclusion: three things to take away today

First, evaluation is a data-reading problem, not a tool-selection problem. Invert the ordering that puts a human reading 100 outputs first, and you end up bolting somebody else's metric suite onto your product and measuring somebody else's failure modes. Fill in one spreadsheet before you pick a platform.

Second, an uncalibrated judge is not a metric; it is a confidence generator. Put a 78 percent referee on a dashboard and more than one case in five is wrong while the scores pile up daily. Every prompt decision made above that sits on noise. Plant deliberately broken outputs in the golden set and confirm the judge filters them out before anything else.

Third, keeping the dimension count low is a quality clause and a budget clause at once. The line about 3 to 5 calibrated evaluators beating 20 to 30 noisy ones is not only about accuracy. Twenty dimensions turn the weekly review into a full-day meeting, and a full-day meeting eventually stops happening. A review that stopped happening catches no regressions.

One last thing. What lasts from this methodology is not the procedure but the pressure it applies to write down what good means. Draft a rubric and you discover that things the team believed were settled were never actually agreed on. The argument that breaks out at that table is worth more than the eval scores that follow it. That is the real reason to build the first twenty rows.
