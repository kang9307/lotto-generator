🛑 Read this first - how to use this guide

This is practical living information meant to help someone who lives alone get through the first few minutes without freezing up. It is not a substitute for medical diagnosis or treatment, and it is not a repair manual. In a home where you live alone, the most dangerous part of an incident is usually not the incident itself - it is the few minutes you spend telling yourself "I can handle this" instead of calling for help. If you see flames or smoke, smell strong gas, find someone unresponsive, have trouble breathing, or have bleeding that will not stop or severe pain, call emergency services right now. When you are not sure, calling and asking is still the safer choice. Calling and finding out it was nothing costs you nothing; the minutes you lose by waiting cannot be taken back.

🚨 Emergency Contacts

  • Fire/Emergency: 911
  • Gas Leak: Local gas company emergency line
  • Electrical Emergency: Local power company
  • Water Issues: Local water authority

The specific numbers in this guide are South Korean, because that is where the official guidance quoted here comes from. If you live somewhere else, use this as a template and save your own country's equivalents. In Korea, two more numbers matter: 112 when a person is the threat (break-in, violence, stalking, a missing child) and 110 for non-urgent administrative questions and counselling referrals. Korea's Ministry of the Interior and Safety consolidated 21 scattered report lines into three numbers - 119, 112 and 110 - back in 2016, and 112 and 119 are linked, so in a fast-moving situation either one reaches the right agency. Do not burn time deciding which to dial.

Emergencies Can Be Stressful

Living alone means dealing with sudden leaks, power outages, or getting locked out. Knowing what to do beforehand helps you stay calm.

When you live with family, someone else is there. One person shuts the valve while another makes the call, and there is usually at least one person who has seen the problem before. When you live alone, you are the one who notices, the one who decides, and the one who acts - all at once. That is why the same incident tends to get worse in a one-person household: the response is slower, and slower means more damage.

The fix is simpler than it sounds. Decide in advance so that you do not have to think in the moment. That is why fire drills repeat the same sequence every single time. Under stress, people do not improvise well; they fall back on whatever order they have already rehearsed. The tables below are that pre-decided order, compressed onto one page. Read them once, screenshot them, and drop the image into your phone's photo album or the first screen of your notes app - that alone covers half the preparation.

One more mindset shift is worth making. Let go of "am I overreacting if I call?" Fire and police services exist precisely to make that judgement for you. If you cannot decide, remember there is someone on the other end of the line whose job is to decide.

Who to Call, by Situation

The first thing that trips people up in an emergency is "which number do I even dial?" Different agencies own different problems, and some old numbers no longer exist. In Korea, for example, 1339 - once the emergency medical information line - was discontinued in June 2013 and folded into 119. Today 1339 is the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency call centre for infectious disease and health questions, so dialling it during an emergency only wastes time. Memorise the first three rows and save the rest in your phone.

First-Minute Response by Situation

The incidents differ, but the skeleton of the response is the same. 1) Get yourself away from the hazard, 2) shut off the source, 3) tell someone. Reversing that order is what hurts people. The most common mistake by far is staying inside a dangerous space in order to "deal with the cause". Getting yourself out always comes before closing a valve.

1. Power Outage

An outage is the most common and least dangerous emergency on this list, which is exactly why it matters to know how far you are allowed to go yourself. The rule is simple: flipping the breaker lever back up is your job; anything deeper inside the distribution panel belongs to a professional.

First Checks

  • Is it just your unit or the whole building?
  • Check your circuit breaker
  • If tripped from overload, reset it

Look out the window first. If the neighbours and the street lights are on, the problem is yours alone, and it is usually a breaker tripped by overload or a ground fault. If you had the microwave, the air fryer and a space heater running at once, overload is the likely cause. Unplug those appliances before resetting the breaker and you narrow the cause down. If it trips again the instant you reset it, stop trying - that can indicate a fault, and it should go to the building office or an electrician.

What to Do

  • Just your unit: Reset breaker, call building management if needed
  • Whole building: Wait (management handles it)
  • Area-wide: Contact power company

In Korea the KEPCO customer centre on 123 takes electrical fault and outage reports around the clock, with no area code needed. There is no point calling repeatedly during an area-wide outage; if the fault is already logged you can simply ask for the expected restoration time. On the other hand, a burning smell, sparks at an outlet or a warm wall is not an outage - it is the start of a fire. In that case switch the breaker off, get outside, and call the fire service.

Be Prepared

  • Keep flashlight or rechargeable lantern
  • Keep phone charger always charged
  • Know where your circuit breaker is

The whole point of outage preparation is putting light where your hand can find it in the dark. A flashlight buried in a drawer is a flashlight you will not find. Keep one somewhere you could touch with your eyes closed - beside the bed, on the shoe cabinet by the door - and another in the kitchen. Your phone's torch is a stopgap only: in a long outage that battery becomes your communication line, so spending it on lighting is expensive. Keep a power bank at 70-80% or better, and leave the fridge closed, since an unopened fridge holds its cold for a surprisingly long time.

Treat candles as the last resort, and never leave one burning when you step away or fall asleep. A large share of the secondary fires that follow outages start with an unattended candle.

2. Water Leak

Water spreads more quietly than fire. And when you live alone, you often find out only when the neighbour downstairs calls. That is why, with leaks, shutting it off quickly is the same thing as reducing the repair bill.

Immediate Actions

  • Turn off water valve (under sink, in bathroom)
  • Mop up water with towels
  • Contact building management or landlord

Keep that order. Mopping first is endless, because it keeps leaking. Close the valve, then mop. If the water is running toward an outlet or a power strip, switch off the breaker for that circuit before you start mopping. Take a few photos or a short video as well - they help enormously later when you and the landlord or insurer sort out who pays. Responsibility for a leak shifts depending on where it originated (upstairs, your unit, shared piping), so your record is your evidence.

By Situation

  • Leak from upstairs: Check with neighbor, report to management
  • Toilet won't flush: Try plunger, call plumber if serious
  • Washing machine hose: Check connection

When the source is upstairs, going through the building office costs less goodwill and usually moves faster than knocking on the door yourself. Management knows the plumbing layout, and if shared piping is at fault part of the responsibility sits with them. Washing machine inlet hoses stiffen over the years and start weeping at the joints; simply getting into the habit of closing the washer's supply valve when you leave the house prevents the worst-case version - a hose bursting in an empty flat.

Know where your water shutoff valve is. You don't want to search for it during an emergency.

Spend three minutes this evening finding and turning the valve under the sink, the ones at the bathroom basin and toilet, and the washing machine supply valve. A valve that has not been turned in years can seize, and discovering that mid-emergency is the worst possible timing. Photograph what you found and save it in your notes app under "my valves" and you are done.

When the Water Is Cut Off

  • Check building notices, texts and lift announcements first (is it planned?)
  • Leave the taps closed so nothing floods when supply returns
  • Do not run an electric or instant water heater with no water in it
  • Let the water run briefly after restoration, since it may come back rusty
  • Keep some bottled water and wet wipes for a longer interruption

A water cut is not an emergency-services matter. Planned shutoffs come through building notices; an unexpected one goes through the building office to the local water authority. The genuinely awkward part of living alone is the toilet, so if you cannot fill a bath or a large basin in advance, keeping a few two-litre bottles as a reserve is worth it.

3. Gas Smell

Of everything in this guide, a gas incident can go from nothing to catastrophic in the shortest amount of time. So the response is not "check and judge" - it is "smell it, run the sequence". The Korea Gas Safety Corporation's own guidance is to stay calm, carry out first-response steps and contact them immediately rather than trying to diagnose it.

Do Immediately

  • Turn off gas valve
  • Open windows for ventilation
  • Don't touch electrical switches (spark risk)
  • Evacuate
  • Call gas company from outside

"Electrical switches" here means more than the wall switch. Extractor fans, doorbells, plugging or unplugging anything, even taking a phone call indoors can produce a small spark. Turning on the extractor fan to "ventilate" is the single most dangerous version of this mistake. Ventilate only by opening windows and the front door by hand.

Make the call after you are outside. In Korea gas incidents can be reported nationwide to the Korea Gas Safety Corporation on 1544-4500, and your local city gas company's control room or the fire service both work. If the smell is strong or you cannot identify the source, the fire service is the safest choice. Tell the neighbours on your floor and below as you leave, because gas problems do not always stop at your front door.

If you smell gas, NEVER touch light switches or outlets!

For prevention, find your intermediate valve and your meter valve now, and get into the habit of closing them when you leave for a long trip. Closing the intermediate valve after cooking is a small habit that meaningfully lowers your risk.

4. Locked Out

It will not endanger your life, but it is the emergency that happens most often to people living alone. Knowing the order keeps you from standing outside your own door at midnight with no plan.

What to Do

  • Check if building management has a spare key
  • Contact your landlord
  • Call a locksmith (costs money)

When you call a locksmith, ask for a rough price on the phone first. Night and after-hours call-outs carry surcharges, and the type of lock changes the figure considerably. A legitimate door-opening service will also want proof that you actually live there, so keeping a photo of your ID or lease on your phone speeds things up. Conversely, be wary of anyone willing to open any door without checking - a company that does that for you would do it to your door too.

Prevention

  • Make spare keys
  • Leave one with trusted person
  • For smart locks, memorize the code

Do not hide a spare key under the mat, in the fire hose cabinet or the meter box. Those spots are common knowledge. Leaving it with family or a close friend is far safer, and that person often becomes the first link in the safety network described later. For digital locks, replace the batteries as soon as the low-battery beep starts, and keep a spare set in your bag rather than inside the flat so a dead lock is solvable from the wrong side of the door.

5. Fire

What injures people in a fire is usually not flame but smoke. That is why every official guideline puts "get out" ahead of "put it out". Korean fire authorities state that once you judge initial suppression is not possible you should report it and evacuate without delay - and that you should always close the door behind you to slow the spread.

Evacuate First

  • Call 911
  • Cover nose/mouth with wet towel
  • Stay low while evacuating
  • Never use elevator
  • Feel door handle temperature before opening

The elevator is banned because a fire can cut its power and trap you, and because the shaft becomes a smoke channel. If you cannot get downstairs, the roof is the alternative. Where there is heavy smoke, hold a wet towel over your nose and mouth with one hand and move low. A hot door handle means fire is already on the other side, so leave it shut and find another route; if you are trapped, seal the gaps around the door with wet cloth, make your position visible from a window, and wait for rescue.

Do not turn back for shoes, a wallet or a laptop. Those few seconds have decided outcomes in case after case. Your phone in your hand is enough, and if you do not even have that, borrow a neighbour's once you are outside.

For Small Fires

  • Use fire extinguisher
  • Pour water (not on electrical fires!)
  • If you can't control it, evacuate

"Small" means lower than you are tall, and positioned so you can stand with the exit behind you. The exit must never be on the far side of the flames. Water thrown on burning oil spreads it, and water on an outlet or power strip risks electrocution. Both of those cases call for the extinguisher.

Fire and Gas Procedures - Making the Sequence Automatic

Here are the fire and gas responses above compressed into one line each. Under pressure, this is about as much as anyone can actually recall.

  • Fire: alert - get out (closing the door) - take the stairs - call from outside - if trapped, seal the gaps and signal your position
  • Gas: close the valve - open windows by hand only - get out - call from outside

How to Actually Use an Extinguisher

  • Carry the extinguisher close to the fire
  • Pull out the safety pin on the handle
  • Stand with the wind at your back and aim the hose at the base of the fire
  • Squeeze the handle and sweep side to side, like sweeping with a broom
  • Watch for reignition even after the flames drop, and evacuate the moment it grows again

Most people buy an extinguisher and never touch it again. Pick yours up once and just confirm the pressure gauge needle sits in the green - even that much makes your hands move better under real pressure. If the weight feels like too much, a lighter unit or a throwable extinguishing device alongside it is a reasonable option.

Home Fire Equipment Is Legally Required in Korea

  • Extinguisher: at least one unit rated 2 or above, per household and per floor
  • Standalone smoke alarm: at least one in each partitioned room - bedroom, living room, kitchen
  • Applies to detached, multi-user and multi-family houses, plus row houses and multiplex housing
  • The alarm runs on its own battery and sounds when it detects smoke
  • When the low-battery chirp starts, replace it immediately rather than later

A standalone smoke alarm is worth even more when you live alone, because you cannot smell a fire while you are asleep. The fewer people there are to wake you, the more that device is doing. Installation is not difficult - most units simply mount on the ceiling - so if you do not have one, it is a weekend job.

Earthquakes and Extended Outages

Earthquakes are rare, but they are the one disaster where you have only seconds to decide, which makes reading about them in advance unusually valuable. The key is that what you do "while it is shaking" and "once it stops" are completely different.

While It Is Shaking

  • Avoid falling objects such as furniture and televisions and get under a table
  • Hold the table leg firmly and protect your head and body
  • With no table available, cover your head with a cushion or bag and stay low
  • Do not run outside while it is still shaking - falling debris from the facade is more dangerous

Once the Shaking Stops

  • Shut off the electricity and gas
  • Open a door to secure an exit (a deformed frame can trap you)
  • Elevators can stop, so use the stairs to get out of the building
  • On the way out, watch for falling glass, signage and roof tiles, and shield yourself with what you are carrying
  • Move to open ground such as a sports field or park

If you feel shaking inside an elevator, press every floor button and get out at the first door that opens. If you are trapped, call for help through the intercom or your phone and do not try to force the doors.

When an Outage Drags On

  • Put your phone in power-saving mode and spend the battery on communication only
  • Keep the fridge and freezer closed (they hold cold well if undisturbed)
  • Avoid elevators until restoration is confirmed
  • Water supply sometimes fails alongside power, so secure drinking water early
  • Get alerts and restoration news from a radio or the Safety Step app
  • After power returns, switch appliances on one at a time rather than all at once

Korea's national disaster safety portal recommends keeping enough household supplies to be self-sufficient for at least three days. For one person that is not a daunting amount - a few bottles of water, a few instant meals, a flashlight and batteries covers most of it.

6. Clogged Drain

Not urgent, but left alone it turns into smells, insects and eventually backflow. In a studio flat the bathroom and kitchen sit close together, so the smell reaches the whole home quickly.

Try First

  • Use plunger
  • Baking soda + vinegar, then hot water
  • Clean the drain trap

A plunger only builds pressure if there is enough water to submerge the drain opening. Pushing on a dry drain does nothing. Baking soda and vinegar do little against a hair clog but are fine for grease film and mild odour. One caution matters more than the technique: never mix chemical drain products. Combining an acidic cleaner with chlorine bleach can generate harmful gas, so use one, flush it through thoroughly, and only then try another. Open a window while you work.

If That Doesn't Work

  • Call a plumber
  • Ask building management

If several drains back up at once, the problem is probably the shared stack rather than your flat. In that case tell the building office before hiring anyone yourself, since who pays may change. For prevention, a hair strainer and a habit of solidifying cooking oil instead of pouring it down the drain are enough.

7. Bug Infestation

Insects are a hygiene issue, but for someone living alone they are also the thing that makes home stop feeling like home. That is reason enough to deal with it quickly.

Emergency Response

  • Insecticide spray (ventilate after)
  • Seal food, take out trash
  • Cover drains

When you spray, do not saturate a sealed room. In a small studio you inhale far more of it, and using aerosols near an open flame adds an ignition risk. Open the windows afterwards and step out for a while before coming back. Insects almost always arrive through the drain, the gap under the front door, or a hole in a window screen - sealing those three routes alone makes a visible difference.

If Severe

  • Call pest control
  • Request building-wide treatment

Treating one unit often just invites them back from next door or upstairs. So if it keeps recurring, asking the building office for building-wide treatment is the real fix rather than repeating it alone. If you want to know what to do about bites themselves, see Insect Bite and Sting First Aid.

If You Collapse While Alone - Preparing in Advance

This is the most important section here. Everything above assumes you can still move. A situation where your body suddenly stops cooperating needs an entirely different kind of preparation, because in a home where you live alone there is nobody to find you.

Signs That Mean Call Right Now

  • Unconscious, or not responding when spoken to
  • Not breathing, or breathing that sounds clearly abnormal
  • Sudden weakness on one side, slurred speech, blurred vision, severe dizziness or headache - possible stroke
  • Crushing pain or pressure in the centre of the chest that persists - possible heart attack
  • Bleeding that will not stop, a large burn, or severe abdominal pain
  • Pain after a fall that stops you standing, or a blow to the head

The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency advises that for heart attack and stroke, the moment symptoms appear you should call emergency services and get to the nearest large hospital emergency room. The treatment window is generally described as within 2 hours for heart attack and within 3 hours for stroke. Arriving inside that window and receiving proper treatment changes the degree of recovery substantially. That is exactly why "I will lie down and see if it passes" is the most dangerous option. When you are on your own, err toward calling and asking even if the symptoms seem ambiguous.

Avoid driving yourself to hospital as well. If things worsen on the way you have no options, and an ambulance is a space where care continues while you travel.

Falls Deserve Extra Caution When You Live Alone

  • Put a non-slip mat on the bathroom floor
  • Do not leave water pooled on kitchen and bathroom floors
  • Grab bars beside the toilet and bath are worth installing
  • Keep rooms and stairways well lit rather than dim
  • Clear away cables and clutter that you could trip over
  • Maintain strength and balance with regular exercise

If you do fall, do not rush to get up. Check yourself for injury first, then rise slowly while holding a chair or another fixed support. If the pain is bad enough that you cannot stand, a fracture is possible, so call emergency services or ask someone nearby for help getting to hospital. When you live alone the deciding factor is whether your phone is within reach, which is why keeping it on the basin while you shower genuinely helps.

Set These Up on Your Phone Today

  • Register emergency medical information - blood type, allergies, medication, emergency contact - so it is visible from the lock screen
  • Check whether your phone has an emergency SOS shortcut, such as pressing the side button several times, and turn it on
  • Designate emergency contacts in your address book
  • Install the Safety Step app for disaster alerts and direct-call buttons
  • Note the Emergency Report app, which lets you file a 112 or 119 report from your phone
  • Keep a charging cable near the bed and sofa too, within reach from where you might end up

What these settings have in common is that they let information reach someone when you cannot speak. It is five minutes of work, and it is also exactly the kind of thing you can never do at the moment you need it. For general advice on being unwell, see Part 12: When You're Sick.

Strangers at the Door - The One Rule

Noise outside your door is stressful in itself when you live alone. There is only one rule worth remembering: you do not verify and then report - you report and then verify.

  • When the bell rings, check only through the intercom or peephole and leave the door shut
  • People impersonate couriers and meter readers, so if you need confirmation, ask the building office with the door closed
  • If the handle moves or you hear someone working the lock, call the police immediately
  • If you cannot speak, stay on the line and let them hear the situation
  • If the door frame or a window looks tampered with, do not go in - report it from outside
  • If you feel followed on the way home, go into somewhere staffed, like a convenience store, not your flat
  • Destroy the name and phone number on delivery labels before binning them

"What if it turns out to be nothing?" is what delays the call. Police call-outs end up being nothing far more often than not, and that causes no problem at all. For broader home security, see Part 10: Safety Tips.

8. Be Prepared

Preparedness is not about buying a lot of things - it is about putting them where you can find them. Korea's national disaster portal recommends storing emergency supplies in a dedicated container or a designated spot. Pick one box, put it all in there, and that box is your kit.

Save These Contacts

  • Building management
  • Landlord
  • Locksmith
  • Plumber

Add one or two family members or friends as emergency contacts, plus the nearest hospital with an emergency room. And for the case where your phone is gone or dead, writing those numbers on paper and sticking it beside the fridge or by the door is more useful than it sounds. Outages and water damage really do take phones out of service.

Building a Safety Net with Neighbours and Family

So far this has been about objects and procedures. But the most reliable safeguard for someone living alone is actually a person who would notice if you were not okay today. However well you prepare, once you are down you cannot make the call yourself. This is not about building an elaborate social life - one very low-effort connection is enough.

  • Agree a rhythm with one family member or friend: "one emoji every night" or "a short call twice a week" is plenty
  • Agree what happens when there is no reply: "if you don't hear from me for two days, ring me" removes their hesitation
  • Decide who holds the spare key: someone has to be able to open the door if checking is ever necessary
  • Learn your neighbours' faces: even a nodding acquaintance is who notices first that something is off
  • Ask your local community centre: many local governments run wellbeing check-in services for one-person households
  • Do not let deliveries pile up: parcels stacking outside a door is itself a signal neighbours read

Public policy is moving the same way. Responding to the growth of one-person households and weakening social ties, Korea's Ministry of Health and Welfare has run a prevention and care programme for people at risk of dying alone, identifying those at risk and providing wellbeing checks and living support - and its scope has widened from older adults to include younger and middle-aged people living alone. Some local governments, Seoul among them, run weekly check-ins linked to welfare services. If you have been spending a lot of time on your own, simply asking your community centre whether a check-in service exists can be where the connection starts.

None of this means "I am at risk". It is the same as wearing a seatbelt: best never needed, awkward to be without. For finding the right distance with neighbours see Part 13: Neighbor Relations, and when time alone starts to weigh on you, Part 18: Dealing with Loneliness.

Frequently Asked Questions

If the power suddenly goes out, how should I check and respond?

First check whether it's just your unit or the whole building that lost power, and take a look at your circuit breaker. If it tripped from overload, just flip it back on; if it's a building-wide issue, contact the management office, and if it's a whole-area outage, report it to KEPCO at 123. If you smell burning or saw a spark, though, do not reset the breaker - report it to the fire service instead.

If water starts leaking in my home, what should I do first?

The first move is shutting off the water valve, usually located under the sink or in the bathroom. After that, mop up or bail out the water and contact the building management or your landlord right away. It's worth knowing where that valve is ahead of time, before an emergency hits. Photos and a short video give you evidence when responsibility is sorted out later.

What should I do if I smell gas in my home?

Shut off the gas valve immediately and open the windows to ventilate. Never touch electrical switches, since a spark could be dangerous - evacuate outside first, then report it to the gas safety corporation. Switching on an extractor fan counts as operating a switch and is dangerous too, and the call itself is safest made from outdoors.

How should I evacuate in case of a fire?

Call 119, cover your nose and mouth with a wet towel, and stay low while evacuating - those are the basics. Never take the elevator, and always check whether a door handle is hot before opening it. Closing the door as you leave slows the spread, and if you cannot get downstairs, head for the roof.

What should I prepare in advance for emergencies?

Save the contact numbers for the building management office, your landlord, a locksmith, and a plumber ahead of time. Keeping emergency supplies on hand - a flashlight, a fire extinguisher, a plunger, and a spare key - means you can respond calmly instead of panicking. Add a smoke alarm check, emergency medical information on your phone, and one person who would notice if you went quiet, and you are genuinely prepared.

Wrap-up

Emergencies can happen anytime. Save contacts and know basic responses—you'll handle things much more calmly.

If only three things survive from this article, let them be these. First, three numbers remove the question of who to call. Second, in every scenario you get yourself out first and shut off the source second. Third, more valuable than any supply is one person who would notice you were not okay.

Three things you can do today: 1) locate your three shutoff valves, 2) put emergency medical information and emergency contacts on your phone, 3) look at your extinguisher gauge and smoke alarm. All together it is under fifteen minutes. Those fifteen minutes buy you the minutes that matter later.

Next up, the final part: One Year Living Alone Review. Let's look back at the journey!

🚨 One last time

Flames or smoke, a strong gas smell, reduced consciousness, difficulty breathing, sudden one-sided weakness or slurred speech, persistent chest pressure, bleeding that will not stop, pain that prevents you standing - if any one of these applies, do not decide alone. Call emergency services now. Nothing in this article is a reason to delay or replace that call. Emergency medical advice and hospital guidance are available on the same line, so when you are unsure, calling to ask is the right move.

Any symptom requiring medical judgement should be assessed by a healthcare professional.
