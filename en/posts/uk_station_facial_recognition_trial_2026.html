This article uses only figures from a report published on 29 September 2026 by Liberty Investigates, based on a document it obtained through a freedom of information request (co-published with The Guardian), and from an outlet reporting British Transport Police announcements. Values marked "back-calculated" are plain arithmetic on two source figures and are not source figures. This is neither a case for nor against facial recognition.

What Was Reported - One Page of Numbers

The summary "500,000 faces scanned, one alert, zero arrests" is short enough to travel far, and being short, it loses what each number counts. So the facts come first, kept narrow and close to the source. From February to July 2026, British Transport Police (BTP) trialled live facial recognition (LFR) at nine London stations. A camera reads the faces of passers-by, compares them with a watchlist compiled by the police, and raises an alert on a match. The report cites a freedom of information document showing that equipment hire and police staffing for the 18 deployments cost £320,786.

The results come in three lines. More than 500,000 faces were scanned; there was one match against the watchlist, and that one was an incorrect identification; and no arrest followed directly from an alert. The table below reads those three lines, and the rest, as "source figure, what it means, what the source does not say". The last column is the point of this article. The meaning of a number is complete only when it comes with the list of questions it cannot answer.

There is also a knack to reading the table. First scan the last column and strike out the questions the number cannot answer. Then use the number only for the questions that remain. The 500,000 scans, for instance, can support "the trial was about this large" but not "there were this many wanted people in the area". Zero arrests can support "no arrest came directly from an alert" but not "the technology was useless". Because this procedure follows the definition of the number and not the sentence of a news story, it leads to the same conclusion whatever tone a citing article happens to take.

500,000 and 1 - How the Two Denominators Relate

The 500,000 and the 1 sit on the same line but count different things. The 500,000 is the faces read as people passed the camera; the 1 is the item the system judged similar enough to a watchlist entry to hand to a person. This is where the familiar remark that "accuracy" carries little information becomes concrete. Depending on the denominator, the same event becomes a number of entirely different size.

  • When the denominator is scans. One wrong alert over more than 500,000 scans gives at most one in 500,000 (0.0002%) wrong alerts per scan (back-calculated). Read it as an upper bound on the chance that a passer-by is wrongly flagged.
  • When the denominator is alerts. One alert out of one was wrong, so alert accuracy is 0/1. That is too small a sample to call a ratio.
  • When the denominator is people on the watchlist. The number of people on the list was not published, so this ratio cannot be computed at all.
  • When the denominator is the times a listed person passed a camera. How often a real target walked by cannot be known from outside the trial, so the "detection rate" that needs this denominator cannot be fixed from the source.

Of these four denominators the source supplies only the first two. The second is practically empty, leaving the first as the only ratio that can be computed. Yet the first tells us about the burden on an ordinary passer-by of being wrongly flagged; it does not tell us how well the system finds targets. Even inside one announcement, those two questions require different denominators. Why thresholds and denominators are indispensable for testing accuracy claims is set out as general theory in section 3 of the earlier article, so this piece only applies that frame to this trial's numbers.

Compressed to one line, the mapping is this: scans are the denominator for "how many passed", alerts are the denominator for "how much the system reacted", and arrests are the result of "how far that reaction went". The first thing lost when the three numbers share one sentence is this distinction. When they are tied together by multiplication or ratio, values without meaning appear, so it is safer to write each number separately with its own denominator attached.

What the Sentence "One Alert, and It Was False" Does and Does Not Say

"There was only one alert, and it was a false one" gives two impressions at once: that the system rarely raised an alarm, and that it was wrong when it did. The first is supported by the source. For the second, a sample of one gives a direction but no size.

  • What can be read, part one: the total number of alerts was extremely small. If only one notification reached a person after more than 500,000 faces were read, then either the system was set not to sound often or faces resembling the watchlist were rare. The two cannot be told apart.
  • What can be read, part two: a false positive happened at least once. The claim that "this system does not produce false positives" is broken by this trial. It is an existence proof that a false alert can occur under the same settings.
  • What cannot be read, part one: a false positive rate. A ratio drawn from one event swings widely with a slightly different sample. Had there been zero, it would have looked low; had there been three, it might have looked ordinary.
  • What cannot be read, part two: where the threshold sat. The same equipment raises more alerts and more false positives as the threshold is lowered. The quoted material does not say where this trial ran. One alert could be the result of a high threshold, in which case the number of missed targets may have grown accordingly.
  • What cannot be read, part three: the human step after an alert. The announcement quoted by the outlet says officers review an alert before acting. Whether this one was filtered out at that review, or whether anyone was actually approached, is not in the source.

In short, one alert is accurate as "the number of times the system sounded" but cannot be read as "the quality of the system". A question about quality needs three more values: the threshold, the size of the watchlist and the number of times a real target passed.

What tends to happen with a sample of one is that a story gets attached to it. One article reads the case as "a limit of the technology exposed", another as "one in 500,000, so practically accurate". The two sentences use the same number in opposite directions, but both presuppose a value the source did not give: the false positive rate. What the source gives is one event and the size of the observation window in which it happened, and anything beyond that is not for the reader to fill in; it is a box still empty.

Zero Arrests Is Not a Verdict - The Purpose Sentence

It is natural for zero arrests to become the headline. But to use that number as a verdict of success or failure, one needs the premise that the trial adopted arrests as its success criterion. The purpose sentence from a BTP spokesperson quoted in the report reads, in gist, that the trial aimed to understand how the technology could most effectively support identifying wanted offenders and people who may pose a risk to passengers and staff.

The verb in that sentence is "understand". It is a sentence about learning how to operate, not one that decides whether to adopt. In a trial with that purpose, results fall into two kinds: observations about what the technology does (scans, alerts) and observations about what operations require (deployments, staff hours, cost). Arrests belong to a third kind, "effect", and the source does not say whether this trial was designed to measure that effect.

  • Way to get no arrests, one. Nobody on the watchlist passed that station at that time. With no target, no accurate system has anything to catch.
  • Way to get no arrests, two. A target passed but the system missed. This case is a matter for the system.
  • Way to get no arrests, three. An alert occurred but was filtered out in human review. In this case the operation prevented a false positive.
  • Way to get no arrests, four. An approach was made after an alert but the conditions for arrest were not met. This is the case where the alert was right and still there was no arrest.

All four end in "zero arrests", yet their implications for the system are opposite. The observed facts do not separate which one occurred. Meanwhile BTP said that during the trial there were a number of associated arrests, for assault, theft, possession of an offensive weapon, breach of a criminal behaviour order and public order offences, but that they did not result directly from an LFR alert. That is also why the arrest cell must be read as "zero directly from alerts". The sentence separates police activity while the cameras were up from the results of alerts, and it carries no judgment that either is good or bad.

Another feature of a trial as a form is that its design limits the interpretation of its results in advance. A trial meant to learn operations tends to pick busy places and times, so even when scans go up, the chance of meeting a target depends on the conditions the trial set. That is why scan counts should be read as an indicator of trial scale, and alerts and arrests as observations under those conditions, each apart from the other; the moment they are tied into one sentence such as "zero arrests per 500,000 scans", the trial conditions disappear. That is also why the table in this article splits the indicators one by one.

Placing the values in this table next to figures from other trials or other countries needs care. If deployment style, watchlist size, threshold or the scope of cost items differ, an indicator with the same name measures a different thing. Cost in particular is the sum of two items, equipment hire and police staffing, so it cannot be compared directly with a cost elsewhere that includes procurement and maintenance. The table is a scale for gauging the size of this trial and not a ranking.

  • The scale of 28,000 per deployment. It is an average that gives a feel for how many faces one deployment read. Since passenger volumes per station and deployment hours are not in the source, it cannot be converted into a share of passenger traffic.
  • Why cost is not divided "per alert". With a denominator of one, cost simply becomes the total. The number is easy to quote but at odds with what the trial set out to find out.
  • Where the "per scan" cost sits. The value is a scale of trial size and not a unit price. It has nothing to do with the unit cost of permanent operation. The source's wording says only that the figure is the sum of two items, equipment hire and police staffing.

The Number of Missed People Lies Outside the Trial

The other half of facial recognition performance is not the false positive but the miss: a person on the watchlist walked past the camera and no alert was raised. The published figures of this trial contain no number for misses, and by structure could not. A miss needs a denominator that can only be counted once someone has passed, and the police themselves do not know that denominator during a trial.

  • Measuring misses requires planting targets. A controlled test in which known people are walked through to see whether they are caught would be needed. The quoted material mentions no such design.
  • False positives, by contrast, are easy to count. When an alert sounds, a person judges whether it is right, so false positives remain on record. The published numbers therefore lean towards false positives, and misses stay silent.
  • With few alerts, two stories are both possible. "There was no target" and "the target was missed" produce the same observation, one alert. They are a pair of hypotheses that observation alone cannot separate.
  • So a conclusion drawn from one alert can be reversed either way. "It did not sound, so it is safe" and "it did not sound, so it is useless" both fail to stand on the same evidence.

This is the heart of the angle this article takes. "One alert, zero arrests" creates a temptation to fill in what the trial did not measure: one direction is reassurance, the other is waste. The source figures support a conclusion in neither direction.

It is also worth noticing that values that stay in accounting and schedules, cost and deployment count, were among the first published from a document obtained through a freedom of information request. Values recorded in accounting documents are easy to count, whereas values about performance require definitions. When the kinds of published numbers are set this way, readers drift towards the numbers that are easy to count. Knowing that tendency builds the habit of asking first which number is missing.

What Would Need to Be Published in the Extension Phase

BTP announced a four-month extension and an expansion to London Underground stations. According to the coverage, the first Underground deployment was at Victoria, cameras rotate between Underground and Network Rail stations, and the trial ends in November 2026. The stated operating policy is that images that do not match the watchlist are deleted immediately and permanently, and that an officer reviews an alert before acting. The algorithm was reported as NEC's NeoFace M40.

Below is what would have to fill the blanks in this table for judgment to become possible when results from the extension phase appear. It is a checklist for the reader, not a demand.

  • Definition of the scan count. Whether duplicates are removed. This decides whether 500,000 is a number of people or of events.
  • A table of alert counts and outcomes. Showing total alerts, those filtered out in human review, contacts and arrests on one line builds the alert-to-arrest ladder.
  • The threshold. Whether it was the same at every deployment or changed; if changed, when and why.
  • Size and make-up of the watchlist. Category-level sizes beyond the statement that it is processed as special category personal data. An aggregate that does not identify individuals is enough.
  • The success criterion, defined in advance. What was to be called a success before the trial began. A criterion attached after the results tends to fit the results.
  • Values by deployment. Not the average of 18 but scans, hours and alerts per session. The larger the variation, the less useful the average.

These six do not need to be published all at once. The first two, the definition of a scan and the alert ladder, would already fill the two largest blanks in this article's table. The rest is the next tier of precision. Read it not as an argument about how much should be disclosed but as a list matching each number that might appear to the question it would settle.

The site's earlier article Facial Recognition in Transit Stations - What Must Be Verified covered the technical branches of facial recognition, the general theory of thresholds and denominators, demographic error, how to verify that crime fell, institutional controls and proportionality. This article re-explains none of them and only applies that frame to the concrete numbers from one trial. The difference between the two is as follows.

  • The earlier article's question. Which axes should be checked to judge facial recognition in public space.
  • This article's question. What each of the three numbers already published from one trial counts, and what it does not say.
  • What this article does not cover. Whether adoption is right or wrong, statutory provisions, cases in other countries, privacy assessment. Those judgments hang on values and institutions, not on numbers.

Separating an article that reads numbers from an article that makes judgments also benefits the reader. The definition of a number can be checked against the source, whereas a judgment differs from person to person. When a judgment is needed, the proportionality questions in the earlier article can be used as they are, and the table here serves only to tell what the numbers going into those questions count.
