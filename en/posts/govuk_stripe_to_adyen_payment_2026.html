GOV.UK Pay Switches from Stripe to Adyen 2026 - What It Means for Public Payment Infrastructure

What a government's PSP swap reveals about fintech and payment strategy

2026-06-07 Finance

Introduction: Why a small contract is a big signal

In June 2026, the UK's Government Digital Service (GDS) announced that it would replace Stripe with the Dutch payments firm Adyen as the payment services provider (PSP) behind GOV.UK Pay, the shared online payment platform for public services. On the surface this is just one government body renewing a procurement contract. But from a payments-industry perspective the move carries several layers of meaning. Who operates public payment infrastructure is not merely a vendor question; it shapes fee structures, where data is processed, and which payment methods citizens can use.

This article is not an attempt to promote or criticise any provider. Using the GOV.UK Pay switch as a case study, it sets out to explain, calmly and concretely, five things: the background to the change, how Stripe and Adyen differ in positioning and fee model, what makes public-sector payment infrastructure special, the fintech market dynamics and the criteria for choosing a provider, and the lessons for businesses and policymakers. Because the matter is still unfolding, anything not yet confirmed is described cautiously and attributed to reporting.

⚠️ Read first - the nature of this analysis

This article is for information and analysis based on public reporting and materials. It is not an endorsement of, or advice about, any specific payment provider, company, or investment. Details such as contract value, timeline, and scope reflect reporting at the time of writing and may change. 1. What GOV.UK Pay is - a shared rail for public payments

GOV.UK Pay is a government-run platform that lets many public services handle online payments in one place. Whenever a citizen needs to pay the government, whether for a passport application, a licence, local taxes, or permit fees, departments and councils connect to one shared rail instead of each building its own payment system. Since launching in 2016, it has reportedly processed more than 135 million transactions and over 9 billion pounds.

The point of this shared-rail model is standardisation and reuse. Individual bodies do not have to build payment features from scratch; they simply plug into proven infrastructure, which reduces cost and security risk and keeps the citizen experience consistent. At the same time, this design creates a single dependency: whoever sits behind the platform as the PSP, actually processing card and bank transactions, determines the overall cost and the range of available features.

1.1 The scope of this change - not everything

An important caveat first. According to reports, this switch covers not all of GOV.UK Pay but only non-Crown organisations, namely local authorities, the armed forces, and police, for card payments and Pay by Bank. Central government, the NHS, and arm's length bodies continue to use Worldpay with no change. The contract is valued at up to roughly 25.3 million pounds (advertised as a range of 0 to about 25.27 million) and runs three years, from 18 May 2026 to 17 May 2029. Around 1,000 services will migrate to Adyen in phases. That is reportedly about 17 percent of payments by volume but more than 70 percent of the organisations using the platform.

2. Why the change - five drivers behind the switch

When a government replaces a proven provider, it is wiser to assume several overlapping reasons than one single cause. Here are the drivers that can be read from public reporting and standard procurement practice.

2.1 Open competitive procurement and cost

GDS says it re-selected the PSP through an open and fair tender. Public procurement is, by design, a structure that periodically re-tests the market to refresh price and terms. In high-volume, low-value public payments, even a small per-transaction fee difference adds up to a meaningful sum over a year, so cost efficiency is an unavoidable evaluation criterion.

2.2 New payment methods - open banking and Pay by Bank

The benefit GDS explicitly emphasised is Pay by Bank, that is, open-banking-based account-to-account payments. Embedding a method that bypasses card networks directly into government services can cut card interchange fees and reduce losses from fraud and lost payments. The very fact that the procurement required suppliers to at least maintain current functionality suggests the core aim was feature expansion, not just cost reduction.

2.3 Data sovereignty and a possible preference for a European provider

European public bodies tend to be sensitive about which jurisdiction processes payment and citizen data. Adyen is a European payments firm headquartered in the Netherlands; it reportedly gained UK banking authorisation in 2023 and has staff in London and Manchester. Reporting did not explicitly name data sovereignty as a deciding factor, but a move to a European provider could plausibly be read as favourable on data processing and regulatory jurisdiction. It is more appropriate to treat this as one background factor than to assert it outright.

2.4 A single platform and enterprise-grade processing

Migrating around 1,000 services without interruption while handling both card and account-to-account payments on one platform requires infrastructure optimised for large-scale, multichannel processing. Adyen has long marketed an enterprise-oriented design that unifies cards, A2A payments, and omnichannel in a single stack.

2.5 Avoiding vendor lock-in and easing future migration

Being overly tied to one PSP weakens negotiating power and raises the cost of future changes. GDS framing the move under a heading about making change simple suggests an intent that goes beyond a single switch: to build a structure in which the provider can be changed again later if needed.

3. Stripe vs Adyen - how the two are positioned

To understand this switch you need to know where each company sits in the market. Both are global payment-infrastructure powerhouses, but they target different customers with different philosophies.

3.1 Stripe - developer-friendly, strong with smaller merchants

Stripe is beloved by developers for its clean APIs, fast onboarding, and rich documentation, and it has become something close to a default among startups and small online merchants. Its flat-rate fee structure is easy to predict, and you can start quickly without a separate sales negotiation. The common verdict is that it is the champion of broad accessibility, with wide market coverage and low barriers to entry.

3.2 Adyen - an enterprise, omnichannel, single platform

Adyen has focused on large global companies, marketplaces, and high-volume merchants, with no free tier and no small-business marketing. Its hallmarks are omnichannel that unifies online and offline, a fee structure that transparently passes through interchange costs, and contracts based on formal underwriting. It is frequently described as an enterprise-oriented platform optimised for depth per transaction rather than breadth of market.

One-line summary: Stripe is seen as strong at "anyone can start fast," Adyen at "process big transactions deeply." Neither is superior; they target different customers.

4. Fee models compared - flat rate vs interchange-plus

The difference between the two is clearest in their fee structures, which is also the key cost driver when choosing a provider.

AspectStripe (flat rate)Adyen (Interchange++)
Fee structureFixed rate regardless of card + fixed feeActual interchange cost + thin margin
Cost predictabilitySimple, easy to predictVaries per transaction, complex statements
Favourable scaleSmall to mid, variable transactionsHigh-volume, high-value transactions
OnboardingFast, self-serviceUnderwriting and contract talks
MinimumsLowMinimum invoice and other requirements

The essence is this. Flat rate (Stripe) charges the same percentage regardless of card type, which is simple but can run above average as volume grows. Interchange-plus (Adyen) passes the card-network cost straight through and adds only a thin margin, so higher-volume merchants tend to save more. That said, the statements are complex, and hidden costs such as minimum invoice requirements and integration effort can weigh on smaller merchants. For a government handling large volumes of public payments, the interchange-plus structure may well have looked attractive on cost.

Caution: the comparison above is based on general market assessments. Actual fees vary greatly by merchant size, sector, country, and contract, and no model is always cheaper.

5. Pay by Bank - account-to-account payments that bypass cards

The technical heart of this switch is not simply swapping providers but expanding the payment method itself. At its centre is open-banking-based Pay by Bank.

5.1 How open banking and A2A payments work

Open banking is a framework in which banks use standard APIs to securely connect account information and payment instructions to third parties, with the user's consent. Pay by Bank is an account-to-account (A2A) payment built on top of this: instead of typing in card details, the citizen authenticates and approves the payment directly in their own banking app, skipping the card-network middle step.

5.2 The benefits for the public sector

For a government, the appeal of A2A payments is clear. First, it bypasses card interchange fees, lowering transaction cost. Second, because no card details are handled, card-fraud and theft liability shrinks. Third, it can raise payment security and identity verification (KYC) by leveraging strong bank-app authentication. The more a council processes large volumes of small payments, the greater the cumulative value of these three effects.

5.3 Limits and challenges

It is not a cure-all, of course. Account-to-account payments do not have refund and dispute processes standardised as finely as cards, and they are less familiar to users than cards, so lifting adoption takes time. And if the bank-app authentication journey is not smooth, it can lead to payment drop-off. That is precisely why the government stressed migrating with no loss of functionality and no discernible difference for paying users.

6. What makes public-sector payment infrastructure special

Unlike private e-commerce, public payments involve different variables, and those differences change the criteria for choosing a provider.

First, continuity and trust are absolute requirements. If citizens cannot pay taxes or fees, the administration itself stalls. Migrating 1,000 services without tolerating a single break is hard. Second, inclusion. Citizens without a particular card or app must not be excluded, so a diversity of payment methods matters more than in the private sector. Third, transparency and accountability. Because these are public funds, fee structures and data handling must be auditable. Fourth, regulation and data jurisdiction. Where citizen data is processed can become a political and legal issue. All of these push the choice toward not the cheapest provider but the most trustworthy and most migratable one.

7. Fintech market dynamics - what this switch tells us

It is a single government contract, but it is a slice of a larger market trend.

7.1 Intensifying competition in enterprise payments

Competition over large, high-value payment contracts is increasingly fierce. The position Stripe built in the startup ecosystem now meets the position Adyen built among large enterprises, on the stage of public procurement. The public sector offers large, stable references, so it carries symbolic value for providers.

7.2 The rise of account-to-account payments

As an alternative to card-centric setups, A2A payments are rapidly gaining attention. A government officially adopting them can be read as a signal that open-banking payments are moving beyond a niche and into mainstream infrastructure.

7.3 Regional providers and data sovereignty

Moving away from reliance on global US-based platforms, there is a growing trend to seriously consider regional providers that are favourable on jurisdiction and data processing. This connects to the broader debate about digital sovereignty seen across cloud, AI, and other digital infrastructure, not just payments.

8. Choosing a provider - a checklist for businesses

Here are selection criteria a business can apply to its own situation from this case. They should be weighed together, not one at a time.

  • Transaction structure and scale: average value, count, and variability decide whether flat-rate or interchange-plus is better.
  • Supported payment methods: beyond cards, how broadly does it support A2A, wallet, and local payment methods.
  • Onboarding and operational complexity: do you need fast self-service, or enterprise support with a dedicated team.
  • Data processing and compliance: in which jurisdiction is data processed, and does it meet regulatory requirements.
  • Migration ease: what is the cost and lock-in risk of moving to another provider later.
  • Reliability and SLA: uptime, incident response, and dispute and refund handling.
Core principle: fees are just one of many criteria. The right choice is not the cheapest provider but the one that fits your transaction structure, regulatory environment, and growth stage, and that you can switch away from when needed.

9. Lessons for businesses and policymakers

This UK case offers plenty of reference points for other contexts too.

First, the policy significance of open banking and A2A payments. Many markets already have developed open-banking and easy-payment infrastructure and long-running debates over card fees. In discussions about expanding A2A methods to government services or public utility payments, GOV.UK Pay's phased migration approach can serve as a practical reference. Second, avoiding vendor lock-in in public and corporate procurement. Not being overly bound to one payment provider or one cloud, and writing migration ease into procurement criteria, is a valid lesson. Third, the point that payments are strategy, not unit price. Before arguing over 0.1 percentage points of fees, you should first design which payment structure your business works best in and how you will handle data and regulation.

10. Open questions and what to watch

This matter is still in progress, and several things are worth watching. Will the no-interruption migration of around 1,000 services actually run smoothly; will citizen adoption of Pay by Bank rise meaningfully; will the expected cost savings and fraud reduction show up in the numbers. It is also worth watching whether this decision becomes a precedent for public payment procurement in other European countries, and what changes it triggers in the European public-market strategy of US-based payment platforms. As these results accumulate, they will offer clues to a bigger question: can A2A payments become a mainstream method that complements cards.

11. Conclusion - a payment provider is both infrastructure and strategy

In one line, the GOV.UK Pay switch from Stripe to Adyen is a signal that public payments are shifting from card-centric to account-centric and multichannel, and from unit-price competition to strategic choice. A payment provider is no longer invisible plumbing behind the checkout; it is a strategic asset that simultaneously shapes cost, data, and available payment methods. Even a government periodically re-tests this asset and switches when it must.

The message for businesses and policymakers is clear. When choosing a provider, go beyond comparing fees to weigh transaction structure and scale, data sovereignty and regulation, supported payment methods, and above all whether you can change later.
