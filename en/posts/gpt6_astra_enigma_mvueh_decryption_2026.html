1. Background of Enigma Message Nr. 172: Unsolved for 21 Years Since 2005

In a technical paper published on 19 September 2026 at 08:41 UTC by Crypto Cellar Research, author Frode Weierud announced that a historical World War II German Enigma cipher message, which had resisted cryptanalytic solution for approximately 21 years since 2005, was successfully broken by the artificial intelligence model GPT-6 Astra. The target message was dispatch Nr. 172 with ciphertext identifier "MVUEH", originally sent on 10 July 1941 by wireless station callsign 2ny to the divisional staff (Ib) of the SS-Totenkopf unit.

Generally, electromechanical cipher devices such as the Enigma generate an astronomical key space through internal polyalphabetic substitution rotors, reflector permutations, and plugboard connections. Allied cryptanalysts during the war relied on electromechanical Bombe devices alongside probable word assumptions known as cribs. Nonetheless, isolated historical intercepts containing transcription defects, abnormal stepping cycles, or isolated key settings have continued to defy automated software attacks for decades.

The original source notes that message Nr. 172 MVUEH had stood as an unresolved problem within cryptanalytic research databases since its initial cataloging in 2005. Rather than simply evaluating candidate plaintexts supplied by human researchers, GPT-6 Astra surveyed unsolved message corpora, prioritized this specific dispatch, implemented dedicated simulation algorithms, and concluded the attack with archival validation.

Generally, the Bombe was an electromechanical apparatus designed by Polish and British cryptanalysts to test simultaneous hypotheses across Enigma rotor positions, rejecting key combinations that produced logical contradictions across the plugboard. Recreating this logic in software requires cycle-accurate modeling of internal wiring permutations and stepping pawls.

The published account explains that the model structured its toolchain by pairing Python for high-level exploratory pipelines with optimized C++ routines for intensive permutation loops. In the interpretation of this article, constructing an original, purpose-built simulator rather than executing generic third-party solvers permitted dynamic handling of corrupted characters and anomalous stepping triggers.

5. Applying the Repeated ROSENOW Crib and Resolving the 72nd-Letter Left Rotor Step

The decisive breakthrough required establishing an accurate crib (known or presumed plaintext). Synthesizing geographic movement records along the operational corridor, GPT-6 Astra deployed the repeated place name "ROSENOW ROSENOW" as its primary crib structure for concentrated decryption.

The primary technical barrier encountered during crib matching was the left rotor turnover. In a standard three-rotor Enigma, the left rotor advances only when the middle rotor reaches its specific mechanical pawl notch. In dispatch MVUEH, however, the left rotor stepped unexpectedly after processing the 71st character, altering the cipher alphabet for the final 10 characters.

Because the stream was divided into two distinct cryptographic states (characters 1–71 and 72–82), earlier cryptanalysts attempting single-state dictionary fits were unable to achieve convergence. GPT-6 Astra successfully isolated the exact turnover boundary at position 72, confirming rotor order 253 and aligning the complete plugboard permutation across both segments.

  • Repeated place name crib selection: Testing the tactical convention of repeating critical geographic coordinates as "ROSENOW ROSENOW".

  • Typographical error accommodation: Overcoming the clerical error where "Bitte" was enciphered as "Btte" without discarding the candidate run.

6. Independent Discovery of Bundesarchiv Reference Files and Plaintext Recovery

To substantiate that the recovered text represented historical reality rather than an algorithmic anagram artifact, the system performed archival cross-verification. The report records that GPT-6 Astra independently identified document references RS 3–3/20a and RS 3–3/63b within the German Federal Archives (Bundesarchiv).

Cross-referencing confirmed that historical march diaries recorded the movement of unit elements through Rosenow on that exact date. The complete 82-character plaintext of message Nr. 172 MVUEH was recovered as follows:

"Please indicate route of march. Currently in Rosenow. Please reply immediately"
(Original German plaintext phrasing: Bitte Marschweg angeben. Befinden uns in Rosenow. Umgehende Antwort erbeten.)

The message constituted an urgent operational request from an advancing detachment in Rosenow seeking routing confirmation from division headquarters. This aligned chronologically with dispatch Nr. 173 SIPVX, in which Waschbusch subsequently reported ongoing divisional arrangements.
