When a Korean Workers Compensation Claim Is Denied - The 90-Day Structure of Review, Reexamination and Litigation

Three stages that differ in nature, and a deadline that ends the case before anyone reads it

2026-09-01 Finance & Economy

A denial notice is usually short. It names the claimed condition, and beneath it sits a line or two saying that a proximate causal relationship between the work and the condition cannot be recognised. Most people read that as the final word. Under the system it is not. The notice is the starting line of the appeal process, and from the moment it arrives the days begin to count.

This article does not re-explain what Korean industrial accident insurance is or what benefits it pays. That ground is covered in the complete guide to industrial accident insurance and claims. What is covered here is one thing: what the system demands after a denial or a partial approval. Not techniques for obtaining approval, but the requirements themselves, read article by article. Everything below describes the law of the Republic of Korea as of September 2026, drawing on the Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance Act, the Administrative Litigation Act, the Administrative Appeals Act, the Administrative Procedures Act, the National Health Insurance Act, the Occupational Safety and Health Act and their subordinate regulations.

This is general information about the structure of a system under the law of the Republic of Korea, not legal advice. The outcome of an individual case turns on its specific facts and medical opinions, so actual handling should be confirmed with a certified labour attorney, a lawyer, or counselling from the Korea Workers Compensation and Welfare Service. Nothing here predicts the approval or the success of any particular case.

The day the notice arrives, a clock starts

Article 26 of the Administrative Procedures Act requires an administrative agency, when making a disposition, to inform the party whether an administrative appeal and administrative litigation may be filed, whether any other objection is available, the procedure and the period for filing, and any other necessary matters. That is why a denial notice from the Korea Workers Compensation and Welfare Service carries the appeal route and the time limit on its face. Not reading that part, and putting the notice in a drawer, is the most common loss in practice.

The clock does not run from the date of the decision. It runs from the day the claimant becomes aware that the decision has been made. If it came by registered mail there is a delivery record, and that date becomes the reference point for every calculation that follows. So there are only three things to do on the day the notice arrives.

  • Keep the envelope and the delivery receipt together, and do not throw them away. If the timeliness of the filing is ever disputed, this is the first thing produced
  • Copy out the stated reasons for the disposition and the appeal notice wording exactly as written
  • Mark the ninetieth day on a calendar, but do not treat that as the deadline. Set a separate readiness date well before it

Ninety days looks generous. It is not. Obtaining copies of medical records, supplementing the treating physician's opinion and securing workplace records each take weeks, and treatment and money problems run in parallel through all of it. What actually causes the failure is not the length of the period but noticing too late that it has been running.

The three stages are not the same procedure repeated

Summarising this as three chances to be looked at again misses the structure. The three stages differ in who decides, in the nature of the decision, and in the governing statute.

First, the review request. Article 103(1) of the Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance Act provides that a person dissatisfied with a benefit decision of the Service may file a review request with the Service. This is self-reconsideration by the agency that made the disposition. Under Article 103(2) the request is filed through the branch that made the decision, and under Article 103(4) that branch must forward it to the Service within five days with an opinion attached. Deliberation goes through the Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance Review Committee established under Article 104. Because this procedure stands in place of the ordinary administrative appeal, Article 103(5) states flatly that an administrative appeal under the Administrative Appeals Act may not be filed against a benefit decision.

Second, the reexamination request. Article 106(1) provides that a person dissatisfied with the decision on a review request may file a reexamination request with the Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance Reexamination Committee established under Article 107. That committee sits under the Ministry of Employment and Labor and consists of up to 90 members including one chairperson, of whom two are standing members and one is an ex officio member. Two fifths of the members are nominated in equal numbers by worker organisations and employer organisations. Members serve three-year terms and may be reappointed. Decisively, Article 109(2) provides that a ruling of the Reexamination Committee binds the Service. It is a quasi-judicial determination made in a body separated from the deciding agency.

Third, administrative litigation. From here it is a court. What is at issue is not the soundness of the disposition but its legality, and the hearing follows litigation procedure. Article 111(2) provides that a ruling on a reexamination request is treated as a ruling on an administrative appeal for the purposes of Article 18 of the Administrative Litigation Act, which is the hinge connecting the earlier stages to the courtroom.

The committee's actual workflow runs as follows: the request is docketed, the file is examined, the panel for the hearing is constituted, the hearing date is notified, the hearing and ruling take place, and the written ruling is served. Separate prescribed forms exist beyond the request itself, including an application for oral hearing, an application for evidence examination and an application to close the hearing to the public. In other words, if a chance to explain in person is needed, that application has to be made separately.

The deadlines are everything

What collapses most often in an appeal is not the argument but the date. A request filed out of time is dismissed without examination of its substance. It makes no difference how complete the evidence is. The numbers written into the statutes are these.

  • Review request — IACI Act art. 103(3). Within 90 days from the day the claimant becomes aware that the benefit decision was made
  • Reexamination request — art. 106(3). Within 90 days from awareness of the decision on the review request. Where the review stage is skipped, within 90 days from awareness of the benefit decision itself
  • Administrative litigation — Administrative Litigation Act art. 20(1). Within 90 days from awareness of the disposition. Where an administrative appeal was filed, this period runs from the day the original of the written ruling is served
  • The second litigation clock — art. 20(2). No suit may be filed after one year from the date the disposition was made, or from the date of the ruling where one was issued, unless there is justifiable cause
  • An unalterable period — art. 20(3). The 90 days in paragraph 1 is an unalterable period. It does not stretch for the party's circumstances

There are deadlines on the deciding side too. Article 105(1) requires the Service to decide a review request within 60 days of receiving it, after deliberation by the Review Committee, extendable once only, by no more than 20 days, where there is unavoidable cause. Paragraph 3 requires notice of any extension to the claimant at least seven days before the original decision period ends. Article 109(1) applies Article 105(1) and paragraphs 3 through 5 to reexamination proceedings, so the same 60 days and 20 days apply to the committee's ruling.

One thing to add. Filing a review or reexamination request does not suspend the effect of the original disposition. A request does not stay execution. Reading the start of an appeal as putting the situation on hold throws off the judgements in the next section.

Can the review request be skipped

The statutes answer this, and the answer splits in two directions.

Going straight to reexamination. The proviso to Article 106(1) allows a person contesting a benefit decision that went through deliberation by the Occupational Disease Adjudication Committee to file a reexamination request without a review request under Article 103. That committee is established under Article 38(1) within a branch of the Service to deliberate on whether a disease under Article 37(1)2 is occupational. So an occupational disease case that passed through the adjudication committee may skip a stage. The deadline is then 90 days from awareness of the benefit decision, under Article 106(3).

Going straight to court. The main text of Article 18(1) of the Administrative Litigation Act provides that a revocation suit may be filed without going through an administrative appeal even where the law allows one to be filed. The proviso to that paragraph carves out cases where another statute requires a ruling before suit, and the Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance Act contains no such mandatory-exhaustion provision. Industrial accident cases can therefore reach court without either earlier stage.

Still, being permitted to skip and being better off skipping are different questions. Taking the earlier stages brings two things. One is time: the proviso to Article 20(1) of the Administrative Litigation Act runs the litigation period from service of the written ruling where an administrative appeal was filed, so the litigation clock is not consumed while the earlier stages proceed. The other is the record: the reasoning the Service and the committee commit to writing becomes the material contested in court. Against that, every month spent in the earlier stages is a month of unpaid medical costs and lost income. The choice turns on the nature of the case and on personal circumstances, and it is not settled by reading the statute alone.

Read the statement of reasons first, and read it precisely

Starting to draft the appeal brief first is the most common ordering mistake. The first task is to identify exactly what the disposition relied on. Denials fall into a handful of categories, and each one calls for something entirely different.

  • Work-relatedness itself was not accepted — proximate causation is the issue. The nature and degree of exposure or strain has to be shown through records
  • The condition named differs from the claim, or the diagnosis is unsettled — the medical diagnosis has to be resolved first. No amount of causation argument moves a case where the condition is not identified
  • A pre-existing condition or natural progression was found — the issue becomes whether the work contributed beyond that natural course
  • Coverage was the problem — worker status, whether the workplace is covered, and the employment relationship at the time of the accident are at stake
  • Partial approval — establish first what was cut. Only some of the claimed conditions approved, only part of the treatment period recognised, or a disability grade lower than claimed are three different disputes

The statement of reasons usually summarises the opinion the decision rested on. If it says whether that was an advisory physician's opinion or the adjudication committee's deliberation, and what materials it assumed, start by checking whether those assumptions match the facts. Where the premise is wrong, attacking the premise is faster than attacking the conclusion. This is where it surfaces that the employment period was recorded incorrectly, that the assigned process was described differently from reality, or that part of the submitted material never entered the review.

If access to the underlying material is needed, inspection or copies can be requested from the Service. How far that goes depends on the nature of each document, so confirming the scope through counselling before filing the request saves time.

What is actually contested

Article 37(1) of the Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance Act provides that where a worker suffers injury, disease or disability, or dies, for a reason falling under an occupational accident, an occupational disease or a commuting accident, it is regarded as an occupational injury, and then adds in a proviso that this does not apply where there is no proximate causal relationship between the work and the injury. That proviso is why denials cluster around causation.

In practice the requirement is examined along two axes. One is whether the event occurred in the course of work performed under the employer's direction and control. The other is whether the work caused it. The first is called performance of duty, the second attribution to duty. Neither phrase appears in the statute as such; they are an analytical frame for splitting the single requirement of proximate causation into two questions. In accident cases the first axis settles fairly easily and the second remains; in disease cases both blur at once.

  • In the course of work — Article 37(1)1 lists work under the employment contract and acts incidental to it, use of facilities provided by the employer, events organised or directed by the employer, and acts during rest periods that can be regarded as under the employer's direction and control
  • Whether work was the cause — the substances and duration of exposure, working posture and repetition, working hours and night work, and whether that burden reached the level of causing onset
  • Specific recognition criteria — under the delegation in Article 37(5), Article 34(3) of the Enforcement Decree and its Table 3 set out the specific criteria for occupational diseases
  • Individual circumstances — Article 34(4) of the Enforcement Decree requires the worker's sex, age, health condition and constitution to be considered when deciding whether a disease or a death from it is occupational

One misreading is worth clearing up. Failing to meet the criteria in Table 3 does not by itself mean the injury is not occupational. Those criteria are the concrete yardstick for deciding recognition; the ultimate requirement remains the proximate causation in the proviso to Article 37(1). Conversely, formally satisfying a criterion does not make causation automatic. The criteria are a threshold for judgement, not the judgement.

Where the burden of proof sits

The Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance Act contains no provision squarely allocating the burden of proof. But because Article 37(1) sets proximate causation as a requirement, the structure leaves it to the side seeking the benefit to show that the requirement is met. That is why the claimant has to build the brief and the file in an appeal.

This is not to say the agencies lack investigative powers. Article 105(4) allows the Service, during the hearing, to summon the claimant or interested parties for questioning or statements, to require the production of evidence, to commission expert appraisal, to have its officials inspect the site, and to request a diagnosis from a medical institution. Article 109(1) applies this to the reexamination stage. But these are investigative tools, not a transfer of the burden. Even where an investigation happens, it is the claimant's brief that points it at something.

An appeal brief is therefore less an assertion than a response. For each ground the statement of reasons relies on, it says which record shows that ground to be factually wrong, or, if the ground is factually right, why the conclusion should still differ, with the material paired to the point. Sentences that appeal to feeling or hardship add length without adding anything the decision-maker can weigh.

The evidence that actually has to be gathered

More evidence is not better evidence. What is better is evidence that answers precisely the point the statement of reasons raised. That said, the same few axes are required in most cases.

There is a line to hold when handling evidence. Do not build records out of memory. A witness statement should separate what was seen from what was heard, and leave uncertain parts marked uncertain. Where attendance records do not exist, say they do not exist and explain what stands in their place. As the later section sets out, presenting material differently from the facts is separately penalised by the system and destroys the credibility of the case itself.

  • Obtain the full copy of the medical record, not a summary. The line a summary omits is sometimes the line that fixes the date of onset
  • Workplace records are easier to obtain before leaving employment. Afterwards the issuing procedure lengthens
  • Failure to obtain the employer's confirmation does not block a claim or an appeal. Proceed without it and state the circumstance
  • A colleague's statement carries weight only when it comes from someone who actually saw the same process. Hearsay should be identified as hearsay
  • Do not submit everything at once; keep an index. What was submitted when becomes an issue again later

Why disease cases are the hard ones

An accident case has a point in time. There is an hour, a place and an activity, and the evidence gathers around that point. A disease case has no such point. When it began is blurred, the causes are never singular, and age, constitution and lifestyle sit alongside the work throughout. That is why the relationship with pre-existing conditions is at issue in nearly every disease case.

Musculoskeletal diseases

Table 3, item 2 of the Enforcement Decree governs these. Work involving frequent repetitive motion, work requiring excessive force, work maintaining awkward postures, vibration work and other work that strains a specific body part are treated as physically demanding work, and the relationship between a history of such work and the disease is examined. What gets contested is usually the boundary with natural degenerative change accompanying age. Item 2 separates the case where an existing disease was aggravated by work, the case where natural progression advanced faster than it otherwise would, and the case where onset followed a sudden application of force during work. Duration of the work history, intensity and frequency, and whether the work was intermittent are examined together.

Cerebrovascular and cardiac diseases

Table 3, item 1 governs these. Intracerebral haemorrhage, subarachnoid haemorrhage, cerebral infarction, myocardial infarction and dissecting aortic aneurysm are the listed conditions, and the burden is divided three ways: a sudden and unforeseeable work-related event or an abrupt change in the working environment within 24 hours before onset; an increase of 30 percent or more in the volume or hours of work in the week before onset compared with the weekly average of the preceding 12 weeks; and objectively confirmed heavy physical or mental burden continuing for three months or more before onset.

The working-hour thresholds used for chronic burden are as follows.

  • Where working hours over the 12 weeks before onset averaged more than 60 hours a week (or more than 64 hours a week over the 4 weeks before onset), the relationship between work and disease is assessed as strong
  • Where the average over the 12 weeks before onset exceeded 52 hours a week, the relationship is assessed as increasing with longer hours
  • Night work is treated as heavier than day work, and 30 percent is added to day-shift hours when calculating working time
  • Even below 52 hours, compound exposure to aggravating factors raises the assessed relationship. The listed examples include work with unpredictable schedules, shift work, work with insufficient rest days, work exposed to a harmful environment, physically intense work, frequent business travel across time zones, and work under heavy mental tension

Occupational cancers

Table 3, item 10 governs these. Here the substance, the duration of exposure and the latency period all enter the requirements together. Lung cancer and laryngeal cancer arising from ten years or more of asbestos exposure, malignant mesothelioma arising ten years or more after occupational asbestos exposure, and ovarian cancer arising from ten years or more of asbestos exposure are paired in that way. On the benzene side, multiple myeloma and non-Hodgkin lymphoma arising ten years or more after exposure to concentrations of 0.5 ppm or above are listed, with a route left open for exposure shorter than ten years where the cumulative exposure thresholds are met.

The hardest obstacle in this category is not medicine but records. Former workplaces have closed, work environment measurement data no longer exists, or the composition of handled substances cannot be confirmed. That is why the epidemiological investigation covered in the next section often sits at the centre of occupational cancer cases.

Epidemiological investigation and advisory physicians

What an appeal contests is not a single sheet of decision but the medical and investigative material that went into it. Knowing where that material is produced is what tells you where to aim.

Epidemiological investigation. Article 141(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act allows the Minister of Employment and Labor, where it is deemed necessary for the diagnosis and prevention of occupational diseases and for identifying their causes, to conduct an epidemiological investigation into the correlation between a worker's illness and harmful factors in the workplace. Paragraph 2 requires employers and workers to cooperate actively and prohibits refusal, obstruction or evasion without justifiable cause. In cases where the exposure history left no records, this investigation is sometimes the only means of reconstruction. On the other hand it takes time, and the case does not move while it runs.

Advisory physicians and the advisory physicians' conference. Article 43 of the Enforcement Decree of the Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance Act requires the Service to establish an advisory physicians' conference within its branches to obtain systematic advice on matters requiring medical judgement in benefit, treatment cost and pharmaceutical cost decisions, composed of five or more advisory physicians. Its deliberation covers whether treatment of a worker under care should be concluded, and that item expressly applies only where the treating physician and the advisory physician differ in medical opinion.

The Occupational Disease Adjudication Committee. Under Article 38(1) it is established within a branch of the Service to deliberate on whether a disease under Article 37(1)2 is occupational. The diseases excluded from its deliberation, the deliberation procedure, and its composition and operation are prescribed by Ministry of Employment and Labor ordinance under paragraphs 2 and 3. As noted above, a decision that went through this committee is the one that may skip the review stage.

In short, the place where the treating physician's opinion diverges from the advisory physician's, the place where the reading of the epidemiological findings diverges, and the place where the work content the adjudication committee assumed differs from reality — an appeal brief usually has to aim at one of those three.

Cost and representation

Who may act as representative is set by statute. Article 111(3) of the Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance Act provides that matters not prescribed by that Act in relation to review and reexamination requests are governed by the Administrative Appeals Act, and Article 18(1) of that Act lists those a claimant may appoint as representative besides a statutory agent.

  • The claimant's spouse, or a blood relative within the fourth degree of the claimant or the spouse
  • Where the claimant is a corporation, or an unincorporated association or foundation with capacity to file, its officers and employees
  • An attorney at law
  • A person authorised by another statute to represent parties in appeals — this is the entry point for certified labour attorneys
  • Any other person permitted by the committee

The litigation stage is different in kind. From there it becomes a question of representation in court, and in practice that means retaining a lawyer. There is no statutory fee scale and rates differ by firm. What to confirm before signing is scope rather than the number itself: how far the retainer reaches, whether moving from review to reexamination is a separate engagement, what the success fee is measured against, and who bears out-of-pocket costs such as appraisal fees and record issuance charges. Put it in writing.

There are also channels available before any money is spent.

  • Korea Workers Compensation and Welfare Service counselling — the main line is 1588-0075, open weekdays from 9 a.m. to 6 p.m. Menu option 2 covers compensation, medical care and rehabilitation. Online enquiries are answered within three days for general matters and five days for legal matters
  • Korea Legal Aid Corporation telephone consultation — dial 132 without an area code. Weekdays 9:00 to 11:50 and 13:00 to 17:50, with call charges borne by the caller. The corporation notes that telephone consultation suits simple matters and that the content should be treated as reference material
  • Reexamination Committee enquiries — the Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance Reexamination Committee is at 82 Beopwon-ro, Sejong Special Self-Governing City, main line 044-202-8499. The request form and the oral hearing application can be downloaded from its website

Making a living while the appeal runs

While a claim stands denied, medical care benefits and temporary disability benefits are not paid. Treatment does not stop for that, and neither do living costs. Within what can be verified, the available routes are these.

Continuing treatment under health insurance. Article 53(1)4 of the National Health Insurance Act provides that benefits are not paid where a disease, injury or accident arising from work or public duty is compensated under another statute. Read the other way, where industrial accident benefits are in fact not payable, room opens to consider health insurance coverage. Article 90 of the Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance Act sets out the settlement between the agencies. Paragraph 1 allows the Service, on receiving a claim for health insurance medical care costs from the National Health Insurance Service, to pay the corresponding amount where those costs are recognised as equivalent to medical care benefits payable under the Act. Paragraph 2 allows the Service, where its medical care benefit decision is revoked, to claim the corresponding amount from the National Health Insurance Service where the benefit is recognised as equivalent to health insurance medical care. A later change in approval status is reconciled between the agencies. When and by what procedure this applies in a specific case varies, so it is better confirmed with the hospital's administration desk and with the Service.

Injury and disease compensation annuity. Article 66(1) of the Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance Act provides that where a worker receiving medical care benefits remains, after two years have passed since medical care began, in a state meeting all three requirements, the injury and disease compensation annuity is paid in place of temporary disability benefits. The requirements are that the injury or disease has not been cured, that the degree of the severe condition falls within the grades prescribed by Presidential Decree, and that the worker has been unable to work because of the medical care. This is a scheme for cases already receiving medical care benefits, so it does not apply directly to a denied claim. It belongs alongside cases where the medical care relationship continues, such as a partial approval or a refusal to extend treatment.

The limitation period pauses while the appeal runs. Article 111(1) provides that filing a review request or a reexamination request is treated as a judicial claim under Article 168 of the Civil Act for the purposes of interrupting prescription. The limitation on the benefit right itself is three years in principle under Article 112(1), and five years for the right to receive disability benefits, survivors benefits, funeral expenses, pneumoconiosis compensation annuity and pneumoconiosis survivors annuity. Knowing that the clock is not simply running out during the appeal is worth having.

Where things usually go wrong

The same mistakes recur. Most of them are about dates and sequence rather than logic.

  • Fixing the wrong receipt date — the period runs from awareness of the decision, not from the date it was made. Where a family member accepted delivery or the notice sat in a mailbox, which day counts becomes an issue in itself. Keep the delivery record
  • Miscounting the last day — whether the first day is included, and what happens when the last day falls on a holiday, follow the general rules on computing periods. Planning to file on the deadline is itself the risk
  • Thinking litigation has one clock — Article 20 of the Administrative Litigation Act sets both the 90 days of paragraph 1 and the one year of paragraph 2. Whichever expires first is the real deadline
  • Counting from the disposition after a ruling was served — under the proviso to paragraph 1, where an administrative appeal was filed the period runs from service of the original of the written ruling
  • Expecting the period to be extended — paragraph 3 makes the paragraph 1 period unalterable
  • Looking for a representative just before the deadline — appoint late and there is no time left to gather evidence. Half of what a representative does is not drafting but securing records
  • Submitting evidence just before the hearing — at the reexamination stage the hearing date is notified and the ruling is made there. Material arriving after the date is set has no time to be read
  • Treating the form of the request lightly — a request that fails the formal requirements goes through correction, and is dismissed if the correction period lapses or correction is impossible
  • Assuming a filing stops the disposition — filing a review request does not suspend execution of the original decision
  • Forgetting the limitation on the benefit right — independently of any appeal, the three or five years under Article 112 continue to run
  • Reading a partial approval as an approval — approval of only some conditions, a truncated treatment period or a lower disability grade are all appealable, and the same clocks apply

What fraudulent claiming leaves behind

As an appeal drags on, the temptation arises to present the file a little more favourably. The system has priced that temptation separately, and both the amount and the penalty are heavy.

  • Article 84(1) of the Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance Act requires collection of an amount equal to twice the benefit paid where the benefit was obtained by fraud or other wrongful means
  • Article 127(3)1 punishes a person who obtained insurance benefits by fraud or other wrongful means with imprisonment of up to two years or a fine of up to 20 million won
  • Subparagraph 2 of the same paragraph applies the same penalty to a person who caused or assisted such conduct. A colleague who wrote a statement or someone who prepared a document is caught with it
  • Article 84(3) likewise provides for double collection where a medical institution or pharmacy received treatment or pharmaceutical costs by fraud or wrongful means

What matters more in practice is what follows. Once one document is shown to differ from the facts, the credibility of the remaining documents falls with it. A single sentence in a witness statement that contradicts an attendance record pushes the whole statement out of the assessment. Where recollection is unclear, it is better to write that it is unclear; where a record does not exist, it is better to say so. What the appeal stages examine is not how impressive the file looks but whether the records and the assertions agree.

Stated again. This is general information based on the law of the Republic of Korea and is not legal advice on any particular case. Deadline computation, the scope of evidence and the choice of procedure differ case by case, so an individual matter should be confirmed with a certified labour attorney, a lawyer, or counselling from the Korea Workers Compensation and Welfare Service. Nothing here predicts or guarantees approval or success in any case.
