Introduction: The Paradox of "Redis Is Faster, So Why MySQL?"

Almost every developer carries one proposition like muscle memory: "Redis is fast, relational databases are slow." As an in-memory key-value store, Redis generally responds faster than a disk-bound database, so the moment traffic spikes, we reach for Redis almost reflexively. And yet Shopify, which runs one of the largest e-commerce infrastructures on the planet, moved in the opposite direction. According to "We replaced Redis with MySQL for inventory reservations—and it scaled" (Emilie Noel), published on the Shopify Engineering blog on May 12, 2026, Shopify moved its inventory reservations, the short hold placed on stock while payment is processed, from Redis to MySQL.

First, the scope needs to be stated precisely. What moved was not the whole inventory system but the reservation step at checkout. The inventory ledger, the record of stock quantities, was already in MySQL, and the source frames the problem as "reservations and the inventory ledger lived in two different systems." Even so, many developers asked the same question: "Why go toward the slower option when you have the faster one?" Buried inside that question is the assumption that "fast equals good." The short version: Shopify's choice was not a regression but a redefinition of roles.

Inventory is a peculiar kind of data. A search result appearing 0.1 seconds late and the last unit in someone's cart being sold to two people at the moment of checkout are problems of entirely different magnitudes. The former annoys a user and ends there; the latter cascades into cancellations, apologies, and a loss of customer trust. This article separates what the Shopify source actually states from this article's own interpretation, and works through the essential difference between a cache and a database and what it means for the e-commerce world.

The one-line summary. Shopify's decision is not "Redis is bad" but the judgment that "reservations and the inventory ledger must live inside one transaction." In the source's words, the core issue was that updates spanning the two stores "couldn't be wrapped in a single atomic step," and throughput was secured not by a cache but by a design built on MySQL 8's SKIP LOCKED plus a cleanup of database connection usage.

1. The Scale of Shopify's Inventory Reservations - Why This Decision Matters

This decision is interesting because of Shopify's scale. According to the source, Shopify "powers over 14% of U.S. ecommerce," and on Black Friday 2025 merchants on the platform hit a record $5.1 million in sales per minute at peak. Peak sales per minute rose 11% over the prior year. The source notes that "every one of those transactions touches inventory," and reservations run on every checkout that touches inventory.

Failure runs in both directions. In the source's example, getting it wrong one way means two buyers purchase the same last unit, and the merchant has to cancel an order, send an apology email, and eat the support cost. Getting it wrong the other way means telling a buyer something is sold out when it isn't, and the merchant loses a sale they should have made. At this scale, the source says, "either failure compounds fast." So changing the data model for reservations at this scale is not a matter of technical taste; it is re-laying the foundation that supports checkout reliability.

That is exactly why we should pay attention to this case. A company that has lived through massive traffic pulled a fast tool that had worked well for years out of the seat reserved for core reservations. There is a lesson here about "something more important than speed."

2. Why Redis Was Used for Inventory Reservations

To be fair, there is something to acknowledge first. According to the source, this reservation feature ran on Redis for years, and the source states that "Redis handled concurrency fine." In fact, earlier attempts to move to MySQL had failed, because a single row with a quantity column could not handle the contention. Redis was by no means the wrong tool.

2.1 Overwhelming Speed

Redis keeps data in memory, so it never touches disk I/O. Both reads and writes respond with very low latency, and for an operation as frequent as decrementing inventory, that speed is genuinely attractive. This is a general point; the source does not cite any latency figures for Redis.

2.2 Atomic Operations

Redis ships with atomic counter operations like DECR and INCR. According to the source, Shopify's old model also kept a quantity key for each item, with reserving done by DECR and releasing by INCR. Reducing a stock count by one can be handled with a single command, making concurrency control intuitive.

2.3 A Simple Data Model

"Item to remaining quantity" is essentially a key-value structure, which maps almost perfectly onto Redis. No schema design, joins, or index agonizing; it just works. Early on, that simplicity likely boosted development velocity (this article's guess).

In short, from the view of "inventory equals a number that goes up and down quickly," Redis was nearly an ideal tool. The problem was that inventory reservations are not only that.

3. The Limits of the Redis Model - Three From the Source, Two General Ones

The source gives three reasons directly; they are 3.1 to 3.3 below. Sections 3.4 and 3.5 are not mentioned in the source; they are general limits often raised when an in-memory store is used like a ledger, and they are this article's interpretation. None of them is a defect in Redis; they are the structural friction of using a tool built for a different role.

3.1 The Atomicity Gap Between Two Systems (the source's core reason)

Reservations lived in Redis, and the inventory ledger lived in MySQL. The claim step, which permanently deducts inventory once payment is processed, required updating MySQL and cleaning up Redis, and the source says those two operations "couldn't be wrapped in a single atomic step." Depending on the order, this could cause overselling (an item sold but never deducted from the ledger) or underselling (an item deducted and still marked reserved). It was the problem of not being able to make several steps succeed or fail as one bundle, playing out across two stores.

3.2 No Multi-Location Awareness (from the source)

According to the source, the Redis model had no awareness of multiple locations (warehouses and stores). Shopify's requirements included "only reserve from locations that can fulfill." A single quantity key per item makes that condition hard to express.

3.3 The Operational Cost of a Separate Cluster (from the source)

The source says the Redis model "added the operational cost of a separate cluster to maintain." It introduces the switch as a question that came up while moving toward a unified database strategy: could MySQL handle the same scale? The rising memory cost of keeping all data in RAM as data grows is often raised in general, but the source does not give memory cost as a reason.

3.4 Durability and Replication Lag (general - not in the source)

Redis is an in-memory store; you can reinforce persistence with RDB snapshots or AOF logs, but depending on configuration a window remains in which writes after the last save can be lost. Replication is asynchronous by default, so writes not yet replicated can vanish at the moment of failover. For data like inventory, where a single discrepancy leads to an oversell, that is a burdensome property. However, the Shopify source does not cite data loss or AOF and replication issues as reasons for the move.

3.5 The Absence of Complex Queries (general - not in the source)

Inventory is not just a number. You constantly need queries like "products below threshold in a specific warehouse" or "stock change history over the past 24 hours." Because Redis is key-value, it does not natively support these joins, aggregations, and range queries well, so the gap gets patched in application code. This too is a general point, not a reason given in the source.

4. Why MySQL - The Design Shopify Chose and the Strengths of a Relational Database

The gist of the source is this: moving reservations into the same MySQL database as the ledger meant everything could be wrapped in ACID transactions to "eliminate these failure modes entirely." What MySQL (with the InnoDB engine) offers is, ultimately, "the ability to handle data so it is not wrong."

4.1 Durability - If It Commits, It Survives (general)

The D in ACID is the promise that once a transaction commits, it is preserved even if the server dies. InnoDB's redo log is written to disk first, so a committed decrement can be recovered after a crash. This is the line separating the cache's "data you can afford to lose" from the database's "data you must not lose." The source does not discuss durability settings.

4.2 One Row per Unit and SKIP LOCKED - A Design That Reduces Contention (from the source)

Instead of one row per item with a quantity column, Shopify uses one row per sellable unit. An item with 10 units has 10 rows, and reserving three units means selecting and moving three rows in a single transaction. With SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED, rows locked by another transaction are skipped and other available rows are returned, so no one waits on the same row and contention drops.

Turning all inventory into rows would make the table grow (the source's example: 50,000 units across 10 locations would mean 500,000 rows), so Shopify maintains a bounded pool capped at 1,000 rows per item/location combination, and a replenishment process refills it from the ledger. The figure of 1,000 was sized from observed peak reservation rates per item/location during flash sales. If the pool empties, the reserve path triggers replenishment inline, and a lock ensures only one transaction replenishes at a time, preventing a thundering herd. The source lists four technical decisions.

  • Composite primary key: The first prototype used an auto-increment ID as the primary key and took two row locks per reservation. Switching the primary key to (shop_id, inventory_item_id, inventory_group_id, id) reduced that to one lock per row.
  • READ COMMITTED: Running SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED on an empty table that needed replenishment produced gap locks (including on the supremum pseudo-record) that blocked replenishment and could lead to deadlocks. The isolation level for these transactions was changed from REPEATABLE READ, the MySQL default, to READ COMMITTED.
  • Consistent lock ordering: Reserve always DELETEs from the units table first, then INSERTs into reserved_quantities; claim only touches reserved_quantities. That removed the circular waits.
  • Batching with UNION ALL: For carts with multiple line items, reservation queries are batched with UNION ALL so all needed units are fetched in one round trip.

4.3 Atomicity - All or Nothing

According to the source, keeping reservations and the ledger in the same database gave ACID across reserve and claim, fixing classes of bugs that were possible with Redis, such as payment succeeding while inventory is not claimed, or the reverse. Inside one transaction, if any single step fails, the whole thing rolls back, so the ghost state of "stock dropped but never claimed" cannot occur by construction.

4.4 Rich Queries and Integrity Constraints (general)

SQL expresses joins, aggregations, and range conditions freely. With foreign keys, CHECK constraints, and unique constraints, the database itself can enforce rules like "stock can never go negative," so business rules need not be left to the application alone. This is a general property of relational databases, not a strength the source cites.

5. Five Technical Challenges of the Migration - How Shopify Handled Them

Deciding that moving is correct and actually moving are entirely different problems. Swapping the core store of a giant live system is closer to changing an engine mid-flight. Below is how the source describes it.

  • Zero-downtime: According to the source, Shopify did not flip a switch; it ran both systems in parallel in "shadow mode." Every reservation was written to both Redis and MySQL, with Redis remaining the source of truth during that period.
  • Data consistency: Because both systems were live, there were no in-flight reservations to migrate. Redis reservations continued to be honored while MySQL built up its own state. It is worth noting that this was not a snapshot-and-copy migration.
  • Performance guarantees: In production, throughput hit a ceiling well below the target, even though reservation latency (such as P90) was acceptable and CPU was not maxed out. The real bottleneck was database connections. By tagging every SQL statement with the business process (/* conn_tag:checkout_completion */) and measuring per-caller connection hold time at the ProxySQL layer, the team found other checkout-path code, not reservations, holding connections for too long. Cleaning up the checkout path removed 50% of reads and 33% of transactions on the primary database, and an InnoDB thread concurrency setting chosen conservatively years earlier was raised where there was headroom. During high-volume flash sales, writer CPU stayed under 50% and reader CPU under 16%.
  • A rollback safety net: Once correctness and performance were confirmed, the source of truth was switched to MySQL, and if anything went wrong the team could revert to Redis with a kill switch. That worked because the dual-write path was still active, so Redis had a complete view of reservations at all times.
  • Validation: In shadow mode, the two systems were compared side by side on real production traffic to confirm that MySQL produced correct business outcomes and met performance requirements. The rollout was gradual, pod by pod, starting with low-traffic pods and working up to the highest-volume merchants.

6. The Performance Trade-off - Speed vs Accuracy

This case is often read as "gave up speed to buy accuracy," but the source says something slightly different. Shopify writes that after moving to MySQL it hit its high-throughput targets during peak 2025 traffic. That an in-memory store is often faster on a single response is a general point, but for inventory reservations the real question is not "how fast" but "how rarely wrong."

The source puts it this way: "this wasn't about making reservations fast. It was about making them safe neighbors." Reservations share a database with cart updates, payment processing, and order creation, so a system that saturates connections or holds locks too long jeopardizes all of them. The bar was sustaining throughput without degrading database health for everything else.

From here on is this article's interpretation. In typical large-scale services, a layered structure in which the database handles accuracy-critical writes and a cache handles frequent reads (showing stock) is common. However, the Shopify source does not say it placed a Redis cache in front of MySQL; instead it closes with "If you're reaching for Redis, Kafka, or a custom coordination layer for high-throughput mutual exclusion, your existing database might already be enough."

In inventory, a response one millisecond faster is a bragging point, but a single oversell is an incident. When accuracy breaks, speed loses its meaning.

So the balance point in this case was to secure accuracy first and reclaim speed through surrounding work such as row design, locking, and connection usage. It is not a losing trade at all.

The moment you see the two tools as a division of labor rather than a competition, the design becomes clear. But as the Shopify case shows, if the source of truth and the operations that change it are scattered across different stores, bringing them into one place comes first.

8. The Other Case in the Source and the General Pattern - Separating Cache and Persistent Store

Only one other company appears in the source. Shopify says it rebuilt reservations on MySQL "inspired by 37signals' approach to database-backed load distribution." No other company cases appear in the source; what follows is the widely used general layering pattern, such as cache-aside or write-through.

The typical setup looks like this. The truth of the data lives in the relational database, and a Redis/Memcached cache sits in front of it to reduce read load. Writes hit the database first, then invalidate or update the cache. In this pattern, even if the entire cache is wiped out, the original data remains in the database; things just get briefly slower while the cache refills.

"A fast store" and "a trustworthy store" are different roles, and mature architectures do not dump both onto one tool. Shopify's inventory reservation migration reads as a case of applying this principle to the most sensitive domain (this article's interpretation). Note, though, that Shopify applied it not by adding a cache but by consolidating reservations and the ledger, which had been split across two stores, into one database.

9. Five Lessons for Developers

Here are the practical lessons this article draws from the case. Lessons 3 and 4 are ones the source states directly.

  1. "Fast" is not a selection criterion; it is just one property. The nature of the data (can it be volatile, must it be correct) decides the tool first.
  2. Keep the source of truth and the operations that change it inside one transaction boundary. When reservations and the ledger are split across two systems, a gap appears where only one side gets updated. A cache is a copy, not the original.
  3. The bottleneck may not be where you expect. According to the source, the team optimized queries and locks for weeks, but the real limit was connection usage in code they were not even looking at. If CPU is low but queuing is high, instrument the full path.
  4. Revisit old decisions. The source says what wasn't possible five years ago (MySQL for this workload) can become possible with new features like SKIP LOCKED, and that rule-of-thumb settings such as thread limits are worth re-checking when the workload changes.
  5. Migration is about validation more than technology. Shopify moved the source of truth only after it had dual-write in shadow mode, side-by-side comparison, a kill-switch rollback, and a pod-by-pod gradual rollout.

10. Applying This to E-Commerce - Implications for High-Concurrency Markets

This discussion does not stop at a single company. Many e-commerce markets see frequent burst traffic from live commerce, time deals, and flash sales. Many people targeting the last unit of the same hot product at once can easily happen on large shopping platforms. What follows is this article's judgment.

In such an environment, splitting reservations and the ledger across different stores turns the same atomicity gap Shopify faced into oversell risk. The implications for e-commerce infrastructure are as follows.

  • Flash sales = extreme concurrency: Writes flood the same row in an instant. Serializing with database locking strategies and queuing (order waiting lines), or spreading contention with per-unit rows and SKIP LOCKED as Shopify did, is key.
  • Accurate display is not accurate decrement: Stock shown on screen should be fast via cache; the actual decrement must be accurate via a database transaction. You must recognize these two as separate.
  • Refund and correction costs are hard to dismiss: With high expectations for fast support and sensitivity to platform reputation, the intangible cost of a single oversell can be large. There is ample justification to invest in accuracy.
  • Tools for small sellers follow the same principle: If you build your own shop, resist the temptation to manage inventory with a Redis counter alone, and make the database the source of truth from the start.

11. The Risk - The Illusion That "Faster Is Always Better"

The bigger risk this case exposes is the psychology of technology choices. We are easily swayed by benchmark numbers, sentences like "X is N times faster than Y." But benchmarks usually do not measure the condition "this data must never be wrong."

Speed-first thinking is dangerous because its bill arrives later, and at the most inconvenient moment. Inconsistency erupts on the busiest sale day, the instant a node dies in a failure, in the split second the updates to two stores fall out of step. It looks fine most of the time and then collapses exactly when you can least afford it.

Before being seduced by "fast," the question to ask is this: if this data disappears or is wrong, who loses what, and how much?

Mature engineering is not about flaunting fast tools, but about honestly weighing the failure cost of each piece of data and placing tools accordingly. Shopify's choice reads as the result of exactly that honest calculation.

12. Conclusion + Three Things You Can Apply Today

Shopify's decision to move inventory reservations from Redis to MySQL is not a story of "Redis was wrong." It was the judgment that "reservations and the inventory ledger must live inside one transaction." And the source does not say it gave up throughput for accuracy; it says per-unit rows, the SKIP LOCKED design, and a cleanup of connection usage on the checkout path let it meet its throughput targets. Accuracy was a non-negotiable constant, and speed was a variable that design could win back.

To close, here are three things you can apply today.

  1. Classify your data by failure cost. What can be lost goes to the cache (Redis); what must never be wrong goes to the database (MySQL/PostgreSQL). This one line settles most storage decisions.
  2. Make a transactional database the source of truth for inventory, payments, and balances, and put the operations that change it in the same transaction. If you need speed, check row design, locking, and connection usage first, and consider a cache after that.
  3. When moving a store, design shadow-mode dual-write, side-by-side comparison, and kill-switch rollback first. Remember that the success of a migration is decided by the validation system, not the new technology.

The next time "Redis is faster, so why?" comes to mind, go one level deeper. The real question is not speed but "what collapses if this data is wrong?"

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. Why did Shopify move inventory reservations from Redis to MySQL?

Reservations lived in Redis and the inventory ledger in MySQL, so at payment claim the two systems could not be updated in a single atomic step, and depending on the order, overselling or underselling could occur. The lack of multi-location awareness and the operational cost of a separate Redis cluster were also reasons. Moving reservations into the same MySQL database as the ledger let everything be wrapped in ACID transactions (Shopify Engineering, 2026-05-12).

Q2. Is Redis bad for inventory systems?

Redis is not bad; it plays a different role. The source itself says Redis handled concurrency fine. But when the source of truth lives in another store, you get the structural limit of not being able to change both places at once. For read caches, sessions, and queues where speed is key, it remains excellent.

Q3. When should I use MySQL over Redis?

Use MySQL when data must not be lost, must be correct, and needs multi-step transactions and complex queries. Use Redis when data can be volatile and simple key-value lookups with extreme low latency are the priority.

Q4. Does MySQL handle high traffic like Redis?

According to the source, Shopify met its throughput targets during peak 2025 traffic with per-unit rows, SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED, a 1,000-row pool per item/location, a composite primary key, READ COMMITTED, and a cleanup of connection usage. In general, indexing, connection pools, read replicas, and a front cache are also worth considering.

Q5. What is the difference between a cache and a database?

A database is the source of truth, responsible for permanence, consistency, and transactions; a cache is a temporary copy for fast reproduction. An empty cache only slows things down, but a wrong database breaks the business.

## 3. Checklist when designing or migrating inventory reservations

Items derived from the source case.

- [ ] Are reservations and the inventory ledger inside the same transaction boundary?
- [ ] If you use a single row with a quantity column, have you measured contention under concurrent decrements?
- [ ] If you use SKIP LOCKED, did you size the pool cap from observed peak reservation rates?
- [ ] When the pool empties, is there a path that lets only one transaction replenish?
- [ ] Have you verified in the lock status that filter columns are in the primary key and each row takes one lock?
- [ ] If gap locks block replenishment on an empty table, have you reviewed the isolation level for those transactions?
- [ ] Do reserve and claim lock tables in the same order?
- [ ] Are multi-item cart lookups batched into one round trip?
- [ ] Can you see connection hold time per caller (SQL comment tags + proxy aggregation)?
- [ ] Do you compare both systems' outcomes side by side on production traffic with shadow-mode dual-write?
- [ ] Can you revert to the old path immediately with a kill switch?
- [ ] Do you roll out gradually, starting with low-traffic units?
