Merge Ten Utilities and You Merge Ten Permissions - The Real Trade in All-in-One Tools

What gets pooled in one place in exchange for menu bar room and memory

2026-09-04 Tech & IT

You have probably seen a screen with nine icons crowding the menu bar. One for clipboard history, one for window layout, one for temperature readouts, one for global shortcuts, one for screen capture. Each of them was installed on some particular day to remove exactly one annoyance, and on that day it was the right call. The trouble is that the same call got made nine times.

So the proposal to fold them into one always sounds appealing. Twelve becoming one promises less clutter and fewer things to look after. That arithmetic is half right. What it leaves out is that alongside the things that shrink, there are things that pool into a single place, and the pooling side usually only becomes visible after something has gone wrong.

This is not a piece about picking a particular app. It is about counting what the shape called consolidation costs and where it pays. It starts on macOS, but the arithmetic is not specific to any operating system. On Windows and Linux the same problem simply wears a different shape.

This article sets out the structural trade between all-in-one utilities and separate tools. It is not a recommendation or an assessment of any particular product, and it does not cover installation or configuration. The operating system behaviour and permission descriptions quoted here were confirmed in each vendor's official documentation as of September 2026.

The trigger - one icon standing in for twelve

In early September 2026 a Korean developer community picked up an open-source app that bundles macOS utilities into one. The repository describes itself as a free and open-source macOS menu bar toolkit, and a single list covers per-app volume control and output device switching, system monitoring, an app switcher with live window previews, window management, clipboard history, text snippet expansion, screenshots and screen recording, a command bar, and app removal and cleanup. It is written in Swift, licensed GPL-3.0, and requires Apple Silicon and macOS 14 Sonoma or newer.

The repository was created on 12 June 2026, and as queried on 4 September 2026 it shows 15,004 stars, 527 forks and 327 open issues. That is a great deal of attention in three months. For someone who has been doing window switching, window layout and app removal in three separate apps, the offer to collapse them into one is a substantive offer.

From here on the subject of this article is not that app but the shape called merging. Proposals like it arrive periodically, and each time the same arithmetic is needed. What shrinks, what pools into one place, and how wide the blast radius is when the pooled thing falls over.

Why utilities pile up

Resident utilities are almost without exception born from small gaps the operating system does not fill. Every one of the gaps is small. You need the thing you copied three items ago, not the last one. You want two windows sitting on half the screen each. You want watts rather than a battery percentage. You want two hundred files renamed to a rule.

Because the gap is small the fix is small too. Download an app, park it in the menu bar, done, and the judgement in that moment is usually correct. The trouble is that over a few years the judgement gets made nine times while the decision to remove one never gets made at all. Resident processes are quiet. They stay running long after you stopped using them, and the fact that they are running is itself forgotten.

The way they accumulate has common features. Knowing them helps later when you are choosing what to clear out.

  • The cost of adoption is close to zero. Download and enable is the whole procedure, so no weighing happens at the moment of adoption
  • The cost arrives later and spread thin. Tens of megabytes and one menu bar slot are not felt at the time
  • Nothing prompts removal. Utilities rarely announce themselves by breaking. They just keep running quietly
  • Overlaps stay invisible. If three apps each have screen capture on different shortcuts, the overlap never surfaces
  • The utility survives the OS absorbing its feature. Out of habit, or because you never noticed the built-in arrived

So utilities piling up is not the result of carelessness but of a structure where adoption is cheap and removal has no trigger. Understanding that structure is what makes it clear exactly what merging solves. The same kind of arithmetic applied to software switching in general was set out along six axes in Before You Switch to a Lightweight Alternative.

What merging genuinely buys you

To avoid this becoming a piece that simply disparages consolidation, the gains have to be counted accurately first. And the gains are real.

The first is menu bar room. It gets treated as a matter of taste, but it is a finite resource. The right-hand width of the menu bar is fixed, so with a menu-heavy app in front the icons get clipped out of view. On models with a camera notch that width is narrower still.

The second is the number of processes and login items. Ten becoming one means that many fewer processes waking simultaneously right after login. How much resource use drops is a separate question, but that the count drops is certain.

The third is consistency of configuration. Ten separate tools do not know each other's shortcuts. Install a new one and an old tool's shortcut quietly stops firing, and finding out why takes a while. Inside one app, the app knows about the conflict and usually says so.

The fourth is that updates and trust decisions collapse to one point. Ten tools is a decision to trust ten developers individually, and ten compatibility checks at every major OS update. Merged, that becomes one.

And the fifth is the number of permission grants. Instead of granting five times to five tools, you grant once to one app. On the surface that is an obvious win. But this item is the pivot of this article, because what fell was the number of grants, not the total of what was granted.

The five things merging costs

The losses come to five. What matters is that they are not independent of each other; each one enlarges the next.

What you loseWith separate toolsWith one merged appWhen it actually bites
Single point of failureOne dies and only that one feature stopsOne dies and ten features stop togetherRight after an update, right after a major OS upgrade
Union of permissionsEach tool holds only what its own feature needsOne process holds all of itWhen the app is compromised or changes hands
Cost of replacementSwap out the one you dislikeNo partial swap. Everything is bound inWhen one feature turns out not to fit your work
Depth of featuresA dedicated tool has years of accumulated detailAn average implementation covering common useWhen that feature is the centre of your work
Maintenance riskOne stalls and the other nine live onTen things disappear at onceWhen development stops and the OS moves up a version

Start with the single point of failure. Resident apps break at every major OS update. That is not a competence problem but a consequence of features that sit tight against system behaviour, so when the layer below changes, what rests on it wobbles. Two or three out of ten separate tools breaking and one merged app breaking so that ten features stop at once are not events of the same size. The first is an inconvenience, the second is a work stoppage.

The cost of replacement reveals what the most practical advantage of separate tools always was: partial replacement. If the window layout does not suit you, change just that. In a merged app you cannot, and what actually happens is usually this. You leave the merged app running and start a second app for that one feature. The reason for merging evaporates on the spot.

Depth of features is closer to being unavoidable by design. Covering common use is a reasonable decision and fits most people. The mismatch appears for the people for whom that feature is the centre of their work. Someone using clipboard history a hundred times a day needs search syntax, format preservation and per-app exclusion rules, and that kind of detail accumulates in tools that have made only that feature for years.

Maintenance risk shows how merging redistributes risk. Ten separate tools spread the risk across ten people, and if three stall, seven live on. A merged app concentrates that risk in one place. With open source the possibility of a fork remains, but a fork being possible and someone actually maintaining one are different stories.

  • Four of the five only appear over time. Satisfaction in the first week does not settle the question
  • Single point of failure and maintenance risk are the short and long versions of one axis. Days for the first, years for the second
  • Cost of replacement varies with how deeply things are merged. Per-feature on and off switches take much of the sting out
  • Depth of features bites in a different place for each person, which makes it the hardest item to judge from someone else's review
  • The remaining one, the union of permissions, is of a different kind. It is a matter of structure, not of time

The union of permissions is the heart of it

macOS splits the permissions a resident utility needs by function. Each one is a separate list under Privacy and Security in System Settings, and Apple attaches a short sentence to each saying what it allows. Reading those sentences as written is the point of this section.

PermissionApple's description (as of September 2026)Common features that require it
AccessibilityAllow apps to run scripts and system commands to control your MacWindow layout, window resizing, shortcut extension, automation
Input MonitoringAllow apps to monitor input from your mouse or trackpad, and to see what you type on the keyboardGlobal shortcuts, text snippet expansion
Screen & System Audio RecordingAllow apps to record your screen and audio, or just your audioScreen capture, window previews, reading other apps' window titles
Full Disk AccessAllow apps to access all files on your computer, including data from other apps, data from Time Machine backups, and certain administrative settings for all users on this MacApp removal and cleanup tools, cache clearing
MicrophoneAllow apps to access the microphone on your MacVoice captured alongside a screen recording

When you use separate tools these permissions attach to different processes. The window tool holds only accessibility, the capture tool only screen recording, the cleanup tool only full disk access. Each holds only what its own job needs, and the system blocks anything beyond that.

Merge them and one process holds all five. Build a union of features and a union of permissions follows. Not because the app is greedy, but because those features genuinely need those permissions. Window layout requires accessibility; window previews require screen recording. The features use those permissions honestly.

So what changes? Two things. First, permissions are granted per app, not per feature. Decide to stop using window layout and the accessibility permission you gave that app stays until you go into System Settings and revoke it yourself. Turning a feature off and taking a permission back are different actions.

Second, the blast radius changes. If a window layout tool is compromised, the permission to move windows is at stake; if an app holding all five is compromised, your screen, your input and every file are in play at once. That is not a statement about probability but a statement about scope.

How wide accessibility permission actually is

The widest of the five is accessibility. Apple's description is one sentence: allow apps to run scripts and system commands to control your Mac. Short, but the word control covers a lot of ground. It can read and manipulate the interface elements of other apps, and it can synthesise key input. That is exactly why window layout works: it reaches an object that is another app's window and changes its position and size.

A clearer signal is how Apple itself classifies the permission. Apple's platform security documentation states that accessibility and automation capabilities require user permission to help ensure they do not circumvent other protections. Which is to say this permission is not one item standing alongside the others but one that was fenced off separately because it can step over them.

Screen recording permission is wider than its name too. Since macOS 10.15 the window name value returned by the window list API has been gated behind screen recording permission, and an Apple engineer's answer on the Apple Developer Forums confirms the behaviour. That is why an app switcher needs screen recording permission to show you other apps' window titles. Window titles carry the name of the open document, the name of the person you are talking to, and the title of the page being visited. Without any recording at all, titles alone reconstruct a good deal of what someone is doing.

The note Apple attaches to the screen recording settings pane is worth reading too. It says that if you allow third-party apps or websites to record your screen and audio, any information they collect is governed by their terms and privacy policies. In other words control after the grant lies with the app's policy, not with the operating system. What the system does for you ends at asking once at the door.

One misreading is worth clearing up. Strong permissions do not mean an app is doing something bad. The point is not the size of the permissions but the number of places they pool. Whether the same five are spread across five places or gathered in one decides what happens when something goes wrong.

Open source reduces this problem without removing it. Being able to read the source and having actually read it are different things, and whether the distributed binary came from that source needs a separate mechanism such as reproducible builds to confirm. The real benefit of open source is auditability, not a completed audit. Where control over a device you own splits away from you is covered separately in The Part You Bought That Is Not Yours.

What to merge and what to keep apart

From here a decision rule can be built. There are three axes, and all three are questions about how you work rather than questions about a product.

AxisWhat to askWhen merging is betterWhen keeping it apart is better
Frequency of useHow many times a day do you use itA few times a week. No depth needed, so an average implementation is enoughDozens of times a day. You miss the details a dedicated tool has refined
Permission strengthWhat permission does this feature requireFeatures needing no permission at all, such as unit conversion, colour picking, calculatorsFeatures needing full disk access or screen recording. Separating them shrinks the radius
IrreplaceabilityDoes your work stop when it stopsConveniences you can live without for a few daysAnything that became part of your workflow. Keep it out of the single point of failure

When the three axes point the same way the answer is easy. A feature used occasionally, needing no permission, that you could live without, costs almost nothing to merge. A feature used daily, holding strong permissions, that you cannot do without, is better kept apart.

The hard cases are where the axes disagree. The standard example is clipboard history. Used dozens of times a day, immediately missed when absent, and storing everything that passes through. Yet the risk of this feature does not appear in any permission list. The risk lies not in permissions but in the storage itself. Values copied out of a password manager and one-time authentication codes land in that history as they are.

So wherever you put it, clipboard history becomes a feature whose policy you have to check. There is a documented reference point on the Windows side. Microsoft states that Windows clipboard history keeps up to 25 entries, allows up to 4 MB per item, supports text, HTML and bitmap formats, and is cleared every time you restart the PC except for items you pinned. That retention count and clearing point are written down at all is the standard. What to ask when choosing a tool is not the feature list but whether sentences like these exist.

  • Count before you merge — how many of your currently running utilities did you actually use in the last month? Removing the unused ones halves the reason to merge
  • Start from the permissions — look at what the merged app asks for and see whether any of it is needed by a feature you will not use. If so, leave that feature off and withhold the permission
  • Do not move everything at once — start with the low-permission, infrequently used features. Clipboard history and cleanup tools go last
  • Leave a grace period before deleting — remove the separate tools after about two weeks of living on the merged app. It is a cheap way to keep a route back
  • Check for per-feature switches — if the merged app lets you disable features individually, both the replacement cost and the permission problem ease at once. This is close to the most important condition when choosing one

The OS keeps absorbing utilities

Utilities have transience built into them. They are born to fill a gap, so when the gap is filled they lose their reason to exist. That is not failure but the normal ending, and it has happened several times in recent years.

FeatureWhen it was utility territoryNow (as of September 2026)
Window tiling (macOS)Handled by third-party window managersBuilt in since macOS 15 Sequoia. Desktop & Dock settings carry options for dragging windows to the screen edge to tile, dragging to the menu bar to fill the screen, holding the Option key while dragging to tile, and margins on tiled windows
Window snapping (Windows)Handled by third-party toolsSnap layouts in Windows 11 and 10. Win+Z opens the layout options, Win+arrow keys snap
Clipboard history (Windows)Third-party clipboard managersBuilt in, turned on with Win+V. 25 entries, 4 MB per item, text, HTML and bitmap
Startup app managementDedicated cleanup utilitiesSettings and Task Manager on Windows, Login Items under General in System Settings on macOS
The utility bundle itselfIndividual apps from individual developersMicrosoft PowerToys. More than 25 free and open-source utilities bundled and shipped by the vendor

The last row of that table is the interesting one. PowerToys ships FancyZones for window layout, Keyboard Manager for key remapping, PowerRename for bulk renaming and Advanced Paste for clipboard paste extension, all in one bundle. It is a case of the operating system vendor building an all-in-one utility itself. And there is one design choice worth noting: PowerToys lets you switch each utility on and off individually. Merged, but left modular, and that is the practical way to soften the replacement cost and permission problems from the previous section.

Absorption creates one problem peculiar to merged apps. When three of the ten features become OS built-ins, a merged app cannot detach those three. Someone on separate tools just deletes three apps; the merged-app user carries the duplicates. The permissions those three required usually stay too.

There are areas that do not get absorbed, of course. Temperature and fan speed readouts, or finding and clearing files left behind by a deleted app, are things an OS vendor has no particular reason to build. Utilities live long in those places. Asking whether a feature is on the absorbable side is a cheap prediction to make when choosing what to merge.

Measuring resident cost in numbers

Merging makes things lighter is a claim in a verifiable form. Yet most people do not verify it and speak from impression instead. Impressions are usually wrong. There is a bias toward feeling that whatever you just removed was heavy, and memories of the state with ten apps running are not accurate.

macOS has Activity Monitor, and two panes matter here. The Memory pane shows Memory Pressure, Physical Memory, Memory Used, Cached Files and Swap Used, with Memory Used broken down into App Memory, Wired Memory and Compressed. Apple describes memory pressure as a graphic representation of how efficiently memory is serving your processing needs, computed by considering free memory, swap rate, wired memory and file cached memory together. The Energy pane shows information about per-app energy use in four columns, with an overall energy impact graph at the bottom of the window.

There is one common mistake in reading these: adding up the memory figures of individual processes into a total. Since Apple itself defines pressure as a composite of several values, a large app memory total is not a problem if memory pressure is low. Why heavy memory use by developer tools is not in itself a problem was set out with language servers as the example in Why Developer Tools Eat Memory.

  • Compare two states. Ten minutes with only the merged app, ten minutes with all the separate tools. Look at the difference, not the absolute value
  • Do the same work in both. Same editor and browser setup, same task. Otherwise what you measured is the day's work, not the utilities
  • Do not judge from a single snapshot. Resident apps mostly wake on a cycle, and that cycle has to fall inside your observation window
  • Use energy impact for battery. The rate at which the percentage falls swings too much with screen brightness and radio conditions to compare with
  • Write the conclusion down as numbers. To avoid measuring again next time the question comes up, that day's values have to survive

Windows has a documented standard too. The startup impact shown in the Startup apps tab of Task Manager is assessed from CPU time and disk I/O, and Microsoft writes the criteria as follows. High is more than 1 second of CPU time or more than 3 MB of disk I/O at startup; medium is 300 ms to 1000 ms of CPU time or 300 KB to 3 MB of disk I/O; low is less than 300 ms of CPU time and less than 300 KB of disk I/O. Once you have seen figures like these, the word heavy acquires a size.

Login items and startup time

Resident utilities almost all require automatic launch, because without it they are useless. So more utilities means more processes waking simultaneously right after login.

On macOS 13 and later, Login Items sits under General in System Settings and holds two lists of different character. One is the items that open at login; the other is the Allow in the Background list. Apple describes the latter as allowing apps to perform tasks when the app is not open, such as checking for software updates or syncing data. On macOS 14 and later, items registered with the new registration framework appear in this list.

There is a clear reason to open this screen once. The list is longer than you remember. Sometimes an app was deleted and the entry stayed; sometimes there is a helper process you do not recall installing. A long list means several processes competing for disk and CPU during the first tens of seconds after login.

Windows manages this in two places, Settings and Task Manager. Linux follows the XDG autostart specification: user entries live in ~/.config/autostart and system-wide entries in /etc/xdg/autostart as .desktop files. When the same filename exists in several directories, only the one under the more important directory is used. A file marked Hidden=true must be ignored, so the way a user disables a system-wide autostart entry is to place a file of the same name in their personal directory. OnlyShowIn and NotShowIn give per-desktop-environment control, and the two are never used in the same file.

On this point the merged app is clearly ahead. Login items drop to one. There is no reason to deny that gain, and it is a real one. The argument of this article is only that the gain has to go on the scales together with the union of permissions.

Free, paid, subscription - a different axis

Whether a small tool is still alive in five years relates to its pricing model, but not in the direction people usually assume. Paid does not necessarily last and free does not necessarily stop. The axes are simply different.

  • One-time purchase — no recurring income. The cost of responding to every OS update has to come out of new sales, and when sales fall the responses come later
  • Subscription — capacity to respond, in exchange for paying in months you do not use it and losing the features when payment stops. It also creates a reason to keep adding features, so small tools tend to grow
  • Free and open source — maintenance rests on a developer's time and will rather than on a contract. In exchange, the source survives a stall and a fork is possible if the licence allows
  • Bundled or funded elsewhere — another revenue source lets the tool be free. When that source changes direction, the tool is cleared out first

The risk of continuing to use an abandoned tool connects straight back to permissions. Resident utilities hold strong permissions, and when a vulnerability is found and not fixed, that permission remains as attack surface. One separate tool in that state and a merged app with five permissions in that state have different radii.

A more reliable signal than the pricing model sits in the history. The following five can usually be checked on a repository or distribution page.

  • Recent release interval — does anything ship even once every few months? When was the last release?
  • Time taken to respond to a major OS update — this is the project's real stamina. Measure from announcement to the responding release
  • Rate of opening versus rate of closing issues — the relationship between the two speeds matters more than the count
  • How many maintainers — if it is one person, is there a written plan for the day that person is gone?
  • Does the licence permit a fork — open source and forkable-and-maintainable are not the same claim, and the licence decides

On Windows and Linux the shape differs

There is a reason all of this is unusually visible on macOS: macOS shows permissions as a list. You can check on screen what you allowed, so the cost of merging is visible too. On other operating systems the same problem wears a different shape.

EnvironmentWhat a utility needsWhere the user has controlWhat that structure produces
macOSFunction-split permissions approved item by itemRevoked per app under Privacy and Security in System SettingsYou can see what you gave as a list. But frequent prompts make it easy to click through them all at once
WindowsOrdinary desktop apps operate without this kind of separate approvalStartup app management and administrator elevation, roughlyThere is no screen on which to check what you allowed. The cost of merging never surfaces
Linux / X11Reaching the X server is enough, so it is technically easyAutostart via the XDG specification. Isolation requires a separate sandboxAs the Wayland documentation states, there is no protection or isolation between X11 clients
Linux / WaylandEverything goes through channels the compositor providesThe compositor's portal settings and permission policyGlobal shortcuts and screen sharing become harder and depend on per-compositor implementations

The Linux side needs a little more detail. The official Wayland documentation states that Xwayland does not protect nor isolate X11 clients from each other, unless the compositor chooses to spawn application-specific Xwayland instances, and the same document explains that this is not usually done because many X11 applications need to communicate with one another through the X server. X11 clients, by contrast, are described as naturally isolated from Wayland clients. In other words the level of isolation differs per app even within one screen.

So the question of whether to merge resolves differently by environment. On macOS you can judge from the permission list, and the fact that the list is visible is itself what makes the judgement easy. On Windows the basis for judgement is not on screen, so the user has to remember what was installed, while Microsoft has demonstrated the shape where the first-party vendor bundles and lets you disable per feature in PowerToys. On Linux the choice of display server settles much of the answer in advance.

One thing is the same in every environment. What stays resident pays the price of staying resident. It takes a slot, it lengthens startup, it holds permissions, and it carries risk in proportion to them. Reading maturity - what a star count does not tell you

Look at the numbers on the app that triggered this again. The repository was created on 12 June 2026, and as queried on 4 September 2026 it shows 15,004 stars, 527 forks and 327 open issues. It helps to separate what those numbers say from what they do not.

What they say is clear. Attention is high and problems are being raised actively. A project nobody uses does not get 327 open issues. That is evidence of real users.

What they do not say is equally clear. Stars are an indicator of attention, not of stability. They rise fastest right after a community pickup, and there is no direct relationship between a star count and the odds that the app will come through the next OS update intact. The open issue count reads two ways as well: it may mean many users or a backlog, and the count alone cannot say which. What to look at is the relationship between the rate of opening and the rate of closing.

Being a three-month-old project also means one thing in particular: it has not yet been through many major OS updates. For a resident app that asks for strong permissions and sits tight against system behaviour, that record of passage is more substantive than any other number, and it is information only time produces.

This is not an assessment of that project. Every mature tool was once a three-month-old project. The point is only that when choosing an app that asks for strong permissions, the axis of time cannot be substituted with a star count. And there is a very cheap response available on that axis: waiting. If the separate tools you use now are not actually causing you trouble, nine icons on their own are not yet trouble.

Summary

Merging ten small tools into one has real gains and real losses. The gains show on day one and the losses show after something breaks. The whole of this arithmetic is counting the losses in advance.

The largest loss is neither memory nor screen room but the place where permissions pool. macOS writes accessibility down as allowing apps to run scripts and system commands to control your Mac, and Apple's security documentation classifies it as requiring separate consent because it can circumvent other protections. When five permissions that sat in five places come to sit five-in-one, it is not probability that changes but scope.

The decision rule has three parts. What you use often is better in a dedicated tool, what holds strong permissions is safer kept apart, and what you cannot do without does not go inside a single point of failure. The rest is generally a gain when merged. And if you do choose a merged app, there is one condition: can features be disabled individually? If they can, replacement cost and the permission problem both ease; if they cannot, both grow together.

Finally, it helps to remember what kind of thing a utility is. It is something that temporarily fills work the operating system does not yet do. Just as window layout did and clipboard history did, part of what utilities do today becomes a built-in feature within a few years. Whether you are handing permanent permissions to something temporary is the one thing worth checking.

What I Could Not Verify

  • The actual memory, CPU and battery consumption of the app that triggered this could not be verified, which is why no such figures appear here. The claim that merging wins on resources can only be confirmed by comparing two states on your own machine
  • What each of the four columns in the Activity Monitor Energy pane is could not be found by name and definition in Apple's support documentation. Only that the pane shows per-app information in four columns with an overall energy impact graph below was confirmed from documentation
  • Whether macOS has a system-level clipboard history feature could not be established, as no such feature was found in Apple's support documentation. The clipboard figures here are therefore only the documented Windows values
  • No material comparing the frequency of security incidents between all-in-one apps and separate tools was found. The permission discussion here rests only on the structural reason that pooled permissions widen the affected scope, not on incident statistics
  • The GitHub star, fork and issue counts are values queried on 4 September 2026, and no research verifying a relationship between those numbers and software quality was found
  • A complete list of which actions require separate user approval for ordinary desktop apps on Windows could not be verified. The text only goes as far as the startup app management and startup impact criteria Microsoft documents
