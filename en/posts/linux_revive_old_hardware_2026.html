Reviving an Old PC with Linux — A 2026 Practical Guide

How to choose the right distro for your hardware, prepare for installation, handle drivers, and make the most of a revived machine

2026-06-29 Dev & Infra

Introduction: A New OS for a Machine You Were About to Discard

On October 14, 2025, Microsoft ended free security updates for Windows 10. From that point forward, hundreds of millions of PCs entered a security dead zone. Upgrading to Windows 11 requires TPM 2.0, UEFI Secure Boot, and a CPU from Intel's 8th generation or AMD's Ryzen 2000 series onward — conditions that exclude the vast majority of machines purchased between 2014 and 2019. The hardware works fine. The OS policy simply draws a line.

That leaves three choices: buy a new PC, pay for Extended Security Updates (ESU), or switch to Linux. This guide is about that third option — not as an enthusiast experiment, but as a practical, documented path. As of June 2026, the Linux ecosystem has matured to the point where the migration process is largely graphical, driver support is dramatically better than a decade ago, and the selection of lightweight distros purpose-built for old hardware is strong.

One honest caveat upfront: Linux is not Windows. Adobe Creative Suite, anti-cheat multiplayer games, and certain business software have no Linux equivalents that match them feature-for-feature. This guide won't pretend otherwise. What it will show is that for the majority of everyday computing tasks, the right lightweight Linux distro on old hardware is a fully viable solution.

1. Why Linux on Old Hardware Makes Sense in 2026

1.1 The Windows 11 Hardware Wall

Windows 11's hardware requirements are not purely about performance — they reflect a security architecture decision (TPM-based attestation, Secure Boot enforcement). The practical outcome is that machines from 2017 or earlier are locked out. A technical workaround install exists, but Microsoft does not support it and has signaled that such installs may be excluded from future updates. Linux has no analogous hardware gate. Some distros run on machines with 512 MB of RAM and a single-core CPU from the early 2000s.

1.2 RAM Consumption: The Core Advantage

Windows 11 at idle consumes roughly 2 to 4 GB of RAM just to maintain its background services. A lightweight Linux desktop like Xfce or LXQt idles at 300 to 800 MB. On the same hardware, that difference is felt immediately as faster application launch times, less disk swapping, and a more responsive experience. The machine did not get faster. The OS got lighter.

1.3 The E-Waste Dimension

A single desktop PC or laptop contains approximately 30 to 50 kg of electronic components, plastics, and rare earth metals. Analysis published in early 2026 estimated that Windows 10's end-of-support event could push over 200 million devices toward premature disposal worldwide. Extending a PC's life by two to three years with Linux keeps it productive and out of the waste stream. This is not marketing language — it is a straightforward consequence of the hardware lifecycle math.

2. Choosing a Distro by Hardware Specs — June 2026

RAM is the most useful single axis for distro selection. CPU generation matters less than people expect, as long as it is 64-bit (which covers virtually all machines from 2007 onward).

2.1 4 GB RAM and Above: Wide Open

With 4 GB or more, the choice of lightweight distros is broad. The two I recommend first for users coming from Windows are Linux Mint 22 Xfce and Xubuntu 26.04 LTS. Both use the Xfce desktop environment, which has a taskbar-and-start-menu layout that feels immediately familiar to Windows users. Linux Mint's Software Manager is one of the most polished app installation interfaces in the Linux world. Xubuntu 26.04 ships with Xfce 4.20 and Ubuntu's full package ecosystem. Either is an excellent daily driver on 4 GB hardware.

2.2 2–4 GB RAM: LXQt is the Right Call

At 2 GB, Xfce starts to feel tight under real workloads. Lubuntu 24.04 LTS with LXQt is designed exactly for this range. It requires as little as a 1 GHz CPU, idles around 700 to 900 MB, and leaves meaningful headroom for a browser tab or two alongside a document editor. Linux Lite 8.0, also Xfce-based, is another solid pick here — it places particular emphasis on making the transition from Windows as smooth as possible.

2.3 1 GB RAM or Less: antiX and Puppy Linux

Below 1 GB, the options narrow but don't disappear. antiX 26 "Stephen Kapos", released March 2026 and based on Debian 13 Trixie, idles at around 256 MB of RAM. It skips systemd in favor of lighter init systems, which contributes to fast boot times and very low resource use. It runs on 512 MB hardware. For machines below that threshold, Puppy Linux loads the entire OS into RAM, eliminating disk I/O and producing a surprisingly responsive experience even on machines most distros would reject.

3. Pre-Installation Preparation

The preparation phase matters more than most guides acknowledge. Skipping it is the most common reason Linux migrations end in frustration.

3.1 Back Up Everything First

Linux installation repartitions the drive. Selecting the wrong option during setup can wipe your existing data. Before touching anything, copy all important files — documents, photos, browser bookmarks, software license keys — to an external drive or cloud storage. Verify the backup is complete before proceeding. This is not optional advice.

3.2 Create a Bootable USB

Ventoy is the tool I recommend for creating bootable USB drives. It lets you copy multiple ISO files onto a single USB and presents a boot menu to select between them — ideal if you want to test more than one distro before committing. For a single ISO, balenaEtcher is simple and reliable. Use an 8 GB or larger USB drive. Always download ISOs from official project websites and verify the SHA256 checksum to confirm file integrity.

3.3 Configure BIOS/UEFI to Boot from USB

Most machines default to booting from the internal drive. On startup, press F2, Del, or F12 (varies by manufacturer) to enter the BIOS or boot menu. Set USB as the first boot device. On UEFI systems with Secure Boot enabled, some distros may fail to boot — disabling Secure Boot temporarily for testing is safe and can be re-enabled after installation if desired.

3.4 Test in Live Mode Before Installing

Every major Linux distro offers a Live mode that runs entirely from the USB without touching the internal drive. Boot into Live mode and verify: Wi-Fi connects, screen resolution is correct, audio plays, and the trackpad or mouse works. If Live mode works cleanly, the installed system almost always will too. This step catches driver issues before they become a post-install problem to debug.

4. Driver Compatibility on Old Hardware

4.1 Wired Ethernet and Intel/AMD Graphics: Largely Automatic

Intel and Realtek wired Ethernet controllers, Intel integrated graphics, and modern AMD GPUs (using the open-source amdgpu driver) are supported by in-kernel drivers and require no manual intervention. The majority of machines from the past 15 years fall into this category for their core connectivity and display hardware.

4.2 Wi-Fi: Watch Out for Broadcom

The most common driver problem on old laptops is Broadcom Wi-Fi (BCM43xx series). If Live mode shows no Wi-Fi, this is the likely culprit. The fix: connect via wired Ethernet, then open Additional Drivers (in Linux Mint or Ubuntu-based systems) and install the proprietary Broadcom driver. An alternative is to replace the Wi-Fi card with an Intel one (usually $10–20 used) or use a USB Wi-Fi adapter with good Linux support (Realtek RTL8812AU-based adapters work well).

4.3 NVIDIA GPUs: Two Driver Paths

NVIDIA hardware ships with the open-source Nouveau driver by default. It handles display output and basic 2D work reliably. For hardware video decoding or 3D acceleration, the proprietary NVIDIA driver is better. On Ubuntu/Mint-based systems, ubuntu-drivers autoinstall or the Additional Drivers GUI installs it with one click. Note that NVIDIA dropped official driver support for very old GPUs (Fermi/Kepler generation, roughly GeForce 400–600 series), so those are limited to Nouveau.

4.4 The SSD Upgrade: Often More Impactful Than the Distro Choice

Many old PCs feel slow not because of CPU or RAM limitations, but because of a spinning hard drive. A 60–120 GB SSD costs very little today and makes an immediate, dramatic difference in boot time and application responsiveness. If you are reinstalling Linux anyway, replacing the HDD with an SSD is the single hardware upgrade most likely to transform the experience. A lightweight distro on an SSD will feel faster than a heavier distro on an HDD regardless of RAM.

5. What You Can and Cannot Do on Linux Daily

5.1 Web Browsing: Fully Capable

Firefox and Chromium are available on every major Linux distribution. Google Docs, YouTube, Netflix, and virtually all web-based services work identically in a Linux browser. The honest caveat for underpowered machines: having many browser tabs open is still RAM-limited. That is a hardware constraint, not a Linux one.

5.2 Document Work: LibreOffice's Honest Assessment

LibreOffice 24.x opens, edits, and saves .docx, .xlsx, and .pptx files. For simple documents, compatibility is excellent. For complex formatting, macros, form fields, and documents heavy with custom fonts, layout drift occurs. For document exchange with Windows users, using Google Docs or Microsoft 365 in the browser is more reliable than relying on LibreOffice-to-Office round-trip fidelity.

5.3 Media: VLC Covers Almost Everything

VLC 3.x on Linux plays virtually every video and audio format without additional codec installation. Local file playback is essentially a solved problem. Streaming services work in the browser. For 4K content on old hardware, hardware decode support depends on the GPU driver — 1080p is generally fine.

5.4 The Honest Gaps

Adobe Photoshop, Illustrator, and Premiere Pro have no native Linux versions. GIMP, Inkscape, and Kdenlive are capable open-source alternatives, but the learning curve and feature gaps are real. Anti-cheat systems in multiplayer games frequently block Linux (even via Proton). Single-player Steam games have strong Proton coverage. Some business software — certain ERP systems, industry-specific tools — simply does not run on Linux and has no equivalent.

Knowing these gaps before starting avoids the frustration of discovering them after committing to the migration.

6. Step-by-Step Transition Checklist

Step 1: Inventory Your Hardware

In Windows, open Task Manager, go to the Performance tab, and note your RAM total and CPU model. Cross-reference with the table above to select a distro. For first-time migrators: Linux Mint Xfce for 4 GB+, Lubuntu for 2 GB+, antiX for 1 GB and below.

Step 2: Back Up, Download, Write

Back up all important files. Download the ISO from the official distro website. Verify the SHA256 checksum. Write to USB with Ventoy or balenaEtcher.

Step 3: Live Mode Test

Set USB as first boot device in BIOS. Boot to Live mode. Verify Wi-Fi, display, audio, and input devices. If Live mode works, proceed.

Step 4: Install

Launch the installer from Live mode. During partitioning, confirm the backup is complete before selecting "Erase disk and install." Set language, timezone, and user account. Installation typically takes 20–30 minutes.

Step 5: Post-Install Essentials

Boot into the new system, connect to the internet, and run system updates. Open Additional Drivers to check for Wi-Fi or GPU driver options. Firefox, LibreOffice, and VLC are usually pre-installed or one click away. After updates complete, the machine is ready for daily use.

7. Conclusion

If you have a machine that is slow on Windows and approaching the end of its supported life, spending 30 minutes creating a Live USB and booting it is a low-cost test with potentially high return. Most machines from the past 10–15 years will pass that test. If they do, a further 30–60 minutes of installation produces a machine that is genuinely usable for everyday computing in 2026.

The distro selection question does not need to be complex: 4 GB+ gets Linux Mint Xfce, 2 GB gets Lubuntu, 1 GB and below gets antiX. Those three lines cover the vast majority of cases. Driver issues are rarer than they used to be, and when they occur, the fix path is usually documented and straightforward. The machine you were about to discard may have years of useful life left.
