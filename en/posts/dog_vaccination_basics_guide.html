This article rests on the vaccination guidelines of the World Small Animal Veterinary Association (WSAVA), the Animal Protection Act and the Contagious Animal Diseases Prevention Act with their decrees and rules at the Korea Law Information Center, and a canine health guide of the National Institute of Animal Science, and a dog's own vaccination plan must be set by a veterinarian after an examination.

Before Vaccination Starts — Maternal Antibodies and the Logic of Timing

A newborn puppy has no active immunity of its own against infectious disease and receives its mother's antibodies through colostrum. These are maternally derived antibodies (MDA), and they provide passive immunity in the first weeks of life. According to the WSAVA guidelines, in most puppies this passive immunity has fallen by 8 to 12 weeks of age to a level that allows active immunization. A puppy with poor MDA becomes vulnerable to infection earlier, while a puppy with very high antibody levels may be unable to respond to a vaccine until it is 12 weeks of age or older.

Maternal antibodies neutralize not only pathogens but also the virus in a vaccine, which WSAVA names as the most common reason a vaccine fails. Waiting until the antibodies have fallen, on the other hand, leaves a stretch without protection. Because no single primary vaccination policy covers every situation, WSAVA gives its advice as a range. The first core vaccination is given at 6 to 8 weeks of age and repeated every 2 to 4 weeks until the puppy is 16 weeks of age or older.

The number of doses is determined by the starting age and the interval. The WSAVA guidelines give the example that starting at 6 or 7 weeks with a 4-week interval means four primary core vaccinations, while starting at 8 or 9 weeks needs only three. The principles can be summarized as follows.

  • Colostrum and passive immunity. Maternal antibodies received through colostrum carry the defense of the first weeks of life.
  • Neutralization of vaccine antigen. When remaining maternal antibodies neutralize the vaccine virus no immunity develops, and WSAVA describes this as the most common reason for vaccine failure.
  • 8 to 12 weeks and 12 weeks or more. Most puppies can respond to vaccination between 8 and 12 weeks, but a puppy with high antibody levels responds only at 12 weeks or later.
  • The last dose at 16 weeks or older. By then maternal antibodies have dropped to a low level and active immunization succeeds in most puppies.

The interval between doses also has a lower limit. WSAVA states that vaccines should not be given more often than every other week, even to an animal at high risk of disease and even when different vaccines are being given. A puppy under 4 weeks of age is not a candidate because maternal antibodies block the ability of live vaccines to prime the immune system. The exception is the intranasal vaccine against canine infectious respiratory disease, which can be used safely from 3 weeks of age.

Dog vaccines go by names such as combination vaccine, DHPPL, coronavirus, kennel cough, influenza and rabies, and the nature of the evidence and the force of the law differ greatly between them. The WSAVA class and the statutory basis of each vaccine are set side by side below.

The WSAVA classes and descriptions are those of Table 1 of the guidelines published in a journal in 2016. In the statute column, no such article means that the main text of the Animal Protection Act and the Contagious Animal Diseases Prevention Act has no article that directly prescribes that vaccine for a dog owner, and municipal ordinances are not included. Article 16, paragraph 3 of the Animal Protection Act provides that, where necessary to prevent the loss or abandonment of registered animals or hazards to public hygiene, a mayor or governor may by ordinance have owners vaccinate them. Which vaccinations an ordinance actually prescribes has to be checked region by region.

Rabies is different. Article 2, item 1 of the Contagious Animal Diseases Prevention Act includes dogs among livestock, and item 2(b) of the same Article lists rabies as a Class 2 livestock disease. Article 15 provides that when a city or county head or similar authority finds it necessary to prevent a livestock disease from breaking out or spreading, the authority may order the owner of livestock to receive measures such as injection. Rabies also infects humans, so vaccination required by law and vaccination recommended on veterinary grounds should be kept apart.

Core, Non-Core and Not Recommended Vaccines in the WSAVA Guidelines

The WSAVA Vaccination Guidelines Group (VGG) divides vaccines into three categories: core, non-core and not recommended. A core vaccine is one that all dogs throughout the world must receive at the recommended intervals, in order to provide life-long protection against infectious diseases of global significance. The core vaccines for the dog protect against canine distemper virus (CDV), canine adenovirus (CAV, types 1 and 2) and canine parvovirus type 2 (CPV-2) and its variants.

The guidelines also recognize that particular countries may add vaccines to the core list. A prominent example is the rabies vaccine. They state that where rabies is endemic, all dogs should be vaccinated routinely for the protection of both pets and people, and add that in many countries rabies vaccination is a legal requirement. Where the law requires the rabies vaccine, it is classed as core.

A non-core vaccine is one whose use is decided from the exposure risk set by the geographical location and lifestyle of the individual and from a risk-benefit assessment. The assessment weighs the risk of being unvaccinated, the risk of an adverse reaction, and the benefit of protection. The non-core items in Table 1 of the 2016 guidelines are Bordetella bronchiseptica, the cause of kennel cough, parainfluenza virus, Leptospira, Borrelia, which causes Lyme disease, and canine influenza virus. A not recommended vaccine is one with little scientific justification for its use. The same table places canine coronavirus (CCV) vaccines here, explaining that CCV infections are usually subclinical or mild and that there is no evidence that the vaccines protect against pathogenic variants.

The composition of products sold in Korea as combination vaccines or DHPPL differs from product to product and has to be confirmed in the product leaflet. The National Institute of Animal Science describes the combination vaccine as protecting against distemper, hepatitis, parvovirus enteritis, parainfluenza and leptospirosis. By WSAVA classes, the first three are core and the last two are non-core. The guidelines acknowledge that some countries can obtain only mixed core and non-core products and urge manufacturers to offer separate core vaccines. The key principles of the WSAVA classification are as follows.

  • Core for every dog. Distemper, adenovirus and parvovirus type 2 are given regardless of where the dog lives or how it lives.
  • Non-core by risk. Only infections whose exposure risk is set by geography and lifestyle are vaccinated against.
  • Not recommended for lack of evidence. A vaccine with little scientific basis for its use is not used.
  • Rabies by law and region. It is treated as core where it is endemic or required by statute.

WSAVA strongly supports regular health checks, which it says shift the emphasis and the client's expectation away from annual revaccination. At the check, only the non-core vaccines that are needed can be selected and given annually. The core and non-core division is not a binary of important and unnecessary vaccines but a framework that separates the minimum protection every dog needs from protection tailored to the individual.

The Structure of the 6 to 16 Week Puppy Series

The canine health guide of the National Institute of Animal Science lists an example of six doses at 2-week intervals from 6 weeks of age, while the WSAVA guidelines give several combinations by starting age and interval. Side by side, the two differ in the vaccines given.

Table 5 of the WSAVA guidelines shows schedules built on the same logic. A puppy first presented at 8 weeks can be vaccinated at 8, 11, 14 and 17 weeks, or at 8, 12 and 16 weeks, and then boosted at 26 or 52 weeks. In every combination the last primary dose is at 16 weeks or older and the interval is 2 to 4 weeks. Many vaccine leaflets recommend two core doses, and some products are licensed with a second dose at 10 weeks. The guidelines explain that this allows early socialization, yet still recommend a last primary dose at 16 weeks or older whenever possible.

The puppy period is also important for socialization. WSAVA recognizes that early socialization is essential to behavioural development and says that, when puppy classes are used, exposure should be restricted to controlled areas and to puppies and adults that appear healthy and are fully vaccinated, preferably away from the veterinary practice. The principles of socialization are covered in Dog Training Basics on this site.

In a puppy given a combination vaccine that contains modified live CDV and CAV-2, a period of immunosuppression lasting about 1 week begins 3 days after vaccination. WSAVA treats this as part of the normal vaccine response and says it rarely, if ever, causes a clinical problem. Three points govern any schedule.

  • Start at 6 to 8 weeks. The first core vaccination begins at 6 to 8 weeks of age.
  • Interval of 2 to 4 weeks. Doses are repeated within this range and never more often than every other week.
  • Finish at 16 weeks or older. The last primary dose is given at 16 weeks of age or older, followed by a booster at 26 or 52 weeks.

Two Statutory Duties — Animal Registration and Rabies Vaccination

The statutes impose duties directly on dog owners and set fines for violations in two ways. One is animal registration under the Animal Protection Act, and the other is the order for vaccination and similar measures under the Contagious Animal Diseases Prevention Act.

Registration rests on Article 15, paragraph 1 of the Animal Protection Act. The owner of an animal subject to registration must register it with the mayor, county head or district head for the protection of animals, the prevention of loss and abandonment, and the prevention of hazards to public hygiene. However, where the animal is not a dangerous dog, registration is not required in areas that a city or provincial ordinance designates. The range of animals subject to registration is set by Article 4 of the Enforcement Decree. It covers dogs aged 2 months or older kept in a house or quasi-house as defined by the Housing Act, and dogs aged 2 months or older kept for companionship in other places, and dogs kept at the premises of an animal breeding business are subject to a separate standard.

The deadline is in Article 10, paragraph 1 of the same Decree. An application must be filed within 30 days from the date the owner acquired ownership of the animal or the date the animal reached the age subject to registration, and for an animal breeding business the date of the business permit is also a starting date. A dog below the registration age can be registered by the same procedure. Registration is completed by fitting a radio-frequency identification device and issuing a registration certificate, and Annex 1 of the Decree sets out the specifications for implanted and external devices. Registration can be delegated to designated agents such as animal hospital operators, and implanting a device must be done by a veterinarian.

Reporting duties continue after registration. Article 15, paragraph 2 of the Animal Protection Act requires a report within 10 days if the registered animal is lost, and within 30 days of the date of the cause for a change in the matters set by Article 11 of the Decree. The matters in Article 11, paragraph 1 include a change of owner, a change of name, resident registration number, address or telephone number, finding an animal after a loss report, ceasing to keep the animal in Korea, death, and a change of the identification device. A person who has received ownership of a registered animal reports within 30 days under Article 15, paragraph 3. A move-in report is treated as a report of an address change. The fine under Article 101, paragraph 3, item 4 for an owner who fails to register is up to 1 million won, and the Decree's scale is 200,000 won for the first violation, 400,000 won for the second and 600,000 won for the third or later. Failing to report a change or a transfer of ownership carries a fine of up to 500,000 won under Article 101, paragraph 4, and the Decree's scale is 100,000, 200,000 and 400,000 won.

Rabies vaccination rests on the Contagious Animal Diseases Prevention Act. Article 15, paragraph 1 provides that when the competent authority finds it necessary to prevent a livestock disease from breaking out or spreading, it may order the owner of livestock to receive measures such as inspection, injection, medicated bath, immunotherapy or medication, in accordance with Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Rural Affairs rules including the method of vaccination. Under Article 15, paragraph 2, an owner who has had injections given under an order can request a certificate of that fact. Violating the order carries a fine of up to 10 million won under Article 60, paragraph 2, item 4.

The amounts are set in Annex 3 of the Enforcement Decree. For orders under Article 15, paragraph 1 or 4, violating an order for foot-and-mouth disease vaccination carries 5 million won, 7.5 million won and 10 million won for the first, second and third or later violations, while violating an order other than foot-and-mouth disease vaccination carries 2 million won, 4 million won and 10 million won. Rabies is not foot-and-mouth disease, so by its wording it falls under the latter row. Violating the order in Article 15, paragraph 5, which has a person fined under paragraph 1 or 4 vaccinate through a designated veterinarian or have the vaccination process checked, carries 5 million won, 7.5 million won and 10 million won. Escalation for repeated violations applies where a fine was imposed for the same violation within the last 3 years, and the authority may reduce the amount within one half of the individual standard. The core of the legal duties is as follows.

  • Registration deadline. Register within 30 days of acquiring ownership or of the date the dog reaches the registration age (2 months).
  • Reporting deadlines. Report a loss within 10 days, and a change of registered details or a transfer of ownership within 30 days.
  • Fines under the Animal Protection Act. Up to 1 million won for failing to register and up to 500,000 won for failing to report.
  • Basis for rabies. Dogs are livestock, rabies is a Class 2 livestock disease, and the duty to vaccinate arises from an order under Article 15.
  • Fines for rabies. Violating the order carries up to 10 million won, and the standard for orders other than foot-and-mouth disease starts at 2 million won.

Article 15, paragraph 6 of the Animal Protection Act provides that the State and local governments may support part or all of the cost of registration. Annex 14 of the Enforcement Rule of the Act sets the registration fee for a new registration at 10,000 won where an implanted device is inserted and 3,000 won where an external device or a registration tag is attached, and the owner must buy or bring the device. Article 20, paragraph 4 of the Contagious Animal Diseases Prevention Act provides that when a city or county head finds dogs or cats without a rabies injection roaming outside buildings, the head may detain them at the owner's expense, destroy them or take other measures, and the Enforcement Rule requires a public notice 10 days before such measures.

Adult Boosters and Antibody Titer Testing

Finishing the primary series does not end protection. The WSAVA guidelines explain that a booster exists to ensure protection in any puppy that failed to respond to one of the primary core vaccines. The booster has traditionally been given at 12 months of age. The guidelines suggest bringing it forward from 52 weeks to 26 weeks to shorten the window of susceptibility. Any point between 26 and 52 weeks is possible, and after a 26-week booster no further core vaccine is needed for at least another 3 years.

The 3-year interval is a statement about core modified live vaccines (MLV). Dogs that have responded to the primary series maintain immunological memory for many years without repeat vaccination. The 3-year interval does not generally apply to killed core vaccines other than rabies, nor to non-core vaccines, particularly those with bacterial antigens. Leptospira, Bordetella, Borrelia and parainfluenza components need more frequent boosters for reliable protection. An adult dog may therefore still be vaccinated every year, but the components can differ from year to year, and typically core vaccines are given every 3 years and chosen non-core products annually. WSAVA adds that a live vaccine given shortly after a killed vaccine for the same disease may be neutralized by the antibody the killed vaccine induced, so the live vaccine is better given first.

The guidelines also address dogs whose history is uncertain. An adult dog that completed the puppy series and booster but was not vaccinated regularly as an adult needs only a single dose of MLV core vaccine to boost immunity. An adopted adult dog of unknown history likewise needs a single dose. Many leaflets advise two doses as for a puppy, but WSAVA calls that practice unjustified. Non-core vaccines are an exception, and many need two doses in an adult dog. In summary:

  • Booster at 26 or 52 weeks. It protects any puppy that failed to respond to the primary series, and 26 weeks is suggested.
  • Core MLV at 3 years or longer. After the booster, intervals are at least 3 years, and this does not apply to killed core vaccines other than rabies.
  • Non-core more often. Annual vaccination is described for Leptospira, Bordetella, Borrelia and parainfluenza.
  • Antibody titer testing. In-clinic kits detect protective antibody to distemper, adenovirus and parvovirus type 2, and a positive result means revaccination is not needed.

As of the 2016 guidelines, in-clinic test kits exist for distemper, adenovirus and parvovirus type 2. A negative result is taken to mean little or no antibody, and revaccination is recommended, although some seronegative dogs are in fact immune and cannot be identified readily. A positive result means revaccination is not required. For a puppy whose primary series ended at 16 weeks or older, blood is drawn at least 4 weeks after the last dose, and if it is positive the puppy skips the 26 or 52 week booster and next receives core vaccine 3 years later. A negative puppy is revaccinated and tested again.

Rabies is not handled by antibody titer. WSAVA says that monitoring rabies antibody is not used in the same way to decide on revaccination because the intervals are mandated by law. Rabies vaccines are licensed with a duration of immunity of either 1 year or 3 years, and the timing of boosters follows that period but in some areas is set by statute. WSAVA notes that in some countries the legal requirement is at odds with the vaccine licence, and that where a product with at least 3 years of immunity is available, national associations might lobby for the law to be changed. A rabies antibody titer above 0.5 IU/ml is required for international pet travel, and in Korea Annex 8 of the Enforcement Rule of the Contagious Animal Diseases Prevention Act likewise sets the quarantine period for imported dogs and cats according to microchip implantation and a rabies neutralizing antibody titer of 0.5 IU/ml or more.

Day-of-Vaccination Precautions and Adverse Reactions

A vaccine is a medicinal product that provokes an immune response, so the condition of the dog on the day matters. WSAVA states that vaccinating an animal that is diseased, hyperthermic or stressed is contrary to good practice and to the advice on most vaccine leaflets. For an animal on immunosuppressive or cytotoxic therapy (other than glucocorticoids), live vaccines should be avoided because they may cause disease, and killed vaccines may be ineffective or may aggravate an immune-mediated disease. After such therapy ends, a minimum of 2 weeks is allowed to pass before vaccinating.

On how often adverse reactions occur, WSAVA begins by saying that there is no definitive answer. In the data of one large US hospital group, adverse reactions of any kind, including very minor ones, were recorded within the first 3 days in 38 of 10,000 vaccinated dogs. The guidelines describe current vaccines as very safe and say that the benefit of protection from serious infectious disease far outweighs the risk of an adverse reaction, while adding that some breeds and families may have a higher risk.

The timing depends on the type of reaction. Type I hypersensitivity generally occurs within minutes of exposure, whereas other types (II, III and IV) can appear hours to months later. The allergic reactions that the guidelines give as examples are facial swelling, hives and itching. Symptoms that call for emergency care include facial swelling (swelling around the eyes and muzzle), hives over the body, repeated vomiting, diarrhea, difficulty breathing, unsteady walking and collapse, and these must be reported to a veterinarian without delay. The standards for the day of vaccination are as follows.

  • Condition before vaccination. A dog that is ill, has a fever or is stressed is not vaccinated.
  • Immunosuppressive therapy. A dog on immunosuppressive or cytotoxic therapy needs a discussion with the veterinarian before any vaccination.
  • Doses are not split. The dose of a vaccine is not calculated by body weight and the whole antigen load is needed, so even a small dog does not receive a split or reduced dose.
  • Keep to the route. Injecting an intranasal or oral live vaccine can cause a severe local reaction or systemic disease and is prohibited.
  • Emergency signs. Facial swelling, hives, vomiting, diarrhea, difficulty breathing, unsteady walking or collapse must be reported to the veterinarian at once.

When a dog with a past reaction is revaccinated, WSAVA says it should be watched closely for up to 24 hours, although such reactions generally occur within minutes. If an adverse reaction appears after vaccination, consult a veterinarian. What the signs mean and what treatment is needed are judged by the veterinarian who sees the dog directly.

Reviewing Vaccination Records and Preparing for the Veterinary Visit

The vaccination history is the basis for deciding how many doses are needed. WSAVA states that an adopted adult dog, or a puppy over 16 weeks of age, with an unknown vaccination history can develop protective immunity from a single dose of MLV core vaccine. Without records, unnecessary repeat vaccination can follow. Article 15, paragraph 2 of the Contagious Animal Diseases Prevention Act directs the authority to issue a certificate of the fact when an owner who had an injection under an order requests it, so an owner vaccinated under a rabies order can request and keep the certificate.

The living environment is the standard for choosing non-core vaccines. Because non-core vaccines are decided by a risk-benefit assessment based on geographical location and lifestyle, the region where the dog lives and its outing habits become the material for the consultation. Table 1 of the 2016 guidelines describes the Leptospira vaccine for dogs in areas where exposure risk is established or whose lifestyle puts them at risk, the canine influenza vaccine for at-risk groups of co-housed dogs such as kennels, dog shows and day care, and the Borrelia vaccine for dogs that live in or visit regions with a high risk of tick exposure.

A history of earlier adverse reactions is also material for the consultation. The guidelines state that if the suspected vaccine is a core vaccine, a seropositive result by serology means revaccination is not necessary, and if it is an optional non-core vaccine, revaccination is discouraged. For rabies, the local authorities should be consulted first to determine whether the vaccine is to be given by law. What an owner should arrange before the visit is as follows.

  • Vaccination history. List the vaccines given and their dates, and bring the rabies vaccination certificate if there is one.
  • Living environment. Tell the veterinarian the region, the walking places and whether the dog uses facilities where it stays with other dogs.
  • History of adverse reactions. Mention any facial swelling or hives after an earlier vaccination.
  • Registration details. Check whether the address or telephone number has changed, because changes can be reported through the animal information system.

Article 11, paragraph 5 of the Enforcement Decree allows the owner of a registered animal to report a loss and changes of name, address or telephone number, or a death, through the animal information system as well. WSAVA's approach is to review the health check together with the vaccination plan. Vaccination is completed when a veterinarian's examination, the guidelines and the owner's records are combined.
