LongCat-2.0: A 1.6-Trillion-Parameter Open Model Trained Without Nvidia

Meituan trained a 1.6T-parameter MoE model on chips believed to be Huawei Ascend - is real chip independence actually possible

Jul 3, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: A Trillion-Parameter Model Born Without Nvidia

On June 30, 2026, Chinese food-delivery and local-services giant Meituan released a large language model called LongCat-2.0. The headline number is 1.6 trillion parameters, but that is not what I actually want to focus on here. What matters more is Meituan's claim that it trained this model from scratch on a roughly 50,000-chip domestic Chinese cluster, without using a single Nvidia GPU.

Over the past couple of years, we've watched Chinese labs like DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi, and GLM pressure Western flagships on price and openness. This time the axis is different. The story isn't the model's brain — it's the factory that built it. With export controls effectively cutting off Nvidia's top-tier data-center GPUs from China, whether a near-frontier model can be completed entirely on alternative hardware is a question that could reshape the entire AI hardware landscape. In this piece I want to work through why a food-delivery company ran this experiment, what it actually proved, and what it still has not proven. Every fact and figure here is cross-checked against public disclosures and multiple outlets as of July 2026.

1. What LongCat-2.0 Is - Meituan's Trillion-Parameter Bet

Meituan is not a search company or a cloud giant. Its core business is food delivery and local-service bookings. It has quietly released models under the LongCat brand for a while, and this release marks a jump to a 1.6-trillion-parameter flagship.

1.1 Core Specs

According to public materials, LongCat-2.0 is a 1.6-trillion-parameter Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model, of which only about 33 to 56 billion parameters (roughly 48 billion on average) actually activate per token. It ships with a native 1-million-token context window. The model card states it trained on more than 35 trillion tokens without a single rollback or loss spike. That stability claim is hard to independently verify, but the fact that Meituan explicitly called out common large-scale training failure modes is itself worth noting.

1.2 Built for Coding and Autonomous Agents

LongCat-2.0 is not a general chatbot. It's positioned around agentic coding — multi-step work where a model reads code, edits it, runs tests, and iterates autonomously. That's the same direction recent Chinese open-weight releases like GLM 5.2 and Kimi K2.7-Code have taken. Given that coding and agentic workflows are currently the most contested battleground in AI, it's not surprising Meituan chose this as its first major fight.

1.3 A Prelude: Owl Alpha

What I find genuinely interesting is what happened before the official reveal. Multiple outlets report that LongCat-2.0 ran anonymously on OpenRouter as 'Owl Alpha' for roughly two months, during which it reportedly reached top-tier usage (reports cite roughly 10 trillion tokens per month). Developers kept choosing it in real workloads without knowing whose model it was, which I read as reasonably strong evidence that its practical quality held up outside of curated benchmarks.

2. What 'Trained Without Nvidia' Actually Means

Let's get to the core question this piece is built around: why does a single line like "trained without Nvidia" get this much attention?

2.1 A 50,000-Chip Domestic Cluster

Meituan says the entire LongCat-2.0 pipeline — pretraining through inference serving — ran on a domestic chip cluster of about 50,000 accelerators, explicitly excluding both Nvidia A100/H100-class GPUs and AMD's MI300X line. Completing a trillion-parameter-class model end to end on non-Nvidia hardware is still rarely documented at this scale.

2.2 Why Nvidia Was Excluded - The Export Control Backdrop

This looks less like a technical preference and more like a forced choice. Since 2020 the US has restricted Huawei specifically, and since 2022 China more broadly, from acquiring Nvidia's top-tier data-center GPUs (A100/H100 and their derivatives). Huawei has essentially no legal channel to buy Nvidia GPUs. So Meituan's "without Nvidia" reads less like a voluntary experiment and more like one company demonstrating a survival strategy that the entire Chinese AI industry is forced to pursue under real constraints.

2.3 What HCCL Points To - The Huawei Ascend Ecosystem

Meituan has not officially disclosed its chip supplier. But technical details in the training infrastructure show the use of HCCL (Huawei Collective Communication Library) for chip-to-chip coordination, which several outlets read as strong circumstantial evidence pointing to Huawei's Ascend chip family. I want to be precise here: this is circumstantial, not confirmed by Meituan. But the direction it points is unmistakable and fits squarely with Huawei's emerging role as China's domestic chip alternative for AI.

3. MoE and Efficiency - Why Only 48 Billion of 1.6 Trillion Wake Up

I think architecture choice is a big part of why non-Nvidia training was even feasible here. In this context, MoE reads less like a pure performance trick and more like a practical workaround for hardware constraints.

3.1 Why Not Activate the Full Model

MoE splits a model into multiple 'expert' subnetworks and routes each input token to only a subset of them. LongCat-2.0 activates roughly 48 billion parameters per token instead of the full 1.6 trillion. That means the model retains knowledge capacity across 1.6 trillion parameters while keeping actual compute cost closer to that of a 48-billion dense model. Decoupling parameter count (capacity) from compute cost (expense) is the core trick MoE offers.

3.2 Why MoE Pairs Well With Domestic Chip Constraints

Here's my own read: MoE is especially attractive when individual chips can't match Nvidia's top-end throughput. Huawei's Ascend 910C is reported to deliver roughly one-third the BF16 throughput of an Nvidia B200. When per-chip power is limited, there are really only two levers: scale the chip count way up (Meituan used 50,000), or reduce the number of parameters activated per token to cut compute demand. LongCat-2.0 appears to have pulled both levers at once — a massive cluster paired with a low activation ratio (roughly 3-3.5% of total parameters).

4. How Good Is the Performance, Really

No matter how dramatic the hardware story is, what ultimately matters is whether the model is actually usable.

4.1 SWE-bench Pro Against the Competition

Per Meituan's disclosures, LongCat-2.0 scored 59.5 on SWE-bench Pro, a benchmark built around resolving real GitHub issues across full repositories. That edges out GPT-5.5's 58.6 and Gemini 3.1 Pro's 54.2, and it reportedly also beat Gemini 3.1 Pro on Terminal-Bench 2.1. It still trails top closed Western models such as Claude Opus 4.7 and 4.8.

4.2 Is It Really Frontier-Class - Reasons for Caution

My take: a headline like "beat GPT-5.5" deserves scrutiny. A narrow margin on a single benchmark (59.5 vs. 58.6) shouldn't be read as an absolute ranking. Benchmarks vary in what they measure, and vendors can selectively highlight favorable numbers. What I find more meaningful is that during its stealth period, real users pushed Owl Alpha to top-tier usage with no benchmark scores in front of them at all — that's a signal I trust more than a marketing chart.

5. The Open-Source Label's Catch - Weights Aren't Out Yet

There's an asterisk here that needs to be stated plainly. LongCat-2.0 carries an MIT license and is billed as "open source," but as of the announcement, both the GitHub repository and the Hugging Face model card only said weights were "coming soon." The full self-hostable weight files had not actually shipped, while the paid API was already live and billed. This mirrors GLM 5.2's staggered rollout, where open weights followed the launch by roughly a week. It's a pattern that keeps repeating among Chinese labs: openness is announced first, and real accessibility follows later. Readers should keep the license claim and day-one usability as two separate questions.

6. The Geopolitics of Trying to Escape Chip Dependence

Let's zoom out. Why did a single model from a food-delivery company become semiconductor-geopolitics news?

6.1 China's Signal: "The Blockade Isn't Working"

US export controls aim to slow China's frontier AI progress by keeping Nvidia's top-tier GPUs out of the country. LongCat-2.0 is being used as proof that a trillion-parameter-class model can still be completed under that blockade. In my reading, the real significance isn't "China has fully replaced Nvidia" — it's closer to "the blockade doesn't fully stop frontier development." That distinction matters.

6.2 The Fact That ~75% Is Still Nvidia

It's worth resisting the hype here. Reports indicate roughly 75% of the chips training AI models in Chinese data centers still run on Nvidia's CUDA platform (mostly the export-compliant H20). Huawei's Ascend 910C is estimated at about 60% of an Nvidia H100's inference performance, and Huawei reportedly plans to produce around 600,000 Ascend 910C units in 2026. LongCat-2.0 is an exceptional success story, not proof that China's AI industry has already become Nvidia-independent as a whole. It's important not to mistake one milestone for an industry-wide transition.

6.3 What Open-Weight Model Proliferation Means for Developers

Zooming further out, LongCat-2.0 is the latest entry in the open-weight wave that runs through DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi, and GLM, but this time the axis has expanded to hardware diversity. The mere possibility that global AI development isn't permanently locked to a single chip supply chain has long-run potential to ease GPU supply bottlenecks and pricing power. But for that to actually translate into lower GPU costs or better access for working developers, the gaps I've flagged here — real weight availability, mature tooling, and independently verified benchmarks — need to close first.

7. Limitations and Caveats

Listing only the upside wouldn't be honest. There are explicit limitations worth stating.

First, the chip supplier is unconfirmed. The Huawei Ascend attribution rests on circumstantial evidence (HCCL usage), not an official statement from Meituan. Second, weights haven't shipped. Despite the "open source" label, the fully self-hostable weights were not available at announcement time. Third, benchmark limits. A narrow lead on one SWE-bench Pro number shouldn't be read as across-the-board superiority. Fourth, representativeness. With roughly 75% of China's AI training infrastructure still Nvidia-dependent, LongCat-2.0 is an exception, not evidence of an industry-wide shift.

Conclusion: Three Things to Check This Week

LongCat-2.0 is a genuine signal, but one that comes with several honest asterisks. Completing a 1.6-trillion-parameter model without Nvidia, on a 50,000-chip domestic cluster, is an achievement worth taking seriously. But the chip supplier remains inferred, the weights hadn't shipped, the benchmark lead is narrow, and China's AI industry as a whole is nowhere near Nvidia-independent. As of July 2026, my conclusion is that the most accurate way to read this event is not as "Nvidia independence achieved," but as "proof that Nvidia independence isn't impossible."

Three concrete things I'd suggest checking this week.

First, track whether weights actually ship. Self-hosting only becomes a realistic option once the meituan-longcat repository on Hugging Face actually publishes usable weight files, not a "coming soon" notice. Second, test it on your own workload. Don't lean on the SWE-bench Pro number — run it against your actual coding tasks through the API and judge for yourself. Third, track the broader chip landscape, not just this one event. Watch Huawei Ascend production volumes and how fast the performance gap with Nvidia narrows over the coming quarters; that trend line will tell you more than any single model release.
