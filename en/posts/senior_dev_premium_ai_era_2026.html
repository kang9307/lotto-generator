Will Senior Developers Cash In on AI's Mess? Testing the 5-Year Premium Scenario

If AI coding shrinks junior hiring and piles up complexity, will senior pay really spike - a scenario, tested

2026-07-18 Trends & Culture

Introduction: The Provocative Forecast Behind "We're Going to Make Out Like Bandits"

As of July 2026, one essay keeps circulating through developer circles. Software engineer Simon Stewart posted "We're Going to Make Out Like Bandits" on his blog, rocketpoweredjetpants.com. The title alone is blunt. Stewart predicts that over roughly the next five years, AI coding tools will shrink junior developer hiring, the AI-generated code that fills the gap will pile up complexity and defects, and as a result, experienced senior developers who can clean up that mess will command sharply higher pay. The piece made the rounds on GeekNews (news.hada.io) and sparked debate on Lobste.rs and elsewhere.

Rather than paraphrasing or summarizing Stewart's essay, this piece tests how convincing the scenario actually is and where it shows cracks. It shares ground with three pieces already published on this blog - Understanding Is the New Bottleneck, We Didn't Hire You to Finish Tasks: Kent Beck on What Juniors Are Really For, and How the Developer Role Shifts in the AI Coding Era - but here the target is the specific bet itself: that seniors are about to cash in.

1. The Author's Scenario - What Happens Five Years From Now

1.1 Where We Stand Today

Stewart frames this as roughly a five-year cycle and places 2026 at about the 1.5-to-2-year mark. AI coding models, he argues, already match or beat junior developers on a large share of tasks, and junior job postings are already shrinking as a result. At the same time, because AI tends to add code rather than consolidate it, he sees repositories ballooning fast.

1.2 The Problem Compounding Underneath

The next stage is quality. Stewart argues AI-generated code is duplicative, accumulates without refactoring, and carries a higher defect rate than human-written code - so bugs multiply as codebases grow. He grants that AI can currently absorb some of that complexity, but argues it has already outgrown what humans alone could manage.

1.3 The Coming Crunch and the "Bandits" Payoff

Here Stewart foresees two trends colliding. One is senior attrition - he points to a sharp spike in critical burnout as seniors carry the weight of managing AI-generated complexity. The other is a supply break: with junior hiring frozen, there's no pipeline of people who will grow into seniors a few years from now. Demand rises, supply falls. He reaches for the COBOL programmer pay spike around Y2K as the analogy, landing on a simple but forceful conclusion: in a market where demand rises and supply shrinks, prices go up. Seniors who can clean up the mess make out like bandits in this scenario - hence the title.

2. How Convincing Is This Logic

2.1 Data That Supports the Direction

As far as I can verify, there's real evidence beyond Stewart's personal observation that supports this direction. Multiple reports describe a real drop in the junior share of new hires in recent years, and research finds entry-level employment has declined specifically in roles most exposed to AI. Pay for AI-related work is also reported to run meaningfully higher than for comparable non-AI roles. The debt side holds up too - studies of organizations with heavy AI adoption show more defects per pull request and much longer review wait times, a pattern this blog also covered in Understanding Is the New Bottleneck. In short, the scenario's two pillars - shrinking junior hiring and rising AI-code debt - each have independent support.

2.2 The Power and the Limits of the COBOL Analogy

The COBOL analogy is vivid but imperfect. COBOL programmers around Y2K were a case of narrow, language-specific expertise colliding with a hard deadline - the year 2000 - producing a temporary spike. The "AI-era senior premium" being discussed now has neither a hard deadline nor a single language at its center; it's a much looser, longer-running shift in supply and demand. The analogy is useful for direction, but I don't think it's strong enough to predict magnitude or timing.

3. The Counterargument - Do Seniors Really Win Alone

3.1 AI Could Erode Senior Work Too

The scenario's biggest weak point is its premise that AI only makes messes while cleanup stays permanently human. But AI models are also improving quickly at refactoring, architecture review, and understanding legacy code - developments the scenario leaves out. If the skill of making messes and the skill of cleaning them up sit on the same improvement curve, there's weak ground for assuming AI won't also erode part of what seniors do today within five years.

3.2 The Long-Term Risk of a Broken Junior Pipeline

Even if senior pay does rise as the scenario predicts, that gain flows only to people who are already senior today. If junior hiring stays frozen for years, there's simply no one growing into a senior role five or ten years out. As this blog argued in We Didn't Hire You to Finish Tasks, hiring juniors is an option premium a company pays into future engineers. If the whole industry drops that option at once, the "bandit" seniors become an increasingly scarce, closed class, and the industry risks a self-defeating crunch where the senior supply itself eventually dries up. Stewart mentions this but doesn't dwell on it.

3.3 Does Complexity Really Require a Human

The deeper question is whether accumulating complexity truly requires a person, or just a better tool. As agentic workflows mature - agents that scan for repeated debt patterns and refactor their own output - the absolute volume of complexity that needs a human senior could end up smaller than the scenario assumes. Organizations are already starting to describe automated agent workflows built specifically to find and fix debt on a schedule.

4. What the Premium Actually Rewards - Understanding, Judgment, Systems Thinking

The detail I think Stewart's scenario misses is this: the premium doesn't attach to the title "senior" - it attaches to the ability to understand and judge. Understanding Is the New Bottleneck, published on this blog, argued that as AI makes writing code cheap, the bottleneck shifts to understanding for verification and understanding for participation. How the Developer Role Shifts in the AI Coding Era argued that this understanding ultimately condenses into judgment around problem definition, design, and verification. Stewart's "senior who cleans up complexity" is really just another name for the same judgment. But that judgment isn't automatically guaranteed by tenure or title - a ten-year veteran who's fallen into the habit of rubber-stamping AI output can be shut out of this premium, while, as covered in We Didn't Hire You to Finish Tasks, a junior who deliberately trains judgment can reach it faster than expected.

5. Career Strategy

5.1 How Juniors Can Fast-Track Senior-Level Judgment

Rather than racing to finish tasks quickly, the priority is training the habit of asking why something was built this way and independently gauging the risk in AI output. Not rubber-stamping AI-generated code, reconstructing "why was this change needed" for yourself, and proposing small refactors on your own initiative - these accelerate judgment. Letting AI handle repetitive implementation and investing the time saved into understanding the system is the most realistic path to surviving the scenario Stewart describes.

5.2 Where Seniors Should Invest

For seniors, the needed investment isn't writing code faster - it's the ability to review and coordinate the output of multiple agents at once, and to design systems so the whole team, not just one person, understands them. If an individual's cleanup capacity can't keep pace with how fast an organization's complexity accumulates, that senior's premium won't last either. The senior who actually commands a premium is less likely to be the person who personally tidies code well, and more likely to be the person who builds a system for the team and AI to manage complexity together.

6. The Organizational View - The Cost of Giving Up on Junior Development

Read from an organization's perspective, Stewart's scenario reads as a warning. Companies cutting junior hiring now to save short-term cost may find themselves buying scarce seniors at market rates five years out. Companies that keep developing juniors' judgment now are more likely to already have people who can absorb that premium internally by then. If this scenario holds, the winners may not be organizations with money to buy seniors, but organizations that never forgot how to grow juniors into seniors.

Conclusion: A Scenario, Not a Prophecy

Stewart's forecast is worth taking seriously as a direction. Declining junior hiring and accumulating AI-code defects and debt are both real, observable trends. But whether that adds up in a straight line to "seniors make a killing" is a separate question. Weigh in the possibility that AI quickly absorbs cleanup skill too, the long-term risk that a broken junior pipeline dries up the senior supply itself, and the fact that the premium attaches to judgment rather than tenure - and this scenario looks like one branch among several, neither purely optimistic nor purely bleak. Only one thing seems certain: whoever collects the premium five years from now, it will be priced on the ability to understand and judge complexity, not on how fast a pair of hands can write code.
