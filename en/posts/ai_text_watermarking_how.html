How Far Can AI Text Watermarking Actually Go?

The principle behind marking text with a vocabulary bias, and the points where using that result as evidence snaps

Aug 16, 2026 Tech & IT

The question changes once marks start appearing

In August 2026 the character of this question shifted quietly. The transparency provisions of Article 50 of the EU AI Act became applicable on 2 August, and Anthropic announced that from the same date new Claude models would weave an imperceptible mark into the text they produce. Google had already been running a comparable technique inside Gemini. Until now, asking whether AI wrote something meant reading it and guessing. Now there is a second option beside it: checking.

These two are not variants of the same activity. Guessing from style stays a matter of impression forever. Noticing that certain constructions recur, that paragraphs run to a uniform length, that connectives arrive on schedule, is a useful skill, but nobody should be disciplined on the strength of it. That axis already has its own treatment in how to spot the traces of AI in writing, so it is not repeated here. This piece is about the other side: embedding a mark and verifying it.

One thing needs saying up front. Marking text shares a name with watermarking an image, but not a difficulty class. Understanding why it is hard explains almost every limit that follows. Why short passages cannot be judged, why translation erases the mark, why only the party serving the model can embed one at all — these all grow from the same root.

What follows treats the mechanism and its boundaries at the level of principle. It does not describe procedures for weakening a mark. It ends with why acting punitively on a detection result is dangerous, and what paths exist other than marking.

What images have and text does not: room to spare

Image watermarking works for a plain reason: there is an overwhelming amount of room to change things a human will never notice. A picture 1200 by 630 holds more than 750,000 pixels, each with three colour channels. Touching only the lowest-order bit of every channel leaves the image visually identical while carrying tens of kilobytes of payload. The mark can even be applied afterwards, as post-processing on a finished file.

Text has no such slack. A 500-word passage is barely 700 tokens, and every one of them carries meaning. Swap in a synonym and the register shifts; adjust a function word and the sentence turns clumsy. Invisible characters are technically possible, but they vanish with one copy-paste or one pass of a plain-text filter. Above all, text has no container. A photograph is a file with somewhere to store metadata; a passage of prose is scraped off a screen and pasted elsewhere with only the substance travelling.

So text watermarking cannot be a coat of paint applied to a finished piece of writing. Exactly one route remains: intervene at the moment the writing is produced, in the procedure by which the model chooses its next word. That single constraint predetermines half of what follows.

AxisImageText
Room to change without being noticedFine pixel adjustment, effectively vastWord choice only, severely limited
When the mark can be appliedDuring or after generationOnly during generation, at selection
Payload per unitKilobytes in a single fileWell under one bit per token

The last row returns later, when evidentiary weight comes up. On the image side you can design a scheme where anyone verifies a signature with a public key. In the text-bias approach, only the key holder can run the test. That asymmetry is not an unfinished feature; it follows from the design.

Split the vocabulary in two: the principle without the mathematics

A language model writes by drawing words one at a time. At each position it scores the entire vocabulary, converts those scores into probabilities, and samples one. Watermarking steps in just before that draw.

Fix a secret key, and use it to split the whole vocabulary into two groups. Call them green and red for convenience. Then nudge the scores of the green words slightly upward. The word slightly is doing real work here. Push too hard and the model starts picking green words in place of natural ones, and the prose curdles. In practice the tilt is set small enough that a reader cannot feel it.

One further device is essential. The split must not be fixed across the whole passage, or particular words would recur conspicuously. So the split is redrawn at every position. The last few words act as a seed, producing a green-red partition valid only at that spot. The same word may land in green at one position and red at the next, depending on what precedes it.

Implementations diverged. The scheme Kirchenbauer and colleagues published in 2023 simply adds a constant to the scores of the green group. SynthID-Text, which Google DeepMind published in Nature in 2024, instead runs candidate tokens through a pairwise tournament. The surfaces differ; the goal is identical. Leave a skew in the record of choices while barely touching the quality of the prose.

  • The model is not retrained. Only the sampling procedure changes. That makes the mark attachable to an already deployed model, and equally easy to remove again.
  • Verification does not need the model. The key and the text suffice to run the test, so there is no heavy inference cost and bulk checking is practical.
  • Quality impact is managed at a measurable level. The SynthID-Text paper reports a live experiment on the Gemini production system, routing a fraction of queries to a watermarked model and an equivalent number to an unwatermarked one, and found no meaningful difference in user feedback across close to 20 million responses.
  • The embedding side and the checking side must share a key. That single condition is the origin of most constraints discussed below.

Detection is a statistical test, not a verdict

Verification runs like this. Using the same key, rebuild the green-red partition for each position, then count how often the word actually used fell in the green group. In unmarked writing green should land around half the time. In marked writing it lands noticeably more often.

The right analogy is a slightly weighted coin. A coin that comes up heads with probability 0.53 instead of 0.5 tells you nothing over ten flips; six heads is unremarkable from a fair coin. Flip it a thousand times and get 530 heads, and the picture changes, because a fair coin produces that result rarely.

Here lies the fundamental break from style classifiers. A classifier judges whether writing feels like AI, using criteria implicit in its training data, so nobody knows how badly it errs on prose it has never seen. A watermark test has an explicit null hypothesis. The claim that green frequency follows a stated distribution in unmarked text is written down, and the test measures how far the observation departs from it.

  • The false positive rate can be chosen in advance. Raise the threshold and the chance of wrongly flagging human writing falls. Targeting something like one in a million and setting the threshold to match is a coherent operation. No such dial exists on a classifier.
  • Raising the threshold is paid for in misses. Fewer false positives means more marked passages going unflagged. There is no setting that reduces both errors at once; the only remaining lever is more text.
  • Only the key holder can test. Publish the key and anyone can verify, but neutralising the mark also becomes easy. Real deployments therefore keep the key with the provider.

That last item defines the evidentiary character of the whole approach. A test result nobody outside can independently reproduce is an awkward species of evidence in a courtroom or a disciplinary hearing, because trusting the party that produced it is the only available check. Better algorithms do not dissolve this. It calls for institutional design instead: accredited verifiers, or some form of key escrow.

Why confidence collapses on short passages

Push the coin analogy a little further and the limit appears by itself. The smaller the tilt, the more flips are needed for confidence. In text, flips are tokens.

What matters is the shape of that growth. The signal from the mark grows in proportion to token count, but random fluctuation grows in proportion to the square root of token count. Their ratio, which is what confidence tracks, therefore follows the square root of length. Quadrupling the tokens only doubles the confidence. The failure on short text is a direct consequence of that relationship.

The practical conclusion is blunt. A paragraph is usually enough for a verdict; one or two sentences are impossible in principle. Impossible here does not mean detection technology has not caught up. It means the information needed to support a verdict was never present at that length. A better detector leaves this wall exactly where it stands.

  • The lengths people care most about sit inside this blind spot: comments, short replies, headlines, taglines, the one-paragraph personal statement on a job application.
  • A long piece mixed with human writing shrinks the usable sample by the same amount. What decides the verdict is not total length but the length of the span where the mark survives.
  • Anthropic help documentation states outright that very short text may carry no detectable mark. It is worth noting that the provider itself records this as a condition.

Why rewriting and translation weaken the mark

The bias does not attach to the meaning of the text. It attaches to the record of choices made at each position while producing it. That one distinction explains the entire robustness problem.

When a person rewrites a sentence, the word choices in that sentence become theirs. The skew that sat there is gone, and the sample available to the test shrinks. The wider the edit, the less remains. Translation is more fundamental still. Splitting a vocabulary in two is defined over a particular tokenizer, and rendering the passage in another language rebuilds the token sequence wholesale. There is no channel through which the original skew could travel.

None of this should be read as a technique for avoidance. It is a description of what the mark is attached to, and it is in fact the starting point for defensive design. Raising robustness means increasing the tilt, and increasing the tilt makes the model pick green words over natural ones more often, degrading the prose. That trade does not go away in principle. Robustness and quality cannot both be pushed to the limit, and that is an intrinsic property of the approach.

  • Switching models switches the mark. Have a different provider model produce the same content, and the first key detects nothing. If the second model marks, its mark is what remains.
  • Partial involvement still leaves a mark. Ask only for proofreading or translation and the model is still choosing words, so a trace can appear. Detection is therefore a signal of involvement, not of authorship.
  • Quotations mostly fall outside judgement. Lift a couple of sentences from a long piece and that fragment alone cannot sustain a test.

What a mark proves and what it does not

Laying those properties out by observed situation makes the evidentiary value legible at a glance. Reading the table, the thing to watch is not each row individually but the asymmetry between the two right-hand columns.

ObservationWhat can be saidWhat cannot be said
Mark detected in a sufficiently long passageThat model family probably had a hand in producing itThe model wrote the whole thing
No mark in a sufficiently long passageThis key does not confirm anythingA human wrote it, or no AI was used
No mark in a short passageIt is not a candidate for judgementAnything at all, in either direction
No mark in a translationIt cannot be used to judge the originalThe original was human-written
Output from a different provider modelThis key does not detect itIt is not AI-generated
Mark detected in heavily edited textA model was involved at some stageThe contribution share or the final author
No way to check for a mark at allOnly the key holder can answerAny independent third-party verification

Anthropic help documentation writes this asymmetry into its own wording. A detected mark means the content may have been processed by Claude and is not fully conclusive; and the absence of one does not mean the content was not AI-generated or processed. When a school or an employer starts treating something a provider hedged that carefully as a settled finding, the documentation and the practice have come apart.

The summary is one line. A positive is weak evidence and a negative is not evidence. Copying that sentence directly into policy would prevent most of the failures described below.

Only one side can embed: the structural constraint

A mark is embedded by intervening in the word-selection procedure. It follows that only the party executing that procedure can embed one. While a provider controls inference through an API and a hosted product, the requirement bites. Download the weights and run them on your own hardware, and the selection code is on your side of the line. Not enabling the option is the end of it.

This is not a hole; it is a consequence of the deployment structure. As long as open-weight models exist, a state where every AI-produced text carries a mark is not an achievable target. The tension between openness and control here maps directly onto the argument laid out in the open-weight AI regulation debate.

Regulation was drawn along the same line. Article 50(2) of the EU AI Act obliges providers of AI systems generating synthetic text to ensure outputs are marked in a machine-readable format. It requires those technical solutions to be effective, interoperable, robust and reliable, and immediately adds as far as this is technically feasible. That qualifier is the summary of this section: the legislators knew complete marking was not on offer.

  • The duty falls on providers, not users. The point regulation can grab coincides with the party that executes inference.
  • Machine-readable marking is not limited to the bias technique. Metadata, provenance information and content credentials count as other layers.
  • The more common marks become, the more their absence is misread. Text from open weights, from pre-marking models, and from translation all land in the same unmarked column.

The danger of punishing on a detection result

This is the section to be most careful about. Even with the principles and limits fully understood, once a result feeds straight into a sanction, the entire cost of error is borne by an individual. In front of a failed grade, a withdrawn offer or a terminated contract, the sentence this tool is not conclusive carries no weight at all.

Two things must be separated first. Most tools now used in education and hiring are style classifiers, not keyed watermark tests. They have no key and no explicit null hypothesis. Calling both of them detectors wrecks the reasoning. The measured limits of the classifier side are already well documented.

  • Liang and colleagues, writing in Patterns in 2023, ran 91 TOEFL essays by non-native writers through seven GPT detectors and found more than half misclassified as AI-generated. The same tools were close to perfect on essays by US eighth-grade students.
  • The authors explanation was perplexity. Writing by non-native speakers moves within a narrower band of vocabulary and syntax, so the model is less surprised. What the tools were measuring was language proficiency, not machine authorship.
  • OpenAI released an AI Classifier in January 2023 and withdrew it on 20 July of the same year over low accuracy. It identified only 26 percent of AI-written text correctly while labelling 9 percent of human writing as AI. That the builder shut it down says something about the difficulty of this axis.

The bias transfers intact to any non-English-speaking context. When coursework in English, English-language personal statements, or manuscripts submitted to international journals go through these checks, non-native writers are disadvantaged structurally. Because polished prose draws more suspicion, the perverse outcome is that the harder someone worked on their English, the greater their exposure.

Watermark testing is in better shape. Being able to set the false positive rate as a design parameter already puts it in a different class from a classifier. But every constraint from the preceding sections comes with it. Short text yields no verdict, edits and translation produce misses, only the key holder can test, and a negative is not proof of innocence. Decisively, even a positive signals involvement rather than authorship. A policy worth writing should state the following explicitly.

  • Never use a detection result as sole evidence. Confine its role to grounds for opening an inquiry, exactly as a plagiarism similarity score is handled.
  • Write the asymmetry into the rules. Include a sentence saying a negative is not automatically clearance and a positive is not automatically a violation.
  • State a minimum length. Exclude anything below a set volume from consideration entirely, rather than aiming a tool at a range where no answer exists.
  • Disclose the tool and the threshold in advance. A tool that will not state its threshold should not ground a sanction.
  • Put human review and an appeal route ahead of the sanction. Build in an opportunity for the person to explain how the work was produced.
  • Check for bias in your own data. Look periodically at whether flag rates spike for non-native writers or particular writing styles.

The alternative: recording provenance instead of marking

A mark is ultimately an attempt to answer where a piece of writing came from. For text there is a better-suited approach to that question. Rather than hunting for traces afterwards, attach the provenance while it is being made.

The provenance-metadata family points that way. Content Credentials, as defined by C2PA, attach a signed record of who made what, when, and with which tool. For images, audio and video this fits well. Text inherits the earlier problem intact: there is nothing to attach it to. That is why Anthropic documents C2PA for image-class file types while using the bias technique for text. Putting provenance metadata on prose requires the document format or the distribution channel to supply a container.

Records of the writing process run in the opposite direction. Detection tries to show that AI wrote something, while edit histories, drafts and commit logs let a person show that a person wrote it. That distinction is decisive. Every detection method examined here is incapable of establishing innocence, because a negative means nothing. Process records permit positive proof. They are not impossible to fabricate, but fabricating them costs something, which is precisely the point.

  • The institutional route changes the target. Instead of banning use and hunting violations, require disclosure of use and treat non-disclosure as the offence. It shifts regulation from an unverifiable fact to a verifiable act.
  • Redesigning assessment is the deeper fix. Moving from output-only evaluation toward submitted process, oral defence and supervised work reduces the need to detect anything at all.
  • Regulation itself asks for layers. Article 50 of the EU AI Act calls for a human-visible disclosure and a machine-readable marking, and the bias technique is one implementation of one of those layers.
  • Stacking layers is the realistic answer. Rely on a single mark and everything collapses at the point where that mark breaks.

What could not be confirmed

Collected in one place, the items left out for want of a sound basis.

  • The actual parameters and decision thresholds used by each provider. Anthropic has signalled forthcoming technical documentation on detection, but as of 16 August 2026 no public specification could be confirmed, and the same applies to the timing and terms of any publicly available detection API.
  • Real-world false positive and detection rates. These vary sharply with length, edit intensity and language, so a single unconditioned figure would be meaningless, and no published figures with stated conditions were found. Every number quoted here comes from academic work or provider statements, with its conditions attached.
  • Detection behaviour on non-English text. Vocabulary splitting is defined over a tokenizer and therefore must differ by language, but no published cross-language comparison could be located.
  • The full text of the SynthID-Text paper in Nature sits behind a subscription, so it is cited only from the abstract and from public reporting and commentary.

One closing note. This field is moving quickly, and the individual facts above are a snapshot of one moment. But the limits that follow from the principle do not expire. Short passages yield no verdict; replacing the record of choices leaves no mark; when execution moves, there is nothing left to enforce against. However refined the implementations become, those three lines stay put. The question worth asking when evaluating adoption is not how accurate the tool is, but who pays when it is wrong.
