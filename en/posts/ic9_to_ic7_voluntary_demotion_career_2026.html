Introduction: Stepping Down from a Top-Tier Engineering Rank

The tech industry career ladder is conventionally designed as a strict one-way street. Moving from junior to senior, senior to staff, and ultimately climbing into the rarified air of executive Individual Contributor (IC) roles is viewed as the default metric of engineering excellence. Each subsequent promotion ostensibly promises greater organizational leverage, elevated compensation, and expansive autonomy. Yet, after reaching the upper rungs of that technical ladder, one distinguished engineer deliberately requested to step down two full rungs.

That engineer is Philip Su, who moved from Microsoft to Facebook (Meta) where he reached the top-tier rank of Distinguished Engineer (IC9), before later joining OpenAI. In an in-depth conversation published on September 21, 2026, on Ryan Peterman's channel, Su laid bare why he actively sought a voluntary demotion from IC9 to IC7 (Senior Staff Engineer), detailing the trade-offs and structural forces behind that decision. This discussion avoids trite moralizing about promotion not being everything. Instead, it offers an unvarnished analysis of how the nature of technical work fundamentally alters at peak levels, and why broad generalists face structural headwinds as corporate scale multiplies.

Drawing directly from Su's reflections and the published interview transcript, this article examines the realities of ultra-senior technical tiers and the nuanced emotional costs of reversing career progression. Rather than framing this choice as romantic rebellion or mere personal idiosyncrasy, we explore the systemic dynamics connecting organizational size with engineering fulfillment.

1. Understanding the Level Ladder: The Weight of IC9

Engineering ladders at top Silicon Valley firms split into two parallel tracks: Engineering Management and Individual Contributor (IC). The IC path exists to allow engineers to expand their strategic and architectural influence without being forced into administrative people management. However, the higher one advances, the more severe the pyramid becomes.

1.1 The Structural Gap Between IC7 and IC9

In standard big-tech frameworks, a Senior Software Engineer sits at level IC5. An IC5 operates independently, delivers complex components, and leads small project initiatives. Above that lies Staff Engineer (IC6), followed by Senior Staff Engineer (IC7). Reaching IC7 is already a significant milestone, signifying multi-team architectural authority and broad technical stewardship across major systems.

IC9, or Distinguished Engineer, sits four full levels above Senior Engineer. As host Ryan Peterman noted during the interview, reaching IC9 is "crazy high, that's like four levels higher than a senior engineer." There are typically only a tiny handful of IC9s across an entire multinational technology enterprise. At this altitude, success is no longer measured by lines of code written. Instead, the mandate centers on defining cross-company technical vision, steering capital allocation, and guiding architectural decisions that impact hundreds or thousands of developers.

1.2 Establishing London and Rapid Ascents

According to background summaries provided by the host, Philip Su's career trajectory was exceptionally steep. He accelerated rapidly to roughly an IC7 equivalent at Microsoft, subsequently joined Facebook as an IC7, and was later elevated to Distinguished Engineer (IC9). His promotion to IC9 at Meta was anchored by his instrumental role in establishing and scaling the company's London engineering office.

Building an international engineering hub from scratch required cross-functional coordination, architectural breadth, and the agility to connect business priorities with engineering execution. As he objectively reflected that he was good at the job while leading the Facebook London site, that performance earned him the top-tier IC9 title.

2. How Work Transforms at Top-Tier Ranks: The Squeeze on Generalists

Philip Su's decision to seek a demotion was rooted in structural shifts rather than a fleeting personal whim. When reflecting on why sustaining IC9 impact grew increasingly unsustainable, he pointed directly to systemic context: "one is my environment changed."

2.1 Organizational Growth and the Primacy of Domain Experts

When Facebook was small, a generalist could handle everything from contract negotiations to coding and speaking to the press. However, by the time Su returned to the Facebook Seattle office, that office alone numbered over 2,000 people and the broader company had grown to approximately 30,000 employees. He illustrated this environmental shift by noting that upon returning, he spent six months fixing bugs on an internal tool.

Su observed that "as Facebook, the company grew, right? Senior ICs do a lot better, I think, in larger companies if they are domain experts." In a mature enterprise, driving architectural impact requires deep domain specialists tackling tasks like SQL optimization, moving SQL to SSDs, or migrating SQL into the cloud. Within a company of tens of thousands, the organizational footprint for a broad technical generalist naturally compressed.

2.2 Meeting Expectations and the Eye of Sauron

Compounding this structural misalignment was the relentless psychological pressure of high expectations. Su admitted: "I did tire a little bit of feeling like there was always so much pressure just to meet expectations."

He likened this experience to having the "Eye of Sauron" continuously evaluating his output. This was not mere publicity, but rather the company constantly questioning whether it was getting its full value out of him. Su noted that up until IC6 he never felt the Eye of Sauron, but began regularly feeling this performance pressure from IC7 onward. Operating at that pace generated profound exhaustion. According to the host's summary, he sought a demotion to IC7 because he wanted to return to hands-on technical work.

The structural drivers creating this high-level pressure can be summarized as follows:

  • The persistent evaluative scrutiny of the Eye of Sauron questioning continuous value delivery
  • The ongoing anxiety of becoming the slowest on a team or facing a performance discussion
  • Shifting daily focus toward complex stakeholder mediation rather than direct implementation
  • Corporate maturation favoring narrow domain specialists over versatile generalist leaders

4. The Psychological Tax of Demotion: Sauron's Eye and Being Passed

Electing to step down in level sounds like an uncomplicated, rational optimization for engineers craving simpler programming tasks. In tech discourse, developers frequently express a desire to shed political meetings in favor of writing code. Yet Su's candid reflection underscores that descending the ladder exacts a severe psychological toll.

4.1 Removing the Eye of Sauron

According to the host's summary, the demotion helped relieve Su from the pressures of high expectations at IC9. However, even with that burden lifted, the realities of organizational hierarchy and internal standing remained. In large tech firms, rank dictates decision-making weight, meeting influence, and access to key technical debates.

4.2 Watching Former Subordinates Advance

Su acknowledged that the most challenging aspect of the demotion was navigating interpersonal dynamics: "the other thing that was hard for me about getting demoted down to seven was more that feeling that as I saw other people who used to be below me rise above me beyond that level, that was very difficult."

Watching colleagues who used to be below him climb past his level was emotionally challenging. Su clarified: "I don't begrudge them their success … I felt wistful, I guess is the right word, which is like, I was not crushed that someone else was doing better, but I did feel a little bit like I once could do that work." Stepping down provided relief from those expectations, but required coming to terms with the wistful feeling of watching others advance beyond where he once stood.

5. Conditions of Peak Immersion: Tiny Teams and Immediate Impact

If reaching top-tier titles and organizational scope fails to guarantee lasting professional contentment, what conditions actually generate deep engagement? For Su, the most exhilarating chapter of his career was building the initial release of Facebook video calling.

5.1 Facebook Video Calling and the Three-Person Team

At the time, Facebook was still comparatively small. Over a six-week sprint in Menlo Park, Su worked from the moment he woke up until he went to bed, losing ten pounds in the process. Beyond engineering, he personally negotiated contracts with Skype and handled press communications. Working in that lean three-person unit, the code they built went directly to production and immediately touched real users.

He remembered feeling "so thrilling that I was directly impacting real users because Facebook was so tiny back then." That palpable, direct connection between writing a piece of software and observing its reception in the wild delivered a sense of meaning that abstract enterprise-wide stewardship could never replicate.

6. Sustaining Life Beyond Work: Mandatory Socialization and The Next Day

Su's reflections extended far beyond corporate ladders into existential psychology and personal equilibrium. Having previously stepped away from employment for a six-month sabbatical, he shared unexpected insights regarding the role work plays in daily stability.

6.1 Work as Mandatory Socialization

He characterized employment as a form of "mandatory socialization." While commuters routinely complain about meetings and office routines, employment serves as an automatic scaffolding that forces individuals to interact, collaborate, and converse with fellow humans daily. During the six months he took off after first quitting Facebook, Su recalled that because former colleagues were busy with work, it was nearly impossible to schedule coffee meetups. Of all his time without work, the best period was a roughly six-to-nine-month stretch when two former colleagues happened to be taking time off as well, allowing them to ski together three times a week.

He realized that "being in a situation where I can find regular socialization is probably key to me being happier." He also strongly recommended the book Four Thousand Weeks by an author named Oliver (whose last name escaped him during the interview), explaining that viewing life as roughly 4,000 weeks made time feel far more precious and helped him clarify personal values when making decisions.

6.2 Visualizing The Day After the Goal

Su proposed a pointed thought experiment for ambitious professionals: "first, visualize today's biggest goal … Then I would say, now pretend you've just reached it. What do you do next? If you have a good answer for that, then you're fine."

Engineers often channel all their mental reserves into an upcoming milestone: a promotion, an IPO, or reaching a specific net worth. Yet upon arriving at the summit, those without a clear vision for the following morning frequently experience disorienting deflation. Having a sustainable answer for ordinary mornings is essential to surviving peak achievements.

6.3 His Distance from the FIRE Movement

Addressing the popular Financial Independence, Retire Early (FIRE) movement, Su maintained clear personal distance. He explicitly affirmed: "the best parts of the FIRE movement, if I were to embrace it myself, which I never did." He noted that for his personality, the most rewarding aspect of FIRE would be the active pursuit of a savings goal, whereas actually reaching the "Retire" destination was probably the dangerous spot.

Reflecting on his career, Su admitted there were a few times where he biased too much toward being maximally available for his employer and not available at all for friends or family. While suspecting that prioritizing relationships might have impacted his career trajectory, he concluded: "I think also that I would have gladly taken that tradeoff."

The Art of Stepping Off the Promotional Treadmill

Philip Su's career narrative provides a vital counterweight to tech's prevailing orthodoxy of relentless advancement. The tasks waiting at the upper boundaries of technical tracks are fundamentally distinct from the creative craftsmanship that draws people into software engineering initially. Executive IC work revolves around governance, risk containment, and organizational mediation under constant performance evaluation.

Moreover, as organizations mature, technical value shifts decisively toward deep domain specializations. Cultivating the self-awareness to identify where one genuinely thrives—and possessing the courage to step down when external status conflicts with internal satisfaction—is as vital as the drive to advance. Stepping off the treadmill is not a simple escape; it carries the genuine weight of social comparison and status adjustment.

Whether an engineer chooses to climb, stabilize, or descend, Su's testimony offers a grounding compass. Knowing where your contributions create genuine fulfillment, and preparing a life rich in relationships for the day after the goal is reached, remains the only durable path through a demanding technology career.
