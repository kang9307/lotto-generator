What a Project Must Decide When AI Enters Its Contributions - The Third Option Beyond Ban and Neglect

There is no way to verify whether a tool was used. So the condition of the rule shifts from the tool to what the submitter certifies

Aug 30, 2026 Dev & Infra

Opening: The Patch Arrived - Should You Ask Who Wrote It

A merge request is open. The code is clean, tests are attached, the commit message follows the template. And yet the rhythm of the prose feels familiar in a way you cannot place. The maintainer now has two decisions at once. Whether to take the code, and whether you are entitled to ask who wrote it.

If that sounds like a matter of taste, go one layer down. The asset a project actually holds is not the code but the right to distribute that code under a particular license. Code can be rewritten. A history with blurred rights cannot. So the question of whether AI is allowed is not a question about tools but a question about rights, which is exactly why communities land in different places.

Let me cut the scope first. What follows is about what a project should settle in its rules when generative AI enters its contributions. The legal status of training data is a separate axis, the copyleft duties that attach at distribution are another, and how maintenance actually gets funded is a third. They sit underneath this piece as background rather than being repeated here.

This is an operations-oriented technical piece and does not substitute for legal advice. Copyrightability, license eligibility, and ownership under employment agreements resolve differently by jurisdiction and by the facts, so decide actual responses with qualified counsel.

What Was Decided - The Confirmed Scope

Start by cutting the trigger to its exact size. The Debian project settled its position on generative AI through a general resolution. Voting opened at 00:00 UTC on 15 August 2026 and closed at 23:59:59 UTC on 28 August 2026, following a discussion period that ran from 23 July to an extended 13 August. The ballot carried eight proposals plus None Of The Above, for nine choices in total.

Choice 5, Responsible Use of Generative AI, was adopted. Its opening sentence states that the project neither endorses nor prohibits the use of generative AI tools, and it goes on to require that all contributions, regardless of how and with which tools they were produced, satisfy the same standards of quality, correctness, maintainability, and legal compliance. What the text actually settles breaks down like this.

  • Neither endorsed nor prohibited. Tool use is given no special status of its own
  • The bar does not move. Existing standards of quality, correctness, maintainability and legal compliance apply regardless of how the work was produced
  • Understanding, review and testing come first. Contributors are expected to understand, review, test and where appropriate modify the output before incorporating it
  • Disclosure is encouraged, not required. Revealing AI assistance is welcomed but not mandated
  • Confidential material must not leave. Embargoed security information that is not yet public, private communications, cryptographic keys, credentials and non-public infrastructure material must not be disclosed to third-party AI services
  • Large automated actions need prior consensus. Mass bug filing, large-scale code modification and similar broad-impact actions call for prior discussion and agreement through the appropriate channels

The resolution summarises itself more crisply than any paraphrase can. Generative AI is neither exempt from nor subject to special rules beyond the standards already expected of contributors. That is not a new regime. It is closer to a restatement of where the existing regime reaches. Counting used a Condorcet-family method, quorum stood at 47.24, and only Choice 5 remained in the final winning set.

No.Proposal in briefPublished outcome
1Amend the social contract to ban LLM contributionsFailed the 3:1 majority - 0.560 (144/257)
2Allow AI-assisted contributions with conditions203 ranked Choice 5 higher
3Reject LLMs as far as practical, amend the code of conductFailed the simple majority - 0.765 (176/230)

What deserves attention in that table is not the winner but the size of the gaps. The ban-leaning proposals did fail their thresholds, yet Choice 1 stood at 144 against 257 and Choice 3 at 176 against 230. The decision is settled and the minority is substantial. Both facts belong in the same reading.

Why This Is Hard - You Cannot Tell Whether a Tool Was Used

Before the values argument, the technical condition. Code leaves no fingerprint. Prose gives statistical signal; code has a narrow expressive range to begin with. A sort helper, a null check, a conventional error-handling block look alike no matter who produced them.

So any attempt at detection fails in two directions at once.

  • False positives - a newcomer who read the docs carefully and followed convention gets accused. That misfire is what makes people leave and not come back
  • False negatives - editing a few lines by hand defeats any classifier. The cost of evasion is effectively zero
  • An undefined boundary - autocompletion, name suggestions, comment translation, grammar correction. Where does assistance begin? The harder you draw the line, the longer the clause grows and the more exceptions it needs

Here is the decisive fork in policy design. Condition a rule on something you cannot verify and the rule ends up resting on self-declaration. A ban is no different. You can declare it, but you cannot identify the violation, so enforcement leans on reports and confessions. Decide nothing at all, and when something goes wrong there is no basis for asking whose responsibility it was.

Recently adopted rules therefore sidestep the difficulty by moving where the condition sits. Instead of the unverifiable question of whether a tool was used, they condition on the contestable question of what the submitter certifies.

Three Options and What Each One Costs

A community facing this generally has three moves. None is free, and each protects and surrenders something different.

OptionWhat it protectsWhat it costsWhere enforcement snags
Outright ban Blocks unclear-provenance code at the source and preserves what the contributor record is meant to mean An unverifiable rule binds only the honest. The grey zone widens and the conversation moves underground Defining assistance. Include autocompletion and translation and practically everyone is in breach
Mandatory disclosure Lets reviewers scale scrutiny to risk, and leaves information in the history If the label reads as a stigma, reporting falls. Only honest contributors carry the tag and the extra scrutiny Non-disclosure cannot be established, so sanctions have nothing to rest on
Contributor accountability Adds no new rules and applies existing standards. The test moves from the tool to the person With no gate up front, the screening load stays with maintainers. Accountability is only ever retrospective A separate clause on bulk automated submission becomes mandatory

These are trades, not ranks. A ban lowers intake risk and accepts the gap between rule and reality; disclosure buys information and accepts adverse selection; accountability keeps the text simple and leaves the load in review. Which one a community picked shows which failure it finds hardest to live with, not that the other answers are wrong.

What Contributor Accountability Actually Means

The accountability model looks novel, but it is really a restatement of what contributor certification has always done. Many projects require one sign-off line on every patch. What that line asserts is set out in the Developer Certificate of Origin 1.1.

  • (a) I created it and have the right to submit it under the indicated open source license, or
  • (b) it is based on previous work under an appropriate open source license and I have the right to submit modifications under that license, or
  • (c) it was provided directly to me by someone who certified (a), (b) or (c) and I have not modified it
  • (d) I agree that the contribution and my sign-off are public and maintained indefinitely and may be redistributed

Nothing there is a statement about quality. The sign-off does not say the code is good; it says I have the right to offer this under this license. That assertion does not change one bit depending on which editor produced the text.

So the accountability model creates no new duty. It restates the weight of the signature that was already there. Exactly one thing changes in practice: the question you ask before pressing submit moves from is it okay that I used AI to can I state where these lines came from. If you cannot state it you do not sign, and if you do not sign you do not send.

The limits are equally plain. A signature is a mechanism for allocating responsibility after the fact, not a gate before it. A patch with a wrongly given sign-off still arrives, and the filtering happens later. How much later is the subject of the next section.

The Fear Is Not Quality, It Is Provenance

Debate tends to slide into whether AI writes bad code, which from an operations seat is aimed at the wrong target. Quality is not frightening. Bad code gets caught in review, and if not there then in tests, and if not there then in user reports. Miss it entirely and the cost is one more commit.

Code with unclear rights behaves completely differently. The problem surfaces long after the merge, and in the meantime the code travels.

  • It is merged and lands in a release. Reversal is still cheap at this point
  • Downstream distributions package that release, and corporate mirrors copy it again
  • It enters product images and container bases and ships to customers. From here it is a notification problem rather than a recall
  • Another project copies it and grows its own derivatives. The history is now a mesh, not a tree

Put simply: a quality defect is fixed by a commit; a provenance defect means rewriting history. Removing the passage is not the end. You must be able to show the replacement was produced independently, and trace every release in between. At scale that work is administration and negotiation, not engineering.

Understand that asymmetry and the conservative projects become legible. The Gentoo policy adopted on 14 April 2024 lists copyright first among its reasons: rules on the copyright of generated content are still emerging worldwide, which creates a risk of infringement and could weaken the project's own copyright claims. The QEMU code provenance document catches the same problem at the signature, reasoning that it is hard to sign the certificate honestly over material whose rights are ambiguous.

One thing is worth stating plainly. Whether AI output is copyrightable and to whom rights attach differs by jurisdiction and is still being worked out. No direction can responsibly be declared settled, and none is declared here. That very uncertainty is why projects are careful. They want to remain able to absorb whichever way it lands, not to claim they already know.

Where the Review Burden Goes

When producing contributions gets cheaper, the bottleneck moves to screening. The structure is not new. Bulk patches from static analysers and waves of typo fixes chasing first-contribution credit have been around for years. What changed is scale, and how plausible the patches look.

A sloppy patch takes thirty seconds to close. The problem is the patch that looks right and is wrong. Conventions followed, tests attached, explanation smooth, one premise off. The reviewer has to read the code from the beginning to find it. That is where review time overtakes authoring time.

Seen that way, the resolution's separate clause on large and automated actions has a clear context: the rule is hung on the shape of the load rather than on the tool. The levers projects can actually pull sit on the same axis.

  • Advance notice for bulk submission - anything above a threshold goes to a discussion channel first. It leaves a trace and can be contested
  • Reproduction steps required from the submitter - the contributor writes how to reproduce and what changed before and after. That box is hard to fill from code you do not understand
  • Issue first - agree on the real problem before looking at code. Purposeless patches get filtered up front
  • Split queues - typo, translation and formatting changes go in a different lane from behaviour changes, with different scrutiny. Publishing a weekly throughput ceiling alongside makes delay read as capacity rather than rudeness

Fairness requires the other side too. Tighten screening and newcomer intake falls with it. Most people whose first patch is rejected three times do not return. The path where a burden-reduction device ages a community is real, and here the policy question meets the sustainability of the maintaining organisation.

The Limits of Disclosure - And Why the Rule Still Earns Its Place

There is one honest objection to a rule that says declare AI assistance. Only those who follow it follow it. Since non-disclosure is undetectable, the rule imposes extra scrutiny and suspicion solely on contributors who respect norms. That is difficult to rebut on the merits.

Rules are still written down, because enforcement is not their only function.

  • They change the default. With no policy, asking is rude; with a policy, asking is normal. The conversation starts from a different place
  • They become the yardstick in later disputes. Having a document that says what was promised is very different from not having one
  • They give reviewers a basis. Spending extra time on a flagged patch becomes policy execution rather than personal preference
  • They shrink the I did not know defence. Telling newcomers what this community cares about is itself a job rules do

Reading the resolution in that light, keeping disclosure as encouragement rather than obligation makes sense. Make it mandatory and non-disclosure becomes a violation you cannot prove. An unenforceable clause erodes trust in the whole document; one rule that is never applied teaches readers that the rest may be ornamental too.

Look at what was kept as obligation and the criterion appears. Do not feed confidential material to outside services; discuss large automated work in advance. Both leave traces and both allow a violation to be identified. What belongs in a rule is not the most important thing but the most important enforceable thing.

Other Communities Decided Differently

Facing the same problem, communities have arrived at different conclusions, because what each is protecting differs. Not because one is ahead and the others behind. Narrowing to published policy documents, here are three side by side.

  • Gentoo (14 April 2024) - expressly forbids contributing content created with the assistance of natural language processing AI tools, citing copyright, quality and ethics. The scope is contributions to its own project, and it states that packaging AI software or use in upstream projects is not what is being blocked
  • QEMU - current policy is to decline contributions believed to include or derive from AI generated content. The reasoning is tied to the sign-off certificate, and discussion of exceptions is left open on the mailing list, to be documented if decided
  • Debian (August 2026) - neither endorses nor prohibits, applies existing standards unchanged, and places understanding, review and testing on the submitter. Disclosure stays at encouragement, while confidentiality and prior consensus for large automated work are required separately

Overlay the three and the dividing line narrows to one question. Where do you put the uncertainty? A ban chooses not to admit it and carries the gap between rule and reality. Accountability absorbs it into the submitter's assertion and carries the review load. Which fits depends on project size, downstream depth, maintainer capacity, and how a community defines itself. That is a judgment of values rather than of engineering, and each community makes it through its own process.

What Contributors Should Actually Do

Whichever way the policy goes, the standard left to contributors converges on one line. Do not submit code you do not understand. Whether a tool was involved is secondary to that. Code copied by hand creates the same problem if you did not understand it, and generated code you can explain line by line is already close to yours.

Six questions are usually worth answering before you press submit.

Question before submittingWhat to do if you stall
Can you write one paragraph on why this change is neededDrop the code and restate the problem first
Can you explain to someone else what each line doesDelete or rewrite the lines you cannot explain
Can you name the failure conditions and boundary valuesTurn the edge cases into tests. Defects usually surface here
Can you state where this code came fromIf not, do not sign. This is the one absolute rule
Do the attached tests actually exercise this changeRevert the change and see whether the test fails
Did you follow project convention and can you show whyRead the contributing guide and the neighbouring code

Contributing as an individual

  • Shrink the patch. A change beyond your understanding usually fits inside it once split
  • Read the contributing guide first. If the project has decided anything about tool use, it is written there. If not, the discussion channel is faster than guessing
  • Do not ship generated output as-is. Most problems get filtered out in the act of understanding and rewriting it
  • Flag your uncertainty in the request body. One line saying confidence is low here saves the reviewer a great deal

Contributing on behalf of an employer - where two policies collide

Internal rules and project policy are written without knowledge of each other. Divergence is natural, and treating it as a contest one side wins is how accidents happen. The answer is simple. Submit only within the intersection that satisfies both. Drawing that intersection takes a little preparation.

  • Does external contribution require internal approval? If so, through which route and whose sign-off
  • May internal non-public assets go into outside services? The resolution carves this out separately. Internal code, design documents and credentials all sit here
  • Is the sign-off made as an individual or on behalf of the company? Submitting from a corporate account and a personal one can lead to different ownership analysis
  • Does the contributing guide conflict with internal rules anywhere? List the conflicts and settle how to handle them before submitting
  • Do not settle divergences verbally. Only a written record works as a basis later

Interpretation of employment agreements and internal rules, and ownership of work product, resolve differently depending on jurisdiction and contract wording. What is written here is the skeleton of a process; the individual calls belong with your own legal counsel.

Conclusion: Three Sentences

First, a rule must be conditioned on something you can enforce. Tool use cannot be verified, so hanging the rule there regulates only the honest. Second, what a project protects is not the code but the right to distribute it, which is why a provenance defect is scarier than a quality defect: one is fixed by a commit, the other rewrites history. Third, the contributor-side standard is understanding, not tooling. You cannot sign for a line you cannot explain, and if you cannot sign you do not send.

If you do one thing, do this. Before opening your next merge request, explain your own diff out loud, line by line. Wherever you stall is a problem that precedes any policy. The rules are just that habit written down. Where organisational rights judgments are involved, though, this piece does not substitute for legal advice, so settle those with a professional.
