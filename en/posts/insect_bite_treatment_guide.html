Insect Bite and Sting First Aid - Mosquito, Bee, and Tick Response and When to Get Help

What to Do Right Now, and the Signs That Mean Stop Reading and Call for Help

Jul 26, 2026 Health

⚡ In a hurry? Do this now

  • Do not scratch. Broken skin from scratching is how most secondary infections start.
  • Wash the bite with soap and running water.
  • Cool it for about ten minutes. Wrap ice in a cloth; never put it straight on bare skin.
  • If a stinger is visible, scrape it out sideways with a card edge or fingernail. Do not pinch and squeeze it.
  • If a tick is attached, grasp it with fine-tipped tweezers close to the skin and pull straight up. Do not twist.
  • Trouble breathing, swelling of the face or lips, hives spreading over the body, or dizziness means call emergency services immediately (911 in the US, 999 in the UK, 112 across the EU, 119 in Korea and Japan).

If symptoms are severe or fail to improve, seek medical care without delay.

Introduction: You are reading this after the fact, so order matters

Most people search for bite advice only after they have already been bitten. At that moment the useful information is not which product to buy. It is what to do in the next thirty seconds, what not to do at all, and where the line sits between something you can manage at home and something that needs a doctor now.

This article covers only the response after the fact. It walks through how care differs by insect, which widely repeated home remedies are actively harmful, and how to tell an ordinary itchy bite apart from a genuine emergency, all in a question-and-answer format. Prevention, including how repellent ingredients differ and what actually works, is covered separately in the complete mosquito repellent buying guide.

The guidance below reflects points where authoritative sources agree, including the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) on tick bites, the UK National Health Service (NHS) on insect bites and stings, and the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency on tick-borne illness. Individual circumstances still vary, so treat what follows as a starting point for judgement rather than a substitute for it.

1. What do I do in the first thirty seconds?

Three steps, in order: wash, cool, do not scratch. This holds even when you have no idea what bit you.

Wash. Use soap and running water. The goal is to reduce whatever saliva, venom or secretion is left on the skin and to keep bacteria out of the micro-wound that already exists.

Cool. Apply a cold pack or a cool damp cloth for roughly ten minutes. Cold constricts blood vessels, which helps limit swelling, and it dulls the itch signal. Ice held directly against bare skin for a long stretch can cause cold injury, so always keep fabric in between.

Do not scratch. The hardest rule and the most important one. Nearly every case of secondary infection discussed later begins here.

One addition. If the bite is on a hand, arm or foot, take off rings, watches and anklets before the swelling arrives. Once the area is swollen they become hard to remove and can restrict circulation.

2. The mosquito bite is unbearably itchy. Is scratching really that bad?

Yes. The itch itself is your immune response to proteins in mosquito saliva, and it usually settles within a few days. The scratching is the problem.

Scratching breaks the skin barrier and carries bacteria from your nails into the wound. NHS guidance makes the same point: scratching breaks the skin surface and allows bacteria in, and a skin infection can develop a couple of days later. Adults with long nails and children who scratch in their sleep are the usual cases that progress to blisters, weeping and impetigo.

2.1 Practical ways to stop scratching

  • Cut nails short. Non-negotiable for children.
  • Reach for something cold instead of scratching. Cooling can be repeated as often as you like.
  • Cover the bite with a breathable dressing so fingers cannot reach it.
  • Shower in lukewarm water. Hot water tends to make itching worse.
  • Wear light long sleeves and trousers to bed to reduce scratching while asleep.

Whether to use a topical or oral medicine for severe itching should be decided with a pharmacist or doctor. This article does not recommend any specific ingredient or product, because some options are unsuitable depending on age, pregnancy and existing conditions.

2.2 My child swells up far more than I do. Is that normal?

Young children often react to the same mosquito with much larger swelling and redness than adults. It is generally described as an exaggerated local reaction, and the area around the bite can become broadly swollen and firm. It usually subsides over several days. However, if the swelling spans an entire joint, comes with fever, or involves a sensitive area such as around the eye, medical assessment is safer than waiting it out.

3. I have been stung and the stinger is still in. How do I get it out?

First, a distinction. Honeybees leave the stinger and venom sac behind in the skin. Wasps and hornets generally do not leave a stinger and can sting repeatedly. No visible stinger does not mean no sting.

3.1 Removal: speed beats technique

The widely recommended method is to scrape the stinger sideways out of the skin with the edge of a credit card or a fingernail. Pinching the shaft with tweezers can compress the attached venom sac and push more venom in.

But there is a common misreading here. Spending time searching for the right tool is worse. How fast you remove it matters more than how you remove it, so if nothing is at hand, flick it out with a fingernail immediately.

3.2 After the stinger is out

  1. Wash with soap and water.
  2. Apply cold, wrapped in cloth, for about ten minutes.
  3. Raise the stung area above heart level to help limit swelling.
  4. Remove tight rings and watches straight away.
  5. Have someone stay with you for at least an hour to watch for whole-body symptoms.

3.3 If you are near a nest, leave first

Bees and wasps can respond as a group when they feel threatened. Rather than swatting, the usual advice is to lower your body and move away quietly. Multiple stings from several insects are dangerous because of total venom dose even without any allergy, so medical assessment is sensible even if symptoms look mild.

4. A tick is attached. Can I just pull it off?

Grabbing and twisting can leave the tick mouthparts embedded in your skin. The CDC procedure is as follows.

  1. Use fine-tipped tweezers to grasp the tick as close to the skin surface as possible.
  2. Pull upward with steady, even pressure. Do not twist or jerk.
  3. Do not squeeze or crush the body, which can force fluids back into the bite.
  4. If mouthparts remain, you may try tweezers, but if they do not come out easily, leave them alone and let the skin push them out as it heals.
  5. Clean the bite area and your hands with soap and water, rubbing alcohol or hand sanitizer.

The CDC explicitly says not to use petroleum jelly, heat or nail polish to detach a tick, because such attempts may cause the tick to force infected fluid into the skin.

4.1 Do not throw the tick away

Keep the removed tick in a sealed container or taped to a card, or at minimum photograph it. If symptoms develop later, which tick bit you and when becomes useful clinical information. Do not crush it with bare fingers.

4.2 The removal is not the point. The next two weeks are

Korean public health guidance advises watching for fever, vomiting, diarrhoea and similar symptoms for about two weeks after a tick bite, and going to a medical facility immediately if they appear while telling the clinician about the tick bite. Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS), for example, is transmitted mainly between April and November by hard ticks carrying the virus, and reported cases have shown high fever in the region of 38 to 40 degrees Celsius along with nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea within two weeks of the bite.

In other words, with ticks, removal is the beginning, not the end. Write the date on a calendar or in your phone; if a fever turns up later, that note makes the connection far easier to establish.

If you have no fine tweezers or your hands are unsteady, having the tick removed at a clinic from the start is a perfectly reasonable option.

5. Are ants, spiders and centipedes different?

The core principles are identical. Only these extras are worth remembering.

  • Ants: some species leave a small blister or pustule. Do not pop it. Popping raises the infection risk. Multiple simultaneous bites with any whole-body symptoms warrant medical care.
  • Spiders: most bites stay local. However, a bite that darkens progressively or ulcerates, severe pain, muscle cramping or systemic symptoms needs prompt assessment.
  • Centipedes and similar: pain tends to be intense. Wash and cool, and seek care if pain persists or swelling grows.
  • Never saw the culprit? That is fine. The response is the same, and the emergency signs in section 6 are what actually matter.

6. Which symptoms mean call for help right now?

This is the most important section. A local reaction stays around the bite. A systemic reaction shows up in places unrelated to the bite. That distinction is the whole judgement.

🚨 Suspected anaphylaxis - call emergency services now

If any of the following appears within minutes to about an hour of the bite, do not wait.

  • Difficulty breathing, wheezing, a tight throat, or a hoarse or changed voice
  • Swelling of the tongue, lips, face or around the eyes
  • Hives spreading to areas far from the bite
  • Dizziness, pallor, cold sweat, feeling about to collapse
  • Sudden abdominal pain, vomiting or diarrhoea alongside the above

What to do: Call your emergency number and say clearly that you suspect anaphylaxis. Lay the person down and raise their legs, or sit them up if breathing is difficult. If an adrenaline auto-injector has been prescribed, use it as directed. Never leave the person alone. Go to hospital for observation even if symptoms seem to improve, because reactions can return hours later.

Even without anaphylaxis, some situations call for care without delay.

  • A sting inside the mouth or throat, or near the eye, where airway swelling is the concern
  • Multiple stings from several insects, where total venom dose matters even with no allergy
  • Anyone who has previously had a whole-body reaction to a sting
  • Fever above 38 degrees Celsius, chills, muscle pain, vomiting, diarrhoea or rash within two weeks of a tick bite
  • Infants, older adults, pregnant people and anyone immunocompromised, where a more cautious threshold is appropriate

7. It is oozing and the redness is spreading. Is that infection?

Possibly. Bacterial infection typically appears two to three days after the bite rather than immediately, and is especially likely where the skin has been scratched open, which matches NHS and other guidance.

Check for these signs.

  • Pus or fluid oozing from the wound
  • Redness that visibly expands day by day
  • The area feels hot and pain increases rather than fades
  • A red line tracking up the limb, suggesting spread along lymphatic channels
  • Fever or chills. A simple insect bite does not usually cause fever

Among these, a tracking red line or accompanying fever and chills warrants same-day medical care. Antibiotic treatment may be required, so waiting it out is not advised.

Prevention loops back to the beginning: do not scratch, keep nails short, do not pop blisters, keep the area clean.

8. Saliva, burning ticks off - do home remedies have any basis?

Mostly no, and some are clearly harmful. Here are the ones that come up most often.

Common home remedyWhy it is a problemDo this instead
Rubbing saliva on the bite The mouth carries a wide range of bacteria; applying it to broken skin raises infection risk Wash with soap and running water
Burning a tick off with a match or lighter Serious burn risk, and an irritated tick may regurgitate fluids, which is linked to greater pathogen transmission Remove with fine-tipped tweezers, pulling straight up
Smothering a tick with petroleum jelly, nail polish or oil Ticks breathe only a few times an hour, so smothering fails; it just extends attachment time and makes the tick slippery Remove it physically as soon as it is found
Pulling a bee stinger with tweezers Compresses the venom sac and can push remaining venom in Scrape it sideways with a card edge or fingernail
Hot water or a hairdryer to override the itch May mask itching briefly but risks burns and skin damage Repeat cold application as needed
Cutting or sucking the bite Adds tissue damage and infection risk with no benefit Wash, cool, watch for warning signs

A pattern emerges. The dangerous remedies nearly all work by provoking the insect or creating more wound. The safe response is the opposite: clean it, cool it, remove anything embedded quickly and correctly, then leave it alone.

9. Conclusion - three lines worth remembering

To summarise. First, the baseline for every bite is wash, cool, do not scratch. Those three are always right, even when you never saw the insect. Second, only bees and ticks add a procedure. Scrape a stinger out as fast as possible with a card or nail; grasp a tick close to the skin with fine tweezers, pull straight up without twisting, then watch for fever for two weeks. Third, symptoms appearing away from the bite site are the emergency. Trouble breathing, facial or lip swelling, spreading hives or dizziness means calling for help without deliberating.

One more thing: do not stop watching after day one. Pus, expanding redness, a tracking red line or fever is not recovery but a possible infection signal. Put differently, even a well-handled bite means the following few days are the more meaningful observation window.
