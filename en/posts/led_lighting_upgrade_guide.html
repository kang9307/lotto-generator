LED Lighting Upgrade Guide: How to Choose, Save on Electricity, and Install Safely

Lumens, color temperature, and CRI to ballast bypass and payback math, all in one place

Jul 14, 2026 Life & Practical

Introduction: Can Swapping One Bulb Really Lower Your Electricity Bill?

Replacing incandescent or fluorescent bulbs with LED lighting is one of the few genuinely practical ways to shave a little off your electricity bill every month, without any major renovation. The trouble starts the moment you try to actually pick a bulb at the store: lumens, Kelvin, CRI, and luminous efficacy all show up on the packaging at once, and it is not obvious which number should drive your decision. If you are dealing with an old fluorescent fixture, there is an added question of whether to keep the ballast or remove it.

This guide, as of July 2026, walks through why LED upgrades actually save energy, how to read a spec sheet and choose the right bulb, what to do about the ballast when replacing fluorescent tubes, how to roughly estimate your savings, and safety and disposal considerations. Figures used throughout are illustrative examples to aid understanding - your actual savings will depend on usage hours, your local electricity rate, and the specific product you buy.

1. Why LEDs Cut Your Electricity Bill

1.1 How LEDs Differ From Incandescent Bulbs

An incandescent bulb produces light by heating a filament, so most of the energy it consumes escapes as heat rather than light. An LED (light-emitting diode), by contrast, produces light directly by passing current through a semiconductor, wasting far less energy as heat. The practical result is that an LED needs much less power to produce the same amount of light. Multiple energy-efficiency sources describe LEDs as using roughly 20-25% of the power an equivalent incandescent bulb needs for the same brightness - in other words, about a 75-80% reduction. Lifespan differs just as dramatically: an incandescent bulb typically lasts around 1,000-1,500 hours, while LEDs commonly last 25,000-50,000 hours, which also means far fewer replacements over time.

1.2 How LEDs Differ From Fluorescent Lighting

Fluorescent lighting (CFLs and linear fluorescent tubes) is more efficient than incandescent bulbs, but it still uses noticeably more power and lasts far less long than LED. Fluorescent lamps are generally rated around 8,000-10,000 hours - a fraction of an LED's lifespan. Fluorescent tubes also tend to wear out faster with frequent on/off cycling, and their brightness can drop in cold environments. LEDs are generally far less sensitive to both temperature and switching frequency.

2. How to Choose an LED Bulb - Reading the Spec Sheet

2.1 Judge Brightness by Lumens (lm), Not Watts

For decades we judged a bulb's brightness by its wattage - 60W, 100W, and so on. But watts are a measure of power consumption, not brightness, and that habit needs to be retired in the LED era. The unit that actually measures brightness is the lumen (lm), usually printed on the package as a number like "800 lm." If you want the brightness of an old 60W incandescent bulb, look for an LED rated at roughly 800 lumens - and that level of brightness typically only requires about 8-9W of LED power. Going forward, check the lumen number first, not the wattage.

2.2 Color Temperature (K): Warm White or Daylight

Color temperature describes the color appearance of light, measured in Kelvin (K). Lower numbers look warmer - more red and yellow - while higher numbers look cooler, with more blue. As a general guide, 2700-3000K ("warm white") suits relaxing spaces like bedrooms and living rooms; roughly 3500-4100K ("neutral/cool white") suits practical spaces like kitchens and bathrooms; and 5000-6500K ("daylight") suits task-focused spaces like home offices and workbenches. Choosing a different color temperature for the same room depending on its use can make a noticeable difference in comfort.

2.3 CRI (Color Rendering Index): How Natural Colors Look

The Color Rendering Index (CRI) is a 0-100 scale that describes how accurately a light source reveals the true colors of objects, compared with natural daylight. Where color temperature describes the color of the light itself, CRI is an independent metric describing how well that light renders color. CRI 80 or higher is generally considered acceptable for everyday residential use, while spaces where color accuracy matters - closets for picking out clothes, vanities, kitchens - benefit from CRI 90 or higher. Some inexpensive LEDs do not list a CRI value at all or list a low one, so it is worth checking the packaging or product page.

2.4 Luminous Efficacy (lm/W): Comparing True Efficiency

Luminous efficacy tells you how many lumens a bulb produces per watt of power, which is a useful way to compare the real efficiency of different products. Typical LEDs deliver roughly 60-100 lm/W, and high-efficiency models can exceed 100 lm/W, while incandescent bulbs often fall below 15 lm/W. Between two products with similar brightness, the one with higher efficacy uses less electricity, so it is worth comparing efficacy or wattage directly when lumens are similar.

2.5 Base Type: Make Sure It Fits Your Socket

Before buying, confirm that the base matches your existing socket. The most common base for household lighting in North America is E26, a 26mm screw base; smaller candelabra fixtures typically use E12, and some small appliances and decorative fixtures use E17. Track lighting and recessed fixtures often use twist-lock pin bases like GU10. You can confirm the exact size by checking the markings on your old bulb's base or measuring the socket directly.

2.6 Check Whether You Need a Dimmable Bulb

If your fixture uses a dimmer switch, make sure to buy an LED explicitly labeled "dimmable." Older dimmer switches designed for incandescent bulbs are often incompatible with LEDs and can cause flickering, an audible buzz, or even damage to the bulb. The safest approach is to pair a dimmable LED with a dimmer switch specifically designed for LED loads, and to check the dimmer's minimum load requirement when running multiple bulbs off one switch.

3. Replacing Fluorescent Tubes With LED - What to Do About the Ballast

3.1 Type A, B, and C: What's the Difference

When replacing a linear fluorescent tube commonly found in offices and kitchens with an LED tube, the key decision is what to do with the existing ballast. There are three main approaches. Type A (plug-and-play) keeps the existing ballast in place and simply swaps in an LED tube - the easiest install, but the ballast keeps drawing power and will eventually fail and need replacing on its own. Type B (ballast bypass, direct wire) removes the ballast entirely and wires the fixture directly to line voltage, delivering the highest efficiency and one fewer part that can fail, but it requires wiring work. Hybrid Type A+B tubes support both methods, which is useful for a staged retrofit, and there is also Type C, which uses a separate external LED driver instead of the ballast.

3.2 Ballast Bypass Wiring: Doing It Safely

Ballast bypass (Type B) work means handling the internal wiring of the fixture directly. Before starting, always turn off the breaker for that circuit and confirm with a voltage tester that no current is flowing through the wires. If you have no wiring experience or are not fully confident, it is safer to hire a licensed electrician rather than attempt it yourself - especially in a commercial space with many fixtures to convert.

Safety warning: any work that involves handling wiring directly carries a risk of electric shock and fire. Unlike a simple screw-in bulb swap, tasks such as ballast bypass or recessed fixture wiring require turning off the breaker first, and a licensed electrician if you are not confident in the work.

4. How Much Can You Actually Save? A Simple Calculation

4.1 The Formula

Your exact savings depend on your electricity rate structure and usage pattern, but a rough estimate is easy to calculate. First, find the difference in wattage between your old bulb and the LED replacement. Multiply that by your daily usage hours and the number of days in a month to get the energy saved in kWh. Multiply that figure by the per-kWh rate on your actual electricity bill to get an approximate monthly savings. Rates vary by plan and usage tier, so use the rate from your most recent bill for an accurate number.

4.3 Upfront Cost vs. Total Cost of Ownership and Payback Period

An LED bulb can look more expensive than an incandescent one if you only compare sticker prices. But over the life of the bulb, it makes more sense to compare total cost of ownership (TCO) - purchase price plus electricity costs plus how often you need to replace it. Dividing the upfront price difference by your monthly electricity savings gives you a rough payback period in months; a simple screw-in LED bulb is generally reported to pay for itself in a relatively short time. If you are converting fluorescent fixtures to Type B and paying an electrician for wiring work, factor that labor cost into the payback calculation as well.

5. Why Fluorescent Lighting Is Being Phased Out - Mercury and Regulation

Fluorescent tubes and some compact fluorescent lamps (CFLs) contain a small amount of mercury, which has driven a broader international movement to phase them out. Parties to the international mercury treaty (the Minamata Convention) recently agreed to fully phase out fluorescent lighting, following an earlier meeting that targeted household CFLs for phase-out first. In step with this, a number of countries and regions have already restricted or are preparing to restrict new sales of CFLs and linear fluorescent tubes. Exact timelines and covered products vary by country and region, so checking your local regulations is the most reliable approach - but the broad trend is a useful practical signal: the longer you wait to replace fluorescent lighting, the harder it may become to find replacement parts down the road.

A used fluorescent tube contains a small amount of mercury vapor and cannot go in regular household trash - it needs to go through your local collection program or designated disposal procedure. If a tube breaks, do not vacuum up the debris; ventilate the area first, then carefully sweep the glass fragments and powder into a sealed container.

6. Safety Checklist for Installation and Replacement

Swapping a simple screw-in LED bulb is usually safe with just the wall switch turned off. But anything that involves handling wiring directly - a recessed fixture, or bypassing a fluorescent ballast (Type B) - is a different matter. Before starting that kind of work, always turn off the breaker for the relevant circuit and confirm with a voltage tester that no current is present. Use a stable ladder, let a hot bulb cool before removing it, and if you are not confident handling the wiring yourself, hire a licensed electrician - it is the safest choice.

7. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Does switching to LED really save a lot on electricity bills?

For the same brightness, LEDs are generally described as using only about 20-25% of the power an incandescent bulb needs, which works out to roughly a 75-80% reduction. Actual savings still depend on your usage hours and electricity rate, so plug your own numbers into the formula above for an accurate figure.

Q2. Should I judge brightness by lumens or by watts?

Judge brightness by lumens (lm), not watts (W). Watts only measure power consumption, not brightness. If you want the brightness of an old 60W incandescent bulb, look for an LED rated around 800 lumens.

Q3. What is the difference between color temperature and CRI?

Color temperature (in Kelvin, K) describes the color appearance of the light - lower numbers look warmer, higher numbers look cooler. CRI is a separate, independent metric describing how accurately that light renders true colors; CRI 80+ is generally recommended, and CRI 90+ is better where color accuracy matters.

Q4. When replacing a fluorescent tube with LED, can I leave the ballast in place?

You can choose Type A (plug-and-play), which keeps the ballast and is the simplest install, or Type B (ballast bypass), which removes it for the highest efficiency. Type A is easy but the ballast keeps drawing power and will eventually fail; Type B needs wiring work, so hire an electrician if you are not confident doing it yourself.

Q5. Can I just throw a used fluorescent tube in the regular trash?

No. Fluorescent tubes contain a small amount of mercury and must go through a designated collection point or recycling program rather than regular trash. If a tube breaks, ventilate the area and carefully sweep the debris into a sealed container instead of vacuuming it.

Conclusion

Switching to LED lighting is one of the few practical upgrades that can noticeably reduce your monthly electricity bill without major renovation work. The keys are judging brightness by lumens rather than watts, choosing the right color temperature and CRI for each room, and thinking carefully about how to handle the ballast when replacing fluorescent tubes. Use the formula above to estimate your savings and payback period, and always turn off the breaker before doing any work that involves handling wiring directly. Given the ongoing phase-out of fluorescent lighting, it makes sense to start upgrading now rather than waiting.
