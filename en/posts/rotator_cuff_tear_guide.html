Rotator Cuff Tears - Where They Part Ways With Frozen Shoulder, and What Decides Treatment

A joint that has stiffened and a tendon that has pulled off the bone are not the same problem, and the difference sets the order of tests and treatment

Sep 1, 2026 Health & Wellness

When a shoulder starts hurting, most people reach for frozen shoulder first. In middle age and beyond that word comes up often, and the two conditions do look alike from the outside. Both wake people at night. Both leave an arm that will not go up. But what separates them in the examination room is neither the intensity of the pain nor where it sits. It is how far the arm travels when someone else lifts it.

The distinction matters less as a label than as a clock. Time behaves differently in the two conditions. A stiffened capsule generally moves toward loosening. A tendon pulled off bone does not. This piece sets out what the rotator cuff is, what a clinician looks at to tell the two apart, and what actually decides the split between non-operative care and surgery. Frozen shoulder itself is covered in the frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis) guide, so only what the comparison needs appears here.

This article is general information. Diagnosis and treatment rest on a clinician's judgement. What follows about telling the two conditions apart is a description of what an examination looks for, not a self-test to run at home. Medications, injections, doses and the decision to operate are outside its scope. If you have symptoms, have them assessed in person.

They look alike, but time runs differently

Chronic shoulder pain narrows to a short list of causes: rotator cuff disorders, adhesive capsulitis, shoulder instability and shoulder arthritis. An evaluation paper in the American family medicine literature sets those four side by side and separates them by examination findings. The trouble is that on the symptom side, the four overlap heavily. The painful spot is similar. The worsening at night is similar.

What does not overlap is what the hands find. And among those findings, the one that splits the group furthest for the least effort is passive range of motion, meaning how far the joint follows when the examiner moves the arm rather than the patient. That single observation largely separates a joint that has stiffened from a tendon that has torn. Everything after it narrows down which tendon and how far the tear goes.

The cuff is not muscle. It is where tendon meets bone

The rotator cuff is a collective name for four muscles. The Korean Orthopaedic Association's public health material lists them as the supraspinatus, infraspinatus, subscapularis and teres minor, and describes them as rotating the arm while also keeping the head of the humerus properly centred in the glenoid. The Korean Shoulder and Elbow Society splits the roles: the supraspinatus raises the arm, the subscapularis rotates it inward, and the infraspinatus and teres minor rotate it outward.

The second function is the one to notice. The power that swings a shoulder through space comes from large muscles such as the deltoid. But for that power to lift the arm rather than shove it, the ball of the humeral head has to be held pressed into a shallow socket first. That is the cuff's job. So when these tendons tear, what fails before raw strength is the fulcrum of the lever. It is less that the force is gone and more that it leaks in the wrong direction.

And the site of the problem is not the belly of the muscle but the footprint where tendon attaches to bone. A pulled muscle settles in days; this does not. Work on partial-thickness tears attributes the poor spontaneous healing to hypovascularity of the so-called critical zone combined with continuing mechanical load. The Korean Orthopaedic Association likewise lists degenerative change in the tendon and reduced blood supply among the intrinsic causes. This is the place a topical patch does not reach.

Where it parts ways with frozen shoulder - passive motion

Adhesive capsulitis is defined in the orthopaedic literature as a painful, gradual loss of both active and passive shoulder motion resulting from fibrosis and contracture of the joint capsule. The bag around the joint has shrunk, so the arm meets a wall at some angle no matter who is doing the lifting. The family medicine evaluation paper describes the same picture as diffuse shoulder pain with restricted passive range of motion on examination.

A rotator cuff tear is built differently. The capsule is intact and the cable running through it has snapped. The force to raise the arm is missing, but the joint itself will often follow when someone else raises it. In large tears involving several tendons, patients may be unable to elevate the arm at all. A study of 100 such patients found this in 80 percent of cases where the supraspinatus and the complete subscapularis were torn, and in 45 percent where the supraspinatus, infraspinatus and upper subscapularis were involved. In the same study, loss of active external rotation tracked with infraspinatus and teres minor tears, and loss of active internal rotation tracked with the subscapularis. That is where the inability to reach a hand behind the back comes from.

None of this is a checklist to run alone. The two conditions are not rare together, and pushing a painful shoulder upward by force is a good way to make things worse. An examiner compares with the other side, holds the shoulder blade down to see whether it is cheating the motion, and varies the direction of resistance. The whole sequence is the finding. One angle is not. What is written here is meant to explain why the examination asks for those particular movements.

How tears happen - sudden and degenerative

There are two routes, and in practice they blend. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons patient material gives falling onto an outstretched arm and lifting something heavy with a jerk as examples of acute injury. The degenerative route is slow wear that occurs naturally with age. Listed risk factors include being over 40, repetitive overhead activity, the decrease in blood supply that comes with age, and the dominant arm.

Splitting them into two is tidy, but the common shape is a small injury finishing off a tendon that had already thinned. That is what the Korean Orthopaedic Association means when it describes cuff disease as arising from several causes acting together, and what the Korean Shoulder and Elbow Society means in naming heavy habitual work, heavy exercise, or repeated minor trauma as risks. A review of the natural history adds smoking, hypercholesterolaemia and genetic factors as influences on the development of cuff tearing.

  • Acute traumatic — the moment is identifiable, and weakness starts right after it. In a younger patient this shape moves the conversation along faster
  • Degenerative — no clear starting point. The phrase that comes up is that it has been building for months
  • Mixed — an ordinary movement becomes the last load on a tendon already thinned. In practice this is the most common route
  • Age — the single largest factor. That does not mean age excludes anyone from being assessed

Tears without symptoms are common

Here is the most important fact for understanding this condition. A tear visible on a scan is not proof that the tear is why the shoulder hurts today. A 1999 ultrasound study of 411 asymptomatic shoulders found tears in 23 percent overall. A screening study of 664 village residents found full-thickness tears in 22.1 percent, and 65.3 percent of those were asymptomatic. The same study noted that half of tears in people in their fifties, and two thirds in those over sixty, produced no symptoms.

A 2014 systematic review pooling 30 studies and 6,112 shoulders points the same way. Rotator cuff abnormalities rose from 9.7 percent in people twenty and under to 62 percent in those eighty and over, and the authors concluded that the prevalence in people without symptoms is high enough that degeneration of the cuff can be regarded as a common aspect of normal human ageing. The Korean Orthopaedic Association makes the same point in plainer words when it notes that the stage of the disease and the severity of symptoms do not always march together.

What this actually changes is the order of operations. Scanning first and then treating whatever shows up as the cause gets less reliable the older the patient is. So symptoms and examination come first, imaging second. That is why the orthopaedic guideline frames imaging as an adjunct to the clinical examination rather than a replacement for it.

The typical picture - painful arc and night pain

Pain usually sits, as the Korean Orthopaedic Association describes, at the front or outer side of the shoulder joint and runs down the upper arm. If tingling runs past the elbow, the neck belongs on the list of possible sources too.

The feature mentioned most often is night pain. The Korean Shoulder and Elbow Society writes that patients cannot fall asleep with the painful arm underneath and wake during the night. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons patient material likewise puts pain at rest and at night, particularly when lying on the affected shoulder, at the top of its symptom list. The pattern of coping through the day and suffering once lying down is common, and it is often what finally sends people to a clinic.

  • Painful arc — raising the arm out to the side hurts through a mid-range band and eases again higher up. It appears in diagnostic test lists under exactly that name
  • Night pain — worse lying on the affected side, waking the person. It is reported particularly often in this condition
  • Weakness at particular angles — a range where force will not load even without pain. Which direction points to which tendon
  • Cannot reach behind the back — restricted active internal rotation, which links to the subscapularis side
  • Crepitus — a catching or crackling sensation on movement can accompany the rest
  • Dropping things — the arm gives way in reach-and-lift movements such as picking up a mug or a pan

Partial and full thickness - where the plan splits

The difference between the two names is simple. In the wording of the orthopaedic patient material, a partial-thickness tear leaves the tendon attached to bone but thinner than normal, while a full-thickness tear means the tendon has come off bone, leaving a hole. Within full thickness, a partial detachment and a complete one are distinguished again.

The plan splits because of how much tissue is left. Work on partial-thickness tears reports the general convention that tears involving more than 50 percent of tendon thickness, whether on the articular or bursal side, are usually considered for repair, while less than 50 percent is generally debrided. The 2025 update of the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons guideline goes a step further, stating at strong recommendation level that in high-grade partial-thickness tears that have failed conservative management, conversion to full thickness or transtendinous in-situ repair can be performed. For low-grade and intermediate-grade partial tears, the same guideline records a consensus-level opinion that physical therapy can improve outcomes.

A misreading grows here easily. Saying that tears do not heal on their own is not the same as saying everyone needs surgery. Plenty of people settle down and regain function with the tear still there, which is what the section on non-operative care is about. What the fact actually changes is the expectation. Waiting for time to fix it and managing to protect what function remains are two different strategies.

What the examination asks you to do, and why

Physical tests come down to arranging a position that loads one tendon in particular and then asking the arm to hold against resistance. Arm forward at 45 degrees with the thumb turned down loads the supraspinatus. Elbow tucked to the side pushing outward loads the infraspinatus. The back of the hand at the waist pushing backward loads the subscapularis. Distinguishing failure from pain and failure from weakness is the point of the whole exercise.

The numbers show that any single test is weaker than it looks. In a 2017 meta-analysis of the shoulder examination, the highest diagnostic odds ratio for any full-thickness tear belonged to the supraspinatus test at 9.24, with sensitivity 0.74 and specificity 0.77, and the authors concluded that no single test showed superior clinical performance. A 2021 meta-analysis of posterosuperior tears found the Jobe test had the best pooled sensitivity at 0.77, while the drop arm sign had the lowest sensitivity at 0.38 but the best specificity.

So combinations are the answer. A 2001 Lancet study that compared 23 clinical tests in 400 patients picked out three: supraspinatus weakness, weakness in external rotation and impingement. When all three were positive, or two were positive in a patient aged 60 or over, the chance of a rotator cuff tear was 98 percent, and the combined absence of all three excluded the diagnosis. The 2025 orthopaedic guideline states at strong recommendation level that clinical examination can be useful to diagnose or stratify patients with cuff tears, but that a combination of tests will increase diagnostic accuracy compared to any single test.

What X-ray, ultrasound and MRI each see

The three are not competitors; they look at different things. A plain X-ray does not show tendon. The orthopaedic patient material states that plain films of a shoulder with rotator cuff pain are usually normal. They are still taken, to rule out the bone side of the problem: arthritis, calcification, fracture, and the indirect sign of a humeral head that has migrated upward in large tears.

Ultrasound and MRI see tendon. The Korean Orthopaedic Association describes both as showing the size and extent of the tear and how far the torn tendon end has retracted. The orthopaedic patient material adds that MRI also indicates how old a tear is and the quality of the muscle. The pooled figures below come from a 2013 Cochrane review of people with suspected tears for whom surgery was being considered.

The same review found no statistically significant difference between the three tests, and left an important caveat alongside that. Both MRI and ultrasound may have poor sensitivity for partial-thickness tears, and the sensitivity of ultrasound may be much lower than that of MRI. The authors also stated plainly that most included studies were small and methodologically flawed, so the strength of evidence is limited. That is why an ultrasound that showed nothing does not translate directly into no tear. The 2025 orthopaedic guideline positions MRI, MR arthrography, CT and ultrasound as useful adjuncts to a clinical exam and radiographs.

Where non-operative care comes first

The evidence for exercise is sturdier than people expect. The 2025 orthopaedic guideline states at strong recommendation level that in symptomatic small to medium full-thickness tears, both physical therapy and operative treatment produce significant improvement in patient-reported outcomes. In a multicentre prospective cohort of 452 patients with atraumatic full-thickness tears, a standardised physical therapy programme produced significant improvement at 6 and 12 weeks, and roughly 75 percent were still without surgery at two years. Those who chose surgery generally decided between weeks 6 and 12; few crossed over after three months.

Evidence directly comparing surgery with non-operative care is more cautious. A 2019 Cochrane review of nine trials and 1,007 participants concluded that it is uncertain whether repair surgery provides clinically meaningful benefit in pain, function or quality of life at 12 months. The same authors immediately fenced that in. The included trials studied mostly small degenerative tears of the supraspinatus, so the conclusion may not apply to traumatic tears, large tears involving the subscapularis, or young people. Repeating the conclusion without that fence produces the opposite of what the review says.

  • Starting non-operatively fits naturally with degenerative tears that have no injury history, small full-thickness tears, and low to intermediate-grade partial tears
  • The aim of exercise is not to reattach a torn tendon but to rebuild the shoulder's fulcrum using the intact cuff and the muscles around the shoulder blade
  • The 2025 guideline records that after repair of small tears, supervised physical therapy visits produced no greater improvement in pain or function than a single instruction session followed by an independent home programme
  • The same guideline does not recommend prolotherapy in full-thickness tears, and does not support routine use of platelet-rich plasma
  • Reassessing progress on a scale of weeks is the crucial part. Carrying an unimproving shoulder forward unchanged is the most common waste of time

What an injection does, and why repeats matter

What an injection does is clear enough. It lowers pain so movement becomes possible. The 2025 orthopaedic guideline states at moderate strength that a single injection of corticosteroid with local anaesthetic can be considered for short-term improvement in pain and function in patients with shoulder pain, and adds that injectable anti-inflammatories may be considered in patients who cannot tolerate corticosteroids. The load-bearing words in that sentence are short-term and single.

Repetition is where the picture changes. The same guideline carries a separate item stating that multiple steroid injections may compromise the integrity of the rotator cuff, which may affect attempts at subsequent repair. That item is graded as high quality of evidence. A 2020 systematic review covering 176,352 shoulders is more specific. Injections given within six months of surgery raised the risk of revision by odds ratios of 1.38 to 1.82, within a year by 1.12 to 1.52, and two or more injections in the preceding year carried odds ratios of 2.12 to 3.26, the highest in the set. The same review noted that injections given several weeks after surgery did not appear to increase retear rates.

Put together: an injection is a tool for managing pain, not a treatment that reattaches tendon, and if surgery is on the table, timing and count become part of the calculation. How many are acceptable and how far apart depends on the shape of the tear and the surgical plan, so no numbers are offered here. Feeling better after an injection is by itself neither evidence that the tear has improved nor that it has worsened.

When surgery is discussed, and how long recovery takes

A review of the natural history sets out both sides of the ledger. The conversation moves earlier for acute tears larger than about 1 to 1.5 cm and for full-thickness tears in younger patients, the logic being that repair should happen while it is still possible and muscle degeneration is limited. It moves later for chronic tears in patients over 70, for shoulders where atrophy and fatty infiltration have already made repair difficult, for full-thickness tears under 1 cm, and for tears that are not full thickness.

Where the benefit of surgery comes from is also written down. The 2025 orthopaedic guideline states at moderate strength that healed repairs show improved patient-reported and functional outcomes compared with physical therapy and with unhealed repairs. In other words, the benefit follows from the repair actually healing, not from the operation having been performed. That makes the factors that lower the odds of healing important. The 2019 edition stated at strong recommendation level that older age is associated with higher failure rates and poorer outcomes after repair, and a 2023 study of 94 small to medium tears found 20.2 percent failed to heal, with age and body mass index as independent predictors.

Recovery is not short. The Korean Shoulder and Elbow Society tells patients that from six weeks after surgery they can manage undemanding daily activities alone, with a return to normal work at six months. The 2025 orthopaedic guideline states at strong recommendation level that after arthroscopic repair of small to medium full-thickness tears, early mobilisation and delayed mobilisation up to eight weeks produced similar clinical and patient-reported outcomes. Retears become more common as tears get larger: a study following open repair of massive tears for a mean of 70 months reported a 44 percent retear rate at six years. That the retear group had still improved significantly over their pre-operative state belongs in the same frame.

Red flags - get seen without delay

The following are not situations to watch for a few days. If any of them applies, get seen without delay. The first in particular is a case where time directly removes options.

  • The arm cannot be raised at all after an injury. Complete loss of strength after a fall or impact means an acute full-thickness tear has to be ruled out
  • Visible deformity, severe swelling, or bruising spreading widely. Fracture and dislocation are checked first
  • Shoulder pain with fever or chills, or a joint that is red and hot. Infection is excluded before any other label is attached
  • Numbness or weakness spreading down the arm. If moving the neck changes the symptoms, the cervical spine is examined alongside
  • Pain through the night regardless of position, with weight loss or systemic symptoms. Causes outside the shoulder need to be checked
  • Sudden worsening of pain with fever after surgery or an injection. Procedure-related complications are checked

Conversely, pain that started gradually over days and rises and falls with activity is not itself an emergency. But if night pain persists over weeks or weakness remains, there is no reason to postpone being seen.

How waiting narrows the options

The best data come from a prospective study following 229 patients aged 65 and under for a median of 7.1 years. Each had a tear in one shoulder that caused no symptoms, and that shoulder was scanned with ultrasound annually. Tear enlargement occurred in 60 percent. Full-thickness tears were 2.93 times more likely to enlarge than partial-thickness ones. Survivorship free of enlargement for full-thickness tears was 74 percent at 2 years, 42 percent at 5 years and 20 percent at 8 years. Pain developed in 57 percent of shoulders, and pain development was associated with enlargement.

The muscle side matters more. In the same study, among 138 shoulders with full-thickness tears, 75 percent enlarged, and progression of fatty degeneration was seen in the supraspinatus in 33 percent and the infraspinatus in 29 percent. Tear enlargement was significantly associated with progression of fatty degeneration. This is the same picture the 2025 orthopaedic guideline draws at strong recommendation level when it says tear size, muscle atrophy and fatty infiltration may progress over five to ten years under non-operative management.

Why does that narrow the options? Once a tendon comes off bone, the muscle pulls it back the way it came, and a muscle no longer being used shrinks and is replaced by fat. There is then simply less tissue left to pull back and stitch down. And the change does not reverse. A study following massive tear repairs for six years reported that muscle atrophy and fatty degeneration did not recover even in repairs that had healed intact. A repair joins a broken attachment; it does not restore a degenerated muscle.

So the accurate sentence is this. Time does not decide the plan, but as it passes the number of available plans falls. This is not an argument for rushing to surgery. As shown above, most people do well without it, and there are clear cases where starting non-operatively is the sensible choice given age and tear pattern. But postponing a decision and choosing non-operative care are different acts, and the second one comes with a schedule of reassessment attached.

Four common misreadings

Certain sentences come up over and over. Seeing why each is half right and where the other half fails is a useful way to hold the whole picture.

  • Patches and heat will fix it — they may reduce pain. They do not reattach a footprint that has come off bone. The trickier part is that relief conceals progression. The finding above that pain development tracked with tear enlargement means, read the other way, that a change in pain is a signal
  • Rest the arm and it will heal — an unused shoulder stiffens. When the 2025 guideline reports similar outcomes for early and delayed mobilisation up to eight weeks after repair, it is describing a protected period, not an instruction to stop moving. An arm held perfectly still out of pain can add adhesive capsulitis on top, leaving two problems
  • All shoulder pain is frozen shoulder — the first fork is passive range of motion, which is the core of this article. Time behaves differently in the two conditions, so getting the name wrong throws off the whole strategy
  • It is just age — prevalence does rise with age, and the systematic review says so. But what that fact means is that a finding on a scan cannot by itself be declared the cause, not that people with symptoms need not be assessed. Those two sentences point in opposite directions

In short - what is actually being watched

A rotator cuff tear is not a condition of weakened muscle but a break at the place where tendon attaches to bone. That is why it parts ways with frozen shoulder on examination, and the fork is how far the arm goes when someone else lifts it. Examination comes first and imaging second, because tears without symptoms are common enough that reversing the order gets less reliable the older the patient.

Treatment mostly starts non-operatively, and the evidence supports that. But since tears do not knit themselves back, watching and postponing have to be told apart, repeated steroid injections can affect later options, and once a tear enlarges and muscle turns to fat the option of repair disappears altogether. That change does not reverse with surgery either. What has to be managed in this condition is therefore not only pain but the window in which options still exist.

To repeat: this article is general information, and diagnosis and treatment rest on a clinician's judgement. The figures cited come from studies with different populations and time frames, so none transfers wholesale to an individual case. The material on telling the two conditions apart describes what an examination looks for; it is not a self-test. If shoulder pain lasts weeks, or comes with weakness or night pain, have it assessed. If anything in the red flags section applies, seek care without delay.

What I could not verify

  • Korean national figures for the number of people treated for rotator cuff disease are widely quoted in the press, but I could not verify them against the source data from the national health insurance review agency, so no such number appears here. Year-by-year patient counts and age distributions are absent for that reason
  • The public-facing materials of the Korean Orthopaedic Association and the Korean Shoulder and Elbow Society contain no passage explaining the frozen shoulder differential in terms of passive range of motion, so that part rests on international literature. I could not locate a formal Korean society document on the differential
  • The 2013 adhesive capsulitis clinical practice guideline from the physical therapy literature refused access to its full text, so I confirmed it only at abstract level and did not cite it in the body
  • Recovery timing is given only within the range the society material states, namely six weeks and six months after surgery. Standard timelines broken down by tear size and age band vary between publications, and I could not settle on a single set of figures to carry across
  • The 2025 orthopaedic guideline comprises 21 recommendations and 8 options, and this article cites only the items it discusses. Recommendations on surgical technique and graft materials sit outside a general reader's decisions and are not reproduced
  • I could not find pooled sensitivity and specificity figures for how specific painful arc and night pain are to rotator cuff tears. They are described here as characteristic features only
