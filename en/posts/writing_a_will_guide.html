Introduction: A Will Is a Final Act of Care for Those Left Behind

When people hear the word "will," they often picture an elderly tycoon with vast wealth, or a plot device in a soap opera about a feuding family. In reality, however, a will is one of the most efficient tools there is for reducing the confusion and conflict your family may face after you are gone — regardless of how large or small your estate is. If you leave no will, your property is divided entirely by the order and proportions the law prescribes, known as intestate succession. That outcome may differ from what you actually wanted, and the gap can turn into a long-running dispute among those left behind.

A will is the act of fixing your wishes — to whom and how your property should pass — in a form the law recognizes. But it is precisely that "form" that is demanding. Korean civil law recognizes exactly five forms of will, and breaking even one requirement can void an entirely heartfelt will. This article is an informational guide that brings together, in one place, why a will matters, the five forms of a will and their requirements, the must-have elements and void traps of the most common handwritten will, forced heirship shares, probate and execution, and tips for storing and updating a will. It centers on Korean civil law, while repeatedly noting that every requirement and figure may differ by time, jurisdiction, and amendment. The provisions were checked on September 29, 2026 against the current Korean Civil Act posted on the Korea Law Information Center (Act No. 21454, in force since March 17, 2026).

1. Why You Need a Will

A will shines brightest when "what you want" differs from "the default the law sets." Without a will, succession is processed automatically by the rules of intestate succession — and in the situations below, that default may diverge from your true wishes.

1.1 Preventing Inheritance Disputes

A large share of inheritance disputes erupt over "what the deceased actually wanted." One heir argues they cared for the parents and so deserve more; another insists everything be split equally by law. A will reduces the very starting point of such quarrels. When your intentions survive as a legally effective document, the fundamental dispute over "what the deceased wanted" is greatly eased.

1.2 Making Your Intentions Clear - A Distribution Different From the Default

Intestate succession follows a fixed order and proportions. But real families each have their own circumstances. You may want to leave the family shop to the child who built the business with you, give more generously to a child with a disability, or leave property to someone who is not a statutory heir — a common-law spouse, or a person who cared for you all your life. Such a distribution is possible only through a will (a bequest).

1.3 Charitable Giving and Giving Back

If you want to donate part or all of your estate to a school, religious body, or public-interest foundation, you can do so by bequest in a will. Even here, though, the limit known as the forced heirship share may apply, so the plan must account for the minimum portion guaranteed to heirs.

In one line: a will is the only lawful way to turn "the default distribution set by law" into "the distribution I want." But it cannot cross the safety line of forced shares.

2. The Five Forms of a Will - At a Glance

Korean civil law limits wills to five forms: holographic (handwritten), recorded, notarial, secret, and dictated (the principle of strict form). The Supreme Court of Korea has explained that Articles 1065 to 1070 of the Civil Act set the forms strictly in order to make the testator's true intent clear and to prevent the legal disputes and confusion that would otherwise follow (Supreme Court Decision 2006Da25103, 25110, September 8, 2006). A will that departs from these five forms — for example, words left on a video or a messaging-app text — is hard to recognize as a legal will on its own. The table below compares the five forms.

Below is a walk through the requirements, strengths, and weaknesses of each form. The two used most in practice are the holographic will and the notarial will.

3. The Holographic Will - The Simplest, Yet the Most Demanding

A holographic will is one the testator writes entirely by hand. It needs no witness and no notary, and costs nothing, so it is the most accessible option. But that very simplicity also makes it the form most often voided for breaching a formal requirement.

3.1 Mandatory Requirements - All Five, By Hand

Article 1066(1) of the Korean Civil Act provides that a will by holographic document requires the testator to write the full text, the date, the address, and the name in their own hand and to affix a seal. All five of the following must be present.

  • Handwriting the full text: the entire content of the will must be written by the testator's own hand. Korea's official Easy Law guide states that typing and printing it, having another person write it, or using a photocopy makes it void.
  • The date: the date of writing must be recorded down to the year, month, and day. The guide states that a will giving only the year and month, without the day, has no effect. Because the date determines priority when several wills exist, it is critical.
  • The address: the testator must write their address, and the guide states that a will without it has no effect. The address need not be the registered one — the place that is the base of daily life is enough — and, as long as the document is recognized as one whole, it may be written on the envelope holding the full text rather than on the same sheet.
  • The name: the testator writes their own name so that whose will it is becomes clear.
  • The seal: a seal must be affixed. The Supreme Court held that a will without the testator's seal has no effect as a holographic will (Supreme Court Decision 2006Da12848, October 25, 2007). On the other hand, it held that a thumbprint is valid as the seal (Supreme Court Decision 97Da38503, May 29, 1998).
A holographic will can be entirely void if even one of the five — full text, date, address, name, plus seal — is missing. In particular, a missing "day," a missing address, and a missing seal are described as typical defects that deprive a will of effect.

3.2 Practical Drafting Tips

To reduce misunderstanding, write clearly with a pen, and if a correction is needed, do not scribble over it carelessly — rewriting is safer. When you must change the text, Article 1066(2) requires that any insertion, deletion, or alteration be handwritten by the testator and sealed. State plainly who receives what, in lean prose, and after writing, keep the will somewhere safe while letting a trusted person know its location so it can be found after death.

4. The Notarial Will - The Safest, But Needs Cost and Witnesses

A notarial will is one in which the testator states the substance of the will by mouth (dictation) before a notary with two witnesses present; the notary writes it down and reads it aloud, and the testator and witnesses approve its accuracy and each sign or affix a name and seal (Civil Act Art. 1068). It incurs a notarization fee.

4.1 Advantages - No Probate, Protection Against Forgery

The greatest advantage of the notarial will is that it need not go through court probate (Art. 1091(2)). Because a public expert — the notary — is involved in drafting, the risk of being voided for a formal defect is small, and the original is kept at the notarial office, greatly reducing the risk of loss, forgery, or alteration. It is especially recommended when you want to minimize the chance of post-death disputes or when the estate is large.

4.2 Beware of Disqualified Witnesses

Not just anyone can serve as a witness to a will. Under Article 1072(1), minors, persons under adult guardianship or limited guardianship, and a person who will benefit from the will, together with their spouse and lineal blood relatives, cannot be witnesses. For a notarial will, persons disqualified under the Notary Public Act also cannot be witnesses (Art. 1072(2)). Putting an interested person forward as a witness can shake the will itself, so witness selection deserves care.

4.3 Something Only a Notarial Will Can Do - Declaring Loss of Inheritance Rights

The current Article 1004-2 of the Civil Act (declaration of loss of inheritance rights) provides that, where a person who would become an heir has seriously breached a duty to support the deceased, or has committed a serious crime against, or otherwise gravely mistreated, the deceased or the deceased's spouse or lineal blood relatives, the deceased may declare an intention that the person lose inheritance rights by a will in notarial form under Article 1068. Note that the requirement is a notarial will, not a holographic or other form, and that a person who would lose inheritance rights under such a will cannot serve as its executor (Art. 1004-2(2)). As the article's title indicates, the loss requires a court declaration, so whether the grounds are actually recognized differs from case to case. How this system relates to forced shares is covered in the forced heirship guide.

5. The Recorded Will

A recorded will is one in which the testator states by mouth the substance of the will, their name, and the date, and a participating witness states the accuracy of the will and their own name (Civil Act Art. 1067). It can be used when writing by hand is difficult. The article does not fix a number of witnesses — it says only "a witness participating" — so one or more witnesses suffice.

That said, audio and video files can be damaged depending on the storage and playback environment, or give rise to authenticity disputes, and after the testator's death whoever holds or finds the recording must submit it to the court for probate (Art. 1091(1)). The witness-disqualification rules noted above apply equally to recorded wills. Convenient as it looks, it demands attention in terms of post-death proof and storage.

6. The Secret Will

A secret will is used when you want to keep the contents secret until death while still securing the will's existence and authenticity (Civil Act Art. 1069). The procedure runs as follows.

  • The testator prepares a document on which the writer's name is entered. The article does not require the full text to be handwritten; this is all it says.
  • The document is strictly sealed and stamped.
  • It is submitted before two or more witnesses as the testator's own will, the date of submission is written on the surface of the sealed package, and the testator and witnesses each sign or affix a name and seal.
  • Within five days from the date on the surface, it must be submitted to a notary or a court clerk to obtain a fixed-date stamp on the seal (Art. 1069(2)).

It is also worth knowing the conversion rule: if a secret will is defective in its form but the document meets the form of a holographic will, it is deemed a holographic will (Civil Act Art. 1071).

7. The Dictated Will - An Exception for Urgent Situations

A dictated will is an exceptional, emergency form used when illness or another urgent cause makes the previous four forms impossible (Civil Act Art. 1070(1)). With two or more witnesses present, the testator states the substance of the will by mouth to one of them; that person writes it down and reads it aloud, its accuracy is approved, and each signs or affixes a name and seal.

Because the dictated will rests on urgency, the after-the-fact controls are strict. A witness or interested party must apply to the court for probate within seven days from the day the urgent cause ends (Art. 1070(2)). This probate is separate from the post-death probate of Article 1091, which does not apply to dictated wills. So if another form is feasible, there is no reason to choose the dictated form; it is best understood purely as a last-resort measure.

8. Common Reasons Wills Are Voided

Most reasons a will is voided lie not in its "content" but in its "form." Here are the frequently appearing grounds for invalidity or dispute.

  • Breach of the handwriting requirement: a holographic will that was typed and printed, or written by another person.
  • Missing or unclear date: the "day" of the date was not written, or it is unspecified, such as "on an auspicious day in year ____."
  • Missing address: the address was not written in a holographic will.
  • Missing seal: no seal was affixed. A will without a seal was held to have no effect (Supreme Court Decision 2006Da12848).
  • Disqualified witness: a minor, a person under adult or limited guardianship, or a beneficiary or their spouse or lineal blood relative was made a witness (Civil Act Art. 1072).
  • Lack of testamentary capacity: it is recognized that the testator lacked mental capacity when writing. A person who has not reached 17 cannot make a will (Civil Act Art. 1061).
  • Forgery, alteration, or duress: the will was not based on the testator's free will.
Key point: most invalidity stems from "defects of form." Even though it costs money, drafting a notarial will greatly reduces these formal risks.

9. Forced Heirship Shares - The Minimum a Will Cannot Cross

Even if you decide in a will that "all my property goes to one person," certain heirs retain a legally guaranteed minimum. This is the forced heirship share (yuryubun in Korea). It is the minimum proportion of an estate that statutory heirs can secure despite a will or lifetime gifts.

9.1 The Ratios

The current Article 1112 of the Korean Civil Act sets the forced share by class of holder as follows.

  • The deceased's descendants (children, etc.) and spouse: one-half of the statutory share
  • The deceased's ascendants (parents, etc.): one-third of the statutory share

Siblings were once granted a forced share too (one-third of the statutory share), but the Constitutional Court held item 4 of Article 1112, which gave siblings that share, unconstitutional in its decision 2020Hun-Ga4 et al. of April 25, 2024, and the item was deleted by the Civil Act amendment of September 20, 2024 (Act No. 20432). For items 1 to 3, which the same decision found nonconforming to the Constitution, the amended Civil Act in force from March 17, 2026 (Act No. 21454) reflected the decision. Because the system has changed several times, checking the current law is essential.

9.2 The Deadline for a Forced-Share Claim

A holder whose forced share is infringed may claim restitution up to the extent of the infringement. But the claim is extinguished by prescription if not made within one year from the time the holder learned of both the commencement of succession and the gift or bequest to be restored, and likewise once ten years have passed since succession commenced (Civil Act Art. 1117). Even with the right, missing the deadline makes recovery difficult, so managing the timing matters.

9.3 Where the 2026 Amendment Touches Wills

The proviso to amended Article 1008 provides that where a gift or bequest to a co-heir was made as compensation for specially supporting the deceased through cohabitation, nursing, or other means over a considerable period, or for specially contributing to maintaining or increasing the deceased's property, it is not treated as a special benefit to the extent corresponding to the contribution. This bears directly on leaving more by will to a child who provided care or helped build the estate. In addition, the method of restoring forced shares has shifted toward a claim for payment of value (money), and when each new rule applies depends on the supplementary provisions, article by article. See the forced heirship guide for the details, and review any actual plan with a professional based on the date succession opens.

Planning tip: even a will that funnels property to one person will spark a dispute if a forced-share holder seeks restitution. Designing the distribution with forced shares in mind in advance greatly reduces later conflict. For renunciation and qualified acceptance from the heirs' side, continue with the guide to renunciation, qualified acceptance, and forced shares and the guide to inheritance renunciation vs limited acceptance.

10. Probate and Execution of a Will

When the testator dies, the will takes effect and moves to the stage of being realized in practice. Here you need to understand two procedures: "probate" and "execution."

10.1 Probate - A Procedure to Confirm and Preserve the Document

Probate is a procedure the court performs to confirm the form and condition of the will and preserve its current state. Under Article 1091(1), a person who holds or finds a will document or recording must, without delay after the testator's death, submit it to the court and seek probate. This provision does not apply to notarial or dictated wills (Art. 1091(2)). A notarial will needs no probate, and a dictated will is separately submitted for probate within seven days after the urgent cause ends, as seen above. When the court opens a sealed will, the testator's heirs, their representatives, or other interested parties must be present (Art. 1092). Note an important caveat: probate merely confirms and preserves the fact that the will exists in form; it does not establish that the will's contents are valid. A will with grounds for invalidity can still be contested even after probate.

10.2 The Executor - The Person Who Carries Out the Will

Execution of a will is the procedure that actually realizes its contents — transferring title to real estate, withdrawing deposits, carrying out bequests, and so on. The testator may name an executor in the will; if none is named, an heir becomes the executor or the family court may appoint one. The more dispute-prone the matter, the smoother execution tends to be when a trusted third party (such as a lawyer) is named as executor. However, in a will declaring loss of inheritance rights, the person who would lose those rights cannot be the executor (Civil Act Art. 1004-2(2)).

11. Practical Tips for Writing, Storing, and Updating a Will

Just as important as meeting formal requirements is "storing it well and updating it in time."

11.1 Which Form to Choose

If you value simplicity and low cost and the content is straightforward, a holographic will is an option. But because the risk of a formal defect is high, when the estate is large or disputes among heirs are expected, a notarial will is recommended. The notarial will needs no probate and carries little risk of forgery or alteration, making it the most stable after death. The fact that a declaration of loss of inheritance rights must be made in a notarial will also affects the choice.

11.2 Storage - A Will No One Finds Is Meaningless

However perfect a will is, it is useless if it is not found after death. Keep a holographic will somewhere safe, but let a trusted person — a spouse, child, or the executor — know its location. A notarial will's original is kept at the notarial office, so there is little worry of loss.

11.3 Updating - When Life Changes, So Does the Will

You may freely revoke or amend a will during your lifetime. It is wise to revisit your will at life's turning points — marriage or divorce, the birth of a child, changes in the makeup of your assets. If a later will conflicts with an earlier one, that part is treated as revoked by the later will, and a lifetime disposition that contradicts the will revokes that part too. So always record the date clearly so that, if several wills surface, their order can be determined. In a year like 2026, when the inheritance provisions of the Civil Act changed, it is worth checking again that an existing will still works as intended under the new rules.

12. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q. Is there a fixed will template I must use?

There is no legally fixed template, but for a holographic will you must satisfy the requirement of handwriting the full text, date, address, and name and affixing a seal. As long as the formal requirements are met, the wording itself is free. Still, writing clearly who receives what, without ambiguity, helps prevent disputes.

Q. Can a married couple make a will together in one document?

A joint will made by two or more people in the same document is generally not recognized. Even spouses should each draft a separate will to be safe. Consult a professional on the specific method of drafting.

Q. Can a will pass on debts too?

Succession comprehensively transfers not only positive assets but also debts. If you judge that debts exceed assets, heirs can consider renouncing the inheritance or accepting it conditionally (qualified acceptance) — and there are deadlines for these. See the guide to inheritance renunciation vs limited acceptance for details, and discuss your own situation with a professional.

Conclusion - A Will That Keeps Its Form Is a Real Will

A will is not only for the wealthy. It is a final act of care that reduces the confusion and conflict your family will face and that fixes your wishes in a form the law recognizes. But for that care to take effect, you must draft it in one of the five forms the law prescribes, meeting every requirement without omission. In particular, never forget that the most common holographic will can be void if even one of its five elements — full text, date, address, name, and seal — is missing.

Finally, three points once more. First, form is the lifeblood of a will. If you value safety, choose the notarial will, which needs no probate. Second, a will cannot cross the heirs' minimum forced share, and a restitution claim has a deadline. Third, every requirement and figure varies by time, jurisdiction, and amendment. Once more: this article is information and education as of September 29, 2026 and is not legal advice. The outcome of any individual case depends on its facts, so for the actual drafting of a will and estate planning, please consult a licensed professional such as a lawyer, notary, or judicial scrivener.

# Korean Will Form Checklist - Five Forms, Probate, and Forced Shares

As of: 2026-09-29
General information based on the Civil Act of the Republic of Korea (Act No. 21454, in force from March 17, 2026), not legal advice. Courts decide whether an individual will is valid on its facts, so before drafting consult a Korean attorney, notary, judicial scrivener, or the Korea Legal Aid Corporation.

## 1. Choosing a Form

| Form | Article | Witnesses | Probate after death | When it fits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Holographic | Art. 1066 | None | Required (Art. 1091) | Simple content, low cost |
| Recorded | Art. 1067 | A participating witness (1 or more) | Required (Art. 1091) | Writing by hand is difficult |
| Notarial | Art. 1068 | 2 | Not required | Large estate, expected disputes, declaring loss of inheritance rights |
| Secret | Art. 1069 | 2 or more | Required (Art. 1091) | Keeping the contents hidden until death |
| Dictated | Art. 1070 | 2 or more | Apply within 7 days after the urgent cause ends (Art. 1070(2)) | Only when illness or another urgent cause rules out the other four |

A declaration of loss of inheritance rights (Art. 1004-2) can be made **only by a notarial will**.

## 2. Holographic Will Check

- [ ] The full text is entirely handwritten (typed, ghost-written, or photocopied versions are described as void)
- [ ] Year, month, and **day** are written (year and month alone are described as having no effect)
- [ ] The address is handwritten (the base of daily life is enough; it may be on the envelope if the document is one whole)
- [ ] The name is handwritten
- [ ] A seal is affixed (no seal, no effect - Supreme Court 2006Da12848 / a thumbprint is valid - Supreme Court 97Da38503)
- [ ] Any insertion, deletion, or alteration is handwritten and sealed (Art. 1066(2))
- [ ] A trusted person knows where it is kept

## 3. Witness Check (Recorded, Notarial, Secret, Dictated)

Persons who cannot be witnesses (Art. 1072)

- [ ] Not a minor
- [ ] Not under adult or limited guardianship
- [ ] Not a beneficiary of the will, or the beneficiary's spouse or lineal blood relative
- [ ] (Notarial) Not disqualified under the Notary Public Act

## 4. After Death

| What | Who | When | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Probate of holographic, recorded, secret wills | Holder or finder | Without delay after death | Art. 1091(1) |
| Probate of dictated will | Witness or interested party | Within 7 days after the urgent cause ends | Art. 1070(2) |
| Opening a sealed will | The court | With heirs, representatives, or interested parties present | Art. 1092 |
| Notarial will | - | No probate | Art. 1091(2) |

Probate only confirms and preserves form and condition; it does not establish that the contents are valid.

## 5. Checking Against Forced Shares

| Holder | Forced share | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Descendants, spouse | 1/2 of the statutory share | Art. 1112 items 1-2 |
| Ascendants | 1/3 of the statutory share | Art. 1112 item 3 |
| Siblings | None | Constitutional Court 2020Hun-Ga4 et al. (Apr. 25, 2024); deleted Sept. 20, 2024 |

- The claim is extinguished **one year from the time** the holder learned of the commencement of succession and of the gift or bequest to be restored, or **ten years** after succession commenced (Art. 1117)
- A gift or bequest made as compensation for special care or contribution is not a special benefit to the extent corresponding to the contribution (Art. 1008 proviso, amended March 17, 2026)
- The method and timing of forced-share restoration depend on the supplementary provisions, so confirm with a professional based on the date succession opens

## 6. When to Revisit

- [ ] Marriage, divorce, birth, death, or a change in assets
- [ ] If several wills exist, their dates settle the order
- [ ] After the inheritance provisions of the Civil Act changed, the existing will still works as intended
