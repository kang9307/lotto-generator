The Country Risk You Buy With a ccTLD - What .io, .ai and .tv Really Are

A domain is a delegation, not a possession - registration is closer to a lease than a deed

Aug 20, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: what you also picked when you picked the name

Most people have sat in front of a domain search box feeding it candidate names for a side project. The .com is long gone, and the only combination that still reads well is free under .io or .ai. So you take it. At that moment you did not only choose a name. You chose the country that administers those two letters, and the rules that country writes.

A retrospective published on 19 August 2026 captures the feeling precisely. An individual registered a domain in 2018 almost as a joke, it grew into a weather balloon tracking service, and within a few years it found itself fielding data requests from public agencies. The author noted that the data could be used in directions never intended, and that sensitive facility information was masked once requests came in. Whether any particular judgement or event was right is not this article's concern. One point is. The time it takes for a casually bought address to become serious is shorter than you expect, and in the meantime the circumstances of whoever administers that address change outside your control.

What follows separates four things: the legal character of a domain registration, the structural difference between country code and generic top-level domains, the codes that have actually been retired, and the preparation you can start today.

1. A domain is a delegation, not a possession

The first document to write down how DNS delegation works was RFC 1591 in 1994. It describes the parties who receive a top-level domain in blunt terms: 「These designated authorities are trustees for the delegated domain, and have a duty to serve the community.」 The same section goes further, saying that concerns about 「rights」 and 「ownership」 of domains are inappropriate, and that it is appropriate to be concerned about 「responsibilities」 and 「service」 to the community.

That language is aimed at top-level domain managers, but it flows straight down to registrants. When we say we 「bought」 a domain, here is what actually happened.

  • A time-limited entry appeared in a database that a registry maintains
  • That entry is kept alive through a registrar under contract terms. Miss a renewal and it expires; breach a condition and it can be suspended or reclaimed
  • There is no title deed and no certificate. The only thing you can verify is that your name sits in that database right now

In practice the distinction shows up in design rather than accounting. Treat a domain as an asset and you build on the premise that it will simply stay yours. Treat it as a conditional right of use and you start by drawing what collapses alongside it if that condition breaks. The second picture is the accurate one.

2. ccTLD versus gTLD - who writes the rules

You cannot invent a two-letter top-level domain. RFC 1591 grounds country code top-level domains in the ISO 3166 list, noting that the list was chosen precisely because ISO already has a procedure for deciding which entities belong on it. In other words, a ccTLD derives its existence from a standards list that lives outside the internet. That single line is the origin of every risk below.

Generic top-level domains such as .com, .org, .dev and .app are grounded inside the internet instead. A registry operator signs an agreement with ICANN, and that agreement defines both policy and failure handling.

AxisccTLD (country code)gTLD (generic)
Basis for existenceThe ISO 3166-1 two-letter code listA registry agreement with ICANN
Who sets policyThe manager designated by each country or territoryICANN consensus policies plus the contract
Registration eligibilityVaries by registry; residency or incorporation tests are commonGenerally unrestricted
Dispute resolutionUDRP adoption is voluntary; local procedures are commonUDRP and URS apply by contract
Operational failure coverNo uniform contractual mechanismRegistration data escrow, Emergency Back-End Registry Operator
Path to disappearanceCode leaves the ISO list, retirement process beginsContract ends or transfers; the string itself survives

The row most often skipped is operational failure cover. On the gTLD side the contract provides for an emergency back-end operator that can temporarily run the registry if the operator breaches a downtime threshold on critical functions such as DNS resolution, the shared registration system, registration data lookup, escrow deposits and DNSSEC signing. Registration data is deposited separately on a schedule. None of this is structurally required of a ccTLD. Plenty of ccTLD registries run beautifully, but that is the registry being good, not the regime guaranteeing it.

3. The codes we use because they read well

To a developer .io means input and output, .ai means artificial intelligence and .tv means video. The registry reads them differently.

  • .io — the British Indian Ocean Territory
  • .ai — Anguilla, a British overseas territory in the Caribbean
  • .tv — Tuvalu, an island nation in the South Pacific
  • .co — Colombia
  • .me — Montenegro
  • .ly — Libya. Beyond these, .sh belongs to Saint Helena and .fm to the Federated States of Micronesia

For small jurisdictions this structure turns into real public finance. Registration income from .ai came to represent a visible share of Anguilla's government revenue, and in January 2025 operational control of the registry moved to a large domain infrastructure provider under an arrangement that reportedly keeps the government sharing in the proceeds. Tuvalu likewise moved .tv operations to a different provider in 2021 on revised terms. Two things are true at once here. The incentive to keep the code is strong — nobody discards a revenue line. And precisely because of that, negotiation and transfer are frequent — when the operator changes, terms, pricing and suspension policy can all change with it.

Using a popular ccTLD means sitting under that negotiating table. What gets said above it is rarely communicated to registrants.

4. Four ways a change of country reaches you

「Country risk」 as a single phrase is not something you can prepare for. The paths that actually reach a registrant split four ways.

  1. Redelegation — the manager or back-end operator changes. Service usually continues, but terms, pricing and renewal conditions shift
  2. Eligibility change — when the criteria move, domains you already hold come into scope
  3. Local rules applied — registry rules about content, name length or purpose are applied after the fact
  4. Code retirement — if the code leaves ISO 3166-1, the top-level domain itself enters a retirement process

The first three happen routinely and have happened repeatedly. The fourth is rare but has no substitute remedy, because you are not losing a domain; the space that domain lives in is being closed.

5. How retirement runs - process and precedent

Fortunately the fourth path has a process. The ICANN Board adopted the ccTLD retirement policy on 22 September 2022, and IANA publishes the procedure. The flow is as follows.

  • It is established that eligibility has lapsed, typically because the code was removed from ISO 3166-1
  • IANA notifies the manager on file that it intends to issue a Notice of Removal
  • The notice is published and the Root Zone Database shows retirement status with a planned removal date
  • The manager builds a retirement plan covering migration, halting new registrations and stakeholder communication, and on the appointed date IANA removes the delegation from the root zone

The clock runs five years by default from the notice, and a request filed within twelve months can add up to five more, for a maximum of ten. This is not the kind of risk that lands overnight. The catch is that five years is not 「plenty of time」 but 「time that starts counting now」. Once a domain is entangled with email, authentication and APIs, migration is not a few weeks of work.

CodeAssigned toWhat happened

.su teaches the most here. It outlived its removal from the list by more than thirty years. For much of that period no retirement policy existed, and registrations kept accumulating; around 100,000 were reported as of 2025. Read the other way, now that the policy is in place the same open-ended deferral is harder to repeat.

6. .io - the case everyone is watching

From here on, facts only. IO is the ISO 3166-1 code assigned to the British Indian Ocean Territory, whose extent is effectively the Chagos Archipelago. On 22 May 2025 the United Kingdom was reported to have signed a treaty transferring sovereignty over the archipelago to Mauritius, and the absence of the territory's name from the treaty text became a talking point in the domain industry. ICANN had earlier published a post on the Chagos Archipelago and the .io domain on its blog on 14 November 2024.

Which position is correct is not for this article to judge. What matters to a registrant is only the conditional chain.

  • If the territory's status changes such that IO leaves ISO 3166-1 → a retirement process begins with five to ten years of runway
  • If only the name changes and the code is retained → the manager may change but the namespace survives
  • If the code becomes a different pair of letters → a migration to the new code follows

As of August 2026 .io registers and resolves normally. The reasonable response is therefore not to move but to become movable. If you run on .io today, the question is not 「will it vanish tomorrow」 but 「do I need to send invoices from this address five years from now」. If the answer is yes, it is time to stand up a second, canonical address.

7. Three ways a rule change takes it away

Far more common than retirement are the first three paths. One case each makes the preparation points obvious.

7.1 Eligibility changed - .eu

.eu carries explicit eligibility rules. Per UK government guidance, you qualify as an EU or EEA citizen regardless of residence, as a non-citizen resident in the EU or EEA, or as an organisation established in the EU or EEA. Registrants who stopped meeting those tests after the UK left the EU faced this schedule.

  • 1 January 2021 — non-qualifying domains were suspended and stopped serving
  • 30 June 2021 — the suspension period ended
  • 1 July 2021 — the domains moved to withdrawn status
  • 1 January 2022 — withdrawn domains were revoked and released for general registration

During suspension, updating the registration data to meet the criteria could restore the name. The point is here. Eligibility is tested continuously, not only at registration. There are ccTLDs where a change of company seat, director nationality or residence shakes the domain loose. As a local example, .kr requires the applicant and registrant to hold an address in South Korea — natural for a domestic business, but a checklist item the moment operations move abroad.

7.2 Local rules applied after the fact - .ly

In September 2010 a link shortening service lost its .ly domain, deleted without notice. The registry subsequently issued a statement indicating that the site was found to conflict with local law and regulation, as reported at the time. Following the same episode it became known that .ly names of four characters or fewer would be reserved for entities based locally.

The lesson is not a verdict on any country. It is that registry terms generally include the power to suspend or delete for rule violations, and the yardstick for interpreting those rules is the law and practice of that jurisdiction. A service that is entirely lawful where you live can read differently where you borrowed the address.

7.3 The operating model collapsed - free domains

A provider that handed out several ccTLDs for free halted new registrations after a lawsuit was filed in 2023, and in 2024 reportedly settled and announced its exit from the domain business. In the same period some countries reclaimed operation of their codes or moved them to other providers. A very large volume of domains in that space was cleared out at once.

An address you received for free sits in the weakest contractual position available. That is fine for testing and coursework. Put outbound email or an account recovery address there and it disappears the day the provider changes direction.

8. What a service operator can do now

The goal of preparation is not to prevent incidents but to stay in a state where you can move. The table below is the minimum set that produces that state.

ItemWhat to check nowWhat breaks otherwise
Canonical addressOne TLD with stable rules as canonical, the ccTLD as a redirectIndexing, backlinks and bookmarks bind to one namespace and migration becomes impossible
Email domainWhether mail rides the same risk as the service domainWhen the domain stops, support and account recovery stop at the same instant
Registrar accountWhether the recovery contact is an address on that same domainIf the domain locks, the route to recover the domain locks with it
Authentication tiesOAuth redirect URIs, SSO identifiers, the domains used for certificate validationChange the address and every login fails at once
DNS operationsWhether DNS provider and registrar sit in the same accountOne account incident removes the name and its resolution together
Migration rehearsalWhether you have actually migrated a staging domain onceA runbook nobody has executed doubles the clock in a real event

If you only do one thing first, make it the recovery contact on the registrar account. It costs nothing and takes five minutes, and if that field is self-referential the rest of your preparation is void.

9. What is actually tied to the domain - email and identity

When a domain stops, the website is not what hurts. You can serve the site from a temporary address. The real damage is wherever the domain was functioning as proof of identity.

  • If your account recovery email at other services lives on that domain, recovery itself becomes impossible
  • If internal SSO identifiers are domain-based, every user account has to be remapped
  • Automated TLS issuance depends on domain control validation. No domain, no renewal
  • OAuth redirect URIs are an allowlist. A callback never returns to an address that is not on it
  • Mail authentication records (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) attach to the domain, so a new domain builds sending reputation from zero, and mobile deep-link verification and namespace ownership proofs at package or image registries frequently rest on a domain too

Run through that list once and the order of work settles itself. Email and authentication move before the web address does. The shape of this problem matches what you meet when moving a code repository or a database: until you actually move, you cannot tell what follows and what does not. It exercises the same muscle as keeping code hosting portable.

10. A realistic line for personal blogs and side projects

None of this means you should rush a hobby project off .io. Risk is probability times duration times cost of reversal, and for personal projects the last two terms are small. Three questions are enough.

  1. Will you still be giving people this address in five years? Is it printed on cards, papers or documentation
  2. Does email attach to this domain? Sending or account recovery, either one
  3. Do login, payment or identity flows anchor on it?

Three noes and you should use whichever ccTLD you like; an address you enjoy is part of what keeps a project alive. One yes and the safer shape is to keep the canonical address on a stable namespace and treat the ccTLD as a short alias. The arrangement can be this simple.

  • example.tld — the canonical address. Canonical tags, email and authentication anchors all live here
  • exa.mple — the short, attractive ccTLD. Redirect only, no email, never an authentication anchor
  • Use the short one for sharing and promotion, but always write the canonical one into documentation and structured data

The value of this arrangement shows up when nothing goes wrong. If the short address disappears, the only thing that changes is a line of marketing copy while the system stands. Names used strictly inside a home lab or a corporate network follow a different axis entirely; that side is covered in the discussion of a top-level domain designed for self-hosting.

What could not be confirmed

This subject mixes in matters still in progress, so several items cannot be written as settled. As of August 2026 the following were not confirmed.

  • The eventual fate of .io — the treaty's entry into force, whether ISO 3166-1 reflects any change, and whether the IO code is retained are all open
  • Whether a formal Notice of Removal issued for .su — reporting of a 2030 phase-out sat alongside statements that no formal notice had gone out. The authoritative status is whatever the IANA Root Zone Database shows
  • Exact ccTLD revenue figures — estimates around .ai and .tv vary widely between outlets and the contracts are not public, so no figure is asserted here
  • Individual registry reclamation and suspension clauses — these differ by country. Do not generalise the cases above to other ccTLDs; read the terms of the registry you intend to use
  • How existing registrants are made whole on retirement — the policy requires a plan including migration guidance, but compensation or priority transfer rights vary case by case

Conclusion: choosing an address is choosing a jurisdiction

A domain is the longest-lived identifier most systems have. Servers get replaced repeatedly and frameworks get swapped, but an address, once announced, cannot be recalled. And the basis for that address sits somewhere we cannot touch — a standards list and the institutions of individual states. That structure is exactly what RFC 1591 pointed at thirty years ago when it wrote responsibility rather than ownership.

So the conclusion is not 「avoid ccTLDs」. That is an overreaction, and giving up a good name has a real cost too. This framing is enough instead: use a ccTLD as a brand, never as the root of your infrastructure. Keep one canonical address in a namespace with stable rules, attach email and authentication there, and spend the two letters you like on everything else. Then whichever way the code goes, all you lose is a redirect.

How long it takes for a domain bought as a joke to turn serious varies. The cost of restructuring after it turns serious is always higher than the cost of splitting things up at the start.
