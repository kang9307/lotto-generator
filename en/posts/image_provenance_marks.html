The Marks on a Generated Image - Metadata, Signed Provenance and Pixel Watermarks Do Different Jobs

What survives, what gets stripped, and what becomes linkable

Aug 25, 2026 Tech & IT

You generate one image, save it, drop it into a chat, and post it on a blog. Along the way the file carries more than pixels. There is a slot holding capture information, there is a signed provenance record, and there may be a value written into the pixel values themselves through changes too small to see. All three get called marks, yet they do different jobs and disappear under different conditions.

Blur that distinction and judgment goes wrong in both directions. One person sees an image with no metadata and concludes there is no label, so a human made it. Another says marks always get stripped, so they mean nothing. Both are wrong, because some layers vanish and some do not.

Recently an analysis of the built-in drawing and photo apps of one operating system drew attention, reporting that images generated on the device also carry a mark invisible to the eye. What follows takes that discussion as a starting point but, instead of grading a particular product, sets out what marking can and cannot do in principle. Methods for removing or bypassing a mark are not covered.

1. What was reported and what the documentation confirms

The discussion started from a developer's reverse-engineering write-up. The claim is that images produced by the built-in apps of that operating system carry, separately from any visible label, a pixel-level invisible watermark; that the value inside it comes from a remote server; and that the same value is also recorded in the file's provenance data. The write-up also noted that even when the model runs on the device, the prompt passes through a server during the moderation step.

It is worth separating what the vendor's own documentation states from what the analysis inferred, because the weight of every judgment downstream depends on it.

StatementStatus
The apps mix on-device generation with cloud services, and safety systems including content filtering run in the cloudStated in vendor documentation
Images produced with generative AI include content credentials based on the C2PA standardStated in vendor documentation
Data about prompts and how the app and models responded is collectedStated in vendor documentation
A separate invisible watermark is written into the pixels, and its identifier is issued by a serverReported by the analysis
The file's provenance data and the pixel watermark are linked by the same identifierReported by the analysis

The analyst's own account is that the bottom two rows sit outside what the vendor has publicly described. This article therefore treats those two rows as reported rather than established, while the top three are documented and are used as fact. Neither source states a motive for the design, so none is guessed at here either.

2. Marking is not one layer

There are three broad ways to leave something in an image. Each is stored in a different place, which is exactly why each disappears under different conditions. This table is the spine of the article.

LayerWhere it is storedStrong atWeak at
File metadata
(EXIF, XMP)		 A region of the file separate from the pixels Easy to read, well standardised, widely supported by tools Just as easy to erase or edit. No resistance to forgery at all
Signed provenance
(C2PA family)		 A manifest slot defined per file format A signature exposes forgery and tampering. History is captured as structure The whole envelope can be detached. Fragile under re-encoding
Invisible watermark Minute changes to the pixel values themselves Survives a full metadata wipe. Can survive a screen capture Carries very little information. Detection is probabilistic

2.1 File metadata - as easy to lose as it is to read

EXIF began as the slot a camera uses to record shooting conditions. Shutter speed and lens data go there, and depending on settings so do coordinates. XMP is a looser format that carries edit history or rights information. The key point about both is that they are text written somewhere other than the pixels.

  • They can be checked by reading the header without decoding the file, which suits automated pipelines, but for the same reason a single editing tool wipes them wholesale and any value can be rewritten at will
  • Many platforms cut this region out at upload time, citing file size and privacy
  • So this layer is a way to carry information through a cooperative environment, not a way to prove anything

The first misreading comes from here: no metadata, therefore no manipulation. Absence can mean it was never written, or that it was removed along the way. Absence is not information.

2.2 Signed provenance - history captured as structure

C2PA-style content credentials go a step further. Who made it, which tool was used, whether AI was involved, and what edits followed are bundled into a structure called a manifest and signed. Once signed, quietly altering a value becomes hard, because the altered value no longer matches the signature.

What a signature protects, however, is the integrity of the content, not the guarantee that it exists. Throwing away the sealed envelope entirely remains easy. The C2PA explainer does not hide this: provenance information alone cannot tell you whether the content is true, accurate or factual, and it complements media literacy and fact-checking rather than replacing them.

2.3 Marks written into pixels - trading capacity for robustness

The third layer uses no separate slot. It nudges the pixel values themselves by an amount the eye does not register, and rides a value on that pattern. Reduced very roughly, the principle runs like this.

  • The image is split into regions where human vision is least sensitive, such as blocks or frequency components
  • Depending on the bits of the value being carried, those regions are pushed slightly up or slightly down
  • Reading it back means recomputing the same regions under the same rule and observing which way each leans
  • Editing perturbs those values, but the original bits can be recovered as long as the majority holds

Because it has to stay invisible, this approach carries very little information. A sentence will not fit; a short identifier is the practical ceiling. In exchange it is mixed into the pixels, so it stays even after every metadata field is stripped. Capacity was sold to buy robustness. Text watermarking sits at the opposite end of that trade, which I covered separately in how text watermarking works and where it breaks.

3. Tying the layers together - hard and soft bindings

The reason for stacking the layers is written into the specification as a concept. C2PA distinguishes two ways of binding a manifest to content.

  • Hard binding - the manifest is tied through a cryptographic hash of the asset's bytes. Change the content and the binding breaks, so tampering shows immediately
  • Soft binding - the tie runs through a fingerprint computed from the content or an invisible watermark embedded in it, so that the same content can be matched even when the bits differ

Combine the two and you get a durable Content Credential: a state in which the credential can be rediscovered from a repository through its soft binding even after the manifest has been detached from the file. The structure reported in the case above, where the pixel watermark's identifier is also written into the file's provenance data, is exactly this shape. It is not an exotic invention but a design the specification has already named.

This is the fork. A hard binding answers is this file that file. A soft binding answers is this picture that picture. The first question ends in yes or no; the second always returns as a matter of degree, of how closely it matches. The two have different natures, so the strength of conclusion you may demand from them differs too.

4. What it means for an identifier to be inside

The value used in a soft binding is usually a short identifier. No name, no account, no location. From that it is easy to conclude that it is not personal data, and taken as a single value that is fair. But look at the repetition of the value rather than one instance and its character changes.

The moment the same value appears across several images, those images become one set. Images scattered across different times and different routes can still be joined if the value matches. This linkability arises automatically from the structure, regardless of who wanted it. What the party that created the value intended has no bearing on it.

So the question to ask about an identifier is not what it contains but these four things.

  • Lifetime - is it regenerated per image, held per session, or fixed until the device changes
  • Scope - is it confined to one app, or does the same value appear across other services from the same vendor
  • Linking records - does the issuer keep a record tying the value to a moment in time, and if so for how long
  • Visibility - can the user know that the value exists and what it is, and is there a way to check

The awkward combination is long-lived, broad in scope, logged, and low in visibility. Flip every axis, so that the value is fresh each time, confined to one app, logged only briefly and open to inspection, and a feature with the same name behaves like a different thing entirely. Whether an identifier is present matters less than which identifier is present.

5. Why these marks are being introduced

Collapse the reasons into surveillance and the design stops making sense. The publicly stated purposes are broadly three. Each has its own merit, and that merit does not cancel the side effects described above.

  • Telling generated content apart - as image quality rose, sorting by eye became effectively impossible. Without an automated signal, search, archiving and training-data collection all wobble
  • Responding to deepfakes - where a real person's face or voice has been synthesised, a signal saying this is synthetic directly slows how fast harm spreads
  • Meeting regulation - as shown below, rules requiring machine-readable marking now exist, and providers need a technical means of satisfying them

There is a model-training angle as well. Breaking the loop in which generated output feeds back into training data requires being able to filter out which side is generated. That purpose does not benefit the user directly, but it returns to them as maintained model quality.

Still, a purpose being legitimate and an implementation being narrowed to fit that purpose are separate questions. If distinguishing generated content is the whole goal, a single bit saying this image is generated suffices. The moment a wider value goes in, that width calls for its own explanation.

6. What marks cannot do

Marks are not a complete answer. To make that sentence precise you have to look at which layer survives which transformation. The table below follows directly from the principles above.

TransformationFile metadataSigned provenanceInvisible watermark
Copy under a new nameSurvivesSurvivesSurvives
Re-encode with an unaware toolCommonly lostValidation breaks or is not updatedUsually survives
Screen captureLostLostDesigned to withstand it
Heavy croppingMay be lostHard binding breaksDepends on the area left
Platform uploadFrequently strippedDepends on platform supportDepends on recompression strength

Vendors of pixel watermarks describe them as designed to stand up to cropping, filters, frame-rate changes and lossy compression. Designed to withstand means the odds were raised, not that it will always be read. A detection result is not a binary present or absent but an estimate carrying a confidence level, and confidence falls as the remaining area shrinks or compression is applied repeatedly.

There is an error in the opposite direction too. No mark, therefore a human made it does not follow. It may have been made with a tool that adds no mark, or the mark may have vanished en route. A mark is usable as evidence when it is present; when it is absent it says nothing at all. This asymmetry is where discussions about marking most often collapse.

7. What a user can check

Of the three layers, the first two are directly visible to a user. Part of the file metadata shows up in the operating system's file properties panel, and image editors or managers show more. A C2PA manifest, fed into a viewer that supports the standard, lists which tools were involved and what edits took place. The third layer is different. It needs a detector, and the detector is usually controlled by whoever embedded the watermark.

  • Simply opening the properties panel once before sharing tells you whether items such as coordinates or device details are still attached. An empty metadata region does not mean the other layers are empty too
  • Platforms differ in what they strip on upload, so being safe in one place does not mean being safe in another
  • When leaning on a detection result, start by confirming which layer the result came from

The point here is not a tool recommendation but the fact that checking is possible. The route to seeing what a file carries out into the world is already open as a standard, and looking once before sending is not the same as sending without looking.

8. Regulation - where machine-readable marking becomes a norm

Marking is no longer only an industry convention. Article 50(2) of the EU AI Act requires providers of AI systems generating synthetic audio, image, video or text content to ensure that outputs are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated. The transparency obligations apply from 2 August 2026. Which technology satisfies the requirement is not specified by the rule itself and is left to standardisation work and practical guidance.

In South Korea, the Framework Act on the Development of Artificial Intelligence and Establishment of a Foundation for Trust took effect on 22 January 2026, and Article 31 covers notice and labeling for generative AI outputs, including a requirement that outputs hard to distinguish from real content be marked in a way users can clearly recognise.

Disclaimer. This section records the existence and effective dates of published laws and rules. It does not determine whether any particular product or provider falls under any particular clause. Applicability in a specific case depends on the provider's role, the shape of the service and the jurisdiction, so seek confirmation from a qualified professional where a concrete determination is needed.

Knowing the regulatory current explains why marks started appearing across many products at once. The timing is not a coincidence. At the same time, what the rules ask for is that generated content be detectable, not that individual outputs be identified. The gap between those two requirements is the design latitude described earlier.

9. Decisions the builder has to make first

The same feature can land very differently depending on a handful of design decisions. If you are adding image generation to a service, this is a sensible order to settle them in.

  • State what goes in, first - write into the product documentation which values land in which layer. Something discovered later and something written down from the start are worlds apart in trust terms
  • Include only the minimum the purpose needs - if the goal is distinguishing generated content, the fact of generation plus tool information is enough. A value pointing at an individual output belongs only where a separate purpose calls for it
  • Keep the lifetime short and the retention explicit - a reused value joins that much more. The record tying a value to a moment creates more real risk than the value itself, so define retention and deletion alongside it
  • Show the user the check route and the failure path - let them see what was attached, and state as product policy whether generation is blocked or proceeds when the mark cannot be written

None of this is a new demand. It is data minimisation and purpose limitation, long-standing principles of privacy-conscious design, carried over to image marking. Only the subject is new.

What could not be confirmed

The items below are ones I could not ground while writing. None of them is used as fact in the body.

  • The specific behaviour of the pixel watermark described in the originating analysis is not confirmed in the vendor's public documentation. The analyst likewise wrote that this part sits outside what has been disclosed
  • In what form and for how long the identifier is retained on the server side, and whether it connects to other services, could not be established from public material
  • Quantitative figures for watermark robustness could not be confirmed beyond vendors' qualitative descriptions, and how far subordinate rules under Article 31 of the Korean framework act recognise invisible marking could not be confirmed either
  • How individual major platforms handle metadata on upload changes often enough that it could not be tabulated without pinning a date

What to watch next

This subject is likely to divide along a few lines.

  • Platform posture - whether more platforms preserve provenance on upload effectively decides how much this system is worth. Today most cut it out
  • Openness of detection - a structure only the embedder can read makes verification dependent on that party. Watch for third-party checking routes appearing
  • Rules getting concrete, and the standard of disclosure - the clause requiring machine-readable marking exists, but which technologies satisfy it is still being settled. Standardisation documents will pull implementations together, and whether documenting what goes in becomes normal practice will separate trust from suspicion

To summarise: marking is three layers, and each disappears under different conditions. A mark is usable as evidence when present and says nothing when absent. And the moment a mark carries a value pointing at an individual output, it acquires a property of linkability that is separate from its labeling function. Hold those three sentences and you will know what to ask in the next debate of this kind.
