When Someone Argues for a Right to Income

Separating programmes that look alike, and marking the point where evidence ends and value choices begin

Aug 20, 2026 Trends & Culture

The claim that people should be able to live even when growth stalls comes back on a cycle. It resurfaces whenever automation dominates the news, whenever employment figures disappoint, whenever the welfare budget is being drafted. A lecture transcript from 2010 has been circulating again recently, and the same conversation went around one more time.

This article does not rule on whether that argument is right. That is not its job, and honestly it is not a question that data alone can settle. What it does instead is set out, in order, what you need to check so the discussion does not spin in place. The main reason this debate stalls is that different programmes are called by the same name. The second reason is that pilot results get read as national results. The third is that factual disputes get mixed with disputes about values. Each is unpacked below.

This article does not endorse or criticise any party, faction or government, and it does not advocate for or against adopting any particular programme. It is general information, not policy advice or investment advice. Figures cited reflect the reporting date of each source, and programmes and amounts may change afterwards.

What the argument actually claims

The text being circulated is Money and Freedom: On Dividend Economics by Kim Jong-chul, published in issue 115 of the Korean journal Green Review in November 2010 and based on a lecture given that June. The argument runs in four steps.

First, most of the money circulating today is not currency printed by the state but deposits created when banks make loans. Second, because that money carries interest, society as a whole must keep expanding to earn the principal and interest it owes. Third, that expansionary pressure is the root of competition, environmental damage and export rivalry, so stopping growth means touching the monetary system itself. Fourth, the way out is a basic income, but one understood not as relief for the weak but as a dividend on assets the community has inherited. The author takes this from Clifford Douglas, who developed social credit theory in Britain in the 1920s and 1930s, and proposes the term citizen dividend in place of Douglas's national dividend.

Two further points attach to this. One is the separation of employment from income: unless the assumption that income is payment for labour is loosened, the proposal does not make sense to people. The other is the route to implementation, which starts with local currencies rather than a single national move. Because the original is a lecture from 2010, it contains references to domestic issues of that moment; those are set aside here. What this article takes up is what you would have to check in order to test the argument.

Start by separating the terms

This is the first place discussions tangle. Someone opposing basic income is sometimes really opposing an expanded minimum income guarantee; someone supporting it sometimes has a negative income tax in mind. Pulling five things apart changes the conversation.

The negative income tax is generally traced to Milton Friedman's 1962 book Capitalism and Freedom. Participation income is the compromise Anthony Atkinson set out in a 1996 article in The Political Quarterly, stripping away the asset test while retaining a norm of reciprocity. These three differ not only in name but in what the administration has to examine. Basic income examines almost nothing, a negative income tax looks only at income, participation income looks at activity. What the examination looks at determines the administrative cost and the size of the gap where people fall through.

Designs also diverge within a single name. Checking five axes usually reveals what a proposal really is.

  • Universality - does everyone get it, or only certain ages, districts or income bands
  • Unconditionality - is job search or social activity required
  • Individuality - per person or per household. Per household means verifying who lives with whom, and the examination returns
  • Cash form - unrestricted cash, or a local currency or voucher with designated uses
  • Adequacy - does the amount cover living costs or only supplement them. This is where the financing requirement multiplies

Programmes that fit this table already exist in Korea. The livelihood benefit under the National Basic Living Security scheme tops up the gap between a threshold and assessed income, so it sits in the minimum income guarantee row. The Earned Income Tax Credit returns money through the tax system to working households below an income band, which is a design in the negative income tax family. That these programmes exist is a verifiable fact; evaluating how they are run is outside this article. What matters here is that you should know which boxes are already occupied before debating a new one. On the details of eligibility, see the Korea Earned Income Tax Credit complete guide.

The detail most often cut when these studies are quoted is the set of conditions. The result travels and the sample, the duration and the funding source stay behind. Laid out with their conditions, the main public cases look like this.

Read across the rows and the differences in conditions stand out before the results do. Finland covered only unemployment benefit recipients, Kenya covered whole villages and therefore captured the local economy's response, and the OpenResearch study paid individuals only, so no local effect could arise. These are studies that share a word but answer different questions. In Kenya, non-agricultural business net revenues rose sharply and the number of enterprises increased by 25 percent precisely because the whole village received money at once. A design that pays a handful of individuals has no reason to produce that.

Why a pilot result is not a national result

This is where arguments collapse most often. Each side brings the pilot that suits it, but a pilot is structurally a different object from a national scheme. There are four reasons.

First, the money is paid without being raised. Participants receive and do not pay the taxes that would fund the transfer. In a national scheme the recipients are also the payers, so the net effect changes. The labour supply reductions quoted above run into this. When taxes rise alongside, work incentives also move in the opposite direction.

Second, the duration is fixed. Someone who knows the money stops in two years behaves differently from someone who expects it for life. The Kenyan study was designed to measure that difference directly, and villages promised twelve years responded more strongly than villages promised two, even when compared at a point where both had received the same amount. Expectations about duration alone change behaviour, which means short trials may understate what a permanent programme would do.

Third, participation is voluntary or the site is unusual. A trial recruited by application produces results for the people who applied, and a trial confined to one village draws people in from neighbouring areas. That happened in Namibia. Indicators inside the village improved, but the figures shift once households affected by in-migration are set aside. In a national scheme there is no outside to move in from.

Fourth, other rules change during the trial. A separate policy change in the second year of the Finnish experiment made the employment figures hard to read. This work happens in society, not in a laboratory, so uncontrolled changes intrude. On top of that, the study ran no baseline survey, so it measured a difference in wellbeing rather than a change. That the group receiving payments reported better outcomes is established; whether the payments caused the improvement cannot be answered by that design.

The case in favour, at its strongest

Building a weak version of the case in order to knock it down is easy and pointless. The sturdiest version runs like this.

  • Screening costs and coverage gaps arrive together. Asset testing gets expensive as it gets accurate, and stays cheap by staying inaccurate. People who qualify but never apply are another cost that screening creates. The OECD simulation itself allowed for near-complete take-up under a universal payment while existing benefits fall short of that.
  • Stigma is hard to measure but real. Selective benefits identify their recipients. Universal payments remove that marker. Nobody can price that gain, but there is no basis for treating it as zero either.
  • The shape of employment is changing. Tying income security to a job assumes a career spent at one employer. As contracts shorten and earnings become uneven, that assumption weakens.
  • Bargaining power comes from having an alternative income. Someone who will not go hungry after quitting can refuse bad terms. The move from wage work to self-employment in the Kenyan study can be read in that direction.
  • There is a view that some income is a share of common assets. Alaska has distributed resource revenue to residents since 1982. What counts as a common asset is arguable, but the idea that such a share exists is already embodied in a working programme.

The case against, at its strongest

The same applies here. The strongest version is not about laziness; it is about arithmetic and distribution.

  • Universal payment is structurally expensive. The same amount goes to people who do not need it, so for a fixed budget the share reaching those who do gets thinner. That is division, not ideology.
  • Replacing existing benefits can leave vulnerable groups worse off. In the OECD simulation the losses concentrated among workless households that had been receiving unemployment insurance or early retirement benefits, and lone parent households in France and the United Kingdom lost out frequently, because the benefits they had been receiving exceeded the average basic income.
  • The reduction in labour supply is an observed fact. The OpenResearch study found employment 2 points lower, 1.3 fewer hours a week and 5 percent lower earnings, rising to 4 points and 1.8 hours among those under 30. The negative income tax trials of the 1970s reported larger figures. If tax revenue falls with the hours, more financing is needed than projected.
  • It cannot reflect household circumstances. A flat individual payment does not account for disability, illness or housing costs that differ from person to person. That is why in-kind provision and categorical benefits exist.
  • More money can mean higher prices, particularly for goods whose supply does not readily expand. The Kenyan study found no change in consumer prices in nearby markets, but the paper itself noted that the estimate was not precise enough to rule out meaningful movement.

Financing: where the numbers diverge

Financing arguments spin because the same programme is costed under different assumptions and the results are then compared as if they were comparable. Whether the budget is held fixed, how much of the existing benefit system is stripped out, and whether labour supply responses are included can move the figure several times over.

It is better to look at one calculation that states its assumptions. The technical background note the OECD published in May 2017 used the EUROMOD model to simulate abolishing most working-age cash benefits and income tax free allowances and spending the proceeds on a basic income. Income above pension age is untouched, and in-kind services such as health, education and care continue unchanged. Holding the budget fixed produced the amounts below.

All four amounts sit well below the poverty line. The same note also modelled setting the payment at existing minimum income levels and adjusting tax rates instead, in which case additional tax revenue accounted for 57 percent of the financing in Finland, 52 percent in France and 76 percent in the United Kingdom. Italy, where the reference benefit is very low, gained room to cut income tax instead. The conclusion under either route was that this is not an effective way to reduce poverty. In Finland and France existing benefits cover poorer households reasonably well, so spending more while targeting less actually raised the poverty rate.

What makes this calculation interesting is that both sides can use it. Opponents can quote the conclusion directly; supporters can point out that the premise was a design that strips out existing benefits. A scheme layered on top of them yields a different table. So when you see this simulation cited, read the assumptions paragraph before the conclusion. The exercise covers four countries and does not include Korea.

How money is created is a separate axis

The basic income argument and the monetary system argument travel together but are in fact separate. What makes the lecture unusual is that it welds them into a single chain, which means the verification has to fork.

The front of the chain has been set out by a central bank. Money creation in the modern economy, published in the Bank of England's 2014 Q1 Quarterly Bulletin, states that most money in a modern economy takes the form of bank deposits and that those deposits are created at the moment a bank makes a loan. It names two common misconceptions: that banks simply lend out savers' deposits, and that central bank money is multiplied up into loans and deposits. The claim that banks create money is confirmed in a central bank document.

The same document, however, also writes the next sentence. Banks cannot create without limit; profitability in a competitive market and prudential regulation act as constraints, and repaying a loan destroys money. In other words, the money multiplier diagram people often quote does not match that explanation, and the reserve-ratio arithmetic in the lecture runs into the same problem. If the route differs, it is a different claim even when the conclusion matches.

The back of the chain, whether a government issuing money directly to fund a dividend would work, is not addressed there. That part is contested rather than established, and this article is not in a position to say which side is right. For reading the relationship between money, prices and output through published indicators, see the complete guide to reading GDP and economic growth indicators.

Where evidence ends and values begin

This is the point of the article. The statements traded in this debate come in two kinds, and failing to tell them apart guarantees parallel lines forever.

Questions that data can narrow, meaning questions where a better study ought to change the answer, look like this.

  • How much do hours worked change when a given amount goes to a given group
  • Under a given design, who gains and loses how much by income decile and household type
  • How much administrative cost actually disappears when screening is removed
  • What effect a given scale of payment has on prices and rents

Other questions are not settled by any amount of data. Here a better study does not change the answer.

  • Whether income is payment for labour or a share in assets the community holds
  • At what exchange rate the cost of paying the wealthy trades against the gain from removing stigma
  • Which to accept between the accuracy of asset testing and the exposure of private life it requires
  • Whether fewer hours worked counts as a loss or as a widening of choice

The last item is the sharpest. The 1.3 fewer hours a week in the OpenResearch study is cited by opponents as evidence of lost revenue and unfunded cost, and by supporters as evidence that people gained room to decline work they did not want. It is the same number. What is being contested is not the figure but the name attached to it. That the same study found rising enrolment in post-secondary education among those under 30 does not push the reading either way, because how to count time spent in education is already a judgement.

The practical use of this distinction is simple. If someone has the facts wrong, showing them the data works. If their values differ, no amount of data persuades them, and that is not because they are being irrational but because the question was never one that data decides. Recognising which is which changes the goal of the conversation from persuasion to accommodation.

A checklist for reading claims like this

Working through these in order filters out most of the confusion.

  1. Which programme is meant. Until it is placed in one row of the table, the rest of the discussion runs empty
  2. How much, to whom, how often. A proposal without an amount and a scope cannot be costed
  3. What it replaces. Layered on top or substituted in produces opposite outcomes for the vulnerable
  4. Where the money comes from. Higher taxes, reallocated spending and money issuance are separate arguments with separate tests
  5. Who produced the costing and on what assumptions. Budget-neutral or not, labour supply and price responses included or not, and which base year
  6. If the evidence is a pilot, read the conditions. Sample and population, duration, funding, whether there was a control group, and what else changed in the same period
  7. Whether the remaining disagreement is factual or evaluative. Factual disputes narrow with data; evaluative ones are settled by procedure

What could not be verified

The gaps left open in writing this are collected here rather than scattered through the text.

  • The 1932 local currency scheme in Worgl, Austria and a 1969 American court case cited in the lecture were confirmed only through that text; the primary sources were not opened
  • No central bank or international institution document assessing the price effects of a government issuing money directly to fund payments was located
  • No fiscal costing for a nationwide scheme in Korea was located. The simulation cited covers only Finland, France, Italy and the United Kingdom
  • The size of the effect of the separate policy change that complicated the second year of the Finnish experiment was not established
  • The scale of in-migration into the Namibian pilot site was not established; only the statement that poverty indicators shift once affected households are excluded

Conclusion: coordinates instead of a verdict

The lecture now circulating contains passages that hold up and passages that do not. The statement that banks create money through lending is confirmed in a central bank document; the money multiplier diagram conflicts with that same document. The proposal to see basic income as a dividend rather than as relief is not a factual claim at all but a proposed way of looking, and so it is not the kind of thing that can be marked true or false. Even within one text, the method of verification changes from sentence to sentence.

So the conclusion here is not that one side is right. It is that recognising where evidence ends and value choice begins is the thing worth taking away. Mark that point and the remaining conversation gets shorter, because you learn when to go looking for data and when more data will not help. The next time you meet an argument like this, start by asking which row of the table it belongs to. That single question clears up more confusion than it looks like it should.

To restate: this article is general information, not policy advice or investment advice. It does not advocate adopting or rejecting any programme, and it recommends no financial product.
