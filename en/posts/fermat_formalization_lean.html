Formalization Is Not Proof - What 13 Million Lines of Fermat Actually Guarantee

What is new here is the verification, not the mathematics. Separating the column a machine now holds from the column that stays with people

2026-09-06 Tech & IT

In the first days of September 2026 the same sentence travelled a great many times. An AI proved Fermat's Last Theorem, it said. Exactly one word in that sentence has been swapped out. The word that belongs in that slot is formalized.

Formalization and proof name different jobs. Proving means establishing, for the first time, why some statement is true. Formalizing means taking a reason that has already been established and rewriting it in a shape a machine can inspect one inference at a time. What was rewritten this time is the proof Andrew Wiles published in 1995. According to the announcement, that paper ran to 129 pages, and it took the mathematical community years to accept it as correct.

So the new thing in this announcement is not mathematics. What makes that interesting is that the clearest statement of the limit comes from the announcement itself. It draws an explicit contrast with recent AI-driven work on the Riemann hypothesis, which produced novel mathematics, and says that what is novel here is the verification, which is checking a mathematical proof the way one would check a mathematical computation with a calculator. The headlines travelled with that sentence removed.

Every figure and quotation below comes from the announcement itself or from the official Lean documentation. Claims made by the party publishing the work are marked separately from the parts a third party checked independently. Anything whose wording could not be located in the primary source was kept out of the body and collected in a later section together with the reason.

The trigger - the one word the headline swapped

The conjecture Fermat left behind in 1637 stood unproven for more than 350 years. Wiles closed that gap in 1995. Which means that no news story appearing in 2026 could possibly have been about a proof in the first place. The proof finished 31 years ago.

Headlines drift toward the word proof anyway, because the two words carry different weight. A proof is an event. A formalization is a process. Events travel easily and processes need explaining, and whatever needs explaining is the first thing to fall off in transit. The structure is identical to the way a benchmark score thins out as its measurement conditions get stripped away. A number without conditions and an event without its process are manufactured by the same mechanism.

Put the word back in its slot and one question remains. What does the fact that a machine checked it actually guarantee? And what does it leave untouched? Separate those two columns and you can read the result without shrinking it and without inflating it, which is the only honest way to read an announcement whose subject is the party making it.

What formalization does - proofs you read versus proofs you check

A proof in a mathematics paper is prose written for a human reader, which is why it is full of omissions. Obvious steps get skipped. Parallel arguments get folded away with a remark that the other case is similar. Notation leans on convention and lets context fill the rest. Those omissions are not defects; they are a design decision that assumes a reader. Write every step out and nobody finishes the paper.

Formalization unfolds all of it. Every step that was set aside as obvious becomes an explicit chain of inference rules, and every piece of notation that leaned on context gets pinned to a precise definition. The result is appalling to read and perfectly shaped for a machine to check line by line against the rules. That is where the figure of 13 million lines comes from. The inflation from 129 pages to 13 million lines is precisely the cost of unfolding the omissions.

It is worth getting the direction right here. Verbosity is not a failure of formalization; verbosity is what formalization does. Unnecessary verbosity is a different matter, and that distinction gets its own section further down, because the announcement itself raises it.

What a proof assistant is really checking

Lean, by its own official description, is an open-source programming language and proof assistant. What a proof assistant checks is exactly one thing. Do the inference steps from the written premises to the conclusion hold formally? Nothing more, and nothing less.

  • It checks whether each step follows from an accepted inference rule and from theorems already established. One misaligned step anywhere and the whole thing fails to go through.
  • It does not check whether the theorem is interesting, whether it matters, or whether it says what you meant it to say. Everything that requires judgement sits outside the checker.
  • It exposes as a side effect what the proof is leaning on. Lean carries a command that asks a theorem which axioms it depends on.

That last item was used here. The announcement states that this proof uses only Lean's three standard axioms. According to the official Lean documentation, the standard library adds three axioms on top of the underlying logic: propositional extensionality, a quotient construction which implies function extensionality, and a choice principle that produces data from an existential proposition. The same document notes that if a theorem makes use of the quotient construction, that shows up in the output of the axiom-printing command.

-- Ask a theorem what it is leaning on
#print axioms theoremName

-- When it rests on the three standard axioms only:
--   propositional extensionality · quotient construction · choice
-- Pull in any other axiom and its name is printed here too

Why does that command matter so much? Because the most common way to cheat in a formal proof is to quietly add one convenient axiom. Add a sufficiently strong axiom and hard theorems become easy. A claim that only the three standard axioms were used means that particular back door was not left open, and unlike most claims in an announcement, this one is a value a machine reports rather than an assertion a person makes.

The kernel - a structure where you only trust one small thing

The way formalization manufactures trust is slightly counterintuitive. You are not being asked to trust 13 million lines. You are being asked to trust the one small program that checked those 13 million lines. Lean's own description says its minimal trusted kernel is what guarantees correctness in mathematical proof and in software and hardware verification.

This arrangement is called a trusted kernel. The part that searches for the proof can be arbitrarily complicated, arbitrarily clever, or frankly arbitrarily bad, and none of that matters. The final artefact has to pass through one narrow gate, and the gate only looks at the rules. It does not ask where the proof came from. The gate is exactly the same size whether a person wrote the proof or a machine did, which is precisely why a machine-written proof of this scale is checkable at all.

Drawn out as a chain of trust, the layers separate like this.

Paper written by people (129 pages)
   |  people read and judge it        <- miss it here and nobody catches it
Formal statement
   |  transcription is still on us    <- the gap formalization does not close
Proof script (13 million lines)
   |  the kernel checks mechanically  <- the layer newly automated
Check passes + list of axioms used

The middle layer drives the rest of this article. What got newly automated is the bottom layer. The middle layer is exactly where it was.

What is not new here

The announcement draws three lines around its own result. Those lines do not shrink the achievement; they locate it precisely, which is more useful than either praise or dismissal.

First, the mathematics is not new. The announcement says in so many words that what is novel here is the verification, and it goes as far as contrasting this with recent work on the Riemann hypothesis that did produce novel mathematics. That this contrast was written by the publishing party is not a detail to skim past. In a document presenting its own accomplishment, it is an explicit marker saying this part was not their doing.

Second, the arrangement of the proof is not new. The formalization is built on the simplified arrangement of Wiles's proof due to Darmon, Diamond and Taylor. In other words it followed a route that people had already smoothed out. It did not cut a new one.

Third, the length of the code is not a virtue. The announcement states that Mathlib is concise and well reviewed, while this proof is likely much longer than it needs to be. That sentence is a reservation the publishing party entered about its own artefact. Length is not a quality metric, and here it points in the opposite direction from the one a large number normally suggests.

What the independent verifier said, and where he added a qualifier

Two passages from the formal mathematician Kevin Buzzard appear in the announcement. He leads a separate long-running project to formalize Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean. According to the project page on Lean's official site, that work proceeds by mapping dependencies between statements with blueprint tooling, and the new definitions and theories it produces are contributed back to Mathlib.

His assessment comes in two parts. In the first, he calls it an extraordinary autoformalization achievement which, as he puts it, Anthropic researchers say only took eleven days, and which proves Fermat's Last Theorem with no assumptions other than the axioms of mathematics; along the way, he writes, we see autoformalization of algebra, harmonic analysis, geometry and number theory, and we learn that AI autoformalization artefacts are now robust enough to be built upon, the proof being multi-layered. In the second, he argues that if automatic formalization of the theorem is possible now then a big step has been taken towards automatic formalization of the modern mathematical literature, that such techniques will lead to new tools rooting out errors in the current mathematical corpus and lightening the load of referees, and that they will let people rigorously check machine-generated mathematics, which is at present typically an extremely costly human-led process.

The instructive part is where he attached a qualifier. When the eleven days come up, he says that the researchers say so. That single clause draws the boundary exactly. What he confirmed independently is that the code actually compiles. How many days the work took was never something he was in a position to check. When that clause gets dropped in transit, a claim by the publishing party starts to look as though a third party had verified it, which is a different and much stronger statement than the one he made.

A proof people reviewed versus a proof a machine checked

The two guarantees are of different kinds. Neither is superior; they simply catch different classes of error, and the errors one is blind to are often exactly the ones the other is good at.

AspectProof reviewed by peopleProof checked by a machine
What is inspectedPersuasiveness of the argument and soundness of the stepsFormal validity of each inference step
Handling of omissionsJudged obvious and passed overCannot be passed over. Everything must be written to go through
Errors it catches wellA wrong approach, a misused conceptFine logical gaps, missing edge cases
Errors it missesOne flawed step buried in hundreds of pagesA statement that was written down wrongly
Resource consumedExpert time, which cannot be substitutedCompute time, which can be increased
ReproducibilityThe same reviewer may reach a different verdict twiceThe same code returns the same answer whenever it is run
Remaining trust anchorThe competence and diligence of the reviewerCorrectness of the kernel, plus the transcription of the statement

Read the last row across and the significance of this announcement comes into focus. The problem that hung over the large proofs of the late twentieth century was that the review burden fell entirely on people. Reading 129 pages over several years consumes the time of the handful of people capable of reading them, and that time cannot be bought or parallelized. Moving some of that burden onto compute time turns a problem of scale into a problem of resources, and problems of resources are the kind that get cheaper.

The column formalization does not close - is the statement right

This is where the most common misunderstanding about formalization lives. When the kernel accepts something, it means the conclusion follows from the statement as written. Whether that statement faithfully renders Fermat's Last Theorem is not the kernel's concern and never was.

Put bluntly, you can write the statement incorrectly and then prove that incorrect statement flawlessly. The kernel will accept it without complaint, because from its point of view nothing is wrong. That is exactly why this project needed a separate measure. The announcement says a comparator confirmed that the formal statement matches the statement of Fermat's Last Theorem that Mathlib already carried. The very need for that comparison shows that transcribing the statement is not a column formalization closes on its own.

QuestionWho answers itIf the answer is wrong
Does each inference step obey the rulesThe kernel, mechanicallyCompilation fails. It does not slip through quietly
Which axioms is it leaning onThe axiom-printing command, as a machine-generated listAn unfamiliar name appears in the list and gives it away
Does the formal statement match the theoremPeople, or a comparison against an existing statementA flawless proof gets attached to the wrong proposition
Do the definitions denote the intended objectsPeople. There is no substitute for reading themYou end up proving a different concept with the same name
Is the kernel itself free of defectsManaged by keeping the kernel small by designThe whole chain wobbles, which is why it is kept small
Is this theorem a meaningful resultPeople. The machine never asks the questionA correct answer that is of no use to anyone

The bottom three rows are what remains with people even after formalization. It is the same structure that keeps recurring when you build a review pipeline for machine output. The checker catches contradictions within the specification, and whether the specification captured the requirement is something a person reads.

Scale - what 13 million lines and six billion tokens say

Large numbers tend to read as persuasive on their own, so it helps to attach each figure to the thing it is a measurement of.

Thirteen million lines is the volume of the artefact. It is not its quality. As noted above, the announcement itself wrote that this proof is likely much longer than it needs to be. In formal proof, length usually indicates a failure to compress. Write the same argument out in full every time instead of factoring it into reusable lemmas and the line count rises while the readability falls, which is the opposite of what a library wants.

The gap between 30,300 and 29,500 is also a readable value. Eight hundred theorems were proved and did not make it into the final route. That is not waste; it is the trace of search. You cannot know in advance which direction will pay off, so you stand up several branches and keep the ones that connect. That ratio tells you something about how the work was conducted. It does not tell you how large the achievement is.

Six billion output tokens is an input quantity. The announcement says these came from a general-purpose internal research model. Input quantity is not an index of difficulty; it is closer to an index of how efficient the current method is. Find a way to reach the same result with far fewer tokens and this number drops accordingly. So six billion is better read as a way of gauging the order of magnitude of what work at this scale costs than as a measure of how big the result was.

Eleven days is wall-clock time. That value depends heavily on how far the work parallelized. The announcement describes multiple agents collaborating on different components, and Prove2Me, the platform that made the collaboration possible, maintains a directed acyclic graph of theorem statements, speeds up Lean compilation, and lets existing theorems be found and reused through natural-language descriptions. Eleven days is therefore not the time to walk one path in order. It is the time to walk many paths at once, which is a different quantity wearing the same unit.

Mathlib and this code are not the same kind of object

Mathlib is the mathematical library for Lean. Its official overview shows formalized material spanning algebra, linear algebra, topology, analysis, probability, geometry, combinatorics and more, and new material arrives through pull requests. The same page maintains a review and triage dashboard alongside a pull request review guide. In short, being in Mathlib means people read the material and accepted it.

Whether these 13 million lines clear that threshold is a separate question, and the reservation the announcement entered about itself points straight at it: Mathlib is concise and well reviewed, while this proof is likely much longer than it needs to be. Split into two thresholds, it looks like this.

  • The threshold a machine opens — does it pass the kernel? This work cleared that one, and somebody outside the project compiled the code to confirm it.
  • The threshold people open — can the next person read it and modify it? Library adoption sits on this side, and this is where length and structure become the obstacle.
  • The relation between them — clearing the first is a precondition for clearing the second. It is not evidence that the second was cleared.

Blur that distinction and passing a checker starts to read as though a library-grade asset had come into existence. What actually exists is one enormous script that passes a checker, and turning that into a reusable mathematical asset takes a different kind of work by a different set of hands.

So what actually opened up

Drawing the lines makes the remainder sharper rather than smaller. What this work demonstrated is that it is feasible to move thousands of pages of existing literature into machine-checkable form in a short span of time. That is a large result on its own terms, and it does not need the word proof to be impressive.

  • Shifting the review burden — part of what a referee had to follow line by line moves onto compute. This is the direction the independent verifier pointed toward.
  • Finding errors in existing literature — formalizing an already published argument surfaces the gaps that were folded away. Formalization does not permit omissions, so what was hiding behind them comes out.
  • Checking machine-generated mathematics — arguments produced by large language models can be put through a checker instead of being read line by line, and at present that reading is mostly expensive human labour.
  • Artefacts you can build layers on — the assessment is that autoformalization output is now robust enough to serve as a base for further work. A result you use once and discard and a result you can stack on are different assets.

All four items are demonstrations of feasibility rather than changes that have already happened. Whether what worked for one theorem works across a literature is a question of a different order, and that answer is not in yet.

How to read the next announcement like this one

News of this kind will keep arriving. Five lines are enough to expose the distance between a headline and its contents.

What to checkWhyIn this case
Proof or formalizationThe two words name different jobsFormalization. The proof finished in 1995
What source was it built onIt separates a new route from an existing oneIt followed an existing arrangement
Which figures are the publisher's claimsA claim and an external confirmation weigh differentlyTime and tokens are claims; compilation was confirmed
How was the statement itself checkedThis is the column formalization does not closeCompared against an existing statement
What is each number a measurement ofVolume and quality are different axesLine count is volume; length is a stated reservation

Those five lines are not confined to mathematics news. The structure in which passing a checker is not the same as meeting a requirement repeats itself daily in software. It holds when agents are reviewing code and when a process rather than a person filters model output for factual accuracy. The columns to separate are the same ones: hand the machine the questions a machine can answer, and know which questions it never asks.

The value of this announcement is that the bottom column got a great deal wider. The limit this announcement teaches is that the middle column is exactly where it was. Hold both sentences at once and the story neither shrinks nor inflates, which is the only shape in which it is worth carrying forward.

What could not be confirmed

The items below were kept out of the body because their wording could not be located in the primary source. The reason is given alongside each.

  • Whether the independent verifier said directly that no new mathematical insight emerged — no sentence to that effect appears in either of the two passages quoted from him in the announcement. The judgement that what is novel is the verification rather than the mathematics is a statement in the announcement's own text, not a quotation from him. Mixing the two sources would misattribute it, so the two are kept apart in the body.
  • Whether he raised doubts about the code on the grounds of Mathlib's inclusion standards — no such sentence was located in the announcement. The reservation about verbosity is the announcement's own wording, and it is attributed that way.
  • How many times longer the code takes to compile than Mathlib, and whether the line count is 13.4 million — a Korean summary carries figures of roughly twenty times on a 96-core machine and 13.4 million lines, but those sentences could not be located in the announcement, so the body uses only the announcement's figure of 13 million lines.
  • The versions of Lean and Mathlib used — no place stating exact version numbers was found. Formal proofs reproduce or fail to reproduce depending on library version, so this is the first thing to establish before attempting to reproduce the result.
  • The actual axiom-printing output for this proof — the announcement states that only the three standard axioms are used, but no published instance of the output itself was located. The command shown in the body illustrates the form and is not this proof's output.
  • The cost of the work — no figure appears in the announcement. Community estimates in the hundreds of thousands of dollars circulate for output tokens alone, but those are third-party estimates and were not carried into the body as numbers.
  • Whether the code is public and under what licence — no sentence naming a repository or usage terms was located. That an independent verifier compiled it means the code reached him at minimum, which is a separate matter from general availability.
  • The boundary implied by largely autonomous — no concrete criterion was found for how much human intervention occurred at which stage. The announcement says only that there was occasional high-level guidance.
