Korea's National Health Screening - Who Is Eligible, When, and What Gets Tested

Whether this is your year, what is covered and where the billing starts, and who pays the fine when a checkup is skipped - the system rather than the numbers

Aug 11, 2026 Health & Wellness
Read this first: this article explains the Korean national health screening system and is general information, not a substitute for medical diagnosis, prescription or treatment. A screening report carries a screening-stage finding, not a confirmed diagnosis, and what a value means for an individual is for a clinician to judge. Every age, cycle and cost figure below was checked against public-institution guidance as of August 2026 and changes when the programme is revised. Confirm your own eligibility and item list through the National Health Insurance Service and the notice letter addressed to you.

Introduction: the first question is whether this is your year

As the year winds down, a list of unscreened employees circulates at Korean workplaces. Some years a notice letter arrives and some years it does not. The colleague at the next desk was screened last year while you are up this year, and the colleague on the production floor goes every single year. It looks arbitrary. It is not: eligibility falls out of three things stacked together, namely insurance status, birth year and job type.

Cost works the same way. The general health checkup is paid in full by the insurance service, yet parts of cancer screening carry a 10 percent patient share, and other parts of that same cancer programme cost nothing at all. Add the extra tests a screening centre offers on the day and the arithmetic falls apart entirely.

This is not an article about reading the numbers on the report. Reference ranges and what each value means are covered separately in Complete Guide to Reading Health Checkup Results. What this piece covers is the stage before that: who is eligible, when your turn comes round, what is actually tested, and how far the free coverage reaches. Preparation, the consequences of skipping, results and the confirmatory deadline, and what happens when you change jobs all sit on the same axis.

1. Who is eligible - insurance status comes first

The starting point is not age but health insurance status. Two people aged 40 can be notified in completely different ways depending on whether they are a workplace subscriber, a regional household member or a dependant.

  • Regional subscribers: household heads and household members aged 20 and over. The notice goes to the household head's registered address.
  • Dependants: eligible from age 20. You do not need to be employed for the screening itself to apply to you.
  • Workplace subscribers: eligible with no lower age limit, with the cycle depending on whether the role counts as office work.
  • Medical Aid beneficiaries: Ministry of Health and Welfare policy guidance lists the general checkup cohort as ages 20 to 64. From age 66 the separate Medical Aid life-transition examination track takes over.

One misconception is worth clearing away. This is not a benefit you apply for; the insurance service selects the cohort and notifies it. There is no application procedure to miss, and a letter failing to arrive is not evidence that you are outside the cohort. Post goes astray routinely right after a move or a change in insurance status.

2. Odd and even birth years - how the two-year cycle actually turns

For anyone on the two-year cycle, your year is set by the final digit of your birth year. Digits 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 belong to even years; 1, 3, 5, 7 and 9 belong to odd years. 2026 is an even year, so someone born in 1970, 1984 or 1992 is up this year.

The reasoning behind the rule is plain capacity management. Screening the whole population in a single year would overwhelm the provider network. Splitting the population in half and alternating halves the load while automatically enforcing the two-year interval at the individual level.

Where it trips people up is that the rule looks at the birth year, not the age you have reached. Someone born in December and someone born the following January are handled in different cohorts even though they are barely a month apart. And the rule only governs people on the two-year cycle: for non-office workers who are due annually, odd and even mean nothing.

  • Even final digit means even years are your turn (2026, 2028 and so on)
  • Odd final digit means odd years are your turn (2027, 2029 and so on)
  • Workers due annually are unaffected by the odd-even split
  • Cancer screening runs on its own per-cancer cycle and does not always land in the same year

3. Office and non-office workers - why the cycle splits inside one company

The cycle for workplace screening comes from the Occupational Safety and Health Act rather than from insurance rules. Article 197 of its enforcement regulation requires a general health examination at least once every two years for office workers and at least once a year for all other workers.

The important part is that the definition of office work is narrow. The same provision defines an office worker as someone engaged in clerical duties such as general affairs, personnel, accounting, sales administration or design, in an office that is not located in the same area as a factory or construction site, and it excludes workers directly engaged in sales work.

That is how an employee who handles nothing but paperwork, in an office attached to a site, ends up classified as a non-office worker and screened every year. The split follows where the work happens and what it consists of, not the title on the business card. If you are unsure which side you are on, the fastest answer comes from whoever handles occupational health at your employer.

  • Classification follows workplace location and job content, not rank or job title
  • Direct sales work is excluded from the office category even in an office setting
  • Non-office workers are due annually regardless of birth year
  • When the classification changes, the cycle changes with it from the following round

4. What the general health checkup looks at

The general checkup is designed to catch cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk factors early. Obesity, dyslipidaemia, hypertension and diabetes all progress quietly and announce themselves late, and that is the axis the panel aims at. The item list is deliberately unglamorous, because it has to be repeatable across the entire population without strain.

Items come in two layers: a common set that every eligible person receives, and additional items attached to particular ages or to one sex. Which ones apply to you in a given year is printed on your notice letter.

5. The six national cancer screenings - ages, cycles and what you pay

National cancer screening is a separate programme from the general checkup. Target ages and cycles are set per cancer, and the cost rules differ too. The criteria published by the National Cancer Information Center are as follows.

High risk is defined rather than guessed at. The liver high-risk group covers people with cirrhosis, hepatitis B surface antigen positivity, hepatitis C antibody positivity, or chronic liver disease caused by hepatitis B or C virus. The lung high-risk group covers current smokers with a smoking history of 30 pack-years or more, plus those designated by ministerial notice as having a high need for lung cancer screening. A pack-year is daily consumption in packs multiplied by years smoked.

The most common billing surprise is that confirmatory testing is not part of the cancer screening programme. The faecal occult blood test costs nothing, but if it comes back abnormal and a colonoscopy follows, that step runs under ordinary health insurance rules. Medical Aid beneficiaries are covered in full.

  • Cancer screening eligibility is determined separately from the general checkup
  • When both fall in the same year, taking them at one centre is usually simpler
  • First-line tests and confirmatory tests follow different cost rules
  • Add-on tests offered outside the national programme are entirely self-paid
  • Items and cycles change as evidence accumulates, so re-read the guidance in the year you are screened

6. Infants and students run on separate tracks

The national programme is not only for adults. Children are covered by different systems by age band.

6.1 Infant and toddler health checkups

These run from 14 days to 71 months of age in eight rounds. Round one falls at 14 to 35 days, round two at 4 to 6 months, three at 9 to 12 months, four at 18 to 24 months, five at 30 to 36 months, six at 42 to 48 months, seven at 54 to 60 months and eight at 66 to 71 months. Oral examinations sit on their own schedule at 18 to 29, 30 to 41, 42 to 53 and 54 to 65 months.

The content differs in kind from adult screening. Instead of blood panels it consists of history taking and examination, body measurements (height, weight, head circumference), developmental screening with counselling, and health education. The design watches whether growth and development are departing from the expected path. There is no patient share.

6.2 School health examinations

School-age children fall under the School Health Act and the School Health Examination Rules. Health examinations target the first and fourth years of elementary school and the first year of middle and high school, while oral examinations cover all years. The examination is divided into physical development, physical ability, a health survey, a mental health status check and the health examination itself. It runs through the school rather than the insurance service, so the school's notice is what to follow.

7. Finding a screening centre and booking

Screening is not available at any clinic; it happens at designated screening institutions. The insurance service website and mobile app handle eligibility lookup and centre search in the same place.

  • Authenticate on the website or app to confirm eligibility and this year's item list
  • Search for centres near home or work
  • Tests needing equipment and staffing, such as endoscopy or mammography, are not available everywhere, so confirm when booking
  • If the notice letter has gone missing, use the online lookup instead and ask the centre what documents it needs
  • Year-end is congested; move after November and the centre you wanted is often full

The screening year runs from 1 January to 31 December. Miss it while eligible and that round is gone; on a two-year cycle the next turn is two years away.

8. Preparation - fasting and medication

The insurance service advises a minimum of eight hours of fasting before testing. For a morning slot that means nothing after the previous evening meal, and the eight-hour rule applies to afternoon slots too. Fasting matters because several items were designed around a fasting state.

Do not adjust medication on your own. Whether to skip a dose is not a decision to make unaided. Drugs that interact directly with fasting, such as diabetes medication, and drugs that interact with the possibility of endoscopy or biopsy, such as antithrombotics, can be dangerous to withhold without instruction. Tell the prescribing clinician or the screening centre what you take when you book, and follow the instruction you receive.
  • If there is any chance of pregnancy, say so before a chest X-ray or mammogram
  • If your appointment overlaps with menstruation, ask whether urinalysis and the cervical smear should be rescheduled
  • The colorectal faecal test uses a container collected in advance and brought from home
  • Sedated endoscopy restricts driving afterwards, so plan your transport first
  • Bring photo identification; without identity verification the appointment cannot proceed
  • Ask whether the oral examination can be done on the same visit

9. What happens if you skip it

This is where individuals and employers part ways.

Regional subscribers and dependants are not fined as individuals for missing a round. The loss is not a sanction but simply the round going unused.

Employees are different. Article 129, paragraph 1 of the Occupational Safety and Health Act places on the employer the duty to arrange health examinations for the workers it regularly employs. Workplace screening is therefore a statutory employer obligation rather than an optional benefit, and non-compliance attracts an administrative fine under Article 175 of the same Act. That is precisely why companies chase unscreened staff in December.

The amount is calculated from the number of offences and the number of workers involved. No figure is given here because the schedule in the enforcement decree was not verified directly; the exact basis should be confirmed through Ministry of Employment and Labor guidance.

10. Results and the second-stage examination

Results are notified within 15 days of the examination according to insurance service guidance. When the general checkup raises suspicion of hypertension or diabetes, the case moves to a second stage, the confirmatory examination.

There is one deadline that is easy to miss. The screening year closes on 31 December, but the confirmatory test can be taken until 31 March of the following year. Screened in December, your report lands at the turn of the year, and the remaining window goes unnoticed.

What to do when the report arrives: first check the summary for any retest or confirmatory instruction, then check whether the window (31 March of the following year) is still open, and see a clinician about flagged items either way. The verdict on the report is a screening finding, not a confirmed diagnosis.

11. Changing jobs, leaving, and moving employers

Because eligibility rests on insurance status, birth year and job type, a change in status mid-year moves the basis of the decision with it. The situations that come up most often work like this.

  • Already screened at the previous employer: that year is done, and there is no reason to repeat it at the new one
  • Screening after the move: eligibility is judged on the new status, so look it up before booking
  • Moving from non-office to office work: the cycle may shift to two years from the next round
  • Leaving employment for regional subscriber or dependant status: judged on that basis instead, and the entitlement does not disappear
  • Records stay with the insurance service across status changes, so previous rounds remain retrievable

It is also worth remembering that what your employer needs and what the insurance service holds are two different things. To evidence compliance with the Occupational Safety and Health Act, an employer needs that year's examination result, so a new employer may ask for documentation depending on when you moved.

12. How other countries fill the same slot

A system where a single public insurer selects the cohort, sends the notice and pays the bill is not the common pattern. Compared by character rather than by figures:

  • United States: there is no single national screening programme; prevention runs through insurance coverage. Which preventive services reach a patient without cost sharing is tied to recommendation grades from an advisory body and to plan rules, so entitlement follows the plan rather than the person.
  • Japan: a specific health checkup aimed at lifestyle-related disease sits alongside cancer screening run by municipalities, with insurers and local government each carrying a different axis.
  • China: employer-organised work-unit physical examinations operate alongside basic public health services, and what a person actually receives varies considerably by region and affiliation.

No target ages, cycles or cost-sharing rates for those three systems were verified here, so no figures are quoted. The comparison exists to make one point about Korea: eligibility is status-based and notified nationally, which is why a person who knows nothing about the procedure still gets a letter when their turn arrives.

13. What this article could not verify

  • Fine amounts for non-compliance - Articles 129 and 175 of the Occupational Safety and Health Act were confirmed as the legal basis, but the calculation schedule in the enforcement decree was not read directly.
  • Whether individual workers can be fined - the employer duty is confirmed in the statute; the existence and conditions of any provision aimed at the worker were not.
  • Medical Aid age band - the ministry policy listing was carried over as printed. Which track covers age 65 was not confirmed.
  • Income-based exemption from the cancer screening share - full coverage for Medical Aid beneficiaries is confirmed, but the threshold for premium-based exemption among insured subscribers was not.
  • Screening for out-of-school youth - a separate track is known to exist, but its targets and cycle were not confirmed from a primary source, so no figures appear.
  • Detail of the mental health check - target ages for the depression component were confirmed; other sub-items and their age bands were not.

For anything left open here, the accurate route is the insurance service lookup, the notice letter addressed to you, and the health and welfare counselling line on 129.

14. Frequently asked questions

No notice letter arrived. Does that mean I am not eligible?

No, and you should not assume so. Post goes astray routinely after an address change or a change in insurance status. The website and app will confirm eligibility independently of the letter.

Can I take the general checkup and cancer screening on the same day?

If both fall due in the same year, doing them at one centre is usually simpler. Tests needing equipment and preparation, such as upper endoscopy, may land on a different date depending on the centre, so confirm when booking.

Is the company checkup different from the national one?

At most workplaces the national checkup serves as the statutory general health examination. A comprehensive package where the employer pays to add items is a different product, and who bears the cost of the additions follows company policy.

I missed last year. Can I take both this year?

The screening year closes annually, so a missed round does not carry over. For anyone on the two-year cycle, missing your year means the next turn is two years out.

Will an abnormal result hurt my chances of buying insurance?

Private insurance underwriting follows the insurer's own criteria and is outside what this article can settle. For how indemnity health insurance coverage and claims work, see Understanding and Claiming Indemnity Health Insurance, and confirm underwriting rules with the insurer concerned.

Conclusion

Korea's national screening is not a programme you apply to; it is one that notifies you when you become eligible. What you need to know is therefore not how to apply but which row you sit in. Insurance status is the first fork, the final digit of your birth year is the second for anyone on the two-year cycle, and office classification is the third if you are an employee.

Cost splits by programme. The general checkup is paid in full, cancer screening divides into items with a 10 percent share and items with none, and from confirmatory testing onward the rules change again. Knowing that much before you book is what lets you judge the add-ons a centre offers on the day.

One last thing. Do not stop at receiving the report. The confirmatory window stays open until 31 March of the following year, and the verdict on the report is not a diagnosis. What the numbers mean is settled together with a clinician; this article's job ends at opening the door in front of that. Everything above reflects August 2026 and changes as the programme is revised.
