We Averaged the p99 of Ten Servers - That Number Is Nothing

Percentiles do not merge. An aggregation error and a collection design, checked with fixed-seed runs you can reproduce

2026-07-30 Dev & Infra

The dashboard looks fine and users say it is slow

There is a p99 line on the response-time dashboard. It has been flat around 90 ms for a month. Support tickets are up anyway. The screen freezes a lot. The pay button spins for ages. Grep the access log yourself and there are plenty of requests over 400 ms. Why did the dashboard not show them?

The usual answer is that p99 throws away one percent by definition. True, but not the cause here. The cause is simpler and worse. That 90 ms on the screen was never a p99 at all. It was the mean of ten p99 values, one from each of ten servers. And a percentile is not the kind of number you can merge by averaging.

This article puts numbers on the difference. The same 100,000 requests were summarised two ways. Taking each server's p99 and averaging them gave 91.04 ms. Pooling every request into one bucket and computing the p99 gave 449.63 ms. That is 4.94x. Both numbers carry the label p99, and neither came out of a bug. Both did exactly the arithmetic they were told to do.

The awkward part is how quiet this error is. The displayed value is plausible, the unit is right, it rises when traffic rises, and it gets worse after a bad deploy. The direction is mostly correct, so nothing prompts suspicion. And a wrongly merged percentile never raises an exception. A broken encoding shows up as garbled text you can see; a broken aggregation just looks like a number.

In practice the problem announces itself through four symptoms first.

  • The dashboard p99 stays flat while support tickets and the count of slow requests in the log both climb
  • Split the same window by server and one host is visibly bad, yet in the fleet-wide chart that spike has disappeared
  • Change the group-by in the dashboard (per host → per region → everything) and the same p99 becomes a different value
  • The per-minute p99 chart has sharp spikes, while the single number labelled p99 for this hour is calm

The last two are the decisive clues, and sections 12 and 9 show why with calculations. The conclusion up front: a percentile is not a value but a property of a distribution, and properties do not average. So the real question is not whether to watch p99 or p95. It is what you collected. Collection design decides metric accuracy.

Environment. Every output below was actually run on Windows 10 Pro (10.0.19045) with Python 3.13.3. Standard library only, no external numerics package. Scripts were saved to files and executed, not passed through shell arguments or heredocs.

Fixed seeds. All randomness is pinned. The base seed is 20260730, and section k uses random.Random(20260730 + k). Normal deviates come from a Box-Muller transform on top of random(). Run it in the same order on the same Python and every number in this article comes back. An example you cannot reproduce is not evidence. The artifact box below carries a condensed reproduction script, and it regenerates the three numbers from section 1 exactly.

All data is synthetic. No real service metric was used. Latencies were generated from a lognormal distribution, which matches the observation that response times have a long right tail, and none of the argument here depends on that particular choice. The default percentile rule is nearest-rank: sort, then take the ceil(0.99 x n)-th value, so the answer is always one request that actually happened. Where a section uses something else, it says so.

1. One counterexample - averaging the p99 of ten servers

Only web-10 differs. Note first that its p50 is 30.00 ms, less than double the others. On medians alone it reads as slightly slow. Its p99, though, is 1020.21 ms, twenty-five times the rest. A host that broke only in the tail does not show up in median metrics.

And the next two lines are the heart of this article. Averaging the per-server p99 gives 138.51 ms. Pooling every request and computing the p99 gives 209.67 ms. A gap of 71.16 ms, shaving 33.94 percent off the truth. A factor of 1.51.

Folding the same ten numbers other ways is more interesting still.

  • Fold with max and you get 1020.21 ms, 386.57 percent above the truth. Badly overstated, but it never lands below the true value. Section 12 shows that is not luck
  • Fold with median and you get 40.59 ms, 80.64 percent low. The bad host is erased completely. Robustness against outliers, the property you normally want, works exactly backwards here
  • Fold with min and you get 39.90 ms, a lower bound. Useless, but at least honest about its direction
  • Fold with mean and you get 138.51 ms, which is neither an upper nor a lower bound and points at no request at all. Some request among the 100,000 may well have taken 138.51 ms, but that has nothing to do with why this number appeared
  • No fold of those ten numbers produces 209.67. That information was already discarded the moment each server summarised itself

All 1001 of them came from web-10. The other nine contributed zero. web-10 handles 10 percent of the traffic and owns 100 percent of the tail. The mean gives it a weight of one tenth. Hence the 90 percent haircut.

This exposes the core of percentile aggregation. To compute the pooled p99 correctly you need to know each server's share of the pooled top one percent. That share bears no relation to its share of traffic (10 percent versus 100 percent). And the moment you keep only one p99 number per server, the material for computing that share goes with it. What was thrown away is not precision but structure.

So why does this error survive so long? Change one condition and the answer appears. Make all ten servers identical and spread traffic evenly.

per-server p99 values : 128.99, 126.51, 125.63, 129.69, 118.68, 129.06, 132.86, 123.23, 129.23, 131.61
MEAN of them          :   127.55 ms
TRUE pooled p99       :   127.59 ms
deviation             :    -0.03 %

The averaged p99 is 127.55 ms and the true p99 is 127.59 ms. An error of 0.03 percent. Effectively correct.

That is why nobody catches it. It is right in normal times. While the fleet is homogeneous and the load balancer spreads evenly, the two numbers sit on top of each other. They part company the moment one host degrades - that is, at the exact moment the metric is needed.

  • A metric that is correct when things are fine and wrong when they are not cannot be used to detect that things are not fine, which is its only purpose
  • It also resists verification. Compare the two values during normal operation and they match, so the review concludes there is no problem. Any validation done only in the healthy state passes this defect
  • The size of the error scales with how badly homogeneity has broken. And broken homogeneity is precisely the event you were looking for. The instrument fails as the signal grows

  • The sign of the error depends on the situation. So no approach of the form multiply by roughly this factor can work. You cannot correct an error whose direction you do not know
  • Case A is a dashboard showing 91 ms when reality is 450 ms. You miss the incident. That is precisely the symptom in the introduction
  • Case B is a dashboard showing 459 ms when reality is 218 ms. You chase an incident that does not exist. There is no cause to find, so the investigation never ends
  • The two lists of per-server p99 values contain the same elements. All that differs is which value sits next to the large traffic, and that pairing is not present in the list of p99 values
  • Count weighting worked in case A because one server was so dominant that its own p99 nearly was the pooled p99. That is a situation, not a principle. Section 5 pushes on this

3. Maxima do merge - the decisive difference between p100 and p99

An objection is available here. Is the maximum not the 100th percentile? Does that fail to merge as well? Running it splits the answer, and that split is the most important conceptual point in this article.

per-group max : 402.68, 409.36, 451.85, 627.13, 577.14, 699.29, 517.51, 531.50, 1107.77, 729.08

MEAN of per-group max :   605.3333 ms
MAX  of per-group max :  1107.7706 ms
TRUE max of pooled    :  1107.7706 ms
mean equals truth     : False   (off by -45.36 %)
max-of-max equals truth: True
min-of-min equals truth: True

The mean of the per-group maxima is 605.33 ms against a true maximum of 1107.77 ms, understated by 45.36 percent - the mean is wrong here too. But taking the maximum of the maxima gives an exact match: equal to four decimal places, and True under a floating-point comparison. Minima behave the same way.

The difference is not in the name but in the algebra. The maximum has a fold rule that builds the whole from the parts: the maximum of a union always equals the maximum of the parts' maxima. p99 has no such rule. There exists no function that computes the p99 of a union from the p99 values of the parts. This is not a statement about a poor approximation; the function does not exist.

  • Maxima fold because each part's maximum carries everything needed about that part. The only candidates for the overall maximum are the part maxima; the remaining values were never candidates
  • p99 is different. The value that becomes the pooled p99 can sit anywhere inside a part. In section 1 the true p99 of 209.67 was neither web-10's p99 of 1020.21 nor the roughly 40 ms of the others. It sat somewhere in web-10's middle
  • So it is true that p100 merges while p99 does not. p100 is not the extreme member of the percentile family but a different kind of statistic, and the similar name is what breeds the confusion
  • There is a practical consequence. Maximum-based metrics are safe to aggregate at any granularity - slowest request, longest queue, peak concurrency. You never need to suspect the computation path. They are also entirely decided by one request, so they are noisy. Safe and useful are separate properties

4. Which metrics merge and which do not

Now check them one at a time. Six groups, deliberately given different sizes (3,000 up to 7,500). Equal sizes would make the plain mean and the weighted mean coincide by accident and hide the distinction. And to keep floating-point rounding from muddying any claim of exact equality, values were converted to integer microseconds first.

The last column is the point of this section. Count, sum, max and min are fully recoverable from the partial results, and True there means integer identity, not approximation. The mean is conditional: the plain mean is off by 11.2681 percent, while weighting by count is exact. p99 and p50 are recoverable by no method at all.

Summarised:

SumSum of the sumsExactThe sum. Addition is associative

The variance row is the most instructive line in the table. Store only the standard deviation and you cannot merge; store count, sum and sum of squares and it recovers exactly. The same metric is mergeable or not depending on what you stored. That property is the whole basis of the histogram approach in section 10.

  • Mergeability is not a property of the metric but a property of the summary you saved. This leads straight to the practical conclusion of this article
  • A mean stored without its count cannot be merged. If a dashboard keeps means and drops counts, even the mean stops merging. And as the table shows, that error lands around 11 percent, which is small enough to go unnoticed
  • No auxiliary number rescues a percentile. The exception is a summary that approximates the distribution itself (bucketed histograms and similar), and that is a structure rather than a single number
  • Read backwards, this is the collection rule. Any metric you will need to recompute at a different granularity must be stored in a foldable form. Store it in a non-foldable form and every later re-aggregation is wrong
  • The two rows marked impossible happen to be the two metrics dashboards use most. That is the size of this problem

5. Why weighting cannot rescue a percentile

Count weighting worked in one case in section 2, which makes it look like a matter of picking better weights. Several weighting schemes were applied to one dataset to see which of them works as a principle.

group  requests   weight      p99
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
a         60000   0.6000    65.06
b         25000   0.2500    87.34
c         10000   0.1000   920.90
d          5000   0.0500  2504.83
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
TRUE pooled p99                 :   413.37 ms
  plain mean of p99              :   894.53 ms   deviation  +116.40 %
  count-weighted mean of p99     :   278.20 ms   deviation   -32.70 %
  square-root-count weighted     :   521.03 ms   deviation   +26.04 %
  tail-count weighted (0.01*n)   :   278.20 ms   deviation   -32.70 %
  max of p99                     :  2504.83 ms   deviation  +505.95 %
  min of p99                     :    65.06 ms   deviation   -84.26 %

The true p99 is 413.37 ms. The plain mean is 116.40 percent high, count weighting 32.70 percent low, square-root weighting 26.04 percent high. None is right, and none is consistently wrong in one direction.

Notice that the row weighted by 0.01 times the request count produced exactly the same value as count weighting. Scaling every weight by a constant does not change a weighted mean. In other words, any weight derived from request counts gives the same answer as count weighting, because no new information entered. That is where weight tuning hits a wall.

So which weights are correct? The correct weights are each group's share of the pooled top one percent. Those were extracted directly.

group  share of traffic   share of the pooled top 1 percent
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
a               60.00 %                         0.00 %
b               25.00 %                         0.00 %
c               10.00 %                        42.86 %
d                5.00 %                        57.14 %
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Groups a and b, holding 85 percent of the traffic, contribute zero percent of the tail. The tail is built by c and d, which hold 15 percent. So the correct weight vector is close to (0, 0, 0.4286, 0.5714) while the count weights are (0.6, 0.25, 0.1, 0.05). The two vectors point in effectively opposite directions.

  • The correct weight is each group's tail contribution, and that only emerges from the raw data. Once only per-server p99 values remain, the information is gone
  • So this is not a problem of choosing weights. It is an attempt to express a value that the weighted-mean form cannot represent at all. The form lacks expressive power; the coefficients are not merely mistuned
  • The practical core is that traffic share and tail share are unrelated. The intuition that the busiest server matters most does not hold in the tail. A quiet node building the entire tail is a common pattern
  • A weighted mean does sometimes land close by luck (10.11 percent in case A). Even then, the only way to know it landed close is to look at the raw data. An approximation you cannot check is not an approximation but a guess

6. What the mean hides - one section only

The reason for using percentiles at all gets one short check, because it is not this article's subject. Two distributions were built with nearly identical mean response times.

shape                     mean      p50      p90      p99     p999      max
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A near-normal             40.00    40.01    47.68    53.92    58.41    66.46
B long tail               39.85    24.36    46.17   473.97  1670.75  6766.82
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
means differ by 0.38 %, but p99 differs by 8.79 x and p999 by 28.60 x.

The means are 40.00 and 39.85, a difference of 0.38 percent. The p99 values are 53.92 and 473.97, a factor of 8.79, and the p99.9 values differ by 28.60x. A report on mean response time would be effectively the same document for both services, while the user experience is nothing alike.

  • The mean is useful when the distribution is symmetric like shape A. Real response times mostly look like B. That is why tail metrics exist
  • B's median (24.36) is below its mean (39.85). In a long-tailed distribution the mean is worse than what most users see, so users faster than the mean are the majority. The mean represents nobody
  • That is as far as this section goes. The relationship between means and tails is its own subject, and this article is about how to merge a tail metric once you have decided to use one. Sections 6 and 7 each get one section and no more

7. There is more than one definition of a percentile - one section only

Before comparing numbers across tools, something else needs checking. Several calculation rules live under the single name p95. Several were applied to one small sample of n=20.

From the same twenty numbers, p95 ranges from 1200 to 2910, a factor of 2.42. And look at the last row. The exclusive method's p99 is 4422 ms while the largest observation in the sample is 3000 ms. It returned a value larger than anything observed.

This is not a bug. The Python standard library documentation states that this method is for a population that can hold more extreme values than those found in the sample, and that the fraction below the i-th of m points is computed as i / (m + 1). It behaved to specification. My own implementation clamps at the maximum, which is why the two agree at p95 and diverge at p99. One name, two answers.

  • When comparing numbers across tools, align the definitions first. Default definitions can differ, so read each tool's own documentation. Nothing here asserts what any particular product does internally
  • Definition differences only matter on small samples. Low-traffic endpoints with a few dozen requests per minute are the risky zone, and section 8 is about that
  • Definition differences are not the cause of the 4x to 5x gaps in this article. Conflating the two problems loses the real cause. When numbers are badly apart, check the aggregation path before hunting definitions
  • That is as far as this section goes too. Digging into the character of each definition is a separate subject

8. On small samples, p99 is another name for the maximum

Aggregation aside, a p99 is unstable when a window holds few requests to begin with. The same population was sampled repeatedly at different sample sizes. Because it is lognormal, the true p99 can be computed analytically, so the estimates can be compared against what they are aiming at.

population: lognormal, median 20.0 ms, sigma 0.80
analytic population p99 = median * exp(sigma * 2.3263478740408408) = 128.6133 ms

     n   trials      p5   median      p95     min      max   p95/p5   which rank is used
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
   100     2000   75.89   109.77   166.90   53.84   300.51    2.199   rank 99 of 100 (1 above it)
  1000     2000  109.97   126.51   148.66   94.90   176.01    1.352   rank 990 of 1000 (10 above it)
 10000      500  122.15   128.53   134.93  116.72   142.29    1.105   rank 9900 of 10000 (100 above it)
100000      100  126.87   128.62   130.55  125.97   131.80    1.029   rank 99000 of 100000 (1000 above it)
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

a single n=100 window, its top 5 values : 63.23, 63.43, 67.21, 75.07, 80.44
nearest-rank p99 of it                 : 75.07 ms   (the 99th of 100, i.e. 2nd largest)
drop that one request and p99 becomes  : 80.44 ms

The population's true p99 is 128.6133 ms. At n=100, the estimated p99 has its 5th percentile at 75.89 and its 95th at 166.90 - a 2.199x spread. Across 2,000 trials the minimum was 53.84 and the maximum 300.51. At n=100,000 that spread narrows to 1.029x.

The rightmost column explains the wobble. The p99 of 100 samples is the 99th value, and exactly one request sits above it. One actual window was opened to check. Its top five were 63.23, 63.43, 67.21, 75.07 and 80.44, and the p99 is 75.07. Remove that one request and the p99 becomes 80.44.

  • The p99 of 100 samples is effectively the second slowest request. The word percentile guarantees no statistical stability
  • So a p99 alert on a low-traffic endpoint moves with a single request. Widening the window or using a lower quantile (p90, p95) is the honest option. Watching p99 over 100 requests is close to watching the maximum and calling it p99
  • Widening a window and merging per-window p99 values are different operations. The latter does not work, as section 9 shows. Widening requires recomputing from raw data across the wider window
  • A percentile chart without its sample count is unreadable. Plot the denominator alongside. A p99 that jumps around in a period of collapsed traffic may be sample starvation rather than a performance change

9. Time behaves the same - sixty one-minute p99 values do not make an hourly p99

The grouping axis so far was the host. Switching to time changes nothing. Sixty minutes of 1,200 requests each, 72,000 in total, with a degradation window inserted for five minutes in the middle - shaped like a real incident.

60 minutes x 1200 requests = 72000 requests

Taking their median gives 68.07 ms, 75.58 percent low. No fold is correct, and the error range spans more than an order of magnitude.

This is the last symptom from the introduction. The spikes in the per-minute chart and the single number labelled p99 for this hour are different quantities, and neither is computable from the other. Nor is either one a bug for disagreeing with the other.

  • Nothing about the time axis makes a percentile additive. Combining twelve five-minute p99 values into an hour, or twenty-four hourly ones into a day, is the same error
  • Check how numbers are computed when you change the time range on a dashboard. Tools that automatically re-bucket to match the pixel width of the chart can quietly re-aggregate right here. If zooming changes the value, that is your signal
  • Set an alert threshold on a one-minute window and write the weekly report on an hourly window, and the two numbers cannot validate each other. An alert firing while the report stays calm can be correct behaviour
  • The median fold gives 68.07 ms. Five degraded minutes vanish entirely into the other fifty-five. Tail events are diluted identically on the host axis and the time axis

10. Histograms - an approximation you can actually merge

Does this mean holding all 100,000 raw values forever? There is an alternative, and its basis is already in section 4. Counts merge. Split values into buckets, store the count per bucket, and those counts can be added across servers. Applied to the same fleet as case A in section 2.

what merging actually did: bucket counts were ADDED across the 10 servers

Bucket boundaries were varied. The coarse seven-bucket layout is 12.11 percent high, fifteen powers of two 9.16 percent, 112 buckets growing by 10 percent 0.25 percent, and 399 buckets at 10 ms intervals 0.03 percent. On the same data, averaging the per-server p99 was 79.68 percent wrong. Even the coarsest histogram beats the averaged p99 by more than sixfold.

What made the difference is not the formula but the order. The histogram path added counts before computing any percentile. The per-server path computed a percentile first. That order is the whole story, and it is also this article's operational conclusion: put the merge before the summary.

It is still an approximation, though, and the nature of the error is worth pinning down.

interpolation inside the last bucket assumes the values are spread evenly

checked here for every scheme above:

  growth10     true 438.68 and est 439.76 both inside [405.27, 445.79] : True   abs error 1.0833 ms, bucket width 40.5265 ms

The bucket holding the answer (256 to 512 ms) contained 2,185 requests. If they were spread evenly, half would sit below the midpoint of 384 ms; in fact 72.72 percent did. So values crowd the low edge of this bucket, and interpolation under a uniform assumption read high.

No general claim is made here. That 72.72 percent and the sign of the error are what was observed for this distribution and these bucket boundaries. A different shape or different boundaries can flip the sign. Do not extract a rule like histogram interpolation always reads high from this single run. If you want to know the direction of your error, run the same check on your own data with your own boundaries.

For the same reason, nothing here asserts how any particular monitoring product estimates quantiles from histograms. Interpolation methods and bucket definitions can differ between implementations. Check the documentation of the tool you use. This section demonstrates a property of the method, not what any product does.

There is, however, a bound with a clear shape that the run does establish. The estimate stays inside the bucket that contains the true rank, and the true value is in that same bucket. So the absolute error cannot exceed that bucket's width. This held for all four layouts: 53.1056 ms of error against a 400 ms bucket in the coarse layout, and 0.1410 ms against a 10 ms bucket in the finest.

  • The way to reduce the error is finer buckets in the range you care about. That is more reliable and more predictable than changing the interpolation method
  • Which range needs to be fine must be decided in advance. Here the p99 lived in the 400 ms region, so that region mattered. Design bucket boundaries to cover the expected latency range. Changing boundaries afterwards makes historical comparison impossible
  • If values land in the final overflow bucket, no percentile can be computed at that point. Set the upper bound generously - above your timeout value is the safe choice
  • Histogram storage cost is bucket count times series count. Accuracy trades directly against cost. The storage cost of logs and traces themselves is covered in the ClickHouse observability piece
  • Beyond histograms there are several families of sketch structures for approximating quantiles, with different shapes of error guarantee. The one thing they share matters most: each has an operation for merging parts, and a single percentile number has none

11. A user meets a screen, not a request

Even a correctly computed request p99 leaves one layer to go. Users do not send one request. One screen fires many. Leave this layer out and you cannot explain why complaints stayed flat after the p99 improved.

If a screen fires k requests and each has a 1 percent chance of landing above the request-level p99, the chance the screen contains at least one is 1 minus 0.99 to the k. That is 9.5618 percent at k=10, 18.2093 percent at k=20 and 63.3968 percent at k=100. It passes a coin flip at k=69.

This was also measured rather than assumed. Screens were drawn 40,000 at a time from the request population of case A in section 2, and screen latency was computed two ways: the maximum of the requests (fully parallel) and their sum (fully serial).

  • So improving the request p99 while increasing requests per screen means the two changes cancel and the dashboard shows nothing happening. Meanwhile the user experience can get worse
  • Requests per screen is usually recorded nowhere. It grows quietly through frontend changes and added widgets. A team watching only backend metrics cannot see the change
  • Parallelising turns the sum into a maximum. In the table at k=20 it fell from 2552.58 to 1404.46. That is still more than three times the request p99. Parallelism reduces the problem without removing it
  • Where possible, measure latency per screen or per transaction rather than per request, and treat request percentiles as secondary. Screen-level measurement also has one raw row per screen, which shrinks the section 4 problem as well

12. Diagnosis - deciding whether the number in front of you was merged wrongly

One question remains. How do you know whether the p99 you are looking at was merged wrongly? Dashboards do not show their arithmetic, and you may not be in a position to read the query. There is a test, though. A correctly computed percentile does not change when you change the grouping. A wrongly folded one does.

the mean column moved from 128.39 to 198.82 as the grouping changed, a 1.55 x

difference is the whole diagnosis.

The same 100,000 requests were grouped into 10, 5, 2 and 1 groups, and the mean of the per-group p99 was taken each time. It moved from 128.39 to 159.47 to 190.62 to 198.82. A 1.55x swing. Not one data point changed; only the grouping did.

Sum, count and max, meanwhile, are identical in all four cases - fixed at 2.67, 100000 and 50869.28. That contrast is the entire diagnosis. A number that moves when you change the grouping was folded wrongly; a number that does not was computed properly.

The max column moved too (916.06 to 634.13 to 340.29 to 198.82), but in one direction only, and never below the true value. Whether that was luck got checked.

the max column moved too, but only downward, and it never went below the

Across 400 random fleet configurations, the true p99 exceeded the maximum of the per-group p99 values zero times, and fell below their minimum zero times. A counting argument explains it. Each group holds at most 1 percent of its own requests above its own p99. Therefore at most 1 percent of the pooled requests sit above the largest of those values. So that largest value is an upper bound on the pooled p99, and the smallest is a lower bound by the same reasoning.

Practically, this is the best available when only per-server percentiles survive. Fold with max, not mean. It is not the answer, but it is a bound, and a bound supports the judgement that things are no worse than this. A mean is neither an upper nor a lower bound and supports no judgement at all. The bound also tightens as groups get coarser, which means it is loosest at high cardinality, and dashboards slice most finely exactly then.

14. Checklist and what not to do

Arranged for direct use in collection design and dashboard review.

  • [ ] Confirmed at which stage the dashboard's percentile panel computes the percentile
  • [ ] Changed the grouping and confirmed the number does not move
  • [ ] Widened the time range and confirmed the p99 does not fall
  • [ ] Counted threshold crossings in the log and cross-checked against the p99
  • [ ] Plotted the request count (the denominator) alongside the percentile chart
  • [ ] Low-traffic endpoints use a wider window or a lower quantile
  • [ ] Latency is collected as a histogram, not as percentiles
  • [ ] Histogram buckets cover the expected latency range and above the timeout
  • [ ] Means are stored together with sum and count
  • [ ] Error rates are stored with numerator and denominator separately
  • [ ] Where only per-server percentiles survive, they are folded with max, not mean
  • [ ] Screen and transaction latency is measured separately from request latency
  • [ ] Percentile definitions were aligned before comparing numbers across tools

And the things not to do.

  • Averaging percentiles across servers or windows - this whole article is about that
  • Adding count weighting and declaring it fixed - the sign flipped in section 5
  • Validating metrics only in the healthy state - the error was 0.03 percent in section 1b
  • Storing percentile values as a time series and discarding the raw data or histogram - it is irreversible
  • Using the p99 of a 100-request window as an alert threshold - one request moves it
  • Hunting percentile definitions first when numbers are badly apart - definition differences are around 0.02 percent
  • Generalising the sign of a histogram error from one observation - it depends on the distribution and the boundaries
  • Improving the request p99 without checking requests per screen - they cancel, as in section 11

# Percentile aggregation review - mergeability table and reproduction code

> Source: braindetox.kr - We Averaged the p99 of Ten Servers
> Basis: Windows 10 / Python 3.13.3 standard library, verified with fixed seed 20260730
> Paste this file into an AI tool as is and say: review our dashboard and collection design against these rules.
> All data is synthetic. These are not real service metrics.

## 0. One-line summary

A percentile is not a value but a **property of a distribution**. Properties do not average.
Merging requires **raw data or a mergeable summary (a histogram)**.
**Put the merge before the summary.**

## 1. Mergeability table

| Metric | How the parts fold | Exact? | What you need in order to merge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Count | Sum of counts | Exact | The count |
| Sum | Sum of sums | Exact | The sum |
| Max | Max of maxima | Exact | The maximum |
| Min | Min of minima | Exact | The minimum |
| Mean | Plain mean of means | **Wrong** | - |
| Mean | Means weighted by count | Exact | **Sum and count** |
| Variance, stddev | Recomputed from count, sum, sum of squares | Conditionally exact | All three |
| Median p50 | No fold rule | **Impossible** | Raw data or histogram |
| p90, p95, p99 | No fold rule | **Impossible** | Raw data or histogram |
| Error rate | Averaging ratios | **Wrong** | **Numerator and denominator separately** |

Mergeability is not a property of the metric but **a property of the summary you saved**.

## 2. Aggregations to never do

- Averaging per-server p99 (observed error -33.94 % to -79.75 %)
- Weighting per-server p99 by request count (sign flips: -36.99 % / -10.11 %)
- Averaging 60 one-minute p99 into an hourly p99 (-36.98 %)
- Taking the max of 60 one-minute p99 as an hourly p99 (+570.19 %)
- Averaging ratios (error rate, success rate)
- Averaging partial maxima (-45.36 %)

## 4. Reproduction code (Python 3.13, standard library only)

```python
import math, random

def p99(xs):
    s = sorted(xs)
    return s[math.ceil(0.99 * len(s)) - 1]      # nearest-rank

def nrm(rng, _s=[None]):                        # Box-Muller
    if _s[0] is not None:
        v = _s[0]; _s[0] = None; return v
    u1 = rng.random() or 1e-12
    u2 = rng.random()
    r = math.sqrt(-2.0 * math.log(u1))
    _s[0] = r * math.sin(2 * math.pi * u2)
    return r * math.cos(2 * math.pi * u2)

rng = random.Random(20260730 + 1)               # section 1 seed

def ln(med, sig, n):
    return [math.exp(math.log(med) + sig * nrm(rng)) for _ in range(n)]

groups = [ln(18.0, 0.35, 10000) for _ in range(9)] + [ln(30.0, 1.50, 10000)]
per  = [p99(g) for g in groups]
pool = [v for g in groups for v in g]

print('mean of per-server p99 :', round(sum(per) / len(per), 2))   # 138.51
print('true pooled p99        :', round(p99(pool), 2))             # 209.67
print('max of per-server p99  :', round(max(per), 2))              # 1020.21 (upper bound)
```

If the three numbers come back, the reproduction succeeded.

## 5. Dashboard review procedure

| Check | What you do | The tell that it was merged wrongly |
|---|---|---|
| Change the grouping | Per host → per region → fleet-wide | **The number moves** |
| Change the time range | 1 hour → 6 hours → 1 day | p99 **falls** as you widen |
| Count threshold crossings | Count log requests above the p99 value | **Far more** than 1 % of the total |
| Bound check | Max of per-server p99 versus fleet p99 | Fleet value is **above that maximum** |
| Single-node contrast | Worst node p99 versus fleet p99 | Fleet value is **far lower** |

**The threshold-count check is the strongest.** If the p99 is X, then by definition 1 % of requests exceed X.

## 6. Collection design rules

- Collect latency **as a histogram, not as percentiles**
- Histogram buckets cover **the expected latency range and above the timeout**
- Keep buckets **fine in the range you care about** (near the current p99) — more reliable than changing interpolation
- Store means **together with sum and count**
- Store error rates with **numerator and denominator separately**
- Store standard deviation as **count, sum and sum of squares**
- Do not store percentile values as a time series while discarding raw data or histograms
- Plot **the request count (the denominator)** alongside percentile charts
- Low-traffic endpoints use a wider window or **a lower quantile**
- Measure **screen and transaction latency** separately from request latency
- **Align percentile definitions** before comparing numbers across tools (defaults can differ)

## 7. Second best when you cannot fix it

If only per-server percentiles survive, **fold with max, not mean.**

- Across 400 random fleet configurations, the true p99 exceeded the max of the per-group p99 **zero times**
- It fell below their minimum **zero times** as well
- Why: each group holds at most 1 % of its own requests above its own p99 → at most 1 % of the pooled requests sit above the largest of those values → upper bound
- A bound supports the judgement that things are no worse than this. A mean is neither an upper nor a lower bound
- The bound tightens as groups coarsen. **It is loosest at high cardinality**

## 8. Two problems that remain after computing the percentile correctly

- **Sample starvation**: the p99 of a 100-request window is effectively the second slowest request. Across 2,000 trials the estimate ranged from 53.84 to 300.51 ms (true value 128.61 ms)
- **Screen-level experience**: if a screen fires k requests, the chance of containing at least one slow request is 1 - 0.99^k. That is 18.21 % at k=20 and passes 50 % at k=69. The serial p99 of a 20-request screen was 2552.58 ms (request p99 was 435.58 ms)

## 9. What not to do

- Averaging percentiles
- Adding weights and calling it fixed
- Validating metrics only in the healthy state
- Discarding raw data or histograms and keeping only percentile values
- Using the p99 of a 100-request window as an alert threshold
- Hunting definitions first when facing a large error (definition differences are around 0.02 %)
- Generalising the sign of a histogram error from one observation (it depends on the distribution and the boundaries)
- Improving the request p99 without checking requests per screen
