Is AI Erasing the Middle Class of Software Engineering - Where Routine Implementation Lost Its Price

What shrinks, what grows, the junior pipeline problem, and how to test the claim against hiring data

Aug 16, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: what lost value is a kind of work, not a kind of person

A developer four years in picks up six tickets for the sprint. One screen, two endpoints, a migration script and three small bugs. A while ago that stack filled a week. Now it is done by Thursday afternoon, and what to do with the rest of the week is genuinely unclear. Ask whether this person is worth what they were worth two years ago and the answer does not come out cleanly. The recent essay arguing that AI is removing the middle class of software engineering pokes at exactly this spot.

The conversation slides into one of two ditches almost immediately. On one side is the claim that engineering is finished. On the other is the claim that a tool got added and nothing else changed. Both are wrong. What is actually happening is narrower and more specific. The profession is not shrinking as a whole. The distribution of work inside it is moving, and that movement lands hardest on people who spent their days in one particular band. This piece splits that band out by type of work, sets down what actually got smaller and what actually got bigger, and then asks what evidence would be needed to decide whether the claim is true.

Two things stay out of scope. Whether to use AI tooling at all is a different argument, and no company layoff is attributed here to AI. Layoffs sit at the intersection of interest rates, currency, fiscal calendars and investment cycles, and single cause explanations of them are almost always wrong. The broader jobs discourse is covered in Is Software Dead? Balancing the Developer Job Crisis Narrative in the AI Era, and what happens to accumulated expertise itself is in How AI Erodes Expertise.

1. The middle class is a band of work, not a rung on the ladder

Read the phrase as the middle row of a leveling chart and the argument tangles immediately. What the original essay points at is not a person who is competent but not exceptional. It is a band of work, and the band is specific. What to build is already decided. Which files to touch, and roughly how, is already decided. What remains is turning that into working code, getting the tests green and merging. Call it routine implementation.

Routine implementation used to take up a large share of engineering hours. It also has one defining property: the answer is already contained in the specification. That made it easy to measure, easy to outsource, and easy to convert into headcount. Entire contract software industries are built on top of that property.

Listing what these engineers were actually doing all day makes the shape of the problem visible.

  • Turning written requirements into screens, endpoints and batch jobs
  • Producing one more instance of a pattern that already exists in the codebase (another CRUD set, another cloned admin page)
  • Reading library documentation and wiring things up the prescribed way (payments, notifications, file upload)

By tenure this clusters roughly between two and seven years, but tenure is not the definition. Someone fifteen years in whose day is mostly those three bullets sits in the same place. Someone three years in who pushes back on requirements and follows incidents to the root sits somewhere else. Turning this distinction into a seniority argument is how the conversation degenerates into who is good at their job, and it loses the thing that actually matters, which is what kind of work carries a price.

2. What shrank: the distance from spec to code

The thing that shrank is the distance between a specification and running code. That distance used to be filled entirely with human time: reading docs, hunting for signatures, digging for a similar file, typing, fixing the typo. That stretch got shorter. How much shorter varies enormously by the character of the work.

Ordered by how much practitioners actually feel it:

  • Routine code generation - CRUD derived from a data model, DTO mapping, form validation. Work where the pattern already exists in the repository and this is one more copy of it
  • Mechanical transformation - language and framework version bumps, syntax migrations, style unification. Work with explicit rules where the compiler checks the result
  • Boilerplate tests - unit tests for functions whose behavior is already fixed
  • First draft docs and comments - putting into prose what can be read off the code
  • The front half of wiring up an unfamiliar API - search and first successful call. Error handling and retry design are explicitly not in this bucket

The common thread is visible. The answer is already documented somewhere, and a machine tells you immediately whether the result is right. Wherever the compiler, the type checker or the test runner acts as judge, the human share fell hard. Wherever the only available judge is a person, almost nothing fell. That contrast is the spine of this entire argument.

One correction worth making. What shrank is time, not the work itself. The code still has to exist and someone still has to decide whether it is right. The way the cost of writing code and the cost of understanding code moved in opposite directions is developed further in The Shifting Economics of Code.

3. What grew: review, integration, incident response, requirements

Speed up generation and the next station backs up. Double the throughput of one line in a factory and inventory piles in front of the next one, and the same thing happens in an engineering organization. The core of the original argument sits here too: it is now possible to produce enormous volumes of code in a day, but the rate at which a person can read and judge it did not change.

So the work that actually grew looks like this.

  • Change review - there is more diff to read, and code that looks plausible but rests on a wrong premise takes longer to review than code that is obviously broken
  • Managing integration points - when each piece arrives faster, contracts between pieces break more often. Who owns interfaces, schemas and event formats becomes the constraint
  • Incident response - nobody carries a mental map of code they did not write, and finding a root cause without that map takes longer
  • Requirements work - as implementation gets cheap the bottleneck moves upstream, and starting while the goal is still vague costs more than it used to
  • Keeping tests trustworthy - when tests are generated alongside the code, someone must confirm the test checks the specification rather than the implementation. A test that mirrors the implementation always passes
  • Preserving the reasons behind changes - if the rationale exists only in a chat transcript, in six months the organization does not have it

3.1 Direction of travel by type of work

Fold the two previous sections into one table and the phrase middle class erosion becomes concrete. Nobody is being deleted. Time is migrating from the left column to the right one.

One caution about reading that table. Work growing in the right column does not automatically create seats to do it. For growing work to become hiring, an organization has to recognize it as distinct work and attach budget to it. Review, integration and documentation are exactly the activities that do not fit neatly into tickets and do not show up cleanly in performance metrics. In practice they get absorbed by the people already there, and that absorption is precisely what people mean when they say the work went up but the headcount did not.

4. Two occupations, two directions, one statistical agency

Impressions do not settle arguments. There is, however, one piece of official statistics that runs in the same direction as this claim. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics does not treat software work as a single occupation in its Occupational Outlook Handbook. It splits it, and the two halves point opposite ways.

MeasureSoftware Developers, QA Analysts, and TestersComputer Programmers
Projected employment change, 2024 to 2034+15% (much faster than average)-6% (decline)
Employment in 2024About 1.7 millionNot confirmed for this article
Projected annual openingsAbout 129,200About 5,500
Reasons BLS givesExpanding software development for AI, IoT, robotics and automation, plus rising security investmentContinued automation of programming work, and higher skilled tasks shifting to software developers

What makes this table interesting is that the U.S. government's own occupational taxonomy draws the same line this article draws. In the BLS scheme a computer programmer is broadly someone who writes code from designs made by others, while a software developer decides what to build and owns whether it works. The declining side is the former. The reasons BLS itself prints are the continued automation of programming tasks, including with AI, and the migration of the harder work into the developer occupation.

Three qualifications have to travel with these numbers.

  • They are projections, not measurements. This is a model looking a decade out from a 2024 base, not a report on what happened by 2026
  • Occupational classification depends on self reporting. The same work is coded as developer at one employer and programmer at another, so part of any decline is reclassification rather than job loss
  • This is U.S. data. Job composition, contract structures and procurement practices differ enough elsewhere that it does not transfer directly

Even so this is the single most useful page in the debate, because it shows how the statements engineering is shrinking and engineering is growing can both be true at once, inside one agency using one methodology.

5. Close the entry path and the seniors stop arriving

Routine implementation was never only cheap labor. It was the on ramp. A new hire took small tickets, learned the terrain of the codebase, got corrected in review until the team's standards sank in, and acquired caution the honest way by shipping something that broke. Narrow that ramp and people grow more slowly.

The trouble is that the loss is invisible when it happens. Skip a year of graduate hiring and this year's numbers improve, because cost falls and review load falls with it. The bill arrives in four to six years. The mid tier is hollow, seniors are retiring or leaving, and buying replacements does not work because every other company is short of the same people for the same reason. IEEE Spectrum's reporting on how expectations for entry level roles are themselves shifting describes the front edge of the same process.

The collapse usually runs in this order.

  • Stage one - graduate hiring is deferred, just for this year. The team feels faster right away
  • Stage two - the remaining people absorb review and integration. Slack for teaching is the first thing to disappear
  • Stage three - with nobody free to teach, new hires do not develop. The phrase juniors these days do not grow starts circulating
  • Stage four - the mid level gap becomes visible. At this point hiring cannot close it. Seniors are not sitting in inventory

The organizational defense is simpler than it sounds. Stop giving juniors fully specified tickets. Hand over work that is only half defined and make them finish the definition, make them explain why that design, put them on other people's changes in review, and have them write the incident retrospective themselves. That argument is developed separately in We Didn't Hire You to Finish Tasks, so only the conclusion is repeated here. Growing people by volume of code is over. Growing them by exposure to judgment always worked.

6. How to test this claim against hiring metrics

The middle class is disappearing can be converted into a testable claim, but only after deciding what to count. Figures of the form engineering postings fell by some percentage neither confirm nor refute it, because posting volume responds far more strongly to the business cycle than to anything technological.

If the claim is true, the signal is not in the totals. It is in the composition.

MetricWhat it should look like if the claim holdsCompeting explanationWhat you need to read it
Minimum experience in new postingsA falling share of roles open to candidates with no minimumIn any downturn, entry roles are cut first regardless of technologyThree or more years collected on one consistent basis
Level mix inside an organizationA thinning middle with thicker endsA reorg or a leveling change alone produces the same shapeYear over year internal data for the same organization
Wording of job requirementsFewer implementation technologies listed, more design, verification and domainIt may just be recruiting copy fashionComparison against the same company's older postings for the same role
Tenure and attritionShorter cycles or higher attrition specifically in the middle bandCompensation competition and remote policy may dominateHR data with separated reasons for leaving
Output per headOutput rising while headcount is flat or fallingMeasuring output as lines of code makes this meaninglessAn output definition at the feature or release level

Try to actually populate that table and you hit a wall fast, and the wall is itself a central fact of this debate. Most of the assertions in circulation are undecidable from public data, because the data that would decide them sits inside individual HR systems. That is why the public conversation runs on personal observation and anecdote, and why opposite conclusions coexist about the same phenomenon.

The recurring traps when reading these numbers:

  • A posting is not a hire. Evergreen reqs, duplicate listings and closed unfilled roles are all mixed into the same count
  • Titles do not mean the same thing across companies. The same senior label is five years in one place and twelve in another
  • Separate the technology factor from the capital factor. The interest rate regime alone moves engineering hiring substantially
  • Watch for survivorship bias. Collect only the accounts of people still there and the paths of everyone who left vanish from the sample

7. How the price gets set: one documented case and the US market

Almost nowhere publishes what software labor is worth. One country does, which makes it unusually useful evidence. Under Korea's Software Promotion Act, Article 46, Clause 4, a labor cost standard for software engineers must be maintained, and the Korea Software Industry Association publishes an annual wage survey against it. Public procurement and contract development price their work off those figures.

7.1 What the published numbers say

The figures applied in 2026 were published on 19 December 2025, based on the 2025 software engineer wage survey (approved statistic no. 375001). The individual amounts matter less than the gaps between jobs.

The survey follows the ITSQF framework across seventeen job families, and the figures include base pay, allowances, bonuses, severance provisions and employer contributions. The average rose 4.7 percent year over year. One thing is worth pulling out. This structure was not created by AI. Planning and audit have been priced above implementation for a long time. That is exactly what makes it useful evidence. Institutions have long attached a higher price to deciding and judging than to implementing. What AI did was not invent a new order but steepen the slope of the existing one.

Caveats belong with it. These are survey results rather than a rate card for any individual, and the values move with job definitions and respondent sample. Still, the fact that the publication is organized by job rather than by grade matters. The design says a price attaches to the work performed, not to a person's rank.

7.2 What the same pressure looks like in the US market

The US has no published price list, so the same information is encoded in leveling frameworks and requisition structure instead. Compensation bands attach to levels, levels attach to scope, and scope is described almost entirely in the vocabulary of judgment: ambiguity handled, blast radius owned, number of teams affected. Read that way, the American system was already saying the same thing the Korean table says out loud. The band that pays is the band that carries consequences.

Two practical readings follow for anyone working in that market. The first is that a years of experience gate on a posting is a proxy the employer uses when it cannot describe the judgment it wants, so a portfolio of judgment work often beats the stated minimum. The second is that when a requisition is written as headcount against a delivery plan, productivity gains flow to the employer as expanded scope rather than to the engineer as free time. Check which column of the plan you are counted in. If you are counted by deliverables, faster tooling returns to you as more scope. If you are counted by ownership of an outcome, it returns as leverage. Moving between those columns does not always require changing companies.

8. What actually cannot be replaced

The advice usually offered here is to develop creativity or to work on soft skills. Neither is actionable. A more precise statement is available. Value attaches to the seat that carries consequences, and consequences still land on people. A model does not pay for a bad decision.

Four capabilities fall out of that principle, and each one comes with a reason it survives.

  • Problem definition - deciding what to build. The answer is not in the codebase but distributed across users, the organization and the market, so no amount of reading code produces it
  • Judgment - deciding whether what came out is correct. When the specification is incomplete, context determines what correct means, and a passing test is only part of the verdict
  • Boundary design - drawing contracts between systems and between teams. Locally correct decisions can be globally wrong here, which requires a field of view you cannot obtain inside a single file
  • Root cause tracing - following a failure all the way down. The path from symptom to cause is different every time and narrows only by reading logs, metrics, deployment history and the organization's past decisions together
  • Domain knowledge - the raw material that makes the other four possible. Settlement rules, reimbursement codes, logistics edge cases: undocumented knowledge that lives only inside the organization and grounds every judgment above

One property runs through the whole list. None of it is work where the right answer is knowable in advance. Work with a fixed answer becomes a target for automation, and work that requires setting the answer remains. And because faster generation makes the judgment step the bottleneck, its price rises rather than falls. The idea that architectural instinct comes from carrying responsibility rather than from coursework is developed in How to Learn Software Architecture in 2026.

9. What one person can do this quarter

Career advice tends to be either too large or too vague. Only things that can actually move inside three months are listed here. One at a time, in order, is enough.

  • Track where your hours go for two weeks. Five buckets: routine implementation, review, integration, incidents, requirements. The percentage sitting in the left column of the table in section three is your current position
  • Volunteer as a reviewer. Review is a bottleneck, so there is usually a shortage, and judging other people's code is hard to train any other way
  • Insert yourself one step before the ticket exists. Getting into the meeting where requirements are still being settled changes your position even if you only ask one question. What happens in this case is the cheapest possible entry sentence
  • Own one incident end to end. The learning curve on root cause work is steep, and one properly executed trace builds standing inside the team faster than it builds a resume line
  • Write one domain document. Pick a rule your team knows that exists nowhere in writing. Your position in the organization and your own judgment improve at the same time
  • Build a personal checking procedure for AI output. Fix the list of things you always verify. Made into a procedure rather than a skill, it survives busy weeks

Two moves are not recommended. Refusing the tooling to turn the clock back does not protect your position, because the repricing of routine implementation already happened in the market regardless of what you personally use. And learning one more framework is not the answer either. Adding implementation technologies is movement inside the column whose price fell.

10. What could not be confirmed

The range of defensible statements here is narrower than the volume of discussion suggests. The following were not written as numbers because the evidence was not available.

  • Employment time series for engineers by level or tenure. No public source separating the mid tier share over time was located. National labor statistics generally aggregate at the occupational level and do not count mid level developers separately
  • Change in the share of entry level postings. Private job board aggregates get quoted, but their sample and method are not published, so they were not used as a primary source
  • Causation between AI adoption and specific company layoffs. No public filing or official record attributing a reduction specifically to AI was found, which is why no company is used as an example here
  • Whether the BLS projections are tracking. The figures cited are a ten year projection from a 2024 base and were not compared against 2026 actuals. The 2024 employment level for computer programmers was also not confirmed and is left blank in the table above

That this list is not short is itself a finding. This debate is currently running on observation rather than data. Whichever side someone argues, confidence should not exceed the strength of the evidence behind it.

Conclusion: what disappears is a path, not a class

To summarize. There is no evidence that software employment as a whole is contracting. In the BLS projections the developer occupation grows quickly, and the list of work that expanded is not short. At the same time the price attached to one specific kind of work, routine implementation, has clearly fallen. Inside one agency using one methodology, only the programmer occupation is projected to decline, and the stated reason is the automation of repetitive tasks.

So the real problem is not how many jobs vanish. It is that the path people used to walk through that work on their way to the next stage is narrowing. Routine implementation was cheap labor and training simultaneously, and no replacement for the training half has established itself. That gap cannot be closed by individuals alone, and unless organizations deliberately distribute opportunities for judgment, it returns a few years later as a hollow middle.

For an individual it compresses to one rule. Look at how much of your time goes into work whose answer is already fixed, and reduce that share a little every quarter. The place to move is toward deciding, judging and owning outcomes. That place is not new. It always paid more, and now that the bottleneck has moved there it pays more still. Neither engineering is finished nor nothing has changed. The point where value attaches has moved, and the move is still in progress.
