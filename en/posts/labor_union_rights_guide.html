A Complete Guide to Joining a Union and Responding to Unfair Labor Practices

From the right to organize and collective bargaining to unfair labor practices and how to file a complaint - the general principles every worker should know

2026-07-10 Law & Legal

Introduction: Why Union Rights Matter

A single worker negotiating pay and working conditions with an employer is rarely an equal match. To offset that imbalance, many countries guarantee workers' collective rights - often summarized as the right to organize, bargain, and act collectively - through their constitutions or labor statutes. Even so, unfair labor practices that penalize workers for joining a union or engaging in union activity still occur around the world.

This guide, current as of July 2026, walks through what a labor union is, the general principle behind the three core collective labor rights, typical steps to join or form a union, what collective bargaining and collective agreements mean, the concept and common patterns of unfair labor practices, how to respond if you believe you have experienced one, the general legality requirements for strikes and other industrial action, considerations for non-regular and small-workplace workers, and frequently asked questions. One thing needs to be said upfront: labor law varies enormously between countries and changes over time even within the same country. This article does not assert any specific statute, court ruling, or filing deadline as universal. It focuses on general principles that are widely recognized internationally, while flagging where national systems diverge.

1. What Is a Labor Union and What Are the Core Collective Labor Rights?

1.1 What a Labor Union Is

A labor union is an organization that workers form on their own initiative to maintain and improve working conditions and to advance their economic and social standing. Some unions are organized within a single company (enterprise unions), while others span an entire industry or occupation, often through branches or chapters of a sector-wide union. The International Labour Organization (ILO) treats freedom of association as a fundamental principle, embodied in its core Conventions No. 87 (Freedom of Association and Protection of the Right to Organise) and No. 98 (Right to Organise and Collective Bargaining), which affirm that both workers and employers have the right to form and run their own organizations free from outside interference.

1.2 The Core Collective Labor Rights - General Principle, Different by Country

Many countries describe workers' collective rights in roughly three parts: the right to organize (forming or joining a union), the right to bargain collectively (negotiating working conditions with the employer as a group), and the right to take collective action (such as a strike, under certain conditions when bargaining breaks down). These three are interconnected - without the ability to organize, workers have little bargaining power, and without a real means of pressure when bargaining stalls, bargaining itself risks becoming a formality.

Exactly who is covered and to what extent, however, varies by country. Treatment of civil servants, workers in special employment arrangements, gig or platform workers, and employees of small workplaces differs considerably across systems. Some countries recognize freedom of association broadly, while others impose sector- or occupation-specific limits.

Key takeaway: the core collective labor rights are a widely shared international principle, not an identical rulebook applied the same way everywhere. Always check the specific scope that applies in your country and industry.

2. Joining or Forming a Union - The General Process

2.1 Joining an Existing Union

In most cases, a worker becomes a union member by submitting a membership application to an already established union and paying dues. You might join a union that already exists at your workplace, or a local branch of a sector-wide union. Eligibility, dues, and withdrawal procedures vary by each union's bylaws, so it is worth reviewing them before joining.

2.2 Forming a New Union

If no union exists at your workplace and you want to start one, many systems follow a broadly similar sequence: (1) gathering founding members and drafting bylaws → (2) holding a founding convention to elect officers and finalize the bylaws → (3) filing a notice or registration with the relevant government authority. The requirements and legal effect of registration differ by country - some allow activity based on freedom of association alone without formal registration, while others require additional steps to gain legal protection or recognition as a bargaining representative.

3. What Collective Bargaining and Collective Agreements Mean

Collective bargaining is the process in which a union, representing workers, negotiates with an employer (or an employer association) over wages, working hours, health and safety, benefits, and other conditions of employment as a group. Many legal systems impose a duty to bargain in good faith on employers - not a duty to reach agreement, but a duty not to refuse bargaining outright or show up only for the appearance of it, without a legitimate reason. When bargaining succeeds, the result is documented as a collective agreement, which in many systems takes priority over individual employment contract terms that are less favorable to the worker.

4. What Is an Unfair Labor Practice? Concept and Common Patterns

When an employer interferes with a worker's exercise of collective labor rights, or penalizes a worker because of it, this is commonly called an unfair labor practice (the term used, for example, in the United States, alongside similar concepts elsewhere such as Korea's "budang nodong haengwi"). The specific legal terminology and lists of prohibited acts differ by country, but the patterns observed internationally tend to fall into a few broad categories.

4.1 Adverse Treatment

Disadvantaging a worker - through dismissal, transfer, denial of promotion, or unequal pay - because of union membership, union activity, legitimate bargaining demands, or participation in industrial action. Commonly cited examples include a worker's performance rating dropping sharply right after joining a union, or strike participants being singled out for transfer to another department.

4.2 Domination or Interference

An employer interfering with a union's organization or operations, or propping up a so-called "company union" (a nominal organization convenient for the employer) to undermine a genuine union. Commonly cited examples include improperly blocking access to facilities a union needs for its activities, or an employer funding and effectively controlling a union's operations.

4.3 Refusal or Avoidance of Bargaining

Refusing to bargain without a legitimate reason, or going through the motions of bargaining while stalling without any real progress. Typical examples include repeatedly ignoring requests to bargain, or sending only representatives with no decision-making authority to repeat purely formal meetings.

4.4 Other Commonly Cited Patterns

Abusing damages claims or criminal complaints against strike participants, coercing workers to withdraw from a union, and conditioning hiring on non-membership in a union are also treated as unfair labor practices or similar concepts in many countries. Exactly which acts qualify legally as an unfair labor practice varies by country and by case, so the examples above should be read as general illustrations only.

Caution: the reason an employer states (poor performance, attendance issues, etc.) may differ from the actual motive (retaliation for union activity). Timing, and whether similar cases were treated consistently, are both relevant, but the final determination belongs to the competent authority.

5. What to Do If You Believe You Experienced an Unfair Labor Practice

5.1 Gather Evidence First

Organize text messages, emails, and chat logs that show dates and context, along with personnel notices, colleagues' statements, and meeting minutes. Material that shows the timing relationship between your union membership or activity and the adverse action is especially important.

5.2 File With a Labor Board or Similar Authority

Many countries maintain an administrative body that hears unfair labor practice disputes - for example, the Labor Relations Commission in Korea or the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) in the United States. Filing with such a body is generally only effective if done within a set period, and the specific timeframe and procedure differ significantly by country and system. It is worth confirming the relevant authority and process in your own country in advance.

5.3 Get Help From Counseling Services and Professionals

Certified labor consultants, attorneys, your union's federation, or government labor hotlines can provide initial guidance. Korea, for example, operates a public counseling channel through its Ministry of Employment and Labor, and many other countries maintain similar public or private counseling services.

6. General Legality Requirements for Strikes and Other Industrial Action

A strike falls under the right to collective action, but it is not permitted without limit at any time. The requirements commonly shared across many legal systems are roughly as follows.

  • Legitimate purpose: the action generally needs to relate to workers' economic and social standing, such as wages or working conditions (purely political strikes are restricted in some systems).
  • Proper procedure: prerequisites such as a breakdown in bargaining and a member vote are often required before taking action.
  • Peaceful means: the action generally needs to avoid violence or damage to property.

Industrial action that fails to meet these requirements can be judged unlawful, putting participants at risk of civil or criminal liability. The specific standards and procedures, however, differ by country, so anyone planning to take part in a strike should confirm the requirements with their union or a professional beforehand.

7. Considerations for Non-Regular Workers and Small Workplaces

Fixed-term and dispatched workers, workers in special or dependent contractor arrangements, and platform workers are sometimes treated differently from regular employees when it comes to the scope of collective labor rights, eligibility to join a union, and standing to file an unfair labor practice complaint. Some countries have moved to recognize broader organizing rights even for workers in special employment arrangements, but protection still frequently varies with employment type or workplace size. Employees of small workplaces may also find that some protections apply only above a minimum headcount. It is safest to confirm individually, with a professional or the relevant authority, which rules apply to your specific employment type and workplace size.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Q. If my employer disadvantages me for joining a union, is it automatically an unfair labor practice?

Generally, a causal link must be shown between the adverse action and your union membership or activity. If the employer cites another reason, whether it is genuine or a pretext becomes the key issue, and case-specific judgment should be confirmed with the relevant authority or a professional.

Q. Do I have to tell my employer that I joined a union?

In many jurisdictions there is no general legal duty to disclose union membership. That said, some disclosure may be practically necessary for the union to act as a bargaining representative. The specifics depend on your union's bylaws and your country's rules.

Q. Can workers raise demands together without a formal union?

Workers acting together to raise concerns is protected to some degree in many countries even without a formal union, though being recognized as a party to collective bargaining often requires forming and registering one.

Q. Can I be fired for taking part in a lawful strike?

Dismissal for taking part in a strike that meets the legality requirements can be treated as an unfair labor practice or wrongful dismissal in many jurisdictions. A strike that fails those requirements changes the liability picture for participants, so check the requirements before joining one.

Q. I am worried my employer will retaliate if I file a complaint.

Many systems separately prohibit retaliation for filing a complaint. How effectively this protection works varies by country and enforcement, so gather evidence and consult a professional before filing.

Conclusion - Rights Work When You Know Them and Document Them

The core collective labor rights are a principle shared across many countries so that individual workers can stand on more equal footing with their employer. But that principle does not enforce itself. Understanding what a union is and why collective rights exist, knowing the common patterns of unfair labor practices, and calmly gathering evidence before seeking help from the relevant authority and a professional - this is the surest way to make these rights actually work.
