Microsoft Comic Chat Goes Open Source - The 1996 Cartoon-Panel IRC Client Returns

The chat program that turned your conversations into comic strips is back under the MIT license - what it was and why it's worth caring about now

Jul 18, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: When the Chat Window Was a Comic Strip

On July 16, 2026, Microsoft dropped a surprising bit of news on its Open Source Blog: Comic Chat, the cartoon-panel IRC client that shipped alongside Internet Explorer 3.0 back in 1996, is now on GitHub under the MIT License. This was the program that turned raw IRC text into full comic-strip panels in real time, and, perhaps more famously, the program that gave the world its first real look at that infamous typeface, Comic Sans.

As of July 2026, this story has been quietly but genuinely spreading through both developer communities and retro-computing fandoms. In a news cycle usually dominated by the latest AI model or cloud infrastructure release, it's worth asking why the source-code release of a chat program that was discontinued three decades ago is generating this much conversation. Rather than repeating Microsoft's press release, this piece works through what Comic Chat actually was, why this particular release matters, and what's genuinely different between 1990s chat culture and today's Discord and Slack.

1. What Comic Chat Actually Was

Comic Chat was conceived in 1995 by David "DJ" Kurlander of Microsoft Research's Virtual Worlds Group, developed together with Tim Skelly and David Salesin, and shipped on August 13, 1996. Notably, the team also published their work as a research paper at SIGGRAPH '96 that same year - this wasn't just a novelty toy, it was the product of genuine computer graphics research.

1.1 An Engine That Auto-Translated Text Into Comics

The core idea was to analyze the plain text flowing through an IRC channel in real time and automatically pick appropriate facial expressions, poses, and gestures for each participant's cartoon avatar, then assemble the result on the fly into comic panels complete with speech bubbles. Users just typed as normal; the software read the tone of a sentence and drew the character looking amused, shocked, or angry accordingly. For manual control, a circular widget beneath the self-view avatar - the "emotion wheel" - let users directly dial in one of eight core emotions (coy, happy, laughing, shouting, angry, sad, scared, bored) and its intensity.

1.2 Rendering and Art

The original characters and backgrounds were drawn by independent comic artist Jim Woodring. Later tools let users create their own characters and backgrounds, effectively turning Comic Chat into an amateur comic-production platform in its own right. One telling example: the long-running underground webcomic Jerkcity, a genuine artifact of that era's internet subculture, was originally created using Comic Chat - a sign that people treated this software as an expressive medium, not just a chat client.

1.3 The Seed of Comic Sans

Here's a connection worth dwelling on. Comic Sans was designed by typographer Vincent Connare in 1994, and Comic Chat's adoption of it for speech-bubble text gave the font its first real public stage - its informal, hand-lettered look was a natural fit for comic dialogue. It spread further through Windows helper-character dialogs, and eventually settled into the paradoxical status of design's most mocked and most recognizable typeface. That this whole saga traces back to a chat program is a detail that's easy to miss.

From version 2.0 onward the product was renamed Microsoft Chat, and it kept shipping bundled with Internet Explorer 4 and 5 and Windows 98 and 2000, localized into 24 languages, at one point even serving as MSN's official chat client. Version 2.5, released in March 1999 alongside Internet Explorer 5, was the final release, and the program quietly disappeared from history once Internet Explorer 6 dropped it entirely.

2. Why This Open-Source Release Matters Right Now

A chat program discontinued 30 years ago becoming news isn't fully explained by nostalgia alone. A few distinct layers are stacked here.

2.1 The Real Value of Digital Preservation

Unlike physical artifacts, software is notoriously hard to preserve. Even if a single executable survives, once the operating system and hardware needed to run it vanish, it becomes practically inaccessible. Microsoft's repository description explicitly frames this release as a "historical artifact," published for research, study, and preservation. The key detail is that Microsoft released the actual source code, not just a binary. A compiled binary only shows you what the program did; the source shows you how it was built to do it. The algorithms for inferring emotion from text, the logic for automatically laying out comic panels - genuine 1990s computer graphics research, implemented in real code - is now fully open to researchers and developers who come after.

2.2 Retro Sentiment and Code Archaeology

At the same time, part of why this spread is simply emotional. For anyone who experienced the late-90s internet, Comic Chat is etched in memory as one of the ways you talked to a stranger you'd just met online. The repository ships with the original 1.0 source from 1996, the 2.5 beta from 1998, an SDK, the Java client JChat, sample bots, and internal design documents - meaning any curious developer can open up 30-year-old code and even compile a modernized build to run it. Excavating and restoring old code - "code archaeology," if you like - is more than nostalgia; it's a genuine chance to see how programmers of that era solved real-time rendering under constraints of memory and CPU power that would seem absurdly tight today.

3. 1990s Chat Culture Versus Today's Discord and Slack

Setting Comic Chat's era side by side with today's tools reveals an interesting contrast. In the IRC days, chat defaulted to open channels full of anonymous strangers, and the means of expressing yourself beyond raw text were extremely limited. Comic Chat filled that gap with a fairly bold move: automatic cartoon avatars and auto-generated expressions. The expression detection wasn't perfect and occasionally produced a mismatched pose, but even that awkwardness became part of the charm for users at the time.

Today's Discord and Slack solved the same underlying problem from the opposite direction. Instead of a cartoon character automatically drawing your emotion for you, they offer a rich toolkit of short, explicit expressions the user actively picks - emoji, stickers, GIFs, custom reactions. Server and channel structures let communities segment finely, and voice, screen sharing, and threads diversify the shape of a conversation itself. What's interesting is that the direction is opposite but the goal is identical: both are attempts to visually compensate for the nuance and emotion that plain text alone can't carry. Comic Chat and today's emoji reactions are, thirty years apart, solving the same problem - Comic Chat through automation, today's platforms through offering a rich menu of choices.

4. The Broader Trend of Open-Sourcing Old Commercial Software

Taken in isolation, releasing Comic Chat's source might look like a one-off event. But Microsoft has been steadily releasing source code for its old commercial software over the past decade or so, and at an increasing pace. Seen against that backdrop, this release is a pattern, not an anomaly.

4.1 MS-DOS and GW-BASIC as Precedents

In 2018, Microsoft re-published the source code for MS-DOS 1.25 and 2.0 on GitHub (it had first been released back in 2014 through the Computer History Museum, but in a much less accessible archive format). The stated reason for the GitHub re-release was straightforward: a searchable repository is far easier to find, read, and reference than a compressed archive file buried in a museum download page. In May 2020, Microsoft released the 8088 assembly source for GW-BASIC 1.0, written in 1983, under the MIT License. Both repositories are explicitly run as "read-only archives," with a stated policy of not accepting pull requests that modify the source. This Comic Chat release follows the exact same grammar: MIT License, historical reference archive, original code left untouched while a separate modernized build is offered alongside it.

4.2 Why Now, and Why This Kind of Software

Three things run through this pattern. First, these are pieces of software whose commercial value has fully evaporated, so releasing them costs Microsoft essentially nothing. Second, their historical and educational value only grows with time - early PC operating systems, interpreted languages, and experimental early-web UX software are all genuine milestones in computing history. Third, the act of releasing itself benefits Microsoft's public image, signaling a company that's friendly to the open-source ecosystem and developer communities rather than the closed, litigious Microsoft of an earlier era. Building goodwill and trust from assets with near-zero commercial risk strikes me as a genuinely good trade.

5. The Value of Code Archaeology and Restoration

For a release like this to actually be useful, it needs to be more than just files dumped on GitHub. What makes the Comic Chat repository interesting is that it preserves the original 1996 Visual C++ 4.x-based source untouched while separately providing a version modernized to build under current Visual Studio. In other words, it offers both a "preserved specimen" and a "restoration you can actually handle."

This approach satisfies two goals at once. The original source stands as an undistorted historical record of that era's programming practices, constraints, and design decisions, while the modernized build lets a developer today actually run the thing and feel how a 30-year-old algorithm behaves. The logic for inferring emotion from text and automatically composing panel layouts looks modest by today's standards, but the fact that it ran in real time in an era with no cloud and no GPU acceleration was itself an achievement. Code archaeology is the work of rediscovering that achievement, and of asking today's developers the same question all over again: how do you solve a problem under real constraints?

6. Why It's Fun From a Developer and Cultural Perspective

Strip away all the practical value and this story is still just genuinely enjoyable. Opening 30-year-old code and looking at function names and comment style, guessing how the team implemented their emotion-inference algorithm, rediscovering that a subculture artifact like Jerkcity came out of this very program - all of that is pure fun. Most striking is the realization that the seeds of ideas behind today's chatbots, emoji reactions, and even AI avatar tech were already being tried, seriously enough to warrant an academic paper, back in 1996. We often treat certain tech trends as brand new, but dig a little and you frequently find the same underlying instinct already alive decades earlier. Comic Chat is a particularly good example of that.

Conclusion: On the Things That Didn't Disappear

Microsoft's open-sourcing of Comic Chat carries more symbolic weight than practical impact. As of July 2026, almost nobody is going to actually deploy this repository in production, but that doesn't diminish the value of the release. Following MS-DOS and GW-BASIC, Microsoft is steadily converting assets whose commercial value has expired into academic and cultural assets instead. A small program that once turned your chat window into a comic strip is now, three decades later, a primary source documenting what the internet and programming looked like back then. Rather than treating old software as pure nostalgia bait, it's worth actually opening the repository and reading the code. There's more to learn there than you'd expect.
