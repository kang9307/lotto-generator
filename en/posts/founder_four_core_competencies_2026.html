Four Core Competencies Every Founder Should Focus On

How to measure and train product judgment, persuasion, distribution design and capital

Jul 27, 2026 Finance
⚠️ Investment notice — This article is for general information only and is not a recommendation to buy or sell any product. Loss of principal is possible; investment decisions and their outcomes are your own responsibility.

Introduction: Look at the Odds Before Talking About Skill

Lists of qualities a founder supposedly needs are everywhere. Passion, execution, grit, leadership - the same words repeat, and after reading them nothing changes about what you do tomorrow. Abstract virtue lists are dangerous for a specific reason: they cannot be falsified. If you fail, the explanation is that passion was lacking; if you succeed, the explanation is that passion was there. That is retroactive storytelling, not a tool for making decisions in advance.

This article takes a different approach. It reorganizes founder skill into four axes - product judgment, persuasion, distribution design and capital - and instead of arguing at length about why each matters, it spends the space on how to measure and train each one. A competency you cannot measure is one you cannot train, and advice you cannot train on is not advice, just encouragement.

Before that, the odds deserve an honest statement. According to Korea's national statistics office, in the 2024 business demography statistics released on October 23, 2025, only 36.4 percent of companies founded in 2018 were still alive five years later. That is the highest figure on record and, at the same time, it means more than six out of ten did not make it past five years. Any conversation about competence only makes sense on top of that number.

This article is general information about entrepreneurship. It does not recommend any specific business, investment or financial product, and it does not provide advice for individual circumstances. Statistics come from the publishing organizations cited, and their survey periods and populations differ from one another. Actual decisions should rest on your own situation and professional consultation.

1. The Anatomy of Failure - What the Data Says

To define competence you first have to see what breaks. The analysis CB Insights published on March 5, 2026 covered 431 venture-backed startups that shut down from 2023 onward, and classified the 385 of them with identifiable reasons. Together those companies had raised 17.5 billion dollars, with a median of 11 million dollars raised before shutting down.

1.1 Running Out of Capital Is the Ending, Not the Cause

Failure factorShareInterpretation
Ran out of capital70%An outcome metric. The real question is why nothing moved before then
Poor product-market fit43%Built the wrong thing, or held on to it far too long
Bad timing or macro conditions29%Outside the founder's control
Unsustainable unit economics19%Mistook a loss-per-sale structure for growth

Because multiple reasons were allowed, the total exceeds 100 percent. The important point is that the 70 percent figure should not be read as a cause. It is the equivalent of cardiac arrest on a death certificate: everyone dies when the heart stops, and nobody calls that the reason. The same study found a median of 22 months between the final funding round and shutdown, which supports the interpretation. Most of these companies did not die because money suddenly vanished; they died because for roughly two years they failed to produce metrics that would justify a next round.

1.2 Korean Data Tells a Different Story

Domestic figures come from a completely different population. The headline numbers from the 2024 business demography statistics are as follows.

  • Active businesses: 7.642 million
  • New businesses: 922,000 (down 33,000 year on year)
  • Closures: 791,000 as of 2023 (up 40,000 year on year)
  • One-year survival rate: 64.4 percent for the 2022 cohort (down 0.5 percentage points)
  • Five-year survival rate: 36.4 percent for the 2018 cohort (up 1.6 percentage points)

This population is not venture-backed technology startups but every newly registered business across all industries. Small merchants and self-employed businesses dominate it, so blending the two datasets and saying the five-year survival rate for startups is 36 percent produces a false sentence. The direction, though, is shared: fewer new businesses alongside more closures suggests that entering the market has become a more cautious decision than before.

2. Competency One - Product Judgment, or Deciding What to Cut

The moment product skill is understood as the ability to build well, the aim drifts. The 43 percent of failures attributed to poor product-market fit rarely stem from an inability to build; far more often they come from building something unnecessary very well. The essence of product judgment is therefore not craft but the decision to delete.

2.1 How to Measure It

  • A rejection log: over the past three months, how many features did you decide not to build, and is the reasoning written down? An empty log means you are accepting requests rather than exercising judgment.
  • The deletion test: pick one feature. Can you say immediately whether users would leave if it disappeared, or does nobody know?
  • A flattening retention curve: at what point does cohort retention go horizontal? If every cohort keeps sliding toward zero, fit does not exist yet.
  • Interview conversion: of ten user interviews, how many actually changed the product? Zero suggests the interviews were confirmation exercises.
  • Usage interval: have you defined the natural cycle of the core action - daily, weekly, monthly - and does real usage follow it?

2.2 How to Train It

  • A weekly deletion meeting: schedule a recurring meeting whose agenda is removal rather than addition. If there is never anything to discuss, that itself is the finding.
  • Write the failure condition first: before the spec, write the metric and deadline that would prove this feature failed. This single habit dramatically reduces time lost to sunk cost.
  • Pay for competitors: subscribe to three similar products and use them in real work for 30 days. Reading reviews and paying money produce very different information.
  • The rule of five: talk to five potential users before starting on any new hypothesis. If five people describe the same problem in different words, that is signal; if they describe five different problems, the problem is not defined yet.

3. Competency Two - Persuasion Through Writing, Speaking and Selling

Founders spend most of their day persuading. They persuade candidates, first customers, investors and a wavering team. Yet this competency is not eloquence. It is closer to the ability to rebuild your conclusion in the other person's language.

3.1 How to Measure It

  • Cold outreach response rate: replies per 100 messages to strangers. Below five percent, the sentences are the problem, not the list.
  • Founder-closed share: of the first ten customers, how many did a founder close personally? A low share means the organization does not know the objections customers actually raise.
  • The 30-second test: explain the product in 30 seconds to someone outside the industry, then ask them to explain it back. If they cannot, there is no message yet.
  • Offer acceptance rate: hires accepted versus offers made. Often this is a communication problem rather than a compensation problem.
  • Objection inventory: how many distinct reasons for rejection have you catalogued? If you cannot list three, selling experience is thin.

3.2 How to Train It

  • One public piece of writing per week: writing where others read exposes the holes in the logic. It is the cheapest and most scalable persuasion training available.
  • Review recorded sales calls: with consent, record and count your own share of speaking time. If the founder talks more than 60 percent in early sales, that is a presentation, not a conversation.
  • Twenty demo repetitions: run the same demo twenty times and you will notice people stumble at the same point every time. That point is the product's real weakness.
  • Refresh the one-sentence summary: rewrite the single sentence describing the company each quarter. A sentence that keeps getting longer signals a product losing focus.

Investors have emphasized this axis for a long time. In an essay published in the Y Combinator library, Paul Graham named being relentlessly resourceful as the defining founder trait, and elsewhere argued that above a certain intelligence threshold what decides outcomes is determination. Those phrases sound vague, but translated into the measurements above they become trainable behavior.

4. Competency Three - Distribution Design, and the Myth That Good Products Spread by Themselves

The most repeated claim in consumer startup discourse is that a good product spreads on its own. That sentence is half true. Organic spread is an outcome, not a strategy. Products that genuinely spread have, without exception, a structure that produces spreading: value that requires sharing, output visible to outsiders, or usage that is itself content. A product with quality but no structure gets quietly loved and quietly disappears.

4.1 How to Measure It

  • Customer acquisition payback period: how many months to recover what you spent to acquire one customer? Without that number, this is spending rather than growth.
  • Organic share: what fraction of new users remains when ads are switched off? One day with ads off answers this.
  • Referral rate: how many new users does one existing user bring on average? Below one, growth stops without paid channels.
  • Channel concentration: what share of inbound comes from the single largest channel? Too high, and a policy change at that channel becomes an existential event.
  • Content half-life: how long until a published piece delivers half its original traffic? Short means it behaves like advertising; long means it is an asset.

4.2 Training, and Constraints Specific to Korea

  • Eight-week single-channel experiments: push one channel at a time for eight weeks. Touching five at once means never learning which one worked.
  • Design the sharing moment: build at least one point where a user naturally shows the output to someone else.
  • Map the search landscape: for Korean consumer services, visibility inside the Naver and Kakao ecosystems works differently from Google. Content formats and keyword strategy need to be designed per channel.
  • Audit app-store dependency: for mobile-first services, store policies and fee structures move unit economics directly. Designing a parallel web payment path is the safer default.
  • Treat communities as a priority: in Korea early spread often happens through specific communities and open chat rooms. Reputation there is hard to reclaim, so early support quality effectively becomes a distribution cost.

5. Competency Four - Capital Is an Ally Only When You Can Say No

Capital sits fourth not because it matters least but because the first three axes working is what makes its terms favorable. If capital grows while the product is unvalidated, you simply travel further and faster in the wrong direction. The fact that failed companies had raised a median of 11 million dollars before shutting down shows that insufficient capital is not the only way to die.

5.1 Where Korea's Funding Environment Stands

According to the Ministry of SMEs and Startups, new venture investment in Korea reached 13.6 trillion won in 2025, up 1.7 trillion won or 14.0 percent year on year. That is the second-largest figure on record after 15.9 trillion won in 2021, and the 8,542 deals were an all-time high. New venture fund formation totaled 14.3 trillion won, up 34.1 percent. Sector concentration, however, is pronounced: ICT services at 20.8 percent, bio and medical at 17.4 percent, and electrical, machinery and equipment at 14.6 percent together accounted for 52.8 percent of the total.

The practical implication is clear. Headlines about a larger total do not mean fundraising got easier for your company. The more money concentrates in specific sectors, the harder relative competition becomes for teams outside them. When planning a raise, it is more accurate to count how many investors actually write checks in your sector, stage and ticket size than to look at aggregate volume.

Public funding is not small either. The Ministry of SMEs and Startups announced a combined 2026 central and local government startup support budget of 3.4645 trillion won across 508 programs at 111 institutions, up 5.2 percent from the previous year. By type, however, loans account for 1.4245 trillion won, or 41.1 percent, followed by R&D at 864.8 billion won and commercialization at 815.1 billion won. Programs such as the pre-startup package, early-startup package and TIPS are widely known, but the fact that a large share of the total is money that must be repaid is frequently overlooked.

5.3 Measurement and Training

  • Runway in months: today's balance divided by monthly net burn. If you cannot answer instantly, this competency is not there yet.
  • Documented next-round milestones: are the metrics and deadline that would justify the next raise written on one page?
  • A twelve-month no-funding plan: actually write the survival plan that assumes no investment arrives. Teams with that plan can walk away from bad terms.
  • Practice refusal: have you ever declined an offer with poor terms? Raising money you cannot refuse is acceptance, not negotiation.
  • Verify the break-even structure: check whether each additional sale loses money. Growth on negative unit economics increases risk with scale.

7. What Makes the Korean Market Different

7.1 Domestic Scale and Expansion Paths

Consumer services aimed at Korea face a fairly visible ceiling. Even effectively winning a category often caps absolute revenue. As a result, two paths are common: designing an overseas route from the beginning, or shifting toward a high-ticket business-to-business model. Either way, that is a decision made during early product design, not an option bolted on after domestic success, because retrofitting it means rebuilding the structure.

7.2 Who You Actually Compete With for Talent

An early team does not compete only with other startups. Large domestic corporations, platform companies and overseas employers hiring remotely all target the same candidates. Substituting equity for cash compensation only persuades when it accounts for the fact that candidates can compute the expected value of that equity themselves. Concrete milestones and real decision authority are often stronger incentives than a vague growth narrative.

7.3 The Entry Cost of Regulated Industries

In heavily regulated areas such as finance, healthcare and mobility, approval timelines determine runway regardless of product quality. Regulatory handling becomes a core competency in those sectors, and even with mechanisms such as regulatory sandboxes, review and approval time has to be built into the funding plan. Conversely, for teams that absorb that cost, the same barrier becomes protection against later entrants.

7.4 How to Read Support-Program Survival Statistics

A survey released by the Ministry of SMEs and Startups in April 2019 reported a five-year survival rate of 53.1 percent for government-supported startups as of 2017, compared with 28.5 percent for startups generally. That figure is quoted often but requires care. Because support programs select participants through review, this statistic alone cannot separate companies that survived because of support from companies that were selected because they were likely to survive anyway. The survey is also several years old. Treating grants as a way to buy time rather than a guarantee of survival is the safer framing.

9. What Competence Cannot Solve

This section undercuts the premise of the article, and it is necessary. You can hold all four competencies and still fail - and many do.

  • Timing: 29 percent of the failures reviewed cited timing or macro conditions. Delivering the right answer before the market is ready produces the same result as a wrong answer. A product two years early and a product that is simply wrong look identical on the income statement.
  • Market size: a small market is small even at 100 percent share. Competence raises share; it does not change the absolute size of the market.
  • Capital cycles: interest rates and liquidity are beyond any single team's control. The same metrics produce different fundraising outcomes in different years.
  • Opportunity cost: three to five years of time, the career progress and savings not accumulated, and the toll on health and relationships are costs that are never recovered. The higher the failure probability, the earlier this item should be calculated.
  • Luck: a first customer turning into a major contract, or a competitor abruptly changing direction, is not a result of skill. Success stories that refuse to acknowledge luck set false expectations for the next person.

The honest conclusion follows. Competence raises the probability of success; it does not guarantee it. When writing about entrepreneurship omits this, readers begin without the ability to separate what they control from what they do not.

10. What AI Changed About the Conditions

10.1 Building Got Cheaper, Defending Got More Expensive

As of 2026, the cost and time required to build an initial product bear little resemblance to a few years ago. Small teams shipping working products in short cycles has become ordinary. The problem is that this change did not happen only to you. As entry costs fall, the number of competitors rises and the speed of duplication rises with it.

One useful test: if a small team could reproduce the core of your product over a weekend, it is a feature, not a company. An idea that fails that test may still make an excellent personal project, but it is a difficult foundation for a business.

10.2 Which Moats Are Left

  • Distribution channels: existing user touchpoints and content assets do not copy the way code does
  • Proprietary data: data that accumulates only through operating the service takes competitors time to match
  • Regulatory licenses: sectors where approval itself is the barrier
  • Switching costs: products embedded deeply enough in a workflow that leaving is expensive
  • Brand trust: reputation accumulated in high-stakes areas such as money and health

As that list suggests, differentiation in the AI era has moved from technical implementation to business structure. Being built with AI is no longer a differentiator, and emphasizing it can read as a signal that the product is easy to replace.

Conclusion: Who Should Start Now and Who Should Wait

The four axes have an order. Product judgment narrows what to build, persuasion gathers the first people, distribution design creates a repeatable path, and once those three run, capital buys time. Reverse the order and resources amplify a wrong direction.

Conditions that favor starting now include having personally experienced the problem and knowing something about the space that others do not, having already spent dozens of hours with potential users and changed your hypothesis as a result, holding at least 12 to 18 months of living expenses along with a plan for what happens if it fails, and being able to point at one axis in the checklist above as a clear strength.

Conditions that favor waiting are equally clear: founding itself is the goal while the problem is still blank; more hours have gone into the business plan than into user conversations; savings cover six months or less with no fallback; securing a government grant is the first objective; or the motivation comes mainly from watching someone else succeed. In that state, waiting is not failure. Time spent close to the problem accumulating experience, relationships and savings is also part of preparing to found a company.

One last look at the odds. The five-year survival rate across all Korean industries is 36.4 percent, and even venture-backed startups fail on product-market fit nearly half the time. Those numbers are not an argument against starting a company. They are an argument for separating what you can control from what you cannot before you begin. The four competencies sit on the controllable side. Concentrating there is the only way to move the probability at all.
