Laryngitis: Symptoms and Causes

Why the voice changes, how long you can wait, and when it is an emergency

Aug 11, 2026 Health & Wellness

Introduction: your throat does not hurt, your voice is simply gone

You get over a cold, the soreness fades, and the voice stays broken. You open a meeting and only half the sound arrives. You force it louder and the next morning there is even less. This is not a sore throat. It is inflammation in the organ that actually makes sound, which is exactly why lozenges and throat sprays feel beside the point.

Laryngitis is common and usually settles on its own. Two things make it more awkward than that summary suggests. First, a condition that almost always resolves by itself shares its opening hours with a rare condition that can close the airway. Second, a voice that stays broken for a long time can be a signal worth taking seriously. So the practical content of any laryngitis guide compresses into two questions: is this an emergency right now, and how long am I allowed to wait?

This piece is ordered around those two questions. It sets out what the larynx is and why the voice changes, draws the line between acute and chronic, then handles red flags and croup in children as separate sections. After that come the two thresholds for being seen, the evidence on antibiotics, what actually helps recovery, and the drivers that turn a short illness into a chronic one.

Medical disclaimer
This article is general health information and does not replace medical diagnosis, prescription, or treatment. Nothing here should be used as a reason to start or stop any medication on your own. In particular, do not take antibiotics on your own initiative and do not use leftover antibiotics from a previous prescription. Drug choice, dose, and duration must be decided with a doctor or pharmacist. Children, pregnant and breastfeeding people, older adults, immunocompromised people, and those with chronic conditions may need different advice from the general adult case. If breathing is difficult, saliva cannot be swallowed, or there is a high pitched rasping sound on breathing in, do not wait - call 911 in the United States, 999 in the United Kingdom, or your local emergency number, or go to the emergency department.

1. Where the inflammation actually sits - laryngitis versus pharyngitis and tonsillitis

The larynx sits in the front of the neck, behind the cartilage you can feel as the Adam's apple. It houses the vocal folds, and above them the epiglottis sits like a lid that closes the airway when you swallow. In other words, the larynx is both the sound generator and the entrance to the airway. Those two jobs explain everything about laryngitis: because it makes sound, inflammation changes the voice first; because it guards the airway, severe swelling narrows the path for air.

The MSD Manual defines laryngitis as inflammation of the voice box, with acute lasting 1 to 3 weeks and chronic lasting more than 3 weeks. Seoul National University Hospital's reference material stresses that acute laryngitis very often appears as one component of a common cold, rather than in isolation. That matches everyday experience: pure laryngitis with nothing else is less common than laryngitis riding along with a blocked nose and a sore throat.

Many people file every throat complaint under one heading. But where the inflammation sits determines what happens next. The table below works backwards from the symptom that stands out most.

2. Why the voice changes - what happens in the vocal fold lining

Voice is produced when the vocal folds come together and exhaled air drives the surface tissue into a wave-like vibration. That vibration is carried by the thin mucosal layer on the surface, not by muscle. Directly beneath it lies a loose, pliable layer known as Reinke space, and its softness is what lets the surface ripple freely.

Inflammation puts fluid into exactly that layer. The StatPearls chapter on acute laryngitis describes the membranous portion of the vocal fold becoming reddened and swollen, with irregular thickening along its edge producing a lower pitch and a scratchy quality. A heavier, waterlogged lining cannot vibrate as fast, and once the two folds differ slightly in thickness the cycles fall out of step and the sound breaks up.

Once you understand that mechanism, several common questions answer themselves at once.

  • Why does my throat feel fine while my voice is gone? The pain-sensitive pharynx and the vibrating vocal folds are different structures. Swelling confined to the folds changes the voice without hurting.
  • Why is the voice the last thing to recover? The swelling in the lining takes longer to drain, and until it does the conditions for vibration are not restored.
  • Why does forcing volume make tomorrow worse? Driving swollen tissue into harder collisions adds more swelling. The vocal folds are tissue that recovers while resting.
  • Why does the voice drop in pitch? A heavier lining vibrates more slowly. That is the low, gravelly voice people notice for a few days after a cold.
  • Why does throat clearing make it worse? Clearing the throat slams the folds together. It adds irritation instead of removing mucus.

One more point matters. Voice change is not exclusive to laryngitis. Vocal fold nodules and polyps, nerve problems affecting fold movement, and tumours of the larynx all produce symptoms in the same place. Identical symptoms do not mean identical causes, and that is the whole logic behind the two-week and four-week rules further down.

3. Acute or chronic - three weeks is the dividing line

The first axis for sorting laryngitis is duration. The MSD Manual splits it at acute 1 to 3 weeks and chronic beyond 3 weeks. The line looks arbitrary but earns its keep. Laryngitis that ends inside three weeks is usually infectious and time resolves it. Laryngitis that runs past three weeks is usually being fed by an irritant that has not stopped, and suppressing it with medication without removing that irritant simply invites it back.

The last two rows carry the most practical weight. For acute disease you ask is this dangerous now. For chronic disease you ask why has this not resolved. Same hoarseness, different question.

4. Causes - what it means that most cases are viral

The MSD Manual Professional version states plainly that the most common cause of acute laryngitis is viral upper respiratory infection. StatPearls agrees that viral pathogens account for the majority and lists rhinovirus, coronavirus, influenza, adenovirus and respiratory syncytial virus among the usual agents. Bacterial involvement is reported less often and typically as a secondary infection after a viral illness, with Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae named.

That fact drives two conclusions. First, antibiotics usually have no target. Second, ordinary respiratory hygiene works: hand washing and cough etiquette apply exactly as they do for a cold.

Infection is far from the only thing that inflames a larynx. The MSD Manual Professional list, organised for everyday use, looks like this.

  • Vocal overuse - speaking loudly or singing. Concerts, sporting events, and long teaching days are classic triggers.
  • Coughing itself - bronchitis, pneumonia, influenza and whooping cough irritate the larynx through the cough alone.
  • Gastro-oesophageal reflux - stomach contents reaching the larynx act as a chemical irritant.
  • Allergic reactions - can present as swelling of the laryngeal mucosa.
  • Inhaled irritants - cigarette smoke, some inhaled medications, heated fumes. Sustained exposure can lead to Reinke oedema.
  • Dry air and dehydration - a dry lining vibrates poorly and drives the urge to clear the throat.

Holding your own situation against that list narrows the cause considerably. Losing your voice the day after a long night of singing and having a smoker's voice that has been rough for six months are the same diagnosis in name only.

5. Red flags - this is not laryngitis any more

This is the most important section of the article. Because the larynx is the entrance to the airway, there are rare paths that lead toward obstruction. If any of the following is present, you are no longer in watch-and-wait territory.

The condition to respect most is acute epiglottitis, in which the epiglottis swells and blocks the entrance to the airway. StatPearls puts adult mortality at 7% to 10%, and under 1% in children after intubation. Hib vaccination reshaped the epidemiology: the childhood-to-adult case ratio fell from 2.6:1 in 1980 to 0.4:1 in 1993, and incidence is reported at about 1 case per 100,000 people per year. In other words, it is now more an adult disease than a paediatric one.

The classic presentation is drooling, dysphagia and distress with air hunger, together with an expression of panic. Children often sit upright propped on their hands in the tripod position. Adults more often present with a muffled hot-potato voice and difficulty lying flat. The Japan Society of Otorhinolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery likewise advises urgent specialist review when difficulty breathing or difficulty swallowing appears, and states that delayed diagnosis can lead directly to a life-threatening blocked airway.

What matters most is that the first few hours are hard to tell apart from a severe sore throat. So judge by signs, not by elapsed time. If the pain is out of proportion, the saliva is not going down, the voice sounds muffled, and sitting up is the only comfortable position, that is reason enough.

6. Croup in children - when the barking cough starts at night

Laryngitis in a child is not the same risk as laryngitis in an adult, for a simple reason: the airway is narrower. The same millimetre of swelling removes a far greater share of a small tube. In children the swelling typically sits below the vocal folds and narrows the airway there, and that picture is called croup.

The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency describes croup as mainly affecting children from 6 months to 6 years, starting with cold symptoms before the characteristic features appear over 1 to 3 days. About 75% of cases are caused by parainfluenza virus, with adenovirus, respiratory syncytial virus and influenza also implicated. Most cases improve within 48 hours, though about 20% require hospital treatment. StatPearls places the peak incidence between 6 months and 3 years, with symptoms lasting typically 3 to 7 days and the worst on days 3 or 4.

Three features are worth committing to memory.

  • The barking cough - usually described as a dog or a seal. Once heard it is recognisable ever after.
  • Inspiratory stridor - a high pitched rasping sound while breathing in, which is different from a wheeze on breathing out.
  • Worse at night - a child who seemed fine all day can deteriorate sharply after falling asleep.

One traditional response is now advised against. The NHS specifically says not to put a child in a steamy room or have them inhale steam. What the NHS does advise is keeping the child upright, comforting them, keeping fluids going, and seeking medical advice if croup is suspected. A frightened, crying child breathes worse, so calming them is part of the management, a point the Korean national health portal also makes.

Clinical treatment centres on corticosteroids, nebulised epinephrine and oxygen where needed. Those are decisions made in a medical setting, not things to keep at home. At home the job is observation and the decision about when to move.

In a child, do not wait if you see this
Struggling to breathe / skin pulling in between the ribs or below the neck / a rasping sound on breathing in / skin or lips that are blue, grey, pale or blotchy / unusually sleepy and unresponsive / limp and floppy / very upset or restless and cannot be calmed / drooling and unable to swallow
Call 911 in the United States or 999 in the United Kingdom. This list follows the NHS emergency guidance for croup.

7. Two weeks and four weeks - the two clocks

The honest answer to how long a broken voice can be left alone is that there are two thresholds, because two different questions use two different numbers.

ThresholdWhat it meansBasis
ImmediatelyDifficulty breathing, stridor, inability to swallow, high fever, a child with a barking cough - regardless of durationNHS laryngitis and croup emergency advice; AAO-HNS escalation criteria
2 weeksBook an appointment if symptoms outlast 2 weeks; hoarseness beyond 2 weeks warrants assessmentNHS laryngitis advice; MSD Manual Professional indication for laryngoscopy; Korea National Cancer Information Center
4 weeksIf dysphonia has not resolved or improved, the larynx should be visualised by laryngoscopyAAO-HNS Clinical Practice Guideline: Hoarseness (Dysphonia), 2018 update

The 2018 AAO-HNS update shortened the period of acceptable conservative management from 90 days to four weeks. The statement reads that clinicians should perform laryngoscopy, or refer to a clinician who can, when dysphonia fails to resolve or improve within four weeks, or irrespective of duration if a serious underlying cause is suspected.

The same guideline lists circumstances that justify expedited laryngeal evaluation before any clock runs out: recent surgery involving the head, neck or chest; recent endotracheal intubation; a concomitant neck mass; respiratory distress or stridor; a history of tobacco use; and whether the patient is a professional voice user.

Korean guidance points the same way. The National Cancer Information Center advises assessment when hoarseness persists for 2 weeks or longer, and notes that while most persistent hoarseness comes from benign conditions such as laryngopharyngeal reflux, it can rarely be a symptom of laryngeal cancer itself, so accurate diagnosis is needed. The balance of that sentence matters: passing two weeks does not mean cancer, it means the question needs answering.

Practically: if your voice broke after a cold and there are no red flags, manage it and watch for one to two weeks. Past two weeks, book the ENT appointment. If you smoke, feel a lump in the neck, struggle to swallow, hear anything odd in your breathing, or notice the voice steadily worsening, do not count to two weeks at all.

8. Why antibiotics are usually not needed

People sometimes leave a consultation disappointed that no antibiotic was prescribed for laryngitis. That is not stinginess. It is what the evidence looks like.

The Cochrane systematic review on antibiotics for acute laryngitis in adults examined 3 randomised controlled trials with 351 participants. Penicillin V and erythromycin appeared to have no benefit when objective outcomes were assessed. Erythromycin did reduce subjectively reported voice disturbance at one week and cough at two weeks, but the certainty of the evidence was graded low to very low. The authors noted methodological problems in the included trials and concluded that such modest benefits may not outweigh the cost, adverse effects, or the consequences for antibiotic resistance.

Guidelines point the same way. The AAO-HNS dysphonia guideline states that clinicians should not routinely prescribe antibiotics to treat dysphonia. Two neighbouring recommendations are worth remembering with it: do not prescribe anti-reflux medication for isolated dysphonia on symptoms alone without visualising the larynx, and do not routinely prescribe corticosteroids before the larynx has been visualised. That is precisely why buying an acid suppressant and self-treating for months is the wrong shape of response.

Exceptions exist, of course. Confirmed bacterial infection, or a suspected condition such as epiglottitis where bacteria are the culprit, changes the calculus completely. The Japan Society of Otorhinolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery describes treating acute epiglottitis with antimicrobials and steroids, with improvement usually over about 1 to 2 weeks. That call is made by someone who has looked at the larynx.

Do not take leftover antibiotics. Using medication left over from a previous prescription mismatches dose, duration and target, and it also obscures the diagnosis if a bacterial infection really is present while driving resistance. Whether an antibiotic is warranted is a decision that belongs in a consultation.

9. What actually helps recovery - voice rest, humidity, fluids

The substance of acute laryngitis care is creating the conditions in which the vocal folds can recover. There is no dramatic intervention, and that is characteristic of the illness rather than a gap in the advice. The NHS self-care guidance, item by item, reads as follows.

  • Speak as little as possible. This is the biggest single variable in recovery.
  • Do not talk loudly - and do not whisper either. The NHS lists both together as things to avoid.
  • Drink plenty of fluids. A dry lining recovers slowly.
  • Keep indoor air from getting dry.
  • Gargling with salt water is mentioned for adults. It is not advised for children.
  • Avoid smoking and smoky or dusty environments.
  • Avoid caffeine and alcohol.

One addition makes a bigger difference than people expect: stop clearing your throat. Habitually scraping at the sensation of something stuck keeps slamming the folds together, and that sensation is often produced by the swelling itself. Swallowing a mouthful of water is a better substitute for the urge.

For symptom relief, the NHS names paracetamol, ibuprofen, cough syrup and lozenges as things a pharmacist can advise on. These make the illness easier to sit through; they do not cure the laryngitis, and some ingredients are unsuitable depending on existing conditions and current medication, so it is worth explaining your situation at the counter rather than picking blind.

Korean hospital guidance runs in the same direction: rest and adequate fluids as the foundation, warm drinks frequently, and speaking as little as possible so that the larynx gets a rest.

10. Three things that turn it chronic - smoking, reflux, vocal overuse

Laryngitis that outlives three weeks usually has an irritant that has never stopped. Seoul National University Hospital's chronic laryngitis material lists smoking, excessive voice use, alcohol, gastro-oesophageal reflux, and air pollution exposure. The symptom set centres on voice change, cough, a lump sensation in the throat and vocal fatigue, while fever and severe pain are uncommon. That combination is itself the clue distinguishing it from acute disease.

10.1 Smoking

Tobacco smoke is a sustained chemical irritant to the laryngeal lining and can lead to Reinke oedema, in which Reinke space becomes waterlogged. That is the mechanism behind a voice that settles permanently into a deep, rough register. More importantly, smoking is a core risk factor for laryngeal cancer, which is why the AAO-HNS guideline names a history of tobacco use as a reason to expedite laryngeal evaluation. In a smoker, persistent hoarseness is a signal to look at the larynx earlier than you otherwise would.

10.2 Laryngopharyngeal reflux

When stomach contents travel past the oesophagus and reach the larynx, chemical irritation repeats. The awkward feature is that laryngeal symptoms can occur without any heartburn at all. The typical pattern is a voice at its worst first thing in the morning, with a lump sensation in the throat and frequent throat clearing. Seoul National University Hospital's lifestyle advice includes limiting spicy and fatty food, stopping smoking, drinking enough fluids, and raising the head of the bed by about 15 cm.

As noted above, though, the AAO-HNS guideline advises against prescribing anti-reflux medication for isolated dysphonia on symptoms alone without visualising the larynx. Buying an acid suppressant and taking it for months on your own runs directly against that. Establishing whether reflux is the cause comes first.

10.3 Vocal overuse

For teachers, lecturers, call-centre staff, sales professionals, singers and creators, laryngitis takes on the character of an occupational condition. The pattern is that acute laryngitis arrives, the person cannot stop talking, recovery is repeatedly interrupted, and over time the folds develop nodules or polyps. The Japan Society of Otorhinolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery lists voice overuse, inflammation and smoking as causes of vocal fold polyps and nodules, with surgery considered when anti-inflammatory treatment and voice training do not resolve the problem.

The AAO-HNS guideline states that clinicians should advocate voice therapy for patients with dysphonia from a cause amenable to voice therapy. For someone who abuses their voice occupationally, changing how they produce sound can be a more fundamental fix than any medication.

11. If your voice is your livelihood - designing the recovery

Telling someone whose income depends on speaking to simply stop talking is not a workable prescription. In practice the approach becomes managing the total load.

  • Budget the talking in advance. Spend voice where it must be spent and shift the rest to text or notes.
  • Use amplification instead of volume. Pushing loudness is what strains the folds most.
  • Cut conversation in noisy places. High background noise makes people push their voice without noticing.
  • Do not substitute whispering. It is not a way of sparing anything.
  • Sip water between stretches of speaking. Keep the lining from drying out.
  • Do not perform through an unrecovered acute episode. Pushing during the acute phase extends the recovery.
  • If it keeps recurring, get the larynx looked at. There is a reason the guideline names professional voice users for expedited evaluation.

One more thing helps: keeping a record. Noting when the voice worsens, how long recovery takes, and which engagements precede a bad spell gives a far more precise conversation in the consulting room after only a few months.

12. Common misconceptions

  • Whispering is a way to rest a lost voice - no. The NHS advises avoiding loud speech and whispering together.
  • Antibiotics speed up laryngitis - neither the Cochrane review nor the AAO-HNS guideline supports routine antibiotic use.
  • Steam is the treatment for croup - the NHS advises against putting a child in a steamy room or having them inhale steam.
  • If the throat does not hurt, it is not laryngitis - painless voice change is common.
  • Hoarseness always clears with time - usually, yes. Past two weeks it needs to be looked into.
  • A long-running rough voice means cancer - no. Most persistent hoarseness is benign. Needing assessment and having cancer are different statements.
  • Frequent throat clearing shifts the mucus - it slams the folds together and adds irritation.
  • Children can be watched on the same terms as adults - no. A narrow paediatric airway closes far faster on the same swelling.

13. What could not be confirmed

Collected here are the items deliberately left without a number or a firm claim.

  • The exact proportion of bacterial laryngitis - the statement that viruses account for the majority is consistent across primary sources, but no reliable figure for the bacterial share was located. StatPearls itself notes that incidence is hard to estimate because many people never seek care. No percentage is used in this article for that reason.
  • Country-level incidence statistics - no public primary dataset giving national incidence or consultation rates by age group was obtained for this piece.
  • Average recovery time in adult acute laryngitis - the NHS 1 to 2 weeks and the 1 to 3 week definition of acute disease are documented, but no sourced figure of the form average N days was found, so none is given.
  • The optimal humidity range - sources agree that maintaining humidity is advisable, but no primary source specifying a numeric humidity target for laryngitis recovery was confirmed.
  • Home remedies - within the source set used here, there is no evidence that honey drinks, pear juice, bellflower root or similar remedies alter the course of laryngitis. Making discomfort easier to tolerate and curing an illness are different claims, and honey should not be given to infants under 12 months for separate reasons.

14. Frequently asked questions

Q. How many days does laryngitis take to clear?

The NHS says it usually resolves by itself within 1 to 2 weeks, and acute laryngitis is defined as lasting roughly 1 to 3 weeks. That describes the usual viral course. The voice commonly stays rough for several days after everything else has settled.

Q. My throat is fine but my voice is gone. Is that laryngitis?

It can be. The structures that register pain and the structures that make sound are different, so painless voice change is common. But nodules, polyps, nerve problems and other laryngeal conditions produce the same symptom, so past two weeks it needs checking.

Q. Is laryngitis contagious?

More accurately, the viral upper respiratory infection behind it is contagious, rather than the laryngitis itself. It spreads the way a cold does, so hand washing and cough etiquette apply directly.

Q. My child has a barking cough but seems fine during the day.

That is the classic croup pattern, with a clear tendency to worsen at night. Most cases settle within days, but if the child struggles to breathe, makes a rasping sound on breathing in, or changes colour, call emergency services immediately.

Q. My voice has been off for weeks but I feel well otherwise. Can I leave it?

No. Feeling well is not reassurance here. Past two weeks, book the appointment; past four weeks without improvement, the larynx should be visualised. A smoking history moves that clock forward.

Conclusion: two questions are enough

Laryngitis is usually viral, the voice changes because the vocal fold lining swells, and the whole thing generally resolves within one to three weeks. The substance of care is talking less, staying hydrated, keeping the air from drying out, and avoiding irritants. Antibiotics usually have nothing to act on, and the evidence does not support using them routinely.

So carry two questions. First, is this an emergency now? Difficulty breathing, a rasping sound on breathing in, inability to swallow saliva, high fever with severe throat pain, or a child with a barking cough who is struggling - stop counting days and call emergency services. Acute epiglottitis can progress to airway obstruction, and early on it is genuinely hard to separate from a severe sore throat.

Second, how long may I wait? Two weeks is when you book, four weeks is when someone looks at your larynx. Any escalation criterion - tobacco use, a neck mass, difficulty swallowing, breathing trouble - voids those clocks and turns the answer into now. Most persistent hoarseness is benign; the thresholds exist because the cost of checking is far smaller than the cost of missing.

This article is general health information and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment.
