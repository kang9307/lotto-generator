The Productivity Mirage - More Tools, Same Amount of Work

Where perceived speed splits from actual output, and how to measure the bottleneck

2026-07-31 Trends & Culture

Opening: Five New Tools, and You Still Leave at the Same Hour

Count what the team has picked up over the last two years. A collaborative document tool. A ticket system for incoming requests. A project board that lives separately from the messenger. Something that summarizes source material. Something that transcribes meetings. That is five. Each one was genuinely impressive the first week. A draft that used to eat half a day arrived in thirty minutes, and nobody stayed late writing up minutes anymore.

And yet the hour you leave has not moved. Neither has the number of requests that slip past the week without being handled. When somebody asks in the quarterly retrospective what actually got better, nobody can answer cleanly. Clearly faster, and yet the same amount finishes. That is the phenomenon this article is about.

Two claims this article is not making, stated up front.

  • Not that tools are useless. Some work genuinely shrank. Section 8 builds a test for which work that is
  • Not that you should buy better tools. If swapping the tool solved it, five attempts would have been enough
  • The argument is one sentence. Productivity is not individual speed. It is system throughput, and throughput is set by the slowest point. Speed up the front and the bottleneck simply moves back

About the numbers below. Every figure in the tables is the result of calculating an identity, not a value observed at some organization. They do not promise a multiple. They show the arithmetic ceiling that no improvement of that shape can exceed. Every team and company that appears as an example is fictional.

About the citations. Exactly one external source was opened and verified for this article. Official productivity statistics were attempted and could not be reached, so no such figures appear here. Section 10 lists precisely what could not be checked.

1. What the Mirage Actually Is - Perception and Output Measure Different Things

A mirage is not something that is not there. It is real light, bent, appearing in the wrong place. The same applies here. The feeling of speed is genuine. It just does not point at output.

Separate what each one measures and the divergence explains itself.

  • What perception measures - the speed of the stretch you personally touch. The thirty minutes of drafting, the twenty minutes of gathering material. Time that is inside your hands
  • What output measures - how many items finished and left, and how long a request took from arrival to close
  • What sits between them - the wait between you letting go and the next person picking up. Nobody experiences this time, because during it nothing happens
  • Which is why your stretch can halve while total lead time barely moves. The thirty minutes you saved are consumed standing in a queue

In most office flows, wait time dwarfs touch time. If a request took three days from arrival to close, the hours during which somebody was actually working on it are often just that: hours. The rest was waiting for a turn. And what we improve is, almost always, those few hours of touching it.

1.1 One Study Where Perception and Measurement Split, and Its Limits

One study measured that divergence head on: a randomized controlled trial run by the nonprofit evaluation organization METR in early 2025 and published that July. It is the only external source opened and verified for this article, so the conditions and limits are recorded alongside the result.

  • Design - 16 developers experienced in large open-source repositories were assigned 246 issues in total (bug fixes, features, refactors), with AI tool use randomly allowed or disallowed per issue. Screen recordings and self-reported times were both collected
  • Result - issues where AI tools were permitted took 19 percent longer to complete. Participants had predicted a 24 percent speedup beforehand, and still believed afterwards that they had been sped up by 20 percent
  • Limits the authors state explicitly - the authors are emphatic that the result is not evidence that AI fails to speed up most developers. They note the sample is not representative, that it says nothing about work outside software development, and nothing about later systems or other ways of working
  • So this article does not generalize from it. Strip away 16 people, 246 issues, early 2025 and mature large repositories and nothing is left. What transfers is not the conclusion but the measurement design: because self-report and observation were both recorded, the gap between them was visible at all

That last point is the part you can carry into practice. The gap between perception and output is itself a metric. An organization that does not record both has no way of knowing how wide its own gap is.

2. What Gets Measured Changes - When the Easy-to-Count Thing Becomes the Target

More tools means more logs, and more logs means more things that can be counted. Something quiet happens at that point. The easy-to-count thing becomes the target, and only the thing that became the target grows.

The important part is that this is not anyone being dishonest. People spend time on what they are evaluated by. That is normal. The problem appears when the thing chosen for evaluation is an activity rather than an outcome.

What you count today (activity)Why it is riskyCount this instead (outcome)
Messages and tickets handledSplitting a reply inflates itCases closed with no repeat inquiry
Documents writtenWritten and read are different thingsShare of documents that produced a decision
Commits and change countSlicing work finer inflates itMedian lead time of a change that reached release
Meeting hours and attendanceAttending is not agreeingDecisions actually settled in that meeting
Active users of a toolLogging in is not outputManual steps that tool removed
Drafts generatedA draft is work in progressShare of drafts that reached approval, and how long it took
First-response timeSeen is not handledTime to final resolution

The left column has one thing in common. Every one of those numbers can be raised by a single person acting alone. Three tests follow from that.

  • Count at the finish line. Items started and intermediate artifacts are activity. Counting what has not shipped as achievement is counting inventory as revenue
  • Penalize rework. A metric that does not subtract returned items is counting work that must be done over as work that was done
  • It must be impossible to raise alone. If one person's behavior moves the number by itself, it is not an outcome
  • And changing a metric changes behavior. So before you switch, spend an hour looking for the way somebody could game the new one on their own

3. Work Does Not Disappear, It Relocates

This is the core mechanism of the mirage. When automation shrinks the front of a flow, that work is not deleted. It is pushed backwards. The total does not change. The position does.

Before the tool - the path of a single request

  intake ── draft ───────── review ── revise ── approve ── send
            [touch 4h]      [1h]      [1h]      [0.5]      [0.5]
            wait 2h         wait 8h   wait 4h   wait 12h

  touch total 7h · wait total 26h · lead time 33h


After the tool - only the drafting stretch got faster

  intake ── draft ── review ────── revise ────── approve ── send
            [1h]     [1.5h]        [1.5h]        [0.5]      [0.5]
            wait 2h  wait 14h      wait 6h       wait 12h

  touch total 5h · wait total 34h · lead time 39h

  what shrank : drafting, from 4h to 1h
  what grew   : review touch time (more drafts means more to check),
                the queue in front of review (more drafts arrive per day)

Those numbers are an illustration, not an observation. The structure, however, is not an illustration. When the front produces more, the next stage receives more, and if the next stage's capacity is unchanged the queue lengthens. Written out as items:

  • Review grows. Twice the drafts means twice as much to read. And if draft quality is uneven, you reach the stretch where reading costs more than writing did
  • Revision grows. Anything made quickly also makes things that must be fixed quickly, especially when it was made without the context
  • Coordination grows. More output means more things to agree on. The arithmetic in section 5 attaches here
  • And this increase usually lands on somebody else. Whoever owns the front feels faster; whoever owns the back feels buried. That is why perception splits in opposite directions inside a single team

Which produces one practical question. When the front speeds up, who receives the work pushed downstream? If nobody can name that person, the improvement has not finished being designed.

4. What Each Additional Tool Costs - The Work Between Tools

When we evaluate a tool we look only at what the tool does. But a tool also takes something as a fixed charge, and most of that charge appears in no metric.

Cost itemWhen it landsWho absorbs itDoes a metric catch it
LearningAt rollout, and again at every new hireThe individualUsually not
Switching (context moves)All day, in small piecesThe individualNo
Upkeep (access, versions, billing)ContinuouslyThe administratorOnly in the budget
Integration (moving work between tools)Every time the flow crosses a toolThe individualNo
Scattered source of truthThe moment the same fact lives in two placesEveryoneOnly when something breaks

What deserves attention is the mismatch between the third and fourth columns. Tool cost is approved as budget and managed by an administrator, while a large share of the real burden lands on individuals and is recorded nowhere. Which means the numbers used to justify adoption never contained that cost in the first place.

4.1 Where the Work Between Tools Comes From

Something exists with two tools that did not exist with one: carrying things across. Copying a requirement from a ticket into a document, a decision from the document into a card, a card status into the messenger.

  • With n tools there are up to n(n-1)/2 boundaries to carry work across. Three tools means three, five tools means ten
  • And each boundary creates a chance that the two sides disagree. When they disagree, a new task appears: finding out which one is right
  • None of this shows up in any tool's usage statistics, because it happens between the tools. Which is how per-tool satisfaction surveys come back positive while the overall experience gets worse

5. Coordination Cost Grows With the Square

Of all the reasons individual productivity and organizational output drift apart, this one has the clearest arithmetic. As people or output grow, the number of pairs that must stay aligned grows close to the square of headcount.

Double a team from five to ten and the pairs go from 10 to 45, roughly a factor of four. Of course not everyone aligns with everyone in a real organization. But the direction is unambiguous. As headcount and output rise, checking, agreeing and meeting rise faster.

  • Which is why improvements to individual speed do not transfer cleanly into organizational output. The extra output re-enters as more to coordinate
  • Conversely, improvements that remove coordination points work without any tool at all. Dropping an approval step, or reducing sign-off from two people to one
  • The conclusion here is not to have fewer people. It is to have fewer people involved per item. Those are entirely different proposals

6. How Much Does Total Time Fall When Only the Front Gets Faster - The Arithmetic Ceiling

Now put the claim into arithmetic. Set total elapsed time to 1, and suppose a stretch worth p of that total becomes s times faster. New total is (1-p) + p/s, and therefore

total reduction = p × (1 - 1/s).

The table below is that formula, calculated. The important word is ceiling, not forecast.

  • The last column is the absolute ceiling. Delete that stretch entirely and you still cannot beat it. If drafting is 20 percent of the total, a tool that produces drafts instantly still caps you at 20 percent
  • So measure p first. Without p, whatever improvement you buy, you are buying it without knowing its ceiling
  • These figures are before tool cost. Subtract the learning, switching, upkeep and integration from section 4 and the real value is smaller
  • And this table does not account for the relocation in section 3. If the back grows by what the front shed, the real reduction is lower still, and in some cases negative

6.1 Buying an Improvement Without Knowing Its Ceiling

A familiar scene: somebody watches a demo that dramatically compresses one stretch and decides to adopt. What a demo shows is s, how many times faster that stretch becomes. What decides the outcome is p, what share of the total that stretch was.

  • A demo always picks a stretch with a large s. That is what a demo is for. This is not the problem
  • The problem is that nobody knows p. It is a number that only exists once you measure your own flow, and the seller cannot know it
  • So the first question in an adoption review is not how many times faster it is. It is what percentage of our total that stretch represents

7. Where Office Structure Comes In - Structure, Not Culture

The same tool produces different results in different organizations, and much of that difference comes from the shape of the back of the flow. Below are structures common in Korean workplaces, described concretely. None of them is being condemned, and each exists for a reason. But all of them attach time to the back.

  • Fixed reporting formats. Even when the content is ready, a step remains: transferring it into the prescribed form. No amount of speed at the front removes that conversion step. In the section 6 table, this step is part of what is excluded from p
  • The number of approval stages. Four stages means four checkpoints, and each checkpoint attaches a wait. As section 5 showed, this is not a headcount problem but a people-per-item problem
  • The expectation of real-time response. When fast messenger replies are expected, individuals must chop their own work into pieces. What falls is response latency; what rises is switching cost. The second row of the section 4 table grows here
  • Verbal agreement duplicated into documents. What was decided in a meeting is written up, and the write-up is then explained verbally again. This is the carrying-across of section 4.1, happening between people instead of tools
  • None of this is a proposal to change culture. Culture is hard to change and out of scope here. Instead, ask this: what share of our flow does the front actually occupy? The more of these structures an organization has, the smaller p is, and the smaller p is, the lower the ceiling on any front-end tool

The practical benefit of this framing is that there is nobody left to blame. The tool is not bad, the people are not lazy, the culture is not backward. The stretch that was improved was simply a small share of the whole, and that is a number you can find by measuring.

8. So What Genuinely Shrank

Ending in cynicism would make this a wrong article. Some work really did shrink, and the test that separates it from the rest compresses into one thing: verification cost.

TestShrinksDoes not shrink
RepetitionThe same task recursEvery instance differs
RulesThe rule can be written downContextual judgment required
Verification costCorrectness is cheap and instant to checkChecking costs as much as doing
Failure costEasy to undo when wrongWrong means external trust damage
AccountabilityNo signature needed on the resultA person must sign
InputInput arrives in a fixed shapeInput must be extracted first

In one line: if verification is cheaper than the original task, the tool saves time. If verification costs as much as the task, the work does not shrink, it relocates to review. The relocation in section 3 happens under exactly this condition.

  • What genuinely shrank. Format conversion, rearranging the same material, first passes at structured documents, capturing what was said in a meeting, finding material when the search target is unambiguous. What they share is that you know it is right the moment you look
  • What did not. Deciding what to decide, coordinating between conflicting interests, final accountability for anything going outside the company, judgment on exceptions. What they share is that checking requires reading as closely as producing did
  • Which is why the same tool splits teams. Not a difference in tool quality, but a difference in the verification cost of the work that team handles. This is a difference in kind of work, not in team capability
  • And one more thing. The parts that shrank really did shrink. Not writing up minutes at night is a real change in a real life. The argument here is not to deny it, but to hold the line that it is not the same thing as organizational output

9. How to Fix the Measurement

Diagnosis ends here and the work begins. The order is: measure the bottleneck first, move the metrics toward outcomes, and keep the comparison design uncontaminated.

Bottleneck diagnosis - done before adding a tool

  Step 1. Draw the flow
     intake → draft → review → revise → approve → send → close
     Only stages that actually exist. The observed procedure, not the documented one.

  Step 2. Record two timestamps per stage
     T_in    when the item entered the stage
     T_out   when the item left the stage
     touch time = time somebody was actually working on it
     wait time  = (T_out - T_in) - touch time

  Step 3. Find the stage with the largest wait time
     The bottleneck is not the longest task. It is the longest queue.

  Step 4. Confirm a queue is forming in front of it
     Backlog keeps growing there means bottleneck. Near zero means not.

  Step 5. Calculate the ceiling
     Work out that stage's share p and write the ceiling first, using section 6.
     If the ceiling is not large enough, do not buy the improvement.

  ★ work in progress = throughput × time in system.
    Throughput is set by the bottleneck. So when only the front
    gets faster, time in system does not fall. The pile of waiting work grows.

The point of the procedure is that choosing a tool comes last. Buying a drafting tool when the bottleneck is review is buying an improvement whose ceiling was already fixed before you started.

  • Measuring wait time is the whole trick. Most organizations measure only touch time, which is why the improvement target is always touch time
  • Move metrics to the finish line. The right column of the section 2 table is that list
  • Read speed and rework together. Speed up with rejections up is not an improvement, it is a cost transfer to the back of the flow
  • Record both self-report and observation. As section 1.1 showed, the gap between them is itself information

9.1 What Contaminates the Comparison

Several things can render a before-and-after comparison meaningless, and most of them have to be handled before rollout, not after.

ContaminantHow it changes the resultWhat to do in advance
No baselineThe comparison does not existCapture the numbers before rollout
Unequal window lengthThe longer window absorbs more extremesMatch the lengths
Peak season, holidays, deadlinesOutput moves regardless of the toolPick windows that do not straddle them
Headcount changeGrowth or shrinkage moves output by itselfRecord headcount across the period
Shift in difficulty mixThe easy-work period looks betterCompare the same kind of work
Reading only the averageA handful of extremes drags itRead median and upper percentile
Criteria set afterwardsYou end up picking the metric that looks goodWrite success criteria before rollout
  • The most common failure is the first row. Reconstructing what it used to be like after the fact means reconstructing a memory already colored by expectation
  • Agreeing a rollback condition in advance matters for the same reason. Decided after the results arrive, it is never triggered
  • Where possible, keep a group that did not adopt, or a pre-rollout trend line. Without one you cannot rule out that things were improving anyway

10. What This Article Could Not Verify

Stated plainly: this article cites no official productivity statistics at all. Not because it chose not to, but because they could not be verified.

  • The OECD indicator page on GDP per hour worked was opened in order to check its definition and the figures for Korea, but access was refused (HTTP 403) and the page did not load. So no international comparison figure appears here
  • Korean official labour productivity statistics were likewise not reached in primary form within this research budget. Numbers quoted in summary articles or blogs were deliberately not used, because relaying a second-hand figure puts it into circulation with its source detached
  • The news item that prompted this topic could not be located in the listing. So nothing it argued is quoted here, and only the subject was taken. The argument in this article is entirely its own
  • The flow example and the hours in section 3 are not observations. They illustrate a structure, and they are labeled as such
  • Section 1.1 rests on a single study. One study cannot establish that AI tools slow people down, and its authors said so explicitly. What this article took from it is not the conclusion but the design of measuring self-report and observation together

What could not be verified has something in common: it is all somebody else's numbers. And the method in this article does not need them. Measuring p, measuring wait time, capturing a baseline are all things done inside your own flow. That is why the article ends with a procedure instead of a statistic.

Conclusion: Do Not Add a Tool Before Measuring the Bottleneck

Five tools in and the hour you leave has not moved, and that is not because the tools were fake. The stretch that improved was a small share of the whole, and what it shed was pushed backwards. Both of those are knowable by measuring, and most organizations do not measure them.

Three sentences. First, feeling faster is real. But perception measures your stretch while output measures the whole flow, so the two are free to move independently. Second, throughput is set by the slowest point. Speed up anything that is not the bottleneck and you lengthen the queue in front of the bottleneck. Third, the share of the stretch sets the ceiling on the improvement. A stretch worth 20 percent caps you at 20 percent even if it vanishes entirely.

So there are exactly two things to measure before adding a tool. Where is the queue forming, and what share of the total is that stretch? Without those two numbers, every adoption decision is made without knowing its ceiling.

There is one thing you can do today. Take ten recently closed requests and write down the intake time, the close time, and the hours somebody actually spent touching each stage. Out comes the ratio of wait time to touch time. The larger that ratio, the smaller the effect any touch-time tool can have on the whole. And that ratio can be measured today, with no tool at all.

# The Productivity Mirage — Converting Activity Metrics to Outcome Metrics and Measuring the Bottleneck

> Source: braindetox.kr — The Productivity Mirage: More Tools, Same Amount of Work
> Nature of the numbers: everything below is **arithmetic from identities**, not measurements from someone's organization.
> Paste this file into an AI assistant and say: rebuild our team metrics against this standard.
> ⚠ This document does not ask anyone to work harder. It asks you to **change what you measure.**

## 0. Premise — Perceived Speed and Actual Output Measure Different Things

| | What it measures | When you feel it | The trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Perception** | The speed of the stretch you personally touch | Immediately | You cannot see outside your own stretch |
| **Output** | Items finished and shipped, plus lead time | Weeks later | Nobody feels it, so nobody defends it |

**Feeling faster is not a fake feeling.** It simply is not output. The two have to be measured separately.

## 1. Activity Metric to Outcome Metric Conversion Table

When you make the easy-to-count thing your target, the easy-to-count activity is what grows.

| What you count today (activity) | Why it is risky | Count this instead (outcome) |
|---|---|---|
| Messages and tickets handled | Splitting a reply inflates it | **Cases closed with no repeat inquiry** |
| Documents written | Written and read are different things | **Share of documents that produced a decision** |
| Commits and change count | Slicing work inflates it | **Median lead time of a change that reached release** |
| Meeting hours and attendance | Attending is not agreeing | **Decisions actually settled in the meeting** |
| Active users of a tool | Logging in is not output | **Manual steps that tool removed** |
| Drafts generated | A draft is work in progress | **Share of drafts that reached approval, and how long it took** |
| First-response time | Seen is not handled | **Time to final resolution** |

**Three conversion rules**
- Count at the **finish line**. Never at kickoff or at an intermediate artifact.
- The metric must **penalize rework**. Anything that comes back is subtracted from done.
- The metric must be **impossible for one person to raise alone**. If one person can raise it alone, it is an activity metric.

## 2. Bottleneck Diagnosis — What You Do Before Adding a Tool

```
Step 1. Draw the flow
   intake → draft → review → revision → approval → release/send → close
   (only the stages that actually exist. the observed procedure, not the documented one)

Step 2. Record two timestamps per stage
   T_in  : when the item entered the stage
   T_out : when the item left the stage
   touch time = time somebody was actually working on it
   wait time  = (T_out - T_in) - touch time

Step 3. Find the stage with the largest wait time
   In most flows, wait time dwarfs touch time.
   ★ The bottleneck is not the longest task. It is the longest queue.

Step 4. Confirm a queue is forming in front of that stage
   If the backlog keeps growing there, that stage is the bottleneck.
   If the backlog sits near zero, that stage is not the bottleneck.

Step 5. Confirm that speeding up a non-bottleneck changes nothing
   Speed up an upstream stage and you simply lengthen the queue in front of the bottleneck.
```

**The arithmetic behind Step 3** — work in progress = throughput multiplied by time in system. Throughput is set by the bottleneck. So when only upstream gets faster, time in system does not fall; **the pile of waiting work grows instead.**

## 3. The Ceiling on Total Savings When Only Part of the Flow Gets Faster

Set total elapsed time to 1. If a stretch worth p of the total gets s times faster, then
**total reduction = p × (1 − 1/s).** The table below is that calculation — a ceiling, not a forecast.

| Share p of the stretch that got faster | 2x faster | 5x faster | that stretch drops to zero |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10% | 5% | 8% | 10% |
| 20% | 10% | 16% | 20% |
| 30% | 15% | 24% | 30% |
| 40% | 20% | 32% | 40% |
| 50% | 25% | 40% | 50% |
| 60% | 30% | 48% | 60% |
| 70% | 35% | 56% | 70% |
| 80% | 40% | 64% | 80% |

- The last column is the **absolute ceiling**. Delete that stretch entirely and you still cannot beat it.
- So **measure p first.** Without p you do not know the ceiling of any improvement you are about to buy.
- These figures are **before tool cost**. Subtract learning, switching and upkeep and they get smaller.

|---|---|
| 3 | 3 |
| 4 | 6 |
| 5 | 10 |

## 5. What Actually Attaches to Each Additional Tool

| Cost item | When it lands | Who absorbs it | Does a metric catch it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Learning | At rollout, and again at every new hire | The individual | Usually not |
| Switching (context moves) | All day, in small pieces | The individual | No |
| Upkeep (access, versions, billing) | Continuously | The administrator | Only in the budget |
| Integration (moving work between tools) | Every time the flow crosses a tool | The individual | No |
| Scattered source of truth | The moment the same fact lives in two places | Everyone | Only when something breaks |

With n tools there are up to n(n−1)/2 boundaries to carry work across. **Adding tools creates a new category of work: the work between the tools.**

## 6. What Genuinely Shrank and What Did Not — The Dividing Test

| Test | Shrinks | Does not shrink |
|---|---|---|
| Repetition | The same task recurs | Every instance differs |
| Rules | The rule can be written down | Contextual judgment required |
| Verification cost | Correctness is cheap and instant to check | Checking costs as much as doing |
| Failure cost | Easy to undo when wrong | Wrong means external trust damage |
| Accountability | No signature needed on the result | A person must sign |
| Input | Input arrives in a fixed shape | Input must be extracted first |

**One-line test.** If verification is cheaper than the original task, the tool saves time. **If verification costs as much as the task, the work does not shrink — it relocates to review.**

## 7. Before-and-After Study Design — Checklist

- [ ] The **baseline was captured before rollout** (not reconstructed from memory afterwards)
- [ ] Baseline window and observation window are the **same length**
- [ ] No **peak season, holiday block, deadline or reorg** straddles the two windows
- [ ] **Headcount changes** during the period are recorded (they move output on their own)
- [ ] The two periods contain the **same kind of work** (the difficulty mix did not shift)
- [ ] Metrics are read as **median and upper percentile**, never as a bare average
- [ ] **Rework and rejection rates** are read alongside speed (catches faster-but-worse)
- [ ] **Both self-reported time and observed time** are recorded (the gap between them is the size of the mirage)
- [ ] Tool **learning, upkeep and integration time are counted as cost**
- [ ] Success criteria were **written as numbers before rollout**, not chosen afterwards
- [ ] There is a **comparison group or a pre-rollout trend line**
- [ ] A **rollback condition** was agreed before results came in

## 8. Six Questions to Ask in the Meeting

1. Is the metric we report an **activity or an outcome**?
2. Which stage in this flow has the **longest wait time**? Has anyone measured it?
3. What **share of total elapsed time** did the faster stretch represent (p)? So what is the ceiling?
4. Can anyone **name the manual steps** this tool removed?
5. When upstream speeds up, **who receives the work pushed downstream**? Is that queue measured?
6. **When was the baseline taken** — before rollout or after?

## 9. Do Not

- ❌ Add tools before measuring the bottleneck — you buy an improvement with an unknown ceiling
- ❌ Adopt an activity metric because it is easy to count — that activity is all that will grow
- ❌ Submit perceived speed as evidence of output — it measures a different thing
- ❌ Reconstruct a baseline from memory afterwards — the comparison does not exist
- ❌ Report upstream savings as time saved overall — subtract the share that moved downstream
- ❌ Blame flat results on individual skill — the bottleneck sits in the structure
- ❌ Transplant a single study result onto your organization — the sample and conditions differ
