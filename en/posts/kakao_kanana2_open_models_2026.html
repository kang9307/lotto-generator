Intro: what you can download and what you can use are different

If you are shopping for a small model to run in-house, the shortlist just got one entry longer. On July 28, 2026, Kakao pushed the weights for four lightweight Kanana-2 models to Hugging Face. A 3B and a 1.3B, each in base and instruct form. The files are downloadable right now.

The first thing to read, though, is not the spec sheet. It is Section 4 of the license. Building and operating your own service needs no separate agreement. The moment you sell to a third party, the picture changes. Selling access through an API, delivering the model as part of a systems-integration engagement, or shipping it inside a device you sell all require a separate commercial license, and the contract names each case explicitly. The very deployment that makes someone want a small model in the first place, putting it on a device, is written into the carve-out by name.

That is not a flaw. It is a condition. And when you check that condition is what actually moves your timeline. Reading Section 4 before you download, and deciding whether your company sits inside or outside Section 4.2, is far cheaper than finishing a performance evaluation and then getting stopped by legal.

This article does not rank Kanana-2 against other models by score. It covers what was actually released, the line the license draws, what the model-card numbers do and do not say, and which organizations should evaluate now versus wait. If you need per-model pricing and context comparisons, those live in the comprehensive AI model comparison. The phenomenon of a small model out-reasoning a much larger one is a separate subject, covered in when a small model beats a big one at reasoning.

1. What shipped - the actual specs of all four

Four repositories. The names are close enough to confuse, so here they are side by side.

RepositoryParametersTypeHow it was built
kanana-2-3b-base~3BPretrained basePretrained from scratch on TPU clusters
kanana-2-3b-instruct~3BInstruction tunedSupervised fine-tuning plus reinforcement learning
kanana-2-1.3b-base~1.3BPretrained baseThe 3B compressed via cascade pruning and distillation
kanana-2-1.3b-instruct~1.3BInstruction tunedPost-training applied to the base above

What all four share:

  • 32,768 token context — natively supported, per the card
  • Three-to-one attention layout — three sliding-window layers to one full-attention layer, with a window size of 1024
  • Roughly 72.7 percent KV cache reduction — the figure the card gives at 32K length
  • 30 percent better Korean tokenization — versus the previous generation, in the card's phrasing
  • No Kakao user data — the card states none was used in either pre-training or post-training
  • One license — the Kanana Open License Agreement across all four

1.1 Two naming traps

The first is 0.9B. The card mentions a 0.9B as part of the family, but it is not in this release. Secondary coverage mixes it in, so be clear that what you can download today is the 3B and the 1.3B.

The second one bites harder. A separate 30B-A3B line also carries the Kanana-2 name in its own repositories. That is a mixture-of-experts design with a reduced active parameter count, and it is a completely different animal in size and purpose from the four covered here. Search results mix the two, so confirm that the repository name contains 1.3b or 3b before you pull anything.

1.2 What a 72.7 percent KV cache reduction means in practice

That number comes from architecture, not from a benchmark. Full attention has to keep every prior token in cache. A sliding-window layer looks at only the most recent 1024 tokens. When three out of every four layers work that way, memory falls sharply on long inputs.

The reduction figure itself is not the point. The point is that serving cost is usually set by concurrent sessions. How many sessions fit on one GPU is your unit price, and the KV cache is what caps that number. The longer your documents, the more this architecture works in your favor. Actual savings will shift with quantization scheme, batch size and the serving runtime, so treat the card's figure as one measured point with stated conditions rather than a number you will reproduce everywhere.

2. The license - where free ends and negotiation begins

The weights come under the Kanana Open License Agreement, dated July 17, 2025 in the document itself. It settles three things: what you may do, what you must attach when redistributing, and where a separate negotiation begins.

2.1 What you may do

  • Use, reproduce, distribute — Section 2.1 grants a non-exclusive, worldwide, non-transferable, royalty-free limited license
  • Create derivative works and modify — stated in the same clause, which means fine-tuning is permitted
  • Develop and operate your own services — Section 4.2. Unless you fall under 4.1 below, no separate commercial license is required
  • Train or improve other models on the outputs — allowed, but the resulting model name must carry Kanana as a prefix

2.2 The three cases that require a separate commercial license

Section 4.1 sets these aside. The common element is offering or reselling to third parties.

  1. Offering or reselling third parties access to the materials or any derivative works through APIs, cloud platforms or other remote access services
  2. Offering or reselling them to third parties, in whole or in part, as part of a system integration or on-premise deployment solution
  3. Offering or reselling them to third parties embedded in on-device domains

The third one tells you the most about the character of this release. The reason to want a small model is to run it on a device, and if that device is a product you sell, you are inside the carve-out. Running the model in your own app for your own service and shipping a product with the model inside it are separate acts under this contract.

The boundary is not always crisp. If you build your own service and that service exposes an API your customers call, is that operating your own service or reselling model access? The text alone does not settle every configuration. If your shape looks like that, start the conversation before the commercial structure hardens. Negotiation time is a real variable in an adoption schedule, and it gets more expensive the later you leave it.

2.3 What you must attach when redistributing

Section 3.1 sets the conditions. They apply whether you redistribute the weights as-is or a fine-tuned derivative.

  • Carry the same compliance obligations into your agreements with recipients
  • Provide a copy of the agreement
  • Mark modified files as changed
  • Include the copyright notice — Kanana is licensed in accordance with the Kanana Open License Agreement. Copyright (c) KAKAO Corp. All Rights Reserved.
  • Prominently display Powered by Kanana on websites, interfaces and documentation
  • Prefix any AI model you distribute that was built on these materials or their outputs with Kanana

The attribution requirement lands directly on consumer-facing UI design. If your configuration counts as redistribution, reserve the space during design review rather than bolting a badge on at the end.

Sections 6 and 7 read as expected. The materials are provided as is with warranties disclaimed, and liability for damages is excluded unless the law requires otherwise. Standard for open weights, but it also means those sentences go verbatim into your internal risk document.

2.4 So is this open source?

Coverage and announcements use the phrase open source. More precisely, this is open weights with a field-of-use restriction. Clause 6 of the Open Source Definition states that a license must not restrict anyone from using the program in a specific field of endeavor, and clause 1 requires that redistribution not be restricted. Section 4.1 touches both.

There is no need to read this as a gotcha. For most corporate users, Section 4.2 is enough. But if your internal policy or procurement standard contains a line permitting only open source licenses, this model does not clear that line as written. That check is document work rather than engineering work, and it takes an afternoon. Why open weights and open source are not synonyms is worked through in the state of open source AI in 2026.

3.1 Read the pattern, not the total

Scanning the columns vertically gets you nowhere, because wins and losses alternate. Group the rows by category and a shape appears.

  • Instruction following is strong. IFEval at 80.96 against 66.91 and 68.39 is not a margin-of-error gap. It means fewer failures when you specify a format and ask for output in it
  • Code leads too. Instruct MBPP at 70.63 and base HumanEval at 55.96, both above the comparison set
  • Math trails. Base GSM8K 61.03 against 75.74, MATH-500 61.20 against 72.00. The gap is just as clear, running the other way
  • Korean knowledge leads. KMMLU 47.92 against 43.08
  • Long context is comparable or slightly behind. RULER-32K 67.50 against 69.01. Supporting 32K and performing well at 32K are different claims, so measure this yourself if long inputs are central

That shape almost decides the workload. Extraction, classification and summarization under a fixed format fit well. Work where solving equations is the core does not. A pipeline that pulls fields out of documents into JSON is a good match. Verifying financial calculations is not.

Attach this whenever you quote the numbers — every figure above comes from the publisher's own model card, not from independent reproduction, and the comparison set was chosen by the same card. When these move into an internal report, the source and that qualifier travel with them. The adoption decision still comes down to running 100 of your own records through each candidate.

4. The three places small models actually pay off

A 3B model is not a shrunken version of a large one. It is a different tool, and the places it earns its keep are specific.

4.1 On-device

The network round trip disappears and the data never leaves the handset. It behaves the same on a plane and in an underground garage. For a feature that touches personal data, the fact that nothing was sent to a server is itself the architectural answer.

Section 4.1 (iii) comes back here. Running it inside your own app for your own service is one thing; selling a device with the model inside it is another. The first sits in Section 4.2 and the second requires negotiation. Saying on-device as a single word erases that distinction.

4.2 Corporate and air-gapped networks

If the data cannot leave, an external API is not a worse option, it is not an option. In that setting your baseline is not a large model but whatever a person is doing by hand today. A different baseline implies a different bar for good enough.

The scale sense changes too. A 3B gives real throughput on a single GPU, and depending on configuration a 1.3B brings CPU inference into view. The things that actually eat performance when running locally are collected in why local LLMs run slow. For most teams, downloading weights is the easy part and standing up serving is the hard part.

4.3 High-volume work where unit cost dominates

Classification, tagging, normalization, first-pass filtering. Simple per-item judgments across millions of items. In these workloads price per call outweighs a few points of accuracy by a wide margin. A setup where the small model filters first and only flagged items escalate to a large one routinely cuts total spend by a single-digit multiple.

Choosing a model by latency is a separate axis, split apart in where the faster model wins. The short version is that volume goes to the small model and only hard cases get promoted, and that routing is what survives contact with production.

4.4 Where it does not fit

  • Frontier reasoning — problems that require constructing several steps unaided. The math rows above are the signal
  • Long agent chains — small errors multiply per step. Ten steps turns 95 percent into roughly 60
  • Rare domain knowledge — fewer parameters hold less. Bolting on retrieval is the right fix
  • Broad multilingual coverage — language breadth is where larger models keep the advantage

5. Reasons to consider a domestic model, and reasons not to

Both sides get equal weight here. Reading only one of them does not produce a decision.

5.1 Reasons to consider it

  • Tokenizer efficiency. When the same Korean document becomes fewer tokens, context headroom, throughput and cost all improve together. The card claims a 30 percent improvement over the previous generation. This is felt in production more often than a benchmark score is
  • Korean knowledge benchmarks come out ahead. The KMMLU figure sits above the comparison set
  • Local conventions and document formats. Administrative forms, business registration notation, address systems and similar material barely register in public benchmarks. There may be an edge here, but confirming it requires your own data
  • The contracting counterparty is domestic. For any company caught by Section 4.1 this is not a nice-to-have. Whether a negotiation can realistically proceed decides whether adoption is possible at all

5.2 Reasons not to

  • Global small models handle Korean. Walking in expecting the gap of a few years ago leads to disappointment at measurement time
  • Ecosystem thickness. Quantized builds, serving runtime support, fine-tuning recipes, accumulated issue threads. This governs how fast you ship. Whether somebody already hit your problem is the difference between an afternoon and a week
  • Simpler licenses exist. If legal review time is a real cost in your organization, this axis can outrank performance
  • Update cadence is unproven. Good today is worth less if there is no successor in six months and you pay the migration cost again

Here is the dividing line. If Korean share is high and format compliance is critical, put it on the shortlist. If the work is English-centric or math and reasoning sit at the core, rank it lower. Either way, put a measurement between shortlisting and deciding.

This list is not specific to Kanana-2. These are the items teams adopting open weights for the first time tend to skip.

Filling that table in for Kanana-2 gives: bespoke agreement; field-of-use limits present in the three cases of Section 4.1; fine-tuning permitted; notice and attribution duties on redistribution; derivative models prefixed with Kanana; outputs usable for training other models subject to the same naming duty; as-is with liability excluded.

Order matters — this check belongs before the adoption decision, not after the proof of concept. Reverse the order and you confirm everything works and then hear that you cannot use it. By then sunk cost is already distorting the judgment.

6.1 What else to read on a model card

  • How much is said about training data. This card explicitly states no Kakao user data was used. It does not describe corpus composition
  • Evaluation conditions. Shot counts and metrics have to be there for reproduction to be possible. This card has them
  • Choice of comparison set. Who was included and who was left out tells you about the card's posture
  • Safety and limitations. Whether there is a dedicated limitations section, and what it concedes

7. Which axes to compare against global small models

Building a scoreboard against Qwen, Gemma and Llama families is not worth doing. Evaluation conditions differ card to card, and copying someone else's table leaves the conditions behind. Pick the axes and measure on your own workload instead.

The cheapest axis to measure is token efficiency. Run 100 of your own documents through each candidate tokenizer, count tokens, and you are done in half a day with a result that converts directly into a cost difference. A token counter is enough for a rough read, and dropping the difference into an LLM API price calculator turns it into a monthly figure. 8. Who should evaluate now and who should wait

8.1 Evaluate now

  • You process Korean documents in bulk and large-API unit cost hurts. If classification, summarization and extraction are the main work, the benchmark pattern above lines up well
  • Your data cannot leave, so an external API was never available. Here this is not an alternative, it is the only path
  • Everything stays inside your own service. That is Section 4.2 territory, so you can start without a negotiation
  • You have already run serving infrastructure. The real cost of open weights is operations, not weights. With that experience, evaluation takes days
  • Format compliance decides success in your pipeline. Emitting a fixed schema falls squarely here

8.2 Wait

  • You sell a product with the model inside. Evaluate, but do not commit a launch date before the negotiation lands. Section 4.1 (iii) hits this head on
  • Reasoning and math are the core of the work. The tables already answer this
  • You have nobody to run serving. Renting a GPU costs less than hiring the person who keeps it running
  • Your service is English-centric or broadly multilingual. Half the reason to pick a domestic model disappears
  • Procurement permits only open source licenses. Check the document before the engineering, and it is over in an afternoon

With this class of model, the longest distance is between it is free so let us download it and we are adopting it. Downloading takes five minutes. Getting to an operable state takes weeks. Whether you can absorb those weeks is what the decision actually is.

9. What could not be confirmed

The following were left out of the body as figures or assertions because a primary source could not be pinned down.

  • Direct comparisons against Gemma or Llama families. The card's comparison set is the Qwen family. No cross-family table exists on the card
  • Third-party reproduction. Every figure here is publisher-reported, and independent reproduction was not located
  • The 0.9B release timeline. The family mentions it; the timing is not stated
  • Real on-device measurements. No published figures for tokens per second, memory footprint or thermal behavior on a phone
  • Commercial license terms, pricing and turnaround. What is negotiated, at what price, over what period is not public
  • Training corpus composition. The absence of Kakao user data is stated; the rest of the composition is not on the card
  • The basis for the 30 percent Korean tokenization gain. Which corpus, against what, was not confirmed
  • Conditions behind the 72.7 percent KV cache reduction. The value shifts with batch size and quantization, so it was not generalized

Conclusion: read the clause first, then count tokens

What a domestic company open-weighting a small model changes for practitioners is not the number of options but the order of review. It used to be measure performance, then weigh cost. Now it is read the license, then measure performance. Some business shapes are eliminated outright, and that verdict takes far less time than a performance evaluation.

The four lightweight Kanana-2 models lead the comparison set on instruction following and code, trail on math, lead on Korean knowledge and land comparably on long context. There are workloads that fit that shape and workloads that do not. If yours fits, it is worth evaluating now. If you sell a product with the model inside, the negotiation comes first.

Two things you can do today. Send Section 4 to legal and get a one-line answer on whether your business shape sits inside or outside Section 4.2, because that answer is the premise of everything after it. Then push 100 of your own documents through the tokenizer and count. That number predicts your monthly bill more accurately than any benchmark score.
