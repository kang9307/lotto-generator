The Complete Water Purifier Buying Guide: RO vs UF, Rental vs Purchase

From reverse osmosis and hollow-fiber filtration through tankless vs tank designs, filter replacement cost, installation, and hygiene - a practical checklist to work through before you buy

Jul 24, 2026 Life & Practical

Introduction: What to Check Before the Filtration Method

Water purifier showrooms and product pages throw around unfamiliar terms - reverse osmosis, ultrafiltration, tankless, under-sink. Most buyers end up deciding based on a door-to-door sales pitch or brand recognition, and in the process miss whether the method actually fits their building's plumbing, or what it will really cost over five years. A water purifier is a multi-year appliance that also requires ongoing filter replacement and hygiene upkeep, so setting a clear standard before you buy pays off for years afterward.

This guide, current as of July 2026, walks through what to check in order: the difference between reverse osmosis (RO), ultrafiltration (UF), and nano/carbon filtration; hygiene and size differences between tankless and tank-type designs; form factors like hot-cold, ice, and under-sink units; filter types, replacement cycles, and cost of ownership (including a rental vs. purchase comparison); installation and self-maintained units; tank hygiene management; how to judge whether you actually need one over tap water; and finally, selection criteria and a checklist by household and purpose. It does not recommend any specific brand or model - the goal is a set of standards you can apply to any product you're considering.

1. Filtration Methods Compared: RO vs UF vs Nano/Carbon

1.1 Reverse Osmosis (RO)

Reverse osmosis forces water under pressure through a semi-permeable membrane with pores around 0.0001 microns, letting through essentially only water molecules. It removes 95-99% of heavy metals, dissolved salts, TDS (total dissolved solids), and pesticide residue - the widest removal range of any method - which makes it well suited to old plumbing, well water, or areas with high TDS. The trade-offs: the process produces wastewater (concentrate), it needs an electric pump, and it strips out some minerals along with contaminants, which is the source of the "dead water" debate around RO. Older units produced 3-4 liters of wastewater per liter of purified water; newer high-efficiency models have narrowed that closer to 1:1.

1.2 Ultrafiltration (UF)

Ultrafiltration passes water through a hollow-fiber membrane with pores around 0.01-0.1 microns. It effectively removes bacteria and turbidity-causing particles while keeping minerals intact, and basic models run without electricity and produce no wastewater. The catch is that it can't remove dissolved substances like salts or heavy metals, so it's best suited to places where the source water - typically municipal tap water - is already reasonably good. Replacement filters cost less than RO, and flow rate tends to be faster.

1.3 Nano and Carbon Filters

Nano filtration is often marketed as a middle ground between RO and UF pore sizes. Activated carbon, separate from the filtration method itself, handles chlorine, odor, and taste improvement as a pre- or post-treatment stage. Pre-carbon and post-carbon stages are commonly paired with either RO or UF, and it's worth being clear that carbon alone, without a membrane stage, cannot function as a standalone method for removing bacteria or heavy metals.

2. Tankless vs Tank-Type: Hygiene, Size, and Output

2.1 Tankless Design

A tankless unit has no storage tank - the moment you press the button, tap water passes through the filters and comes out instantly. With no tank, there's structurally no standing water for bacteria to grow in, making hygiene management the easiest of any design. Power draw is lower than tank-type units, and the compact body takes up less space. The trade-off is that instant heating/cooling can make hot-water temperature less stable than a tank-type unit, and the amount of water you can draw at once is more limited.

2.2 Tank-Type Design

A tank-type unit stores a supply of purified water and dispenses it on demand. It can deliver larger continuous volumes and holds a more stable hot-water temperature, but the very fact that water sits in a tank creates conditions for bacterial growth, making regular sterilization essential. In low-usage households, water can sit in the tank for extended periods, which calls for extra attention to hygiene.

3. Choosing a Form Factor: Hot-Cold, Ice, and Under-Sink Units

3.1 Hot-Cold Units

The most common form factor, combining plain, cold, and hot water in one unit. Each added function generally pushes the rental fee up a little, and power draw rises compared with a purified-water-only unit.

3.2 Ice Units

Adds ice-making to the hot-cold functions, storing ice in an internal bin that you dispense as needed. This tier carries the highest purchase price and rental fee, and the ice bin adds an extra hygiene task.

3.3 Under-Sink Units

The main body sits inside the cabinet below the sink, with only the spout exposed above the counter. This makes the best use of kitchen space, but comes with installation constraints, and ice functionality is generally unavailable. Reported power draw generally ranks tank-type highest, then ice units, then hot-cold units, then cold-only units, then tankless (purified-water-only) units lowest - worth factoring in alongside the form factor you choose.

3.4 Countertop Units

The standard form that sits directly on the counter, simple to install and relatively easy to relocate. The trade-off is that it takes up counter space you might want for food prep.

4. Filter Types, Replacement Cycles, and Cost: Rental vs Purchase Totals

4.1 Filter Types and Replacement Cycles

Water usually passes through several filter stages in sequence, and each stage has a different role and replacement interval. As a general guideline, the sediment (pre-treatment) filter lasts 3-6 months, the pre-carbon filter about 6 months, the UF membrane 12-20 months, the RO membrane typically around 2 years, and the post-carbon filter about 12 months. These are averages for typical usage - shorten the interval if usage is heavy or source water quality is poor.

4.2 Rental vs Purchase: Total Cost

Renting typically requires no upfront cost, or a modest installation fee (roughly $40-110), plus a fixed monthly payment - with filter changes and inspections usually bundled into the fee, so ongoing management is light. Hot-cold and ice tiers push the monthly fee into the $30-40 range, and a mandatory contract term of 3-5 years is typical, so a simple five-year total often lands in the $2,200-3,000 range. Buying, by contrast, runs roughly $220-450 for a tankless unit and $600-1,100 for an ice unit up front, with ongoing filter and electricity cost adding roughly $8 or so per month afterward - which often brings the five-year total below the rental figure. That said, contract terms, partner discounts, and whether free maintenance is included can flip the comparison, so check current quotes from specific brands directly. Contract details that are easy to overlook - mandatory term, early-termination penalties, and ownership transfer conditions - are covered in more depth in our companion piece, Water Purifier Rental vs Purchase (Korean).

4.3 Selection Summary

If the upfront cost is a burden, or you'd rather leave filter changes and inspections to a technician, renting tends to make more sense. If you'll use it for five years or more and self-maintenance isn't a hassle, buying and maintaining it yourself is often the cheaper total-cost path.

5. Installation: Plumbing Connection, Electricity, Space, and Self-Maintained Units

5.1 Installation Types

Installation broadly splits into under-cabinet connections (below the sink) and wall-tap connections. Check the location of the plumbing you'll connect to and whether a power outlet is available beforehand, and for an under-sink unit, measure the cabinet's interior dimensions in advance since installation space is constrained. Most tankless and tank-type units need electricity, though some purified-water-only UF models run without a power source.

5.2 Self-Maintained Units

Self-maintained units, where you buy and replace filters yourself, carry no inspection-visit cost, keeping upkeep cheap. Easy-to-swap filters like sediment, pre-carbon, and post-carbon are reasonable to handle yourself, but for structurally more complex stages like the RO or UF membrane, read the manual carefully or pair self-maintenance with periodic professional inspection.

6. Hygiene: Tank Bacteria, the Spout, and Sterilization Cycles

6.1 Tank Bacteria

Because water sits in a tank-type unit, scale buildup and conditions favorable to bacterial growth can develop. This calls for extra care during high temperature and humidity periods, like summer.

6.2 Spout Maintenance

The spout, where water ultimately comes out, is touched by hand and exposed to open air, so it needs separate cleaning. Simply wiping down the spout surface regularly meaningfully reduces contamination risk.

6.3 Sterilization Features and Cycles

Even a single sterilization feature - UV, electrolyzed water, or high-temperature sterilization - can reportedly cut bacterial levels by more than 97%. Check whether the unit has an automatic sterilization cycle and whether sterilization is included in scheduled visits before you buy; if it's self-maintained, stick to the manufacturer's recommended self-sterilization interval.

7. Tap Water vs a Water Purifier: Judging by Your Local Water Quality

Tap water undergoes regular testing against roughly 60 items - including E. coli and heavy metals - under official drinking water standards, and authorities maintain that water meeting those standards poses no safety concern. Even so, the share of people who drink tap water directly is quite low, around 5%, and the reasons cited include concerns about aging pipes or building storage tanks, plus chlorine odor. In other words, the legal safety of tap water itself is a separate question from whether it's actually pleasant to drink after passing through your specific building's plumbing and tank. When judging whether you need a purifier, it's reasonable to weigh (1) the age of your building's plumbing and how well its storage tank is maintained, (2) how noticeable chlorine odor or water hardness feels locally, and (3) whether anyone in the household is sensitive to taste or smell. Conversely, if you're in a newer building with direct plumbing and don't notice meaningful odor or taste issues, a lighter option like a filtered showerhead or pitcher filter may be enough.

8. Selection Criteria by Household and Purpose, Plus a Checklist

8.1 Choosing by Household Type

For a single person or a short-term rental situation, a small tankless unit you can relocate easily, or a short-term rental plan with no long commitment, works best. Couples and two-person households that prioritize convenience may prefer renting; if long-term residence is certain, buying wins on total cost. Larger families with kids or frequent hot-water use benefit from a tank-type hot-cold unit that delivers stable hot water and higher volume. Offices and small shops with multiple users, and no one to handle maintenance, tend to find a rental plan with included visits more convenient.

8.2 Final Checklist Before You Buy

Working through these in order keeps you anchored to what matters instead of getting swayed by a sales pitch.

  • Have you checked your building's pipe age and local water quality (hardness, chlorine odor)?
  • Does the filtration method (RO/UF) match your situation (old plumbing, well water, or not)?
  • Have you chosen tankless or tank-type based on how much hygiene upkeep you can realistically manage?
  • Have you checked each filter's replacement cycle, genuine filter price, and estimated annual cost?
  • Have you calculated both the five-year rental total and the five-year purchase-plus-self-maintenance total?
  • Have you confirmed installation space (under-sink or not) and the location of plumbing and power?
  • Does the unit have an automatic sterilization feature (UV, electrolyzed water, or high-temperature)?
  • If renting, have you confirmed the mandatory term, early-termination penalty, and ownership transfer terms in the contract?
  • Have you matched the form factor to your household size and how often you'll use hot water or ice?

9. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. Should I choose an RO or a UF water purifier?

RO suits old plumbing or well-water situations thanks to its wider removal range; UF suits standard municipal water supply where retaining minerals and easier upkeep matter more.

Q2. Is renting or buying more economical?

Renting suits short-term use or a preference for hands-off maintenance; buying and self-maintaining is often cheaper in total cost for five-plus years of use.

Q3. How often do filters need replacing?

Sediment and pre-carbon: 3-6 months. UF membrane: 12-20 months. RO membrane: typically around 2 years. Post-carbon: about 12 months.

Q4. How do I maintain hygiene on a tank-type unit?

Even one sterilization feature (UV, electrolyzed water, or high-temperature) can meaningfully cut bacterial levels. Confirm the feature and its automatic cycle, and clean the spout separately.

Q5. Is tap water safe to drink directly, or do I need a purifier?

Tap water meeting official standards is considered legally safe, but aging pipes and storage tanks can leave odor or debris concerns. Decide after checking your own building's plumbing condition.

Conclusion: The Fit With Your Situation Matters More Than Any Single Method

A water purifier can't be judged on RO versus UF alone. It becomes a choice you're happy with for years only once you've matched the filtration method to your plumbing and water quality, picked a tankless or tank-type design that fits how much upkeep you can manage, worked out the five-year rental versus purchase total, and committed to regular hygiene maintenance. The surest way to apply all of that is to use the comparison tables and checklist in this guide to check off an actual sales pitch or product spec sheet, item by item.

Specific prices, rental terms, and filter replacement costs vary by timing and brand, so check current official materials and multiple quotes before you buy or sign a contract.
