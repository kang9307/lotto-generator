Introduction: the foundation line of every retirement plan

Every Korean payslip has one line that never disappears: the National Pension deduction. A meaningful sum leaves your account each month, yet few people know exactly where it goes or how it comes back. Some distrust it, saying "you never get back what you pay." Others call it "the sturdiest pillar of old age" and even consider topping it up. The reason opinions diverge so sharply is that the National Pension is not a simple savings plan but a public pension paid for life based on your income and insured period, and surprisingly few people understand that structure all the way through.

This article walks through Korea's National Pension from start to finish, as an informational guide. It covers what the National Pension is, who must enrol, how contributions are calculated, when and how much you receive, what determines your benefit amount, and detailed schemes such as early and deferred pensions, retroactive payment and refund repayment, and survivor and disability pensions. The focus is the Korean system, but the concept of a public pension is common to many countries, so it should help anyone grasp the big picture. This matters because the amended National Pension Act, passed by the National Assembly on 20 March 2025, took effect on 1 January 2026, changing the contribution rate, the income replacement rate and the credits at once, so read every figure here together with the date it refers to.

This article was first written in June 2026 and updated on 29 September 2026 after re-checking the figures against National Pension Service and Ministry of Health and Welfare guidance. What changed is collected in the update history below.

What the National Pension is

The National Pension is a public pension run by the state. While you work, you pay part of your income as contributions; when age makes earning difficult, you receive a monthly pension based on the rights you have built up. The key word is "social insurance." It is not a savings account that hands back exactly what you paid in, but a system built on solidarity across generations and members to protect against risks such as old age, disability and death.

The purpose: securing retirement income

The central purpose is securing retirement income. As life expectancy rises, the years after retirement keep growing, and few individuals can fund that long stretch entirely on their own. The National Pension is paid for life and is reported to be adjusted each year in line with prices, so it offers some protection against inflation eroding the value of money. These two features, "for life" and "price-linked," are often cited as strengths that private products struggle to match.

The first layer of the three-layer pension

Retirement planning is often pictured as a "three-layer pension." Layer one is the state-guaranteed National Pension, layer two is the retirement pension set aside by employers, and layer three is the private pension you prepare yourself (pension savings, IRP, and so on). The National Pension is the most basic first layer, the starting point of any retirement design. Since it alone rarely guarantees a comfortable retirement, it is important to view it together with layers two and three.

In one line: the National Pension is not "a savings plan that returns what you paid" but "a public pension paid for life based on income and insured period." It is best understood as the first-layer foundation of retirement.

Who enrols - enrollment types

The National Pension makes enrollment mandatory in principle for a certain age range, with the type depending on the kind of income activity.

Workplace and individually insured persons

As a widely cited rule, residents of Korea aged 18 to under 60 are in principle subject to enrollment. Employees become workplace insured persons and split the contribution evenly with their employer. Those with income who are not workplace insured persons (the self-employed, freelancers, and so on) become individually insured persons and pay the full contribution themselves.

Voluntary and voluntary continued enrollment

People without income who are not required to enrol may still join if they wish. This is voluntary enrollment, available to those aged 18 to under 60 who are not workplace or individually insured, such as homemakers or students. Meanwhile, those who have reached 60 but lack enough insured time to receive a pension, or who want to extend their insured period for a larger pension, can use voluntary continued enrollment to keep paying until age 65. It is widely used by people reinforcing their retirement preparation.

How much you pay

Your contribution is set by multiplying "how much you earn" (standard monthly income) by "what percentage is taken" (the contribution rate). These two are the heart of the contribution.

Standard monthly income - ceiling and floor

The income used to assess contributions is called standard monthly income, and it has a ceiling and a floor. Very high earnings are recognised only up to the ceiling, and very low earnings only up to the floor. According to the National Pension Service, the ceiling and floor reflect the rate of change in the three-year average of all members' average monthly income; the Minister of Health and Welfare announces them by the end of March each year, and they apply for one year from July. From July 2026 the ceiling is 6.59 million won and the floor is 410,000 won.

Contribution rate - 9.5 percent in 2026, 13 percent by 2033

The contribution rate was long held at 9 percent of income, through 2025. Under the amended National Pension Act passed by the National Assembly plenary session on 20 March 2025, it rises by 0.5 percentage point a year for eight years from 2026, and 13 percent applies from 2033. The rate in 2026 is therefore 9.5 percent. Workplace insured persons split this rate evenly with their employer, while individually, voluntarily and voluntarily continued insured persons pay it all.

Wider contribution support for low-income individually insured persons

Support for low-income individually insured persons also widened from 2026. According to the Ministry of Health and Welfare press release of 29 December 2025, support of 50 percent of the contribution for 12 months used to go only to individually insured persons who resumed paying after a gap due to unemployment, leave and similar reasons; from 2026, any individually insured person with monthly income under 800,000 won is eligible, whether or not they are resuming payment. The ministry said those covered can receive up to 37,950 won a month. Check your eligibility and how to apply with the National Pension Service (1355).

The contribution rate changes every year until 2033, and the standard-monthly-income limits change every July. Figures here such as 9.5 percent, 6.59 million won and 410,000 won are as of September 2026, so check your own contribution with the National Pension Service (1355) or the "My Pension" service.

When you receive it - eligibility and benefit age

Paying contributions does not let just anyone receive a pension at any time. You must meet certain conditions, and the age at which payments begin is fixed.

Minimum insured period - 10 years

To receive the old-age pension you must be insured for a minimum period. According to the National Pension Service, you need at least 10 years (120 months) of insured time. If you reach the benefit age without meeting it, you may instead receive your contributions plus interest as a lump-sum refund. For this reason, people just short of 10 years often use voluntary continued enrollment to secure pension entitlement.

Benefit age - 60 to 65 by birth year

The age at which the old-age pension begins has been raised in stages from 60 to 65 depending on birth year. According to the National Pension Service, those born in 1969 or later receive it from age 65. Those born earlier fall into bands such as 61 to 64 by birth year. Exactly when you start depends on your birth year, so it is best to confirm your own standard with the National Pension Service.

How the benefit amount is determined

The question everyone really wants answered is "how much will I get?" The old-age pension amount is not simply "the sum of what you paid," but is set by a formula that reflects the average income of all members together with your own income and insured period.

Two axes - income and insured period

The two axes that decide the amount are your average income while insured and your insured period. Notably, the formula also includes the average income of all members (often called the "A value"). Because of this, lower earners receive a higher proportion relative to what they paid, a redistribution effect. Unlike a savings plan that favours high earners, the National Pension is relatively more generous to lower-income members.

Income replacement rate - 43 percent from 2026

The income replacement rate indicates what percentage of your average insured income you would receive based on 40 years of contributions. This ratio fell in stages after the system began and stood at 41.5 percent in 2025; under the amended act it was raised to 43 percent in one step from 2026 and fixed there (National Pension Service reform guide). The Ministry of Health and Welfare explained that because the replacement rate applies only to income in the years you pay contributions, the pensions of people already receiving benefits do not change, and the increase benefits current members who will keep paying. Keep in mind this assumes an ideal 40 years of contributions; most people are insured for less, so the rate they actually feel is often lower.

The core: the benefit is driven by two axes, "long" (insured period) and "steady" (income). That is why extending your insured period is so often cited as the surest way to raise your pension.

Early and deferred pensions

The benefit age is fixed, but depending on your circumstances you can receive it earlier or later. That choice, however, comes at a price.

Early old-age pension - sooner but reduced

If your circumstances warrant it, such as ending income activity early, you can receive the pension up to five years before the benefit age. This is the early old-age pension. In return the amount is reduced: according to the National Pension Service it falls by 6 percent for each year early, so claiming five years early pays 70 percent of the basic pension, four years 76 percent, three years 82 percent, two years 88 percent and one year 94 percent. The reduced amount applies for life, so weigh your immediate need against the lifetime total.

Deferred pension - later but increased

Conversely, if you do not need the pension yet and want more, you can postpone receipt by up to five years. This is the deferred pension. According to the National Pension Service it rises by 0.6 percent for each month deferred (7.2 percent per year), adding 36 percent if you defer the full five years. It can suit people who are healthy and have other income, allowing them to wait.

There is no single right answer between early and deferred. It depends on health, life expectancy, whether you have other income, and your immediate cash needs. Reduction and increase rates can also change over time, so confirm the latest standard before deciding.

Schemes to meet and extend your insured period

The National Pension rewards a longer insured period. Several schemes therefore exist to fill gaps or extend your time.

Retroactive payment - paying missed periods later

Retroactive payment lets you later pay contributions for periods you missed due to unemployment, business closure, or career breaks, and have them counted as insured time. Since a longer insured period raises the benefit, people use it to reinforce their retirement preparation. There are limits and conditions on which periods qualify and how you pay, so you cannot necessarily fill every past gap.

Refund repayment - returning money you took back

Refund repayment lets you repay, with interest, contributions you previously received as a lump-sum refund, restoring lost insured time. It is used by people who took a lump sum when leaving a job and later re-enrolled, to revive old periods and raise their benefit.

Voluntary continued enrollment - even past 60

Voluntary continued enrollment, seen earlier, lets you keep paying past 60 up to 65 to extend your insured period. It is used to secure entitlement for those short of 10 years, or to raise the benefit for those already eligible. Retroactive payment, refund repayment and voluntary continued enrollment each have different conditions and limits, so it is best to consult the National Pension Service for your situation.

Expanded childbirth and military service credits

The amended act that took effect on 1 January 2026 also widened the credits that add insured time. The childbirth credit used to start from the second child; it now grants 12 months from the first child and 18 months each from the third child, and the former 50-month cap has been abolished. The military service credit rose from a maximum of 6 months to a maximum of 12 months (Ministry of Health and Welfare and National Pension Service guidance). Details such as which births and which service periods the new rules cover should be confirmed for your own case with the National Pension Service.

Beyond old-age - survivor and disability pensions

The National Pension is not only the "old-age pension you receive with age." It also includes protection against the unexpected during your insured years.

Survivor pension

The survivor pension is paid to the bereaved family who depended on a member or pensioner when that person dies. It is paid according to a legal order of survivors such as spouse, children, and parents, and the amount varies with the deceased's insured period and other factors. It serves as a safety net against the sudden death of a family breadwinner.

Disability pension

The disability pension is paid according to the degree of disability when illness or injury during the insured period leaves a lasting impairment. If the old-age pension guards against "the risk of outliving your income," the disability and survivor pensions guard against "unexpected accident, illness or death." In this way the National Pension is comprehensive social insurance covering three risks at once, old age, disability and death, which is its biggest difference from simple savings.

Relationship to other retirement preparation

The National Pension is the first-layer foundation of retirement, but it is often not enough on its own. That is why it is important to design it together with the second and third layers.

Retirement pension (layer two)

The retirement pension (DB or DC type), in which employers set aside and manage employees' severance benefits, is the second layer. If the National Pension is the state-guaranteed base, the retirement pension is an added layer built through working life. People often move it into an IRP at retirement to manage it as retirement funds.

Private pension (layer three) - pension savings and IRP

Private pensions you prepare yourself, such as pension savings and IRP, form the third layer. They let you build extra retirement funds while receiving tax deductions, filling the gap the National Pension alone may leave. A commonly recommended design is to build the base with the National Pension (layer one), then add depth with the retirement pension (layer two) and private pension (layer three). For the tax benefits and management of private pensions, a separate guide is helpful.

Three-layer design in brief: National Pension (base, lifelong, price-linked) → retirement pension (workplace-based) → private pension (tax-deductible, self-directed). View all three together to complete the retirement picture.

Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

Q. Will I really end up unable to receive it?

The worry that "if the fund runs out you get nothing" comes up often, but the National Pension differs from private products in that the state is responsible for payment. The amended National Pension Act wrote a state payment guarantee into law, stating that the State must guarantee the stable and continuous payment of pension benefits under the act, effective 1 January 2026. The National Pension Service projects that the reform delays fund depletion from 2056 to 2071, a projection that assumes a 1 percentage point rise in the fund's investment return. Still, as the system keeps being adjusted, it is wise to check your expected benefit periodically through the "My Pension" service.

Q. What happens to contributions if I leave my job?

If you retire and lose income, you can apply for a payment exemption, and you switch to individually insured status when income resumes. Periods not paid are excluded from your insured time, so many people, where they can afford it, consider continuing through voluntary enrollment or retroactive payment. For handling that fits your situation, it is safest to check with the National Pension Service.

Q. If both spouses enrol, do both receive a pension?

If each spouse enrols and meets their own entitlement, each receives an old-age pension in principle. However, where a survivor pension overlaps with one's own old-age pension after a death, an adjustment for overlap may apply, so the specifics vary by point in time and conditions and should be confirmed.

Conclusion - long and steady shapes your retirement

The National Pension is the most basic first layer of retirement. The contribution leaving your account each month can feel like a loss, but it is paid for life, linked to prices, and covers the three risks of old age, disability and death at once, strengths that private products struggle to replace. And the surest path to a larger benefit is, as almost every part of the system concludes, simply to extend your insured period, long and steady.

Finally, three points again. First, the contribution rate, benefit age and replacement rate vary over time. The contribution rate rises every year until 2033 and the income limits change every July, so treat the figures here only as general standards. Second, the National Pension is just layer one; view it with the retirement and private pensions. Third, always confirm your expected benefit and the latest standard with the National Pension Service (1355). Once more: this article is for information and education only and is not financial or pension advice. For your specific situation, please consult the National Pension Service or a licensed professional.
