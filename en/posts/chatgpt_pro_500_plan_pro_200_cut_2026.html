What was announced — three changes at DevDay on 29 September

At OpenAI DevDay 2026 in San Francisco on 29 September 2026, the top layer of the personal ChatGPT subscription changed. The coverage agrees on three changes. A new Pro 500 plan at $500 a month was created, the usage included in the existing Pro 200 is being reduced, and in return existing subscribers receive a one-time usage credit. GPT-6.1 Sol and the always-on agent Dots were announced at the same event and can bury these items, but for a plan subscriber these three are the most direct.

The angle of this article is not whether prices went up. The aim is to read, in the original wording, what the multiple beside a plan name is a multiple of, how the new speed layer consumes usage, and how the same date applies differently depending on when a person subscribed. The general principle for reading the denominator of a limit label is covered in The Same 20x Measures Different Things, so it is not repeated here. The July change in how Claude subscription limits are allocated is in Claude Fable 5 Included in Max and Team Premium Limits at 50%, and the model pricing announced the same day is in The Price Structure of GPT-6.1 Sol and How to Choose.

The details of the announcement read slightly differently depending on the source. So each value below carries the material whose wording it comes from. The points where sources diverge are four.

  • The basis of the multiple. 20x and 10x are multiples with the Plus allowance set to 1. The absolute value of the Plus allowance itself does not appear in the coverage.
  • The scope of application. One report puts “new $200 subscribers” in its headline while another says “existing subscribers get a time limit”. They describe the same adjustment from different sides.
  • The nature of the credit. The amount of $2,500 and the year-end expiry are the same across reports, but no material states where it can be used.
  • The Ultrafast multiple. The Codex 8x and the API 6x are sometimes quoted mixed together.

The denominator of 10x — the Plus allowance as a ruler

The central sentence in the coverage is that the Work and Codex included usage of Pro 200 falls “from 20 times to 10 times the Plus allowance”. Digital Today quoted it with “about” attached, as “about 20 times the Plus plan level to about 10 times”. This multiple stands on a denominator: how much Plus can use per day, or per week. Depending on what that denominator is, the same 10x points to a different quantity.

There are two easily missed points here. First, 10x is not an absolute amount for Pro 200 but a relation to Plus. If the Plus allowance changes later, the real quantity behind the same 10x changes with it. Second, the multiple concerns the usage included in Work and Codex. The limits for ordinary chat and other features are not inside this multiple, and GPT-6 Pro chat is counted separately as a number of weekly messages (from 200 to 100). Limits in different units coexist within one plan.

What OpenAI said it would not reintroduce reads in the same context. The Next Web reports that OpenAI said it will not reintroduce the five-hour limit on Pro 200. Whether the window of a limit is five hours or a weekly total is a factor that changes how it feels a great deal depending on usage pattern, and this remark is about the kind of window. The nature of windows is explained in section 4 of the limit-reading article mentioned above.

  • 10x and 20x are relative values with the Plus allowance set to 1.
  • The multiple attaches to the included usage of ChatGPT Work and Codex, while GPT-6 Pro chat is counted separately as weekly messages.
  • Digital Today quoted “about 20x” and “about 10x”, and WinBuzzer quoted “10X the Plus allowance”.
  • The remark about not bringing back the five-hour limit concerns the kind of limit window and is separate from the multiple.

Dividing price by multiple — a derived table

Because both price and multiple are in the coverage, “what does one Plus allowance cost” can be computed. This calculation is derived. Because the multiples are “about”, because they apply only to Work and Codex included usage, and because credits and chat limits are left out, it is for seeing structure, not a precise value.

The structure the table shows is simple. After the adjustment, every plan from Plus to Pro 500 converges on $20 per 1x. Only Pro 200 before the adjustment was at $10. In other words, Pro 200 was a “bundle with a lower unit price”, and the adjustment looks like removing that exception. This is only arithmetic from the price list and does not mean OpenAI adjusted it with that intent. The intent does not appear in the coverage.

The implication of this derivation for real use is one. After the adjustment, moving to a higher plan does not lower the unit price. It is closer to a linear structure in which a person who uses more pays proportionally more, not one where a heavier user pays less per unit. What Pro 500 adds is not a lower unit price for usage but a ceiling of 25x in total quantity and access to Ultrafast. Then the core of the judgment becomes separating whether what you need is total quantity or speed.

What this derivation cannot show is also stated plainly. If the unit of included usage is consumed differently by task difficulty and by model, then even the same “multiple” means a different amount of finished work per task. The unit price of usage bought additionally with credits is also not in this table. So the table is a map of the relative positions of the plans, not an invoice of cost per task.

Ultrafast — a tier that divides speed, not usage

Ultrafast, which exists only on Pro 500, is described by OpenAI as a premium speed tier for work where speed matters most. The reported figure is up to 300 tokens per second in Codex, up to 8x standard. Digital Today distinguished that speed reaches up to 300 tokens per second in ChatGPT Work as well, and that the 8x is on a Codex basis.

What is easy to confuse is the multiple. The coverage quoting OpenAI material carries both “up to 8x in Codex and up to 6x in the API”. The same Ultrafast has a different multiple by product. The 6x and the 6x unit price of API Ultrafast seen in the GPT-6.1 Sol article are API values, and the 8x in this article is a value for the subscription product. The two must not be merged as the same thing.

The way of consumption is also written differently. According to the quoted material, Ultrafast uses the limits included in the subscription at 8 times the standard rate and purchased credits at 6 times. It is a structure in which the limit shrinks as fast as the speed rises. This sentence came from a secondary source quoting OpenAI release notes, so how it is actually deducted in an account should be checked against the usage display on screen.

  • Ultrafast can be used only on Pro 500 at launch. On Plus, Pro 100, Pro 200 and Business it is reported not to open even if credits are bought.
  • Up to 300 tokens per second in Codex, up to 8x standard. The API is separate, at up to 6x.
  • There is a quotation that included subscription limits are consumed at 8x and purchased credits at 6x.
  • Work where speed is the bottleneck and work where total quantity is the bottleneck are different problems. Ultrafast addresses the former and the plan multiple the latter.

One more derivation is added. If the 8x consumption is assumed to apply to the whole included subscription limit, then using the entire 25x allowance in Ultrafast yields, on a standard-speed basis, a little over 3x of work (25 divided by 8). This value has three layers of assumption. Whether the 8x rate applies to the whole limit, whether every task is run in Ultrafast, and whether the multiple is exactly 25 are all premises. Taking away only the direction, “buying speed reduces the work the same limit can do”, is enough.

Existing and new subscribers — same date, different application

The date of 30 October is the same for everyone, but the way it applies splits by when the subscription began. That is also why the headlines differ by outlet. WinBuzzer aims its headline at “new $200 subscribers”, and The Next Web also carries the grace period and the credit for existing subscribers. Lining up the wording of the three reports in a table gives the following.

The empty third row is the unresolved part of this overhaul. The cutoff that defines “existing subscriber” is not in a published document, and the coverage only announces individual notices. Digital Today writes that “existing subscribers keep current limits for a period and then receive a one-time credit corresponding to the revised usage”, using the phrase “corresponding to the revised usage” for the credit. Whether this points to the same credit as the $2,500 figure in The Next Web cannot be matched from the text alone.

In the end, what a current subscriber needs is not an outlet summary but the notice that arrived on their own account. This overhaul attaches different deadlines and credits to different people under the same plan name, so the sentence “Pro 200 subscribers will see this” is only partly true by any reading.

The 2,500 dollar credit — what is written and what is blank

The one-time credit for existing subscribers is an item the coverage shares. But how far the material goes on “where is it used” differs by source. Sorting what is written first gives the following.

  • Amount. Worth $2,500. The Next Web has “a one-time grant of usage credits worth $2,500”, and the secondary material has “62,500 credits ($2,500 worth, OpenAI says)”. If 62,500 credits are $2,500, one credit corresponds to $0.04 (derived).
  • Expiry. “Expire at the end of the year” (The Next Web). The secondary material specifies 31 December 2026. Credits not used by the deadline disappear.
  • Nature. It is a usage credit, not a refund of subscription fees. The explanation that it “corresponds” to the revised usage (Digital Today) hints at a compensating purpose, but no formula is given.
  • Scope of use. Only the general principle that credits are usable “in supported features” was quoted (GeekNews summary), and no material lists the usable places by model or product. As seen in the previous section, the limit that credits cannot open Ultrafast is in the release notes quotation.

With a credit it is easy to think “I can keep using after hitting the limit”, but until the usage scope and consumption rate are published, there is no way to know at what rate the credit is consumed in the features one mainly uses. Since expiry at year-end is clear, keeping a usage record in advance is the surest preparation for sizing the value of the credit.

GPT-6 Pro from 200 to 100 a week — divided by the day

A limit with no multiple attached also changes. The weekly GPT-6 Pro chat message limit of Pro 200 falls from 200 to 100 (The Next Web, Digital Today). This limit is on a different axis from the included usage of Work and Codex, because it is counted in messages, not in tokens or amount of work.

Divided by the week, the feel changes. Dividing 200 a week by seven days gives about 29 a day, and 100 gives about 14 a day (derived). But a weekly limit does not mean “14 a day”; it means up to the 100th message within a week. A person who uses it in bursts can reach it in two days, and a person who uses it evenly every day may never reach it. It is the nature of the same total reaching a different point depending on usage pattern.

  • The weekly limit is the total number of messages within a week, and the distribution is the user’s own.
  • Whether one long message and one short message each count as the same one against the limit is not in the quoted material.
  • Whether this limit applies immediately to new subscribers or on 30 October to existing ones follows the timing structure of the previous section.

Five questions to size it against your own pattern

This overhaul cannot be read as “who gains”. Subscribers differ in the features they use, the times they use them and their bottleneck, so the same adjustment has no felt effect on one person and a large one on another. The five questions below are ones whose answers come out of your own usage record. Once the answers are set, they become the basis of your own judgment.

  • 1. How often do you hit the current limit. Count how many limit-reached notices you received in a month. If you never reached it, a halved multiple may be felt only a little.
  • 2. Is what you hit the Work and Codex included usage, or GPT-6 Pro chat. As seen in earlier sections, the two limits have different units. The effect of the adjustment splits by which one is the bottleneck.
  • 3. Is the bottleneck total quantity, or waiting time. If total quantity is short, the plan multiple applies; if waiting for responses is the problem, the speed tier applies. Read it together with the quotation that speed consumes the subscription limit at 8x.
  • 4. Do you use it in bursts or spread out. A weekly limit is reached in the weeks you use in bursts. Looking at the record by day reveals the distribution.
  • 5. If a credit arrives, until when and on what can it be used. Read the usage scope and expiry in the notice email first, and compare with expected usage through year-end.

The record does not need to be complicated. Over one month, writing just five columns, “date, feature mainly used (Work, Codex, chat), whether the limit was reached, the time it was reached, and whether waiting time was the problem”, is enough to answer the questions above. Comparing with the figures the usage screen provides also reveals the gap between feel and reality. Choosing a plan by feel alone makes the most memorable day stand in for the average.

If even these five questions do not produce a conclusion, waiting may be reasonable. The adjustment starts on 30 October, and right after an overhaul it is common for limit labels and deduction methods to be corrected in the documents. The tendency of subscription limit labels to change midway has already been covered in earlier articles. Use the official pages, which are updated from time to time, as the standard, but record them together with the date, which is the habit that lasts longest on this subject.

What could not be verified

  • The original text of the official OpenAI announcement, plan page and help pages. Those pages did not open because of an access block (HTTP 403), so wording quoted in coverage and search summaries was used.
  • The absolute value of the Plus allowance. The value that is the denominator of the multiple is not in the coverage, so it cannot be converted into how many tasks 10x really is.
  • The cutoff date that separates existing subscribers, and the timing of the credit grant. Coverage said only that affected customers will be notified by email.
  • The products and features where the $2,500 credit can be used, and its consumption rate. Apart from the general principle of “supported features”, no material lists them.
  • The original price wording of Pro 100. It is a value inferred from the plan name and the GeekNews title, “from $100 a month”.
  • Whether the 8x rate of consumption for Ultrafast applies to the whole included subscription limit, or differs by product.
