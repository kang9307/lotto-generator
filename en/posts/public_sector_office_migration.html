What Changing the Office Toolset Actually Changes - The Blocked Spot Switzerland Already Wrote Down

Fix the denominator and the scope first, then look at what takes longer to solve than file formats

2026-09-08 Tech & IT

The sentence 「the Swiss federal government is replacing Microsoft on 3,000 computers」 travelled widely over the past few days. Nothing in it is invented. Yet the picture that forms in a reader's head from that sentence alone sits at some distance from what the Swiss Federal Chancellery actually published.

The distance opens in three places: the denominator behind the figure of 3,000, the range the word 「replace」 quietly covers, and the stage this programme is currently at. Once those three are pinned down, one question remains, and it is the interesting one. What does changing an office toolset actually change?

The sharpest fragment of an answer is already on the record. Switzerland ran a single trial with 172 participants and wrote down what worked and what did not. Document editing, file storage, collaboration, mail and calendar were rated positively. Large video conferences ran into limits. That one line is the spine of this article, because it points at a general rule: in an office migration, the last thing to fall into place is not the file format but real-time collaboration.

The figures here come from the Swiss Federal Chancellery release of 2 September 2026, the Chancellery's own project page for the feasibility study, the release announcing completion of the Microsoft 365 rollout, the W012 directive on digital sovereignty, federal staffing statistics, the ODF and OOXML standards documents, and the Korean statute database. Values that appear only in press coverage and could not be matched against a primary document are attributed as such, and everything that stayed unresolved is gathered with its reason in a section near the end. No product is ranked against another, and no government's policy is assessed politically.

The trigger - cutting the range the word replace covers

On 2 September 2026 the Swiss Federal Chancellery published a release launching a programme for a digitally sovereign workplace. The substance fits into two sentences. About 3,000 staff should be able to use sovereign workplace software from the end of 2027, and that software will be stood up as a standalone solution running alongside Microsoft 365.

The second sentence carries more weight than the first. The original text uses the phrase for parallel operation. This is not a picture of switching off the old environment and switching on a new one; it is a picture of adding a second one. The summary 「is replacing」 borrows a final state and applies it to the present, while what has actually been decided sits several steps earlier. The scope is limited too, aimed at staff in particularly critical business processes rather than at whole departments.

Another document from the same government hardens that reading. The Microsoft 365 rollout across the Swiss federal administration was completed in mid-December 2025. It began in October 2024, reached roughly 54,000 workplaces, and finished with the defence department and the data protection commissioner. The foreign ministry had completed its own migration by the end of August 2025, including around 160 diplomatic missions abroad. This new programme therefore arrives nine months after that rollout closed, on a separate track.

The two decisions look contradictory but live inside the same rulebook. The rollout release states that users must not store especially sensitive data or confidential documents in the Microsoft cloud, and describes a labelling scheme that keeps classified material in federal data centres. Jurisdiction, in other words, has been held down by rules rather than by product choice. The new programme aims at the space those rules cannot cover.

The number - 3,000 has two denominators

The English article that carried the story described 3,000 as roughly 7 percent of the federal workforce while, in the same sentence, giving the total as 54,000 computers. Divide one by the other and you get 5.6 percent. For 7 percent the denominator has to sit near 43,000. Swiss federal administration staffing stood at 43,513 people in 2024, or 38,962 full-time equivalent positions. So 7 percent uses headcount as the denominator and 5.6 percent uses workplaces, and both denominators ended up inside one sentence.

This is not pedantry about notation. The fact that people and workplaces are not one to one is precisely what sets the shape of the work. If one person uses an office machine, a home machine and a field device, then changing the toolset means as many deployment and configuration jobs as there are workplaces, not as there are people. If several people share one machine in shifts, what grows is not deployment but account and permission work. The same 3,000 produces two different schedules depending on which case dominates.

A Swiss outlet reported it first, and other outlets wrote that the Chancellery confirmed the process. The cyber command sits on a track of its own, and most of the documents it handles carry a classification label, which puts it in direct collision with the cloud storage prohibition described above. The two stories get bundled under one word in coverage, yet their motives and timelines differ.

What parallel operation tells you about the design

Parallel operation sounds like a compromise, but it is the standard shape of this kind of move. An office environment is not a product; it is the shell around a set of procedures. A document rides an approval chain, the approval chain rides an org chart, and the org chart rides an account system. Swap the tool wholesale and that entire chain moves at once. Overlap two environments and you can verify, one procedure at a time, whether it completes end to end in the new one.

The overlap has a price. The same document can exist in two places, so a rule is needed for which copy is authoritative. Accounts exist twice, and this is the period in which someone offboarded on one side survives on the other. Search has to run twice, and audit trails accumulate along two branches. A parallel period is therefore not a period of relief; it is a period in which the number of things being managed goes up. Deciding how long it lasts becomes the central variable of the plan.

Narrowing this phase to staff in particularly critical business processes reads the same way. Deploy broadly and shallowly and the parallel period stretches; deploy narrowly and deeply and you can first prove that one full procedure survives the crossing. It is structurally the same problem described in Keeping Code Hosting Portable, where the repository follows and everything around it does not. Separating what travels from what does not is the first step of any migration.

What the trial already separated

The basis for this decision was a feasibility study run by the Federal Chancellery, named for testing office automation with open source software. A total of 172 participants drawn from several departments took part in a user acceptance test. The scope covered document editing, mail and calendar, contacts and tasks, file storage, audio and video conferencing, chat, and identity and access management. That list alone shows how many separate things the phrase 「office environment」 actually contains.

The published conclusion comes in two layers. One layer says the browser-based solution tested is fundamentally suitable for central standard processes. The other says technical, operational and organisational hurdles remain before broad productive use. The second does not erase the first. It marks what sits between 「it works」 and 「you can deploy it as it is」.

Of those eight rows, exactly one failure has a name attached to it. Why that name happened to be large video conferences is the most generalisable part of the whole story.

Why large meetings are the last thing to fall into place

Documents and meetings ship in the same bundle, but their properties are opposites. A document is asynchronous. If the format matches, opening it today or tomorrow yields the same thing. A meeting is synchronous, and a frame that arrives late is treated exactly like a wrong frame. That difference widens in opposite directions as headcount grows.

What a media server actually does

In the published composition, the video part uses a widely deployed open-source media server family. That family takes the selective forwarding approach: instead of compositing everyone's video into a single new stream, it picks incoming streams and passes them on. Because the server never re-encodes, the computation load drops sharply, which is how one machine can carry many rooms at once.

The price is bandwidth. Not compositing means the number of streams never shrinks. With N people in a room, one participant may need up to N-1 downstream flows, and the total the server pushes out grows close to the square of the headcount. This is why doubling the size of a meeting does not double the requirement.

What actually grows with headcount

Two mitigations are therefore used together. The first sends only the video of the last few active speakers: in a room of 100 you receive three or four streams instead of 99. The second has each sender publish the same video at several qualities simultaneously so the server can choose the right one per receiver. The project documentation describes an allocator choosing one layer, or no layer at all, from the several layers formed by combinations of resolution and frame rate.

Neither is free. The first keeps swapping who is visible, which degrades the experience in meetings where people talk over each other. The second increases the sender's upstream bandwidth and its local computation. In a browser-based implementation the encoding and decoding run on the participant's own machine, so that load lands on the user rather than the server. The fact that public sector desktops tend to be older hardware converts directly into a performance problem at this point.

When one room spans several servers

When a single machine is not enough, one room is spread over several servers, and a dedicated path for relaying media between them appears. The public documentation states that a bridge participating in a multi-bridge conference gains an additional channel, one for audio and one for video, responsible for forwarding media to all other bridges and receiving media from them. From this point on, quality is determined less by server capacity and more by which participant landed on which server and by the latency between them.

Put plainly, a room of 20 and a room of 300 are not the same feature. Both are written as a single line called video conferencing in a feature list, and as a single line in a pilot plan. The larger one, however, only works when bandwidth design, endpoint specifications, server placement and inter-server link quality all line up. That the one named failure in the Swiss trial was large meetings is hard to read as coincidence.

There is one more thing. A gathering with very many participants is a meeting in name only; in practice it is closer to a broadcast. A few people speak and the rest listen. The right architecture for that shape differs from the architecture for everyone sending to everyone. What organisations ask for, though, is always both shapes inside one product. That request is one line in a procurement document and two different systems on the build side.

Moving something static versus keeping people in sync

Video conferencing is just one instance of a larger pattern. The general form behind it is this: an office migration mixes two categories of task. One is moving something that stands still. The other is keeping several people's state aligned in real time.

The first is verifiable. Move the file, open it, compare it against the original. You can build regression tests, and when something breaks you can point at where it broke. It takes time, but the method is settled.

The second behaves differently. When two people edit the same paragraph at once, the file does not decide which version wins. The merge algorithm on the server does. That is why 「supports the same file format」 does not follow through to 「supports the same collaboration」. Locking a document so one person edits at a time and letting several people edit simultaneously produce files in the same format, but they produce different working habits.

Most of the complaints that surface in an organisation after a migration originate here. The spreadsheets all open. The fonts are roughly right. And yet the habit of editing together during a call no longer works, or it works but another person's cursor lags. That class of friction is invisible to a checklist that opens files one by one. It is exactly why a pilot phase puts real people in front of the system.

Item by item - what catches and what remains

Treating an office migration as 「swapping in a different product with the same features」 is where plans go wrong. Each item catches in a different place, and the ratio between what a product swap solves and what it does not varies from item to item. The table below splits that ratio out.

Read the table vertically and a rule appears. The column of things a replacement product solves is mostly work done by machines. The column of things that remain is mostly rules set by an organisation. Swapping the product carries the first column across. The second is not carried; it is rewritten. Which is why the accurate name for this work is not replacement but reconstruction.

Document formats - same skeleton, different vocabulary

Document format is the most frequently mentioned axis in this story and, in practice, one of the better solved ones. The office document formats in wide use today share essentially the same skeleton: a compressed archive holding several XML documents plus attached resources. Change the extension and unpack it, and the structure is right there.

Two standards, and one of them splits again

A shared structure does not make them mutually readable. The skeleton is the same and the vocabulary is not. The element name for a paragraph, the way styles inherit, the rule for expressing a merged table cell all differ between standards. What a converter does is not unpacking an archive but transcribing that vocabulary, and values blur wherever no equivalent term exists on the other side.

One of the two standards splits internally. The international standard for office XML contains both a strict variant and a transitional one. The latter is a collection of compatibility constructs kept so that older binary formats and the behaviour of older products can be represented, and the format description maintained by the US Library of Congress treats this distinction as a separate entry. The fact that a large share of the files circulating in practice are saved in the transitional variant is what actually sets conversion difficulty. It is the textbook case of the same standard name not meaning the same thing.

Macros, forms and signatures each fail somewhere else

Once format conversion is done, the leftovers have very different characters. Grouping them under one name leads to a single countermeasure, when in reality they are separate kinds of work.

  • Macros — these are not documents but programs living inside documents. A format converter does not carry them across. Carrying them means rewriting them, and rewriting means someone has to know what the macro does. In older organisations that condition is the one that most often fails
  • Forms — they look like files but are part of a procedure. What matters is less the position of an input box than which field of which system it feeds. Moving a form means reconnecting that wiring
  • Signatures — a signature attached to a document fixes the byte sequence as it stood at signing time. Change the format and the bytes change, so the signature breaks. Conversion implies re-signing, and any historical document that cannot be re-signed needs a separate path that preserves the original format
  • Retention rules — long-term preservation formats and editing formats want opposite things. An editing format must remain changeable; a preservation format must render the same way years later. Force both onto one format and one of them gives way

What the four share is that the thing needing change is not the document but the rules around it. That portion does not shrink no matter which product is chosen.

Accounts and permissions - the item that rarely gets its own line

Migration plans usually list documents, mail and meetings. The account system tends to be filed under infrastructure and never gets a line of its own. That identity and access management appeared inside the trial scope is a signal about how much weight this item carries.

  • Single sign-on — binding several components to one identity has established methods. This part generally works out
  • Groups and inheritance — the department structure is expressed as groups and permissions descend through it. The shorter the reorganisation cycle, the more tangled those rules have become
  • Conditional access — rules about which network, which device and which hour access is permitted from. Expressiveness varies by product, and that variation becomes a difference in security policy
  • Audit trails — records of who opened what and when change shape when the environment changes. During the parallel period two branches accumulate, and stitching them together later requires reconciling timestamps and identifiers

Accounts are especially awkward because their failures are quiet. A broken document announces itself immediately; permissions left too wide announce nothing until an incident. The point that bulk data leaves an organisation through more routes than intrusion is set out separately in The Four Routes One Word Hides.

The moment you exchange something with the outside

Even when everything inside the organisation is settled, one area remains: the outside. Public bodies feel this most. Other ministries, regional offices, contractors and members of the public each use their own tools, and that choice is beyond reach.

Only one rule holds here. The side you open with is yours to fix; the side they open with is not. Documents leaving the organisation are therefore safest in a format that looks the same regardless of what opens it, and where editing has to travel back and forth, there is no substitute for agreeing the format in advance. Change the format without that agreement and the burden shifts to the other party, and it comes back as support requests.

On the meeting side the same problem is more immediate. An external participant has to be able to join from a single link, and there is no way to know what browser they are on. This is exactly where a browser-based implementation gains an advantage, and also exactly where the spread of endpoint specifications turns straight into a spread of quality. The load described in the earlier section reappears here.

Three motives that demand three different remedies

When a public body sets out to change its office tooling, the stated reasons converge on three. Whose law reaches the place the data sits, how tightly the organisation is bound to one supplier, and cost. The three are routinely written into one sentence, yet each demands a different remedy.

MotiveWhat it actually demandsDoes swapping the product solve itWhat still has to be true
Data jurisdictionWhich law applies to the facility holding the data and to the entity operating that facilityAlmost neverOperator, site location, the contractual structure of the operating staff. Open source running in someone else's jurisdiction meets the same condition
Supplier lock-inWhat comes with you when you leavePartiallyFormat, data, procedure and people are each locked separately. Unlocking one of the four does not open the door
Licence costThe change in total cost of ownershipNot something that can be assertedWhere a subscription fee used to sit, integration, operations and support salaries arrive. Scale and in-house capability decide the direction

The first row is the core of this story. The Swiss federal administration adopted Microsoft 365 while simultaneously running a rule that keeps confidential documents out of that cloud. Jurisdiction, in other words, had been held down by rules without changing the product. The place where those rules stop working is an organisation whose documents almost all carry a classification, which is why the first talk of a full move came from the military side. That ordering is not accidental; it is set by the reach of the rule.

According to coverage, the head of the cyber command called Microsoft 365 a very good solution while adding that it does not fit the particular requirements of an armed force. That remark marks the character of the whole affair precisely. It is a question of fit rather than of which product is better, and fit is decided by the classification of the information an organisation handles. The way the single word 「own」 actually splits into separate layers is set out layer by layer in What the Word Own Actually Bundles.

The second row is the most operational one for anyone preparing a move. Lock-in latches in four separate places. Format lock-in is a file that will not open. Data lock-in is an export that technically runs but arrives deformed. Procedural lock-in is a regulation written on the assumption of that tool. People lock-in is having nobody who has operated anything else. Build a plan on the first two alone and the schedule slips on the other two.

Cost does not vanish, it relocates

One figure has been published for this programme. The first phase is estimated at roughly 9 million Swiss francs, funded from money parliament allocated for developing a sovereign Swiss open-source office automation platform. The same material states that the cost of a broad rollout and of regular operation will be derived from the results of this phase. The total programme cost, therefore, is not public.

A common move is to divide that figure by 3,000 to get a per-person number and compare it with existing subscription fees. That comparison does not hold. The first is a one-off build cost and the second is recurring, and in this case no public value exists for the second. Subtracting two figures with different units is a definitional problem rather than an arithmetic one.

Structurally, cost does not vanish; it relocates. What used to occupy a single line in a contract under the name of licence fees reappears, scattered across the workforce plans of several units, under the names of integration, operations, support and training. Whether that relocation is a gain depends on the organisation's scale and its in-house capability. Where operating staff already exist, it falls; where they do not, it rises. Which is why the word 「saving」 is neither true nor false without conditions attached.

Nor does open source by itself settle the cost structure. In the Swiss case, a provision requiring federal bodies to publish the source code of software they develop or have developed has been in force since 2024, and its reach was extended to decentralised units from May 2025. That is a publication obligation, not a promise of free operation. Code being open and someone being paid to run that code are separate budget lines.

Korea touched the same problem one layer down

The Korean public sector answered the same question, but at a different layer. Switzerland is changing the tool; Korea opened the format.

At the level of the text, it reads as follows. The Regulation on the Operation and Innovation of Administrative Affairs defines an open document format by two requirements: that its technical standard and specification be published, and that it be machine readable in the sense used by the Act on Promotion of the Provision and Use of Public Data. What matters is that the definition is written as a property rather than as a product name. Instead of designating a particular product, it sets a condition.

A 2026 amendment sits on top of that. Presidential Decree No. 36333 was promulgated and took effect on 19 May 2026, and its stated grounds include placing a duty of effort on the head of each administrative agency to process electronic documents so that they can be used by artificial intelligence. The same amendment allows official forms to be provided in translation, and widens special performance awards to staff who are not civil servants.

Press coverage framed the amendment as the trigger for limiting attachments in the central and local government document system to an open format, and gave 18 May as the effective date. The cabinet approval date reported as 12 May, the 18 May effective date given in coverage, and the 19 May promulgation and commencement date shown in the statute database do not line up. Whether the decree itself carries wording that designates an attachment format is not settled outside the definition clause and the duty-of-effort clause, so the two layers are kept apart here.

Opening a format and changing a tool do not substitute for one another. Opening the format increases the number of things that can read: other programs, other agencies, and machines. Changing the tool changes the writing side: what people do every day, and how procedures run. The first faces outward and the second faces inward. An actual attempt to measure how well machines read Korean government documents is set out in KOLongDoc.

The national standard for Korean public document formats is KS X 6101. That standard, which specifies a word processor markup language, uses the same skeleton described in the earlier section: XML inside a compressed archive, with a published specification, which satisfies the first requirement of the definition above. The format layer, in other words, already meets the condition. What remains is how well that condition holds up in actual exchanges.

Questions that need answers before the plan exists

Turning all of this into a plan needs an order. The eight items below have to be settled before a tool is chosen. Choose the product first and the product will answer them for you.

  1. Fix the denominator. Headcount or workplaces. Deployment schedule and account workload diverge from this single choice
  2. Fix the end of the parallel period. When, and on what evidence, does the old environment get switched off. Without that condition, parallel stops being a phase and becomes permanent architecture
  3. Write the maximum meeting size as a number. Twenty and three hundred call for different systems. This is where a single procurement line splits
  4. Decide where the authoritative copy lives. The rule for which side wins when the same document exists twice. Without it, merge accidents follow
  5. Pick one outbound format. It is the format opened by parties outside your control, and relationships that need editing get a separate agreement
  6. Count the macros and forms. How many exist, and whether anyone left knows what they do, is one of the larger schedule variables
  7. Separate signing from retention. Split, in advance, documents that break on conversion from documents that have to stay in their original format
  8. Create the budget line for operating staff. It is what moves into the space the licence fee vacated, and a plan without that line collapses in its first year

The sixth item is hard to count by hand. Shared locations are scattered, and the older a document is the less likely anyone knows where it lives. Automating the count itself puts the later discussion on top of numbers rather than impressions.

# Count format distribution and macro-bearing files across a shared location
find /srv/share -type f \( -iname '*.doc*' -o -iname '*.xls*' -o -iname '*.ppt*' \
     -o -iname '*.od[tsp]' -o -iname '*.hwp*' \) -printf '%f\n' \
  | awk -F. '{print tolower($NF)}' | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn

# Only the extensions that can carry macros (the m-suffixed family)
find /srv/share -type f -iregex '.*\.\(docm\|xlsm\|pptm\|xlsb\)$' | wc -l

Those two lines produce a starting point rather than a conclusion. The format distribution sets the scope a converter has to cover, and the macro count sets the size of the rewriting job. Seating 172 people in front of the system, as Switzerland did, is ultimately the same category of work: turning sentences in a plan into numbers.

What could not be confirmed

The items below were kept out of the main text because their wording could not be matched against a primary source. The reason is given alongside each.

  • Whether the release names the product — the Federal Chancellery release uses the generic phrase for sovereign workplace software. The product name and the component list appear on the same body's project page, so the main text relies on that page and keeps it separate from the wording of the release
  • A decision to expand to 54,000 workplaces — the discussion of expansion and its timing appears in press coverage, and this release carries no expansion figure. No expansion target is therefore used as a value in the main text
  • October 2026 for the cyber command — this is Swiss press reporting, and the statement that the Chancellery confirmed the process is likewise the outlet's own. No primary announcement from that organisation is retrievable
  • The maximum meeting size tested in the trial — the public summary says only that limits appeared with large video conferences, without stating at what headcount or with what symptoms. Detail on this sits in the technical report and the test result report published alongside
  • Individual assessments for contacts, tasks, chat and identity — the items named as positively rated are document editing, file storage, collaboration, and mail and calendar. Assessment sentences for the remaining areas are absent from the public summary, so those cells are left blank
  • Annual licence cost of the existing environment — the value would be required to calculate any saving, and it is not in the public material. No direction of cost change is asserted
  • The textual basis for the attachment format restriction — the definition clause for open document formats and the duty-of-effort clause on artificial intelligence are both traceable, but whether the same decree restricts attachments to a particular extension is unresolved. The effective dates also diverge between 18 May in coverage and 19 May in the statute database
  • Actual conversion loss rates between the three formats — what a standard settles is the scope of the specification; where a given implementation loses a value sits outside it. Since the document templates in use differ by organisation, no generalised figure is used
