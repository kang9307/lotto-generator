I Know I Saw It Somewhere - Why Finding What You Already Read Is Not a Search Engine Problem

Recall matters more than ranking. What you have to store to find things again, and why storing it creates the risk

Sep 2, 2026 Tech & IT

You definitely read it. A few weeks ago, somewhere in the middle of some article. The site name is gone and so is the title, and what remains is roughly what that paragraph said. You open your browser history. A list of addresses pours out. None of them is the one.

This failure is not a failure of searching. It happens because the thing you would search was never stored. History holds titles and addresses; what stuck in memory was the body. That mismatch is very nearly the whole of what personal record search is, and it is not a shrunken version of web search. It is a different problem.

This is not a how-to or an installation guide for any product, and it is not a recommendation to use or avoid any tool. The scope is limited to handling your own records, and no method of collecting anyone else's records is discussed in any form. Documents and repository figures cited here were checked in early September 2026.

The trigger - a category called the personal search engine

In early September 2026 an open source project was introduced on a Korean developer community. The gist was that it indexes the full text of the web pages you visit and the files you keep, so you can search inside them later. The interesting part is not how finished the tool is but why this category appeared separately, now. Browsers already keep history, operating systems already search files, and note apps are everywhere. And yet a new tool shows up in the same spot every year.

This piece is about the shape of that gap. First, where this problem diverges from web search. Then privacy, placed up front, and only after that storage, retrieval and cost. The reason for that order is simple. If you settle the technical choices first and attach privacy afterwards, it usually does not attach.

  • Covered — why this is not a ranking problem, what has to be stored for something to be findable, the character of the risk that storage creates, and what arrives after long use
  • Not covered — installation and configuration, product comparisons and recommendations, and every method of collecting other people's records
  • Citation rule — repository figures and product documentation are dated, and numbers that could not be verified are left out

A different problem from public web search

Public web search picks the top ten from billions of documents. Personal record search is about not losing one item out of the few thousand to few tens of thousands of things you have seen. That inverts the value of ranking. On the web the difference between first and fortieth is decisive; inside your own records, fortieth is fine because you can scan the list. But if it is not in the list at all, that is the end of it. What matters here is recall, not precision.

The nature of the query differs too. Web search means I want to know about this; personal search means I want to see that again. The second has a peculiar property: the user already knows the answer exists. So when web search fails you shrug and assume there is nothing, whereas when personal search fails you are left in the state of knowing it is there and not finding it, which holds people for a long time.

There is also nothing to rank with. The public web weighs documents by links, clicks and citations, and your records carry none of those signals. What they carry instead is different. When you saw it, how long you stayed, where you came from, whether you opened it again.

  • A small corpus means technically easy, not trivially safe. The difficulty simply moved from search algorithms to data management
  • Prioritising recall makes results messy. Making that messiness bearable is the real UI task in this field
  • Time becomes a powerful axis. On the web a date filter is a side feature; here it is often the primary sort key
  • The last row governs everything else. This index is not a collection of public documents but a record about you

Privacy sits at the centre of this

Indexing the body text of the pages you visit is not switching on a feature. It is gathering into one place what used to be scattered. Until then that information was split up: some inside the browser, some on each site's servers, some left nowhere at all. The size of the risk becomes obvious the moment you picture that single file leaving the machine.

What is inside it has already been written down as a list, by law. Article 23 of Korea's Personal Information Protection Act and Article 18 of its Enforcement Decree set aside as sensitive information ideology and belief, joining or leaving a trade union or political party, political opinions, health, sex life, genetic data, criminal records, physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics generated for the purpose of identifying a specific individual, and race or ethnicity. An ordinary month of browsing overlaps more than half of that list.

So the design question splits into three. Where does it live, what gets excluded, and how does it get deleted? The first governs the rest. Kept locally, the maximum size of the risk is bounded by one machine, and that boundary is one you know. The moment it leaves, that boundary stretches to include the vendor's lifespan, policy changes, legal jurisdiction and breaches, and it cannot be pulled back. Once something is sent, there is no way to verify deletion other than believing the claim.

How this data is expected to be handled becomes clear from how far a large company went on the same problem. The conditions Microsoft wrote into its Recall documentation are that baseline. Snapshots are stored only on the device and are not sent to Microsoft; opening it requires confirming your identity with Windows Hello. Snapshots and the associated vector database are always encrypted and the keys are protected by the TPM. The feature is opt-in, so an administrator cannot enable it on a user's behalf. The lesson is not the product but the level of requirement.

The exclusion rules are in the same document. Sensitive information filtering is on by default, private browsing in supported browsers is filtered by default, and DRM-protected content is not stored. But the limits are written down too. Even for a filtered site, parts can remain in the form of embedded content, the browser's history, or an open tab that is not in the foreground. Exclusion rules lower the probability; they do not create a guarantee.

  • Where the index file lives and what locks it. An index that opens without authentication is effectively one plaintext file
  • What is on the default exclusion list. Whether private browsing, financial and medical domains and internal systems are out from the start decides the character of the thing
  • Whether deleting really deletes all of it. Text removed but vectors left behind, or an emptied index with snapshot files still on disk, are common
  • Whether the outbound path is a default or a choice. If sending embeddings to an external API is the default, that is a different kind of product
  • Whether shared devices were considered. How separation works on a shared laptop, or where several people use one account

Why browser history is not enough

What a browser keeps is written out precisely in the extension API documentation. In Chrome's history API, HistoryItem has six fields: id, url, title, lastVisitTime, visitCount and typedCount. The VisitItem that represents an individual visit carries id, visitId, referringVisitId, visitTime, transition and isLocal. The Firefox definition lists the same six.

There is no slot in that list for the body of the page. You cannot search what is not there. So browser history works only when you remember the title, while what people actually remember is usually the body. A title passes by as you read; the body stays.

What browser history keeps
  HistoryItem : id · url · title · lastVisitTime · visitCount · typedCount
  VisitItem   : id · visitId · referringVisitId · visitTime · transition · isLocal

The questions memory asks, and how they land
  「the paragraph that blamed load latency on the cache」  -> no body field. Not searchable
  「that document I was reading one night last month」     -> lastVisitTime narrows it, hundreds remain
  「the page I reached by a link from some article」       -> traceable via referringVisitId
  「the post with scheduler in the title」                 -> searchable via title

There are range limits too. Chrome help states that history covers the last 90 days and that internal pages, pages viewed in Incognito and pages you deleted are not saved. The documents you end up wanting again are usually older than 90 days. Bookmarks fall short in a different way. What a bookmark asks for is not storage but a judgement, and the pages you actually come back looking for are the ones that did not look important at the time. The moment of judgement and the moment of need are out of step, so bookmarks miss structurally.

  • Browser history is a data structure built to help navigation. Address bar autocomplete was the purpose; recollection was not
  • So the absence of body text is design, not defect. Storing it grows the store and creates risk, and there was no reason to pay that by default
  • Bookmarks demand judgement in advance; history demands none but throws away the body. The space between them is empty
  • Memory does not only hold onto sentences. Position on screen, the image alongside, what you were doing that day are all cues, and text alone does not catch those

What you have to store to find it

Adding stored fields adds answerable questions. But each field answers a different question and costs something different. Before jumping to the conclusion that you should store everything, it is worth laying that correspondence out.

What is storedThe question it answersWhat it costs
Address and titleWhat was that site called againAlmost nothing. The browser already does it
Body textWhich page had that sentence in itIndex size. And every sensitive body comes in whole
Visit time and orderThe thing I was reading Tuesday nightSmall. Though it lays your daily pattern bare
Referral pathWhich article did I follow the link fromSmall. The graph of your interests becomes a profile
Dwell time and scroll depthShow me only what I read to the endSmall. Your reading habits become a record
Screen snapshotExactly what was on screen at the timeThe largest. Images, layout and logged-in state all remain

What to read here is not the list but the gradient. Higher up is cheap, harmless and hard to find; further down is findable, expensive and risky. Findable and safe are the two ends of a single axis. This is not a conflict a good implementation removes but a choice of where to stand, and leaving it switched on without knowing where you stand on that axis is the worst option.

  • Snapshots are a different kind of item. Text shows only a fragment in results, whereas a snapshot shows everything the moment it opens
  • Dwell time and scroll depth cost almost no space while improving ranking substantially. The best cost-to-effect item is usually here
  • Body text has the largest effect and the largest risk. That is why many designs keep per-domain exclusion rules for this field alone

When you remember the word, and when only the meaning

After storage comes retrieval. The difference between the two branches usually called keyword search and semantic search, stated precisely, is that the shape of the memory decides which one answers.

Full-text search splits documents into tokens and builds an inverted index. It is strong on things with fixed spelling: error messages, product names, function names. Its expressiveness is wider than people expect. Going by the SQLite FTS5 documentation, phrase, prefix and proximity queries plus column filters and boolean combinations are all inside the query syntax.

What full-text search can express (SQLite FTS5 syntax)

  writeback cache               documents containing both words
  「write-back cache」           this exact phrase in this order
  writeb*                       words beginning like this
  NEAR(cache latency, 10)       both words within 10 tokens of each other
  title : scheduler             only in the title column
  cache NOT redis               the first present, the second absent

Semantic search turns document chunks and the query into vectors and picks the near ones. It catches meaning even when not a single word overlaps, so it answers memories where only the meaning survived. It comes with two constraints. One is input length. The published model card for a widely used small sentence embedding model states 384-dimensional output, 22.7 million parameters, and that by default input longer than 256 word pieces is truncated. So long pages have to be split, and how you split them decides search quality. If the sentence you remember happens to straddle the boundary between two chunks, using it as a cue will not surface the page.

The other is the shape of failure. Semantic search cannot say there is nothing. It always returns the few nearest neighbours. Full-text search returns zero when there is nothing, and zero is information in itself. Five wrong results carry no such signal. So in practice people converge on running both. What to run vector search on top of is handled separately in What Should Vector Search Run On.

Shape the memory tookWhich side answersHow it fails
An exact word or error stringFull-text searchSlightly different spelling gives zero results
Meaning survived, wording did notSemantic searchFive things that are close but not it
Roughly when it wasTime filterIf you misremember the period it falls outside the range
Where you came fromReferral path tracingBreaks if the intermediate hop was not recorded
Only a visual impressionSkimming snapshotsPast a certain volume, skimming becomes impossible
  • Running both raises recall and makes results messier. So the rule for blending results decides the real quality
  • Semantic search impresses for a few days and disappoints later. Getting a wrong result while knowing the exact word breaks trust fast
  • Full-text search alone holds up for years but keeps failing on the 「I saw it somewhere」 shape of query
  • If you must pick one, full-text search comes first. It is cheap, it gives you the zero signal, and its results can be explained

The price of running it locally

The burden of an index running continuously divides into storage and compute. On storage there is a published measurement to calibrate against. The SQLite FTS5 documentation includes a test that indexed a mail corpus of 1,636 MiB on disk, where the FTS index alone was 743 MiB with detail=full, 340 MiB with detail=column and 134 MiB with detail=none. The same corpus swings more than fivefold depending on what gets recorded. Dropping position information shrinks it but costs you phrase and proximity queries.

A copy of the original text sits on top of that. By default FTS5 stores a copy of the original row alongside the index. There is a mode that keeps only the index, but then you cannot show a body snippet in results. Snippets are close to mandatory, so in practice you end up holding both the text and the index.

The storage burden of embeddings is actually small. One 384-dimensional float vector works out to 1,536 bytes by simple arithmetic, and splitting ten thousand pages into five chunks each still lands in the tens of megabytes. The problem is compute. Full-text indexing walks a document once; embedding runs a model once per chunk, and doing that in real time while you browse is something you hear in the fans. So real designs defer. Store immediately, index for full text soon, batch the embeddings for when the machine is plugged in. The side effect is that the page you just read is not in semantic search yet.

Screen snapshots are a tier above. The minimum specification Microsoft states for Recall shows the scale: a Copilot+ PC meeting the Secured-core standard, a 40 TOPS NPU, 16 GB of RAM, 8 logical processors and 256 GB of storage. Enabling it needs at least 50 GB free, and saving pauses once free space drops below 25 GB. The allocation is 25 GB on a 256 GB device, 75 GB at 512 GB and 150 GB at 1 TB or more, with the oldest deleted first once the limit is reached.

  • Index size depends more on what you decided to record than on how many documents there are. Whether to keep position information swings it several times over
  • For embeddings the bottleneck is compute, not storage. On a machine running from a battery that burden is felt directly
  • Deferring reduces load but creates a window in which recent documents are missing from search. The length of that window sets the perceived quality
  • Snapshots are another category. Storage, compute hardware and power requirements all move up a tier

What cannot be stored and what must not be

Not every page can be stored, and not everything storable should be. Where those two overlap is the most uncomfortable part of this problem.

First, the side where storage does not hold. Search result screens, infinite scroll lists and live dashboards differ every time you open them even at the same address. For pages like that, finding it again is not a coherent request. What was stored is not the address but that screen at that moment. So this is where snapshots become necessary and simultaneously most dangerous. Dashboards generally have things on them you should not be keeping.

Next, the side that must not be stored. Mailboxes, account balances, medical records and internal systems are usually the things you most want to find again and the things you most must not store. There is another layer here. Pages behind a login do not contain only your information. Received mail carries the sender's sentences; internal documents carry colleagues' names and judgements. This is the point where indexing my own records actually becomes copying other people's information onto my machine.

So the exclusion list is not an add-on but a precondition. Even so, exclusion is not complete. The same Recall documentation states that filtering does not prevent browsers, internet service providers, websites or organisations from knowing that the site was accessed. Keeping it out of a local index and leaving no trace are two different things.

  • For pages whose content differs at the same address, the unit of recollection becomes the time, not the address. That is another reason the time axis becomes the primary sort
  • For content behind a login, value and risk grow in the same direction. That overlap does not separate through settings
  • Other people's information mixing in is not a matter of taste. On shared devices and at work it becomes a question of scope
  • Exclusion lists work better at domain granularity. Per-page exclusion does not stay managed and usually survives only the first week
  • The most practical rule is this. If you hesitate about excluding it, exclude it. Adding it back later is far cheaper than pulling it out later

The gap the existing tools leave open

Why the spot is empty becomes visible when you line up what each existing tool does and does not do.

ToolDoes it keep the bodyScopeWhat is missing
Browser historyIt does notPages visited, last 90 daysNo body, so a sentence will not find it
BookmarksAddress onlyWhatever you picked at the timeAt picking time you do not know what will matter
Note appsOnly what you copied overWhat you wrote down by handWhat you did not copy over does not exist
Desktop searchIt indexes file contentsFiles on your machineWhat you saw on the web is out of scope
Web archive servicesIt keeps themPublicly reachable pagesNot what you saw, but what someone saved

Desktop search shows the boundary clearly. Windows Search in its default Classic mode indexes the Documents, Pictures and Music folders plus the desktop, and Enhanced mode indexes the entire PC. It indexes properties including file names and full paths, and for files with text it indexes the contents; switching a file type to properties only shrinks the index at the cost of content search. The trade between how much you store and how well you can search from the previous section is sitting right there in an operating system setting.

macOS makes exclusion an explicit feature. Under Spotlight in System Settings there is a Search Privacy list, and a folder or disk placed there is not searched. Apple's documentation also writes down the cost. If you exclude certain files and folders you may not be notified when updates become available for some apps, and if you exclude your entire internal disk you will not be notified about any updates. A decision to leave something out comes with a price in a place you did not expect.

Neither of them covers what you saw on the web. The browser side knows about this gap. Chrome has history search powered by AI, and per the help documentation, once it is on the page contents of the sites you browse are saved and encrypted to your device. But it is opt-in, it requires being in the US and using Chrome in English, and results use the content present at the time of the visit even if the site has changed since. The people building browsers reach the same conclusion that you must store the body for recollection to work, yet the coverage offered is narrow. That gap is exactly where separate tools keep appearing.

Note apps are different in kind. What a note app has is only what you decided to copy over, and the mismatch in timing of that decision is identical to the bookmark problem. What happens when that rot occurs at organisational scale is set out in Your Internal Knowledge Base Rots Quietly.

  • Existing tools were each built for a different purpose, and recollection was nobody's purpose. The gap is a boundary, not a mistake
  • Between the operating system's file search and the browser's visit history, the slot for web body text is empty. That slot is why this category exists
  • That browsers have begun filling it also reads as a signal that separate tools may not have long lifespans

What goes wrong over the long run

The real test of a tool like this is not the first week but the second year. First, it grows. The rate of growth matters more than the size. Two hundred pages a day is seventy-odd thousand pages a year, and at some point rebuilding the index becomes an overnight job. From then on, changing the schema becomes effectively impossible, because changing it requires a rebuild and the rebuild takes a day.

Second, it ages. What was stored is the content at the time of the visit, and in the meantime the page may have been edited or removed. That is a property rather than a defect, and once the original is gone your copy becomes the only record. Whether you may cite that copy is a separate matter. It is unverified and only you hold it.

Third, it duplicates. The same document arrives repeatedly under different addresses, at different times, as a print version, a mobile version and a translated version. When the top ten lines are ten variants of one document, that search has failed. Deduplication requires deciding first what counts as the same, and that criterion is not self-evident.

Fourth, you stop using it. This is the most common ending. You forget the index exists and just search the web again. Personal tools die by being forgotten rather than by breaking, and there is a reason. If opening the tool has even slightly more friction than typing in a search box, you will always pick the second when you are in a hurry.

  • The schema has to be decided while a rebuild is still cheap. After it grows, it is effectively frozen
  • Ageing is both a defect and a value. But if you cite a copy of a vanished original, you have to say so
  • No tool can decide the deduplication criterion for you. Leave it undecided and the top results fill up with one document
  • The commonest ending is being forgotten. So for this tool, a design that fits inside existing habits often matters more than performance

Build it or use one

The usual advice is clear: do not build it, use what exists, because the cost lives in maintenance rather than in the first version. In this area that advice wobbles a notch, because the more sensitive the data, the more the cost of putting it on someone else's service grows non-linearly. Normally a service shutting down is an inconvenience; here the manner of the shutdown decides where your entire record ends up.

That does not make building it romantic either. The first version really is a weekend. What is long is what follows. Every schema change triggers a rebuild, extensions break when the browser updates, and running without backups until you lose one index file loses you years.

The criteria reduce to three. Is the storage location under my control? If anything leaves the machine, is that the default or a choice? Can I take the data with me when I stop? The third is the one most often skipped. A design with a way in and no way out leaves you hostage to that index a few years later. And being open source does not automatically satisfy the first condition. Being self-hostable and having local as the default setting are two different statements. A licence is not a guarantee either; a licence decides where the code may go, not where the data goes.

  • The real cost of building is not the first implementation but schema changes and rebuilds. Design those two first and the rest follows
  • With someone else's tool, look at the defaults. Not what the documentation says is possible, but what happens when you configure nothing
  • Check for the way out — export and complete deletion — at the beginning. Later there is no room to check
  • Open source is verifiability, not a verification result. And a licence says nothing about where the data goes

Where the tool that triggered this stands

Here are the public figures for the project that prompted this, as of early September 2026. The repository was created on 4 January 2026 and has 3,526 stars, 161 forks and 56 open issues. The latest release is v0.18.0 from 23 August 2026, and the last code push was 1 September. The language is Go and the licence is AGPL-3.0.

Those numbers say two things. One, it is active: more than eighteen releases in eight months and commits still landing. Two, it is eight months old and still 0.x. Star count is a measure of interest, not of stability. That people like it and that it will still read your index in three years are different claims, and for a vessel holding personal records that distinction weighs more than usual.

The feature list names full-text indexing, automatic browser indexing, query capability, optional semantic search, a crawler and browser import, web, terminal and MCP clients, and multi-user support.

More important is where the documentation draws its own boundaries. By default there is no telemetry and no cloud sync, the browser extension sends indexed page content only to the server you configure, and turning on optional semantic search sends document text to the embeddings endpoint you choose. That last sentence lands on exactly the fork from the third section. An arrangement where local is the default and outbound transfer is a choice guarantees nothing, but it does tell you what to ask.

  • For maturity, more useful than star count are repository age, release cadence and the character of open issues. The first two are visible directly in the figures
  • 0.x means the storage format may change. For data you accumulate over years, that single line weighs more than any feature
  • Sentences that state defaults and boundaries tell you more than a feature list. A tool with no such sentences at all is the hardest to judge

In summary

Finding again what you already saw is not a shrunken web search problem. The corpus is tens of thousands rather than billions, so recall matters more than ranking, and the query is not what I want to know but what I want to see again. The reason browser history cannot do this is equally simple: it has titles and addresses and no body, and the body is what people remember.

So you move toward storing the body, and at that moment the nature of the problem changes. What was scattered gathers into one file, and inside it sits much of what the law sets aside as sensitive information. The difference between keeping it local and sending it out is not convenience but how far the boundary of the risk stretches.

What remains is a single axis. Every measure that makes things findable also increases risk. Add the body and you can search by sentence, and every sensitive body comes in whole. Add snapshots and the screen comes back exactly, logged-in state included. There is no way to remove this axis, and knowing where you stand on it is all you can do.

What I could not verify

  • Which indexing engine and store the project uses internally could not be confirmed from its public description. The discussion of index size here rests on published measurements in the SQLite FTS5 documentation rather than on that project
  • The actual time and power cost of local embedding is written without figures because no reliable measurement could be confirmed. The claim that compute is the bottleneck comes from the difference in method, not from a measurement
  • Whether Chrome's AI-powered history search automatically excludes Incognito or particular sites, and whether stored page contents can be deleted separately, could not be confirmed from that help page
  • Which provisions of the Personal Information Protection Act apply, and how, to an individual storing their own records on their own device could not be confirmed. The sensitive information list in Article 23 is cited only as a way to gauge the size of the risk, and no legal conclusion is drawn
  • No public data was found on user numbers or one-year retention for tools in this category. The claim that they end in being forgotten is an observation, not a statistic
