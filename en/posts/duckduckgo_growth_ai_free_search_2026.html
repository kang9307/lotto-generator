Users Are Fleeing AI Overviews - Why DuckDuckGo Grew 28% and What It Means for Search in 2026

Introduction: 28% Growth and Why People Are Leaving Google

If you asked me to name the single most interesting number in the English-language search market in May 2026, I would not hesitate: DuckDuckGo's roughly 28% jump in traffic. Why should the growth rate of a search engine with one or two percent market share matter at all? Because this growth is not the product of an ad campaign. It is a clear signal of user pushback against the Google search experience itself.

This post is my own analysis, inspired by a GeekNews (hada.io) discussion from May 28, 2026, titled "Google AI Overview vs DuckDuckGo." Rather than restate that thread, I want to re-read the data through a single lens: why and how user behavior is changing, and what that means for the wider search market.

One framing note up front. In an earlier article I covered survival strategies for content creators in the AI Overviews era. That piece took the creator's point of view. This one is the mirror image: the user's point of view. What are people tired of, where are they going, and how does that migration reshape the search landscape? This is not an SEO survival story. It is a story about the shifting geography of search.

1. The Data - What DuckDuckGo's 28% Growth Actually Means

Let us be cold about the number first. DuckDuckGo's daily queries are estimated to be up about 28% year over year. But this does not mean "Google is collapsing." Google's absolute share remains overwhelming, and the reality behind the 28% is closer to DuckDuckGo moving from roughly 1% to 1.3% of the market.

Where I find the real meaning is in the direction and pace of growth. For the past decade, DuckDuckGo's growth was driven mostly by privacy concerns. But the curve from late 2025 into the first half of 2026 has a different texture. When you track search trends and community sentiment, this round of growth looks like the result of a new driver layered on top of privacy: fatigue with AI Overviews.

  • Shifting inbound queries: searches like "search without AI," "google ai overview off," and "duckduckgo vs google 2026" rose alongside the traffic.
  • Clustered switch timing: the acceleration in DuckDuckGo growth overlaps with the periods when AI Overviews expanded.
  • Changing audience: the user base used to skew privacy-sensitive; recently it includes ordinary users who simply "want to see links again."

My reading is that this 28% is not a market-share metric but a thermometer of frustration. The growth rate of a small engine has become a mirror reflecting the satisfaction level of a giant one.

There is a second-order effect worth naming. When a product is dominant, its users rarely have a vocabulary for what bothers them - there is simply nowhere else to go, so the friction stays invisible. The moment a credible, frictionless alternative becomes one click away in the browser settings, latent dissatisfaction converts into measurable switching. DuckDuckGo did not suddenly become 28% better; it became 28% more chosen, because the cost of leaving Google finally dropped below the annoyance of staying. That is the quiet mechanic behind the headline number, and it is the part most market-share charts fail to capture.

I would also caution against over-reading a single quarter. Search behavior is sticky, defaults are powerful, and a 28% jump from a low base can decelerate quickly if Google ships even a modest concession. The honest framing is this: the number proves that a meaningful pool of users is now actively shopping for an alternative, not that the migration is irreversible. Whether it compounds depends almost entirely on how Google responds in the next two or three quarters.

2. Five Causes of AI Overview Fatigue (From the User's Side)

Why are people tired of AI summaries? Here are five causes I have distilled from my own use and from watching the users around me. These are not creator-side traffic losses. They are purely the friction a person feels while searching.

2.1 The answers are long and inaccurate

You search to confirm one quick fact, and the first thing on the screen is a verbose AI summary. Worse, that summary is sometimes wrong. When confidently phrased text presents incorrect information, the user pays a higher cost. The intent to "check quickly" turns into a burden to verify.

2.2 Sources are hard to trust

Because AI summaries synthesize multiple pages, it is unclear which sentence came from which source. To answer "where did this actually come from?", the user has to dig back through small citation links. On trust-critical topics like health, finance, and law, this friction grows.

2.3 You cannot find a link to click

The strength of traditional search was comparing several options at a glance. AI Overviews narrow everything to a single answer and push the links a user actually wants to choose below the fold. For someone like me who prefers to cross-check several sources and decide for myself, that layout is suffocating.

2.4 The answer feels like an ad

When an AI summary, top ads, and shopping cards stack up, users start to read the first screen of results not as "information" but as "a screen selling me something." Accumulate that subtle aversion and people start looking for "clean search" instead.

2.5 Privacy concerns

The more sophisticated AI search becomes, the more users wonder whether their queries are feeding model training or profiling. As the questions we type into AI become more personal and specific, demand for search that does not track us rises in parallel.

It is worth stressing that these five causes are cumulative, not isolated. Any one of them, on its own, is a minor irritation most people would tolerate. The reason 2026 feels like a tipping point is that all five now stack on the same screen at the same time: a long, possibly wrong answer, with murky sourcing, that buries the links you wanted, looks like an ad, and may be logging your most personal question. That is not five small annoyances - it is one compounding experience of "this screen is no longer working for me." The switch to an alternative is rarely triggered by a single bad result; it is triggered by the slow accumulation of that compounding feeling until one ordinary Tuesday a user finally changes their default and never looks back.

3. AI-Free Search Options Compared - DuckDuckGo, Brave, Kagi, Startpage, Mojeek

So where are people going? Here are five leading alternative search engines that either do not force AI or let you turn it off, along with my own impressions from using each.

  • DuckDuckGo: the most mainstream alternative. It builds results on the Bing index plus its own crawling, with privacy (no tracking) at the core. Its AI feature (Duck.ai) is separate and not forced into search. Lowest barrier to entry.
  • Brave Search: a rare engine with its own independent index. Paired with its browser, ad and tracker blocking is strong. AI summaries (Answer with AI) are optional.
  • Kagi: a paid, subscription search engine. Zero ads, and users can personally promote or demote specific sites in their results. Extremely well reviewed among power users and developers.
  • Startpage: a proxy that shows Google results anonymously. Quality is Google's, but tracking is removed. No AI summaries by default.
  • Mojeek: a fully independent crawler and index. The result volume is smaller than Google's, but it is the most "independent" in that it relies on no Big Tech index at all.

In my experience these five have quite different personalities. If you want familiarity, DuckDuckGo or Startpage; if you want independence, Brave or Mojeek; if you want quality and control, Kagi.

  • Startpage: ultimately depends on Google; limited independence.
  • Mojeek: smaller result volume and freshness compared to Big Tech.

5. Google's Response - AI Overview Toggles and a Possible Classic Mode

Google is certainly aware of this trend. Here are the response signals I have observed and the scenarios I consider plausible.

  • Web-only filter (udm=14): as the parameter that returns traditional links without AI Overviews became known, some users bookmarked it. Google has also surfaced a "Web" tab in places.
  • AI Mode separation: experimental moves to peel AI answers into a separate mode or tab, keeping default search relatively traditional.
  • Regional toggle experiments: tests adjusting how often AI Overviews appear in certain regions.
  • A possible classic mode: my prediction is that Google is unlikely to ship a full official "turn off AI" toggle, because AI answers are central to its ads and dwell-time strategy. But if user churn crosses a threshold, partial concessions are possible.

The core point is this: Google will not abandon AI, but it will leave a minimal exit open to slow churn. That is exactly why bypasses like udm=14 keep surviving.

6. The Developer and Power-User Strategy - Using Multiple Engines

Developers and power users, myself included, have already abandoned the premise of "one search engine". Search has become an act of switching tools by purpose.

  • Quick fact checks: DuckDuckGo's Instant Answers or bangs (!w, !gh, and so on) jump you straight to the destination.
  • Technical docs and code: Kagi or Brave for clean, ad-free results, or searching the domain directly.
  • Deep research: AI search (Perplexity, ChatGPT Search) to grab the big picture, then traditional search to verify the originals.
  • Privacy-sensitive searches: Startpage or Mojeek, with no tracking.

DuckDuckGo's bang commands in particular are my most-used feature. Type !gh fastapi and you jump to GitHub search; !mdn fetch takes you straight to the MDN docs. That experience of going directly to the destination with no AI summary in the way has, paradoxically, become more precious in the AI era.

The deeper point is that power users have stopped treating "the search box" as a single decision and started treating it as a routing layer. The real question is no longer "which search engine is best?" but "given this specific query, what is the shortest path to a trustworthy answer?" For a known destination, a bang or a direct domain wins. For a synthesis task, an AI tool wins. For something I need to verify or cite, a traditional results page with ten real links wins. Once you internalize that routing mindset, the entire "Google vs the rest" framing dissolves - no single engine is your home, and that detachment is itself a form of resilience against any one provider degrading.

For developers specifically, there is a practical workflow tip here. Setting a privacy-respecting, AI-optional engine as the browser default while keeping bangs and a couple of AI tools a keystroke away gives you the best of both worlds: the clean, link-first baseline for 80% of queries, and instant escalation to AI when a query genuinely needs synthesis. I have run exactly this setup for several months, and the friction I described in section 2 has essentially vanished from my daily work - not because AI disappeared, but because I stopped being forced into it for queries that never needed it.

7. Implications for Content Creators - Is AI-Free Search SEO Different?

If users move toward AI-free search, does SEO change for content creators? My analysis is "partly yes."

  • DuckDuckGo and Startpage: they use the Bing and Google indexes respectively, so existing Bing/Google SEO carries straight over. Almost no separate optimization needed.
  • Brave and Mojeek: as independent indexes, clear titles, clean HTML structure, and fast loading matter relatively more. Excessive scripts and popups hurt.
  • Shared upside: these engines do not absorb your body text into an AI summary, so clicks reach the actual site. For small sites, that is an opportunity rather than a threat.

My recommendation is simple. Do not chase AI search alone. Cover the fundamentals - register with Bing Webmaster Tools, mind speed, structure, and titles - and AI-free search traffic follows naturally. 8. The Korean Market - Naver vs Google AI vs DuckDuckGo

The Korean market is different again. In my analysis, Korean users feel the "AI-free search" trend relatively weakly.

  • Naver's share: still large at 30-40%. Naver has not rolled out a full Google-style AI Overview, so Korean users do not feel "AI fatigue" as strongly as English speakers.
  • Google's Korean share: first place at around 50%, but AI Overview deployment on Korean informational queries is more conservative than in English.
  • DuckDuckGo awareness in Korea: very low. With uneven Korean-language quality, it is still early to recommend to ordinary users.
  • Naver CUE: and HyperCLOVA X: Korean-style AI search is underway. A "Korean version of AI fatigue" may emerge later.

My view is that Korea is in the stage before AI-free search demand takes off in earnest. The English-language trend will likely arrive in Korea with a one- to two-year lag. In the meantime, how Naver folds AI in will be the fork in the road for the Korean version of this trend.

9. The Future of Privacy Search - Outlook for Late 2026

Here is the picture I expect for the second half of 2026.

  • AI-free settles as a category: beyond "privacy search," "clean search without AI" establishes itself as an independent value proposition.
  • The rise of paid search: subscription models like Kagi secure a meaningful niche audience. "Paying for search quality" becomes normalized.
  • Convergence with the browser wars: integrated strategies bundling search, browser, and AI (like Brave) strengthen.
  • Partial concessions from Google: as churn accumulates, minimal exits such as better access to the "Web" tab.
  • Reappraisal of independent indexes: the strategic importance of indexes that do not depend on Big Tech (Brave, Mojeek) grows.

10. The Risk - AI Search Has Real Strengths Too (A Balance)

Here I want to deliberately hold the balance. AI-free search is by no means superior in every case.

  • Complex synthesis questions: "compare A and B and recommend the one for situation C" - questions that require combining many sources - are overwhelmingly faster with AI search.
  • Exploratory learning: when you first grab the whole picture of an unfamiliar topic, an AI summary is an excellent starting point.
  • Language barriers: instantly summarizing and translating foreign-language material has huge value.
  • Accessibility: for users who struggle to read long documents, summaries are real help.

My conclusion is that this is not "AI search vs AI-free search" but "choosing the right tool for the situation." Quick facts, comparisons, and source verification belong to traditional search; synthesis, exploration, and summarization belong to AI search. Treating the two as enemies may itself be the anachronism.

11. Conclusion and Three Things You Can Try Today

DuckDuckGo's 28% growth looks like a small number, but inside it is a clear message: users do not want AI forced on them. The search market is slowly moving from an era where a single dominant player decided everything to a multipolar system where users choose tools by purpose.

Let me close with three things you can try this week.

  1. Try DuckDuckGo's bang commands. Use !gh, !w, and !mdn for a single day and feel the pleasure of jumping straight to the destination with no AI summary in the way.
  2. Bookmark Google's web-only results. Save a search URL with &udm=14 appended and use it "when you just want to see links."
  3. Run one alternative as your default for two weeks. Set Brave Search or Startpage as your default and compare, firsthand, what feels worse and what feels better.

The one-line lesson I took from changing how I search is this: a search engine is a tool, not a faith. There is no reason to be loyal to one. The moment you realize that freedom is the real meaning behind this 28% growth.
