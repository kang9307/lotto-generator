Car Camping Setup Guide: Sleeping in Your Vehicle Safely

Build the carbon monoxide and ventilation layer first, then add leveling and gear on top

Jul 27, 2026 Lifestyle

Read this first - never run a flame inside your vehicle

Whether you are inside the cabin or inside a docking tent attached to the tailgate, burning fuel indoors is absolutely forbidden. That includes charcoal, firewood, compressed briquettes, butane heaters and kerosene stoves. Carbon monoxide (CO) has no color and no smell, so you cannot detect it yourself, and once you are asleep you will miss the early warning signs such as headache and dizziness. Fatal incidents involving heating inside tents and vehicles are reported in South Korea every year.

This guide assumes flame-free car camping. It does not explain how to run heaters inside a vehicle. Instead it treats a carbon monoxide alarm and a guaranteed ventilation path as the first pieces of gear you buy.

Regional note: the regulations, fines and seasonal conditions described here are based on South Korean law and climate. The physics of carbon monoxide and the setup techniques apply anywhere, but you should check the rules that apply in your own country before you travel.

Introduction: Car camping starts with a safety design, not a shopping list

Car camping means sleeping inside your vehicle instead of pitching a tent. Setup and takedown are fast, it holds up well against wind and rain, and you never have to carry gear from the car to a pitch. Those advantages have made it popular with camping beginners and solo travelers alike. Yet almost every guide you find opens with a shopping list: leveling mats, blackout curtains, power stations. That order is backwards.

The one thing that separates car camping from tent camping is that you are sleeping inside a sealed box of metal and glass. Tent fabric breathes to some degree; a closed vehicle cabin exchanges far less air. The same volume of combustion gas reaches a dangerous concentration faster, and moisture cannot escape, which is why condensation is so much worse. So the correct preparation order is (1) eliminate carbon monoxide, (2) guarantee a ventilation path, (3) choose a legal location, (4) level the sleeping surface, (5) add power and convenience gear.

This article follows that order. It does not cover how to choose a tent or what to pack for general camping. That ground is already covered in Camping Gear for Beginners: The Complete Starter Guide. Here the focus is strictly on the variables that exist only when you sleep inside a car.

1. Carbon monoxide: the single most important topic in car camping

1.1 Why a car interior is more dangerous than a tent

Carbon monoxide forms when a carbon-based fuel burns incompletely because oxygen is limited. The problem is that your senses cannot detect it. According to the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency and university hospital medical references, carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless, and poisoning produces headache, dizziness, nausea, abdominal pain, vomiting and confusion, progressing to drowsiness, coma, seizures and respiratory arrest. Concentration figures commonly cited in the literature describe headache and dizziness around 400 ppm, headache, nausea and confusion after one to two hours at 1,000 ppm, and death within minutes at extreme concentrations.

A vehicle cabin is especially hazardous for three reasons. First, the volume is small. A passenger car holds only a few cubic meters of air, so concentration climbs fast. Second, it is relatively airtight. With doors and windows shut, air exchange is far lower than through tent fabric. Third, exposure happens while you sleep. Awake, you would feel the headache and step outside; asleep, you lose consciousness without ever registering the signal.

1.2 Which appliances actually caused the incidents

When the Korea Consumer Agency analyzed camping-equipment incidents, items used for heating or cooking by combustion accounted for roughly a third of all cases, the largest single share. Carbon monoxide poisoning made up a small fraction of the total count, but the agency flagged it specifically because every case led to severe outcomes such as death or loss of consciousness. Broken down by appliance, the pattern looked like this.

  • Braziers and fire pits (charcoal, firewood, briquettes) - the leading cause by a wide margin, with charcoal dominating. Glowing embers look calm and finished, yet they keep emitting carbon monoxide.
  • Gas heaters (butane, LPG) - second most common. The reasoning that it will only be on for a few minutes is exactly what recurs in the incident reports.
  • Kerosene and oil heaters - third. An appliance rated for outdoor use becomes dangerous the moment it comes indoors.
  • Gas-fired heated mats - the mat itself is fine, but placing the burner unit inside the cabin or the vestibule routes exhaust into the sleeping space.
  • Generators - Korean media have reported deaths where a generator was running beside or inside the sleeping space. A generator emits exhaust directly and must never operate near where anyone sleeps.

Fire service statistics also show that carbon monoxide incidents cluster in the winter months from November through January. The motivation to stay warm converts directly into risk. The conclusion is simple: when it is cold, the answer is not to light something, but to build a setup that keeps you warm without any flame at all.

1.3 A CO alarm is the item you buy before the mattress

Registered campgrounds in South Korea are subject to safety standards that include carbon monoxide alarms. Private vehicles are not covered, so you have to bring your own. An alarm typically costs somewhere in the range of a cheap accessory, making it one of the least expensive items on the list while contributing more to safety than anything else you will buy.

  • Placement - carbon monoxide has a density close to air and disperses through the cabin. Mount or place the alarm at or above head height, near where you sleep. An alarm rolling around the floor is one you will not hear.
  • Function check - press the test button before you leave and confirm both sound and battery. A unit that has sat flat for months is the same as no unit.
  • Certified products - choose a model carrying a recognized certification mark. The price difference is negligible.
  • An alarm is not a permit - owning one does not make indoor flames acceptable. It is a last-resort safety net against unexpected ingress from outside exhaust, a neighboring vehicle or an equipment fault.

2. Why you should not sleep with the engine running

2.1 Exhaust can find its way inside

Leaving the heater on all night sounds comfortable, but sleeping with the engine running is not advisable. Vehicle exhaust contains carbon monoxide, and when the car is stationary that exhaust tends to linger around the body of the vehicle. The following conditions raise the chance of it entering the cabin.

  • The tailpipe is partially blocked by snow, leaves, mud or sand. Heavy snowfall in winter is the classic scenario.
  • The car is parked with the exhaust facing a wall, so the plume reflects back and pools around the vehicle.
  • The tailgate or a rear door is left open for ventilation. Unlike driving, a stationary vehicle can draw exhaust back in through a rear opening.
  • An older or previously damaged vehicle has compromised underbody sealing.
  • Other vehicles or a generator are idling nearby in a crowded parking area.

On top of all this, a sleeping person cannot feel the early symptoms. Even if the probability is low, when the failure mode is death there is no reason to accept the bet.

2.2 Idling itself may be restricted

Under air-quality ordinances, local governments in South Korea designate idling restriction zones where running the engine beyond a set period can be penalized. The zones, permitted duration and seasonal exemptions vary by municipality, so confirm the local standard for wherever you are staying. Beyond the legal angle, engine noise and exhaust smell at night are a direct nuisance to nearby vehicles and residents, and that friction is a large part of why car camping keeps coming up as a target for regulation.

2.3 So what do you do when it is cold?

The answer is insulation and electricity, not flames and engines. Concretely: warm the cabin with the heater before bed, shut the engine off, hold your body heat with a sleeping bag rated with margin and an insulating mat, and if needed run a low-wattage electric blanket from a portable power station. Stop trying to generate heat and instead stop the heat you already have from escaping. That is the foundation of cold-weather car camping.

3. Ventilation: the lifeline of car camping

Even with no flame at all, you still need ventilation. Two adults breathing all night in a sealed cabin drive up carbon dioxide, which brings headaches, stuffiness and poor sleep quality, while the moisture in breath and sweat settles on glass and headliner as condensation. That damp bedding you find in the morning is almost always this.

3.1 Building a ventilation path

  • Open two windows diagonally - crack one front window and the opposite rear window so air flows through. Opening a single window creates no circulation.
  • Window screens - vehicle-specific mesh or magnetic screens let you keep windows open without inviting mosquitoes and insects. Essential in summer.
  • Rain visors - a deflector above the window lets you leave a gap open even in the rain.
  • Air circulation - point a small USB fan toward the ceiling to mix stagnant air. Power draw is low, so it barely dents a power station.
  • Never seal completely - blocking every gap with reflective panels for warmth is dangerous. Always leave a minimum opening.

3.2 Practical ways to cut condensation

  • Warm the cabin, then open the windows briefly to purge the humid air before you shut the engine off.
  • Keep wet clothes, towels and shoes out of the cabin. They are a moisture source.
  • Place a desiccant pack near the windshield.
  • In the morning, open everything and stand the mat up to dry the floor first. Condensation left under the mat grows mold.
  • Reflective panels move condensation onto the panel surface instead of the glass, which makes cleanup easier, but do not use them to seal every gap.

4. Leveling: a three-stage process for building a bed

Leveling means filling the steps and slopes that appear once the rear seats fold, to create a flat sleeping surface. Sleep quality in a car is decided almost entirely here. The process runs in three stages.

  1. Create the base - fold the rear seats and slide the front seats as far forward as they go. Look for the step between the seat backs and the cargo floor, and for any slope introduced by the folding mechanism.
  2. Fill the step - use a vehicle-specific foam mat set, a folding leveling kit, or a platform with adjustable legs to fill the void and cancel the slope. If this stage is sloppy, no amount of thick padding on top will stop your back from aching.
  3. Add cushioning and insulation - lay a self-inflating mat or foam topper over the top to absorb remaining bumps and block cold from the floor. In winter this layer matters as much as the sleeping bag.

Also check the parking angle. A vehicle tilted nose-up or nose-down leaves your head low or your feet raised, which ruins sleep and can bring on a headache. Choose the most level spot available, and use leveling ramps to raise one side if you have to.

5. Which vehicles work for car camping

There is no strict line between cars that can and cannot be slept in, but the difference in effort and comfort is real. 7. Power: portable stations and battery drain

7.1 Why you should not lean on the car battery

Running devices from the cigarette lighter or USB ports with the engine off can flatten the vehicle battery. Being unable to start in the morning and having to call for assistance is a common outcome, and repeated deep discharge visibly shortens the life of a lead-acid battery. In winter the cold reduces available capacity, leaving even less headroom. The rule, therefore, is to run camping loads from a portable power station kept separate from the vehicle electrical system.

7.2 Sizing the capacity

  • Add up power draw (W) multiplied by run time (h) for everything you will use overnight. A 40 W electric blanket for eight hours is roughly 320 Wh.
  • Assume usable capacity is lower than the rated figure. Allow twenty to thirty percent for conversion losses and cold-weather performance.
  • Confirm that rated output (W) exceeds the draw of your devices. A large battery with a small inverter still will not run a high-draw appliance.
  • Check the charging options: charging while driving, from mains at home, or from a solar panel, since this shapes how you operate the unit.
  • Batteries dislike heat. Do not leave the unit baking in a parked car in summer, and keep it out of direct sunlight.
  • Stop using any pack that is damaged or swollen. Thermal runaway in a confined cabin is a serious hazard.

8. Season by season: summer and winter fail differently

8.1 Summer - heat and insects

In summer the enemy is heat. Once the engine is off the air conditioning stops, and a sealed cabin warms quickly. A car parked in the sun during the day climbs well above ambient, so choose a sleeping spot with shade and airflow.

  • Window screens are not optional. If you cannot open the windows you will not sleep.
  • A USB circulator running off the power station, combined with diagonal ventilation, drops perceived temperature noticeably.
  • Use a windshield shade and side reflective panels to cut radiant heat.
  • Cabin temperature spikes at sunrise, so plan an early morning departure by default.
  • Food poisoning risk rises. Watch cooler temperature and utensil hygiene.
  • Sites beside streams and rivers carry a flash flood risk during overnight downpours. Check weather alerts and avoid low ground.

8.2 Winter - hypothermia and condensation

In winter the enemy is cold from the floor and moisture in the air. On the assumption that no flame is involved, build warmth like this.

  • Floor insulation comes first. Even a premium sleeping bag loses loft where your body compresses it, so always lay an insulating mat underneath.
  • Pick a sleeping bag rated with margin below the forecast low. How to read those ratings is covered in the beginner camping gear guide.
  • A low-wattage electric blanket is easier on a power station. Keep it on a low setting while you sleep.
  • Pack dry clothes plus a hat and socks. More heat leaves through your head and feet than most people expect.
  • A vacuum flask of hot water is a safe source of local warmth.
  • If snow is forecast, weigh the risk of being stranded first. Avoid mountain roads that are hard to clear and keep the fuel tank full.
  • Carry a scraper and dry towels for morning frost and condensation.

10. Legal and etiquette points to keep in mind

10.1 Sleeping in a car after drinking

Sleeping in a vehicle after drinking sits in a legally ambiguous zone. The general explanation is that a drink-driving offense under Korean road traffic law requires the vehicle to have actually been driven, yet in practice disputes are reported over whether the ignition was on, whether the gear selector was moved, and whether the vehicle moved at all. Falling asleep with the engine running to keep the heater on makes the situation more complicated. Because outcomes turn on the specific facts, this article does not state a conclusion. If it is a real concern for you, confirm with a lawyer or the relevant authority. The simplest way to avoid the argument entirely is to not start the engine after drinking and to keep the key out of reach.

10.2 Noise, light and waste

  • At night, avoid speakers and loud conversation. Noise is where much of the public backlash against car camping originated.
  • Do not aim searchlights or string lights at neighboring vehicles.
  • Take all waste with you. Never pour dishwater or food waste into drains or onto the ground.
  • Where there are no toilets, arrange a portable toilet and a disposal plan in advance.
  • Do not occupy more than one parking bay, and keep tarps and tables clear of walkways.
  • Avoid anything that looks like long-term squatting in a bay, which is part of the background to the recent legal changes.

11. Checklist before your first night

Work through the following the day before you leave. If any safety item is missing, postponing is the better call.

  1. Carbon monoxide alarm tested and battery confirmed
  2. Verify you are not carrying combustion heaters or stoves for indoor use (charcoal, briquettes, butane heaters, kerosene stoves, generators)
  3. Window screens or rain visors packed, diagonal ventilation path confirmed
  4. Check whether camping and cooking are permitted at your destination through the operator notice
  5. Lie down on the leveled surface as a rehearsal, ideally outside your home
  6. Confirm the sleeping bag rating has margin over the forecast low
  7. Power station fully charged, total device wattage calculated
  8. Jump starter or roadside assistance number on hand
  9. Fuel level, tire pressure and washer fluid checked
  10. Weather forecast reviewed, with plans changed for wind, rain, snow or cold alerts
  11. Nearest toilet, convenience store, fuel station and emergency medical facility located
  12. Location and return time shared with someone
  13. If the signal is weak, pack a power bank and offline maps
  14. Trash bags and a removal plan ready

Conclusion: follow the order and car camping becomes a safe way to travel

The appeal is obvious. No tent to pitch or strike, real resilience against wind and rain, and the freedom to stay somewhere you like and drive off in the morning. But that convenience rests on the condition that you are sleeping inside a sealed metal box. Risk begins the moment you carry tent-camping habits, particularly the habit of lighting something for warmth, into that box.

Three things, then. First, no flame of any kind inside the vehicle. Warmth comes from bag rating, insulating mats and a low-wattage electric blanket on a power station. Second, always leave a ventilation path and carry a carbon monoxide alarm. Buy it before the mattress. Third, stay where camping is permitted. Camping and cooking outside designated national park areas, in public parking lots, or outside designated beach areas can draw fines, and more importantly it shortens the life of car camping as a pastime.

With those three in place the rest is preference. How precisely you level, how large a power station you buy, whether you add a docking tent, are all decisions you can make as you go. For a first trip, pick a licensed campground close to home, stay a single night, and bring the minimum. One night is all it takes to learn exactly what you were missing.
