The Complete Guide to Lease Repair and Maintenance Disputes

From boilers, leaks, and mold to move-out restoration - sorting out who is responsible for what

Jul 10, 2026 Law & Legal

Introduction: Who Actually Pays for the Repair

Anyone who has lived in a rental long enough eventually runs into a breakdown of some kind — the boiler stops working, water starts dripping from the ceiling, or a patch of mold quietly spreads across a wall. The first question that comes to mind is almost always the same: "Is this on me, or is this on the landlord?" The answer is rarely as simple as it seems. The same problem can be resolved differently depending on whether it stems from ordinary aging of the building or from the tenant's own carelessness, and above all, the governing standard itself changes depending on the country and jurisdiction you live in, and the specific clauses written into your lease.

This article does not attempt to state any single country's statutes or case law as settled fact. Instead, it lays out the general principles that recur across many jurisdictions and rental practices, so that when a dispute arises you have a framework for thinking through the issue and a sense of what steps to take. The actual outcome in your situation must always be confirmed against your own lease and local law, and with a qualified professional where needed. This guide reflects principles and practices commonly referenced internationally as of July 2026.

1. General Principles of Repair Responsibility - Landlord vs. Tenant

The most widely used dividing line rests on two questions: "what broke" and "why it broke." Responsibility is typically split along these two axes.

1.1 What Falls to the Landlord

Across many legal systems and standard rental practices, the core structure and essential systems of a building are treated as the landlord's responsibility. This includes the roof and structural walls, heating and plumbing systems, electrical wiring, and maintaining the overall safety and habitability of the unit. The logic is that these components deteriorate naturally over time regardless of anything the tenant does, so it makes sense for the owner to bear the upkeep. In some jurisdictions, even a lease clause that tries to shift this burden entirely onto the tenant may have limited legal effect.

1.2 What Falls to the Tenant

Conversely, routine upkeep and minor consumable replacement, along with damage caused by the tenant's own negligence, are generally treated as the tenant's responsibility. This includes replacing lightbulbs, basic cleaning to prevent clogged drains, or a window broken through carelessness. There is also a view that if a tenant discovers a defect and fails to report it, allowing the damage to worsen, the tenant may bear some responsibility for that additional harm.

1.3 Why the Standard Differs by Country and Contract

One point deserves emphasis here. The division above is a general principle, and actual application varies significantly by country, local ordinance, and the specific clauses of an individual lease. Some jurisdictions protect a landlord's basic repair duty so strongly by law that it cannot be waived by contract, while some leases explicitly shift maintenance of specific equipment onto the tenant. Treat "generally speaking, this is how it works" as a starting point, but always confirm the final answer against your own lease wording and local law.

2. Five Common Repair Disputes

Here are the issues that most frequently generate conflict in real rental relationships.

2.1 Boiler and Heating Failures

Because a boiler or heating system is often classified as a core building system, natural aging or breakdown is generally treated as the landlord's responsibility. However, if the unit froze because no precautions were taken against freezing, or if the tenant kept using an obviously malfunctioning unit until it failed completely, tenant negligence can become a live issue.

2.2 Water Leaks

Leaks are a dispute where identifying the cause matters enormously. Structural causes — aging pipes, a failed waterproofing layer, or a problem from the unit above — are often treated as the landlord's responsibility. On the other hand, if the cause is something the tenant installed, like a poorly connected washing machine hose, the conclusion can shift.

2.3 Mold

Mold is a classic case where responsibility splits sharply depending on cause. Mold driven by a leak or a structural/insulation defect that causes condensation is often treated as the landlord's responsibility, while mold mainly caused by prolonged lack of ventilation or living habits may be viewed as the tenant's. Where mold itself is treated as a naturally occurring form of deterioration, it also intersects with the normal wear and tear concept in restoration disputes.

2.4 Built-in Appliance Failures

Built-in appliances included in the lease — a refrigerator, air conditioner, or washing machine — are typically treated as part of the leased property, so a natural failure during ordinary use is generally the landlord's responsibility. That said, appliances are frequently covered by separate lease clauses (who repairs what, exceptions for aged units), so always check the contract.

2.5 Wallpaper and Flooring Damage

Wallpaper and flooring naturally discolor or wear down over time, and this is often treated as ordinary wear and tear. In contrast, damage with clear negligence or intent — graffiti, cigarette burns, or pet damage — tends to be treated as the tenant's responsibility.

3. The Right Way to Request a Repair

When something actually needs fixing, how you request it can determine whether you're in a strong position if a dispute later arises.

3.1 Put It in Writing

Don't rely on a verbal conversation alone — request the repair through a method that leaves a record, such as text message, email, or certified mail. Clearly documenting the date of notice, the nature of the problem, and what you're asking for prevents a later dispute over whether a request was ever made.

3.2 Preserve Evidence

Photograph and video the damaged area in a way that preserves the date. For a leak, capture the water and the wet marks; for mold, document its extent and location. This evidence becomes the central material if the matter later escalates to mediation or litigation.

3.3 Set a Reasonable Deadline

When requesting a repair, it helps to specify a reasonable deadline for action. Without one, it becomes difficult to judge whether the landlord actually neglected the request, and if you later pursue remedies like self-repair or a rent reduction, you'll need to show that you gave adequate time and the landlord still failed to respond.

4. What to Do If the Landlord Ignores the Problem

If you've sent a written request with a reasonable deadline and the landlord still does nothing, the following remedies can be considered step by step. These reflect general procedural concepts used across various jurisdictions — actual availability and requirements depend entirely on local law.

4.1 Repair-and-Deduct

Some jurisdictions allow a tenant to hire someone to make the repair and then bill the landlord for the cost or deduct it from rent. Such remedies almost always come with strict conditions — prior written notice, a reasonable waiting period, and a cap on the amount that can be claimed. Acting without meeting these requirements can leave the tenant worse off, so confirm the exact procedure before proceeding.

4.2 Requesting a Rent Reduction

Where an unrepaired defect makes normal habitation difficult, many legal systems recognize a basis for reducing rent in proportion to the severity of the problem. However, the scope and requirements depend on how serious the defect is and how much it impairs habitability, so it is safer to negotiate with the landlord or go through the proper process rather than unilaterally paying less rent.

4.3 Dispute Mediation Bodies

Many countries and regions maintain bodies that mediate landlord-tenant disputes before they reach litigation. These bodies tend to involve less cost and time, and are well suited to hearing both sides and proposing a reasonable settlement. This is generally worth considering before filing a lawsuit.

4.4 Litigation - The Last Resort

If mediation fails to resolve the issue, or the harm is substantial, litigation may be the path forward. Because litigation is costly and time-consuming, having thorough records of your written notices along with photo and video evidence from the earlier stages makes a significant difference in how the case proceeds.

5. Tenant Restoration Duties and Normal Wear and Tear

Just as contentious as repair responsibility is the question of restoration obligations at move-out.

5.1 The Scope of Restoration Duty

When a lease ends, a tenant is generally obligated to return the unit to a condition similar to how it was at move-in. This does not mean restoring it to the exact state it was in on day one. Across many legal systems and practices, it is commonly accepted that a tenant is not required to reverse every natural change that occurs during ordinary occupancy.

5.2 Normal Wear and Tear Is Generally Excluded

Gradual changes such as faded wallpaper, a natural loss of sheen on flooring, or a small nail hole — commonly referred to as normal wear and tear — are, as a general principle, often excluded from a tenant's restoration obligation. By contrast, graffiti, intentional damage, severe pet-related destruction, or harm that worsened because it was neglected tends to be treated as the tenant's responsibility. The common view is that the burden of proving damage exceeds normal wear and tear — and thus justifies a charge — typically rests with the party making that claim, which is usually the landlord.

5.3 How to Avoid Restoration Disputes

The most effective way to prevent this kind of dispute is to document the condition of the unit with photos and video at both move-in and move-out. Recording existing defects or worn areas at the start of the tenancy prevents a situation where the tenant is later blamed for pre-existing conditions. It is also a good habit to request an itemized statement for any deposit deductions, along with the supporting documentation, in writing.

6. Always Read the Contract's Special Clauses

Everything above describes general principles that mostly apply when a lease is silent on the point. In practice, leases very often include specific clauses — "the tenant is responsible for air conditioner repairs," "wallpaper must be redone by the tenant every two years." Such clauses are frequently enforceable under freedom of contract, but as noted earlier, a landlord's basic duty regarding the building's core structure and safety often cannot be fully waived by a clause alone in many jurisdictions. Before signing, read every maintenance and repair clause carefully, and clarify any ambiguous wording before you sign rather than after — this is the single most reliable way to reduce future disputes.

7. Conclusion - Documentation Is Your Strongest Argument

At its core, a lease repair dispute comes down to two questions: "whose responsibility is this," and "can it be proven." The general principle points toward landlords bearing responsibility for core structure and systems, and tenants bearing responsibility for routine upkeep and negligence-caused damage, but the precise answer always depends on local law and your lease's special clauses. The practical keys to avoiding a dispute — or coming out ahead in one — are three simple habits: written notice, photo and video evidence, and setting a reasonable deadline. Following these three habits alone puts you in a far stronger position in most repair disputes.
