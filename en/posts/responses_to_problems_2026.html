Three Ways Organizations Respond to Problems Without Solving Them

Shifting, Preserving, and Creating New Problems - and How to Tell a Real Fix from a Fake One

Jul 20, 2026 Trends & Culture

Introduction: Why Problems Don't Disappear - They Change Address

Watch a problem that never quite goes away inside an organization long enough, and something odd becomes visible. It is rarely because anyone is lazy. Meetings keep happening, owners keep getting assigned, status reports keep getting filed. Yet the problem stays exactly where it was, or quietly becomes someone else's problem, or reappears in a new shape right where it was supposedly fixed. A piece that recently surfaced on GeekNews (news.hada.io) put a name to this familiar frustration.

The author breaks down what organizations actually do when they face a problem - not solving it, but responding to it in one of three ways: pushing it elsewhere, preserving it, or creating a new problem in the process of addressing the old one. All three make a problem look like it disappeared without actually reducing it. This piece works through each pattern, asks why organizations keep falling into them, and lays out - as of July 2026 - how to tell a real fix from one of these three imitations.

1. Pushing the Problem Around

1.1 The Trap of Local Optimization

The most common response isn't eliminating a problem but relocating it. Tell a call center to cut average handling time, and agents rush calls, which increases repeat calls from customers whose issue was never actually resolved. The team's own number improves while the customer now calls three times instead of once. Speed up a warehouse's shipping metric by trimming inspection, and the returns team absorbs the difference. What these cases share is that each individual acted rationally within the goal they were handed. The failure isn't in the people - it's in the assumption that optimizing each piece locally adds up to a better whole.

1.2 Why Shifting Is So Tempting

Problem-shifting persists for a simple reason: it improves a visible metric quickly, blurs accountability, and typically dumps the fallout onto someone else's KPI. The very structure of managing an organization through quarterly targets and team-level scorecards creates this incentive. From a manager's seat, improving a number inside your own span of control is the safest move available. Fixing the whole system sits outside your authority, and even attempting it rarely earns credit. The problem doesn't vanish - it just moves to a corner nobody is measuring.

2. Preserving the Problem

2.1 The Shirky Principle

The second pattern is quieter. Instead of solving a problem, an organization unconsciously settles into keeping it in a manageable, chronic state. Media theorist Clay Shirky's insight, now known as the Shirky Principle, names this precisely: institutions tend to preserve the very problem they present themselves as the solution to. This isn't malicious conspiracy. When a team, department, or role exists for a long time to handle a specific problem, its budget, headcount, and reason for existing quietly come to depend on that problem persisting. If the problem truly disappeared, so would the reason the team was funded.

2.2 How a Workaround Hardens Into Permanence

In software organizations this pattern wears a familiar face. An incident hits, and someone bolts on a workaround at 2am. A ticket gets filed to fix the root cause next sprint. But once the workaround quiets the alarm, that ticket sinks to the bottom of the backlog. Months later nobody remembers why the workaround exists, and touching it feels too risky because no one knows what else it's holding up. The problem hasn't gone away - it's frozen inside the codebase, preserved. Once the whole organization starts making decisions that assume the problem is still there, removing it becomes the more dangerous move.

3. Creating a New Problem

3.1 Weinberg's Law

The third pattern is the most paradoxical: you genuinely fix a problem, and things don't actually get better. Consultant Gerald Weinberg captured this in one line - eliminate your number-one problem, and your number-two problem gets promoted. Organizational attention and resources are finite, and they always flow toward whatever currently looks most urgent. Removing the top problem just redirects that attention to the next one; it doesn't shrink the total amount of trouble in the system.

3.2 Solutions That Manufacture Their Own Problems - a Tech-Org Lens

In technology organizations there's an extra layer: the fix itself becomes the source of a new problem. Move to microservices to solve a deployment-speed problem, and you inherit inter-service networking, observability, and cascading-failure complexity you didn't have before. Add an approval step to catch mistakes, and the paperwork and wait time needed to clear that step becomes the new bottleneck. Bring in automation to cut repetitive work, and now someone has to maintain the automation and handle its edge cases. Media critic Neil Postman's question - what problem does this solution create? - is exactly what technology organizations should ask before adopting any new tool or process, not after.

4. The Shared Root Beneath All Three

The three patterns look different on the surface but share the same roots. First, incentives: organizations mostly evaluate people by how much their own metric improved, not by how much the underlying problem shrank. When the metric and the real problem diverge, people follow the metric. Second, visibility: shifting a problem makes it invisible from where it used to sit, and preserving a problem makes it invisible through sheer familiarity. Invisible problems lose priority by default. Third, politics: actually eliminating a problem usually means touching someone's authority, budget, or reason for existing, which invites internal resistance. Fourth, short-term pressure: in a structure that demands quarterly or annual proof of progress, a workaround that moves a number today looks far more attractive than the long work of fixing a root cause.

5. Spotting the Fake Fix

A few signals make these patterns visible. If one team's metric improves while another team's complaints rise at the same time, suspect shifting. If the same workaround, the same exception-handling code, the same "temporary" fix has sat untouched for several quarters, suspect preservation. If adopting a solution required a new recurring meeting, a new approval gate, or a newly dedicated owner, suspect creation. One question applies across all three: did this action reduce the total amount of the problem, or just change its location or shape?

6. Moving Toward a Real Fix

Making the shift to a genuine fix takes four things working together, as I see it. First, measure toward the root cause - track a metric tied to the underlying issue (repeat-contact rate, rollback frequency) separately from the surface metric (handling time, deploy frequency), so shifting and real improvement don't get confused with each other. Second, give the problem clear ownership - when it spans multiple teams, name a single owner with authority to cross those boundaries, so the fix targets the actual cause rather than just the next handoff point. Third, simplify - most solutions that create new problems do so by adding complexity, so "does this make the system simpler or more complex" belongs in the decision criteria itself. Fourth, be honest about scope - accept that not every problem can be solved at once, and put real resources behind the few that matter most, rather than lightly touching twenty problems and shifting or preserving most of them.

7. What Leadership and Culture Have to Do With It

In the end, breaking these three patterns depends less on individual willpower than on how an organization is designed. A leader who, on hearing that a metric improved, makes a habit of asking where it got worse leaves less room for shifting to hide. Putting an expiration date and a mandatory review on every workaround keeps preservation from calcifying. Making "what new problem will this create" a formal question before adopting any solution at least brings creation into a predictable range. Culturally, an organization needs to be a place where admitting a problem exists isn't read as evidence of incompetence. The moment hiding or shifting a problem feels safer than naming it, an organization slides into one of these three patterns almost automatically.

Conclusion: Solving Problems Is a Practice, Not a Finished State

Solving a problem is less a finished state than an ongoing practice. Organizations rarely reach a moment where a problem is completely gone; most real progress comes from repeatedly noticing and peeling back the three temptations - shifting, preserving, creating - a little at a time. As of July 2026, as more organizations go remote and distributed and lean harder on dashboards and metrics, the conditions for all three patterns have, if anything, gotten easier to trigger: blurrier ownership boundaries make shifting easier, and more dashboards make it easier to manage only what's visible. Which is exactly why this insight matters more now, not less. The next time you hear that a problem has been "solved," ask one question: did it actually shrink, or did it just move?
