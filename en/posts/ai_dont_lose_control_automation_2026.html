Don't Let AI Take the Wheel — Lessons From Two Automation Failures

What over-delegating to AI coding agents actually looks like — and the practical principles for staying in control

2026-06-27 Tech & IT

Introduction: The Faster the AI, the Harder It Is to Check

As of June 2026, AI coding agents are genuinely capable. Tools like Claude Code, Cursor, and Copilot Workspace can modify multiple files, run tests, and propose commits from a single instruction. The productivity gains are real. But as speed increases, something quietly degrades: the human verification loop.

A post on GeekNews earned 30 points under the title "Don't Let AI Take the Wheel." The author described two automation projects built with Claude Code that both failed. An algorithmic trading system went 0 wins and 8 losses, losing 3% of starting capital. An auto-publishing blog received reader criticism: "Do you even read what you publish? I don't." The common thread in both failures was simple: the author handed off work in domains they couldn't evaluate, and never verified the outputs.

This piece uses those failures as a starting point for a broader analysis. What structural traps activate when you over-delegate to an AI agent? What do you lose when you retreat from "in the loop" to "on the loop"? And what practical principles keep you in control while still using AI effectively? The position here is not anti-AI. The concern is specifically automation without control — not the tool itself, but the absence of a human in the judgment seat.

1. What the Two Failures Reveal

1.1 Trading: Automation Without Evaluation Ability

The first failure was deploying AI-generated trading strategies without reviewing them. The result: 0 wins, 8 losses, 3% capital drawdown. The code ran correctly. The problem was not code quality — it was that the author could not tell whether the strategy made sense. Without domain knowledge of trading, there is no way to distinguish a coherent AI output from a nonsensical one. The AI may have produced internally consistent logic; whether that logic works in actual markets is a completely separate question.

This is the classic pattern of automation without evaluation ability. To accept or reject AI outputs, you need the minimum knowledge to recognize when something is wrong. Without that, automation does not filter errors — it accelerates them.

1.2 Publishing: Automation Without Critical Reading

The second failure is slightly different in character. AI-generated content was published without review, and a reader noticed: the writing described experiences the author had never had as if they were personal. The AI produced plausible-sounding text; the author published it as their own voice.

This is automation without critical reading ability. The skill required here is not grammar or writing — it is the ability to read AI output critically and ask: does this accurately represent what I actually know and have actually experienced? Without that step, the author's byline became a wrapper for AI pattern-matching, not genuine perspective.

1.3 The Common Structure: Quiet Transfer of Control

Set the two cases side by side and the pattern becomes clear. You start using AI as a tool. The tool performs well, so you begin skipping review steps — first occasionally, then routinely. At some point you are only consuming results, not evaluating them. Decision-making authority has drifted from you to the AI without any explicit handoff. This is what control loss looks like in practice: not a dramatic moment of surrender, but a gradual habit of skipping verification until nothing is left to verify.

2. In the Loop vs. On the Loop — What Changes

2.1 The Distinction

AI governance discussions often use two positions. In the loop means a human participates directly in each decision step. On the loop means AI executes autonomously while a human monitors and intervenes when needed. On the loop has higher throughput — the AI runs continuously while the human focuses on larger context. But the structure carries a critical requirement: the human must know what to look for and must be capable of acting when something is wrong.

2.2 The Automation Bias Trap

The problem is that on-the-loop positions are prone to automation bias — the psychological tendency to stop critically evaluating outputs when a system appears reliable. The more smoothly things run, the less vigilant the human becomes. Research in 2026 finds that enterprise AI agents fail nearly 70% of multi-step tasks in simulation tests. And nearly half of enterprises that deployed multi-agent systems experienced a Severity 1 incident involving hallucinated data or unauthorized autonomous behavior — despite having oversight processes formally in place. Teams assume their monitoring will catch problems. They rarely practice what catching a problem actually looks like.

2.3 Monitoring Without Intervention Ability Is Theater

The real risk of on-the-loop is not the position — it is the capability gap. You can watch a dashboard all day; if you do not know what anomalous looks like, you cannot intervene. The EU AI Act (Article 14, enforcement August 2026) and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework both require human oversight that is trained, measurable, and demonstrable — not just nominally present. A poorly trained reviewer approving AI outputs they do not understand is worse than no checkpoint at all: it adds false confidence without adding real safety.

3. Three Automation Patterns That Produce Failures

3.1 No Scope Boundaries: AI Can Do Anything, Including Things You Did Not Intend

As AI coding agents become more capable, what they can do expands without a corresponding expansion of your awareness. Code modification can drift into deployment scripts. Content generation can drift into immediate publication. Without explicit boundaries on what the AI may and may not do, the blast radius of an error grows unpredictably. Irreversible actions — deploy, publish, trade, send — carried out without a scope limit are where single mistakes become serious damage.

3.2 No Checkpoints: Problems Are Invisible Until the End

Long automation pipelines without intermediate review points mean you only discover errors after everything has run. In the trading case, a checkpoint after the first loss — a structured pause to analyze what happened — would have prevented losses two through eight. In the publishing case, a review step between generation and publication would have meant the author caught the problem before readers did. Checkpoints do not slow automation. They prevent error accumulation from compounding into larger failures.

3.3 Compounding Error Loops: Early Mistakes Corrupt Everything Downstream

When AI agents chain multiple steps, errors at step one are inherited by every subsequent step. Optimizing code around a flawed strategy produces more efficient execution of a wrong plan. Applying SEO to inaccurate content reaches more people with the same inaccuracy. Automation amplifies everything — quality and error alike. This is why validation at the earliest stage of any pipeline has disproportionate impact on the final outcome.

4. Practical Principles for Keeping Control

Enough diagnosis. Here are three actionable principles that apply in 2026 for anyone using AI coding agents or automation pipelines.

4.1 Build Evaluation Ability First, Automation Tools Second

Principle: Before automating a domain, acquire the minimum knowledge needed to recognize when outputs are wrong.

This is the lesson both failures point to. To delegate trading to AI, you need to be able to read a strategy's logic and risk metrics at a basic level. To delegate writing to AI, you need to be able to tell whether a claim is accurate and whether the voice is genuinely yours. Evaluation ability is not the commitment to do everything yourself — it is the commitment to remain the final arbiter of AI output. Without it, faster automation just means getting further from the right answer before anyone notices.

4.2 Set Explicit Scope Limits and Gate Every Irreversible Action

Principle: Define in writing what the AI may touch, and require human approval before any action that cannot be undone.

When using Claude Code or similar agents, scope constraints stated in the instruction make a practical difference. "Modify only this file," "create the draft but do not publish," "propose the trade but wait for my approval." At a structural level this is the harness concept: defining system-level boundaries between what the AI can execute autonomously and what requires human action. This is a design question, not a trust question. Even well-calibrated tools extend further than intended when scope is unspecified.

4.3 Insert Checkpoints Into Every Pipeline Explicitly

Principle: At each pipeline stage, design an explicit review point — and make it structurally impossible for the next stage to run until the checkpoint is cleared.

This looks like losing speed. In practice it reduces the cost of error recovery, which is almost always larger than the time saved by skipping review. For a trading system: make the first N trade outcomes a mandatory daily human review, with a circuit breaker that halts the system if losses cross a threshold. For publishing: make human review between generation and publication a structural gate that cannot be bypassed. The principle is not a compromise between speed and control — it is the recognition that speed without control produces expensive corrections.

5. What It Actually Means to Use AI as a Tool

5.1 Colleague vs. Agent

The author of the original post framed it clearly: "AI is a colleague, not an agent." A colleague contributes ideas and does work, but final decisions and their consequences stay with you. An agent receives the decision itself. The moment you treat AI as an agent rather than a colleague, accountability becomes blurry. Was it you or the AI that deployed the code, published the post, executed the trade? That ambiguity makes post-failure analysis harder and improvement slower.

5.2 AI Executes; Humans Judge

The functional division: AI is good at fast draft generation, pattern-matching at scale, and connecting large amounts of context. Humans need to maintain the judgment of what is correct, the final interpretation of context, and the accountability for outcomes. The smoother and faster the AI tool, the more effort it takes to consciously hold that division. The feeling of effortless AI execution is precisely the condition under which it is easiest to let judgment slip away unnoticed.

5.3 Control and AI Use Are Not Opposites

To be explicit about the position here: AI coding agents are powerful, and used well they substantially increase what one person can do. But that utility depends on the human being able to evaluate outputs, bound the scope of execution, and catch errors before they cascade. Control is not the opposite of AI use — it is the condition that makes AI use sustainable. Failing twice and stopping is not the same as building checkpoints and improving continuously. The latter is both more controlled and more productive.

6. Where to Start Today

6.1 Audit Your Evaluation Ability in Each Domain You Are Automating

For each area where you currently delegate to AI: can you tell when the output is wrong? If not, that knowledge gap is the first thing to close — before expanding automation, not after. This does not require becoming a domain expert. It requires reaching the threshold where you can recognize a bad result. Below that threshold, automation speed is inversely correlated with the quality of your outcomes.

6.2 Find Every Irreversible Step in Your Current Pipelines

Map the steps where an error cannot be easily undone: deployments, publications, financial transactions, sent communications. Check whether there is a human review gate before each one. If not, add it now. This single change substantially reduces the worst-case cost of any AI error in your stack.

6.3 Automate in Small Units and Validate Each Before Expanding

Rather than automating a full pipeline at once, start with the smallest meaningful unit. Evaluate that unit's AI output quality, understand its failure modes, and develop your pattern for catching errors. Then extend scope. This is slower in the short term and much faster in reaching reliable automation — because each expansion builds on verified ground rather than accumulated unexamined assumptions.

Conclusion: The Faster the Tool, the More Deliberate the Judgment Needs to Be

AI coding agents in 2026 are genuinely capable — and that capability is paradoxically what makes control loss more dangerous, not less. The smoother the tool runs, the harder it is to notice when you have stopped judging and started just consuming. The lesson from both failures is not to avoid AI, but to recognize that the faster the automation, the more deliberately the human judgment role needs to be maintained.

Evaluation ability first. Scope limits by design. Checkpoints in the pipeline. With those three things in place, AI automation becomes an expansion of your capability rather than a transfer of it.
