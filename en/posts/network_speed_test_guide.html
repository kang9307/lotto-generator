How to Test Your Internet Speed Correctly and Find What Is Slowing You Down

Measure properly, isolate the bottleneck in order, and know when the line itself is at fault

August 5, 2026 Tech & IT

First decide whether the line is slow or the measurement is wrong

When the internet feels slow, almost everyone does the same thing: open a speed test site, hit the button, see a disappointing number, and call the provider. The trouble is that more often than not, that number describes the test conditions rather than the connection. A laptop sitting in a back bedroom, attached to the 2.4 GHz band, with cloud sync quietly uploading in the background, produces a figure that says nothing useful about the line you are paying for.

Speed problems are not solved by reading one number. They are solved by eliminating one segment at a time. Between the provider's exchange and the pixels on your screen there are at least six links in the chain: the access line, the optical modem or ONT, the router, the cable or the Wi-Fi hop, the network chip in your device, and the browser running the test. Any single one of them can cap the whole path, and the whole path then converges on that cap. Order is what makes the difference. This guide walks through measuring correctly, narrowing the cause in sequence, and fixing each cause in exactly that order.

If your real problem is coverage across a large home rather than raw line speed, the complete mesh Wi-Fi guide covers that separately, and if you are worried about safety on networks you do not control, using public Wi-Fi safely is the right page. What follows is about one question only: is my own line delivering what it should?

A five-minute routine that tells you whether your line is healthy

A measurement becomes meaningful the moment you control its conditions. Done in this order, the whole thing takes about five minutes.

  1. Connect the computer to the router with an Ethernet cable. If the laptop has no LAN port, use a USB Ethernet adapter. This single step removes every Wi-Fi variable at once.
  2. Stop all other traffic in the house. Someone else's stream, a game download, a NAS backup, a smart TV pulling a firmware update — pause them all.
  3. Quiet the test machine itself. Close OneDrive, Google Drive and iCloud sync, pause Windows Update, quit Steam and Epic downloads, and exit video conferencing apps.
  4. Turn off VPNs and proxies. A corporate VPN routes your traffic through the company network and returns a figure unrelated to your actual line.
  5. Leave one browser window open. Extensions, especially ad blockers and security add-ons, can interfere with the test script, so a private window is the safer choice.
  6. Run the test at least three times, across different hours. Weekday afternoons and the 9 to 11 pm peak genuinely differ. If evenings alone collapse, that pattern is itself a diagnostic clue.
  7. Screenshot each result with the date and time. If you later dispute the service with your provider, having this record changes the conversation entirely.

If that controlled test returns 80 to 90 percent of your advertised plan speed, the line is fine. If a wired test lands anywhere near half the advertised figure, that is where the real diagnosis begins.

If a 1 Gbps line delivers 125 MB per second, nothing is wrong

The most common misunderstanding about speed is also the most anticlimactic once explained: units. The Mbps and Gbps printed on your plan use a lowercase b for bits. The MB/s shown in a file copy dialog or a download manager uses an uppercase B for bytes. One byte is eight bits, so the two differ by a factor of eight.

A 1 Gbps line therefore tops out at 125 MB per second in theory. Subtract TCP/IP header and Ethernet frame overhead and the realistic ceiling sits around 110 to 118 MB/s. If a file is arriving at 110 MB/s and you have been thinking "I pay for a gigabit, why am I only getting 110," you were in fact using virtually the entire line. Why speed test sites disagree, and which one to use when

Test the same line at the same minute with two tools and you get two answers. That is not sloppiness. The tools are measuring different things.

Speedtest-style services connect to dedicated, well-peered servers and open multiple parallel TCP connections to push the link as hard as it will go. The result lands close to the physical ceiling of your connection. Fast.com instead downloads dummy media directly from Netflix's Open Connect CDN, which puts it much closer to what video streaming actually feels like. A fast.com figure below your Speedtest figure is completely normal, and the gap between them is informative in itself: it separates "what my line can do" from "what video traffic actually gets."

Some countries add a public option. In South Korea, the Ministry of Science and ICT and the National Information Society Agency (NIA) run a public internet quality measurement service. Being a neutral third party is the point of it, though wired testing there needs a dedicated measurement program installed rather than running in the browser.

Comparing the three and noticing that A is fine while B is not is already diagnostic information.

Four numbers that matter more than the download figure

Most people read exactly one thing on a results screen: the large download number. Yet the discomfort you actually feel usually lives in the small numbers beside it.

  • Upload. Your face breaking up on a video call, a cloud backup that never finishes, your inputs registering late in a game — these are typically upload starvation. Unless you are on a symmetric fibre plan, upload can sit far below download, so check what your contract actually promises.
  • Ping (latency). The round-trip time for data. Opening a single web page involves dozens of request cycles, so a high ping makes browsing feel sluggish no matter how wide the pipe is. On a wired connection to a nearby server, single digits to the low twenties in milliseconds is healthy.
  • Jitter. How much the ping fluctuates. Even with a low average, high jitter breaks up voice calls and causes momentary rubber-banding in games. It is the signature symptom of an interference-heavy Wi-Fi environment.
  • Packet loss. The share of data that never arrives. Anything above one percent noticeably degrades video calls and gaming, and because TCP retransmits what is lost, throughput drops along with it.

To find out where the delay and loss originate, trace the route from a terminal. On Windows that is tracert 8.8.8.8; on macOS and Linux, traceroute 8.8.8.8. If the first hop, your own router, is already slow to answer, the problem is inside the house. If times spike only once the path enters the carrier network, the problem is the line. For a continuous view, run ping -t 8.8.8.8 on Windows for a few minutes and watch the loss percentage accumulate.

A five-step sequence that narrows the cause

Diagnosis works by repeating the same test while removing one variable at a time. Follow this order and the culprit usually surfaces within three steps.

  1. Test wired. If you get 80 percent or more of the advertised rate, the line and the router are cleared. The problem is Wi-Fi or the device.
  2. Bypass the router and test against the modem. If wired through the router was still slow, plug the computer directly into the optical modem and retest. A good result here means the router is the culprit. (If your connection uses PPPoE, you may need to enter the account details on the computer.)
  3. Test with a different device. Put another laptop or desktop on the same cable. If everything else is fast and only one machine is slow, blame that machine's network adapter, driver or security software.
  4. Swap the cable and the port. Try a different Ethernet cable and a different LAN port on the router. This resolves the issue far more often than people expect.
  5. Test at a different hour. Fine in the afternoon but collapsing every evening points to congestion somewhere upstream, or to a shared line in a multi-unit building.

The bottleneck is nearly always Wi-Fi

The faster your plan, the more extreme this becomes. University of Chicago researchers ran a study across more than 50 homes for nearly two years, measuring the Wi-Fi hop and the access link simultaneously and collecting over 13,000 paired samples. In households whose access link exceeded 800 Mbps, the home wireless network was the bottleneck 100 percent of the time. If you buy a gigabit plan and connect only over Wi-Fi, a large share of the bandwidth you pay for never reaches the device at all.

What standards and bands realistically deliver

The figure on the box assumes the best possible combination of antenna count, channel width and modulation. Real environments fall well short of it because of protocol overhead, the half-duplex nature of shared airtime, and retransmissions. A Wi-Fi 5 (802.11ac) product labelled 1.3 Gbps that delivers a couple hundred megabits in daily use is not defective; that is the normal range.

Wi-Fi changes that genuinely help

  • Confirm you are actually on 5 GHz. With a single merged SSID, devices routinely settle onto 2.4 GHz and stay there. At least for testing, split out a dedicated 5 GHz SSID and join it explicitly.
  • Reduce the number of walls between router and device. 5 GHz weakens noticeably through even one wall. Test within about three metres, in line of sight.
  • Get the router off the floor, out of the corner, off metal shelving. An astonishing number of routers live behind a television or inside a cabinet. Waist height or above, in the open, is the baseline.
  • Change the channel. In apartment blocks packed with routers, channels 1, 6 and 11 are the only non-overlapping set on 2.4 GHz. The 5 GHz band has more room, so force a fresh auto-scan.
  • Do not widen the channel blindly. Running 40 MHz on 2.4 GHz increases interference with neighbours and can make things slower. In crowded areas 20 MHz is the stable choice.
  • Keep distance from microwaves, cordless phones and Bluetooth gear. They all share the 2.4 GHz band.
  • Update the router firmware. Performance fixes and security patches arrive together.
  • If coverage is still the problem, change the hardware layout. Beyond a point this stops being about placement and becomes about adding nodes, which is the subject of the mesh Wi-Fi guide.

One bad cable turns a gigabit line into 100 Mbps

If a wired test stalls precisely around 100 Mbps, the physical layer has almost certainly negotiated 100BASE-TX. There are three usual reasons: the cable grade is too low, the conductors inside are damaged, or the port itself is a 100 Mbps port.

Old four-conductor (two-pair) cables are a frequent offender. Fast Ethernet works on two pairs, but gigabit needs all four pairs, all eight conductors. A cable that looks perfectly fine can quietly refuse to link at gigabit because it only has four wires inside. Look into the transparent part of the connector and you can count them.

After the cable, look at the port. Checking it is straightforward.

  • On Windows, open the adapter properties or run Get-NetAdapter and confirm the link speed reads 1 Gbps. If it says 100 Mbps, the cable or the port is at fault.
  • Router LAN port specifications. Some budget and older routers have a gigabit WAN port but 100 Mbps LAN ports, or 100 Mbps throughout. Check the spec sheet.
  • USB Ethernet adapters. A USB 2.0 adapter cannot deliver full gigabit by specification. Verify it is plugged into a USB 3.0 or faster port.
  • Physical damage. A run crushed in a doorway, a sharp kink, or a broken retaining clip will drop the link rate or cause intermittent dropouts.
  • Router processing capacity. Even with gigabit ports, an older router with weak NAT throughput cannot pass a full gigabit. Comparing a direct-to-modem test with a through-router test exposes this immediately.

When the device cannot keep up with the line

Sometimes the line, the router and the cabling are all healthy and one specific laptop is still slow. These are the things to suspect.

  • Wireless adapter generation. Built-in radios in laptops five or six years old are often single-stream or early Wi-Fi 4 and 5 silicon. However good the router is, the ceiling is on the client side.
  • Drivers. Updating to the manufacturer's current driver produces visible gains in some cases, especially where a generic driver was pushed in by an operating system update.
  • Power management. Battery-saving modes reduce wireless adapter output. For testing at least, set a high-performance power plan and disable the adapter's permission to power down.
  • Security software. Real-time traffic inspection and web filtering can eat throughput. Disable them briefly, retest, and compare — then turn them back on.
  • Browser and extensions. The test runs inside a browser, so the environment shapes the number. Cross-check with a private window or a native app.
  • CPU and storage limits. On very low-spec machines, simply handling a fast line is work. A slow large download is sometimes a disk write bottleneck rather than a network one.

If everything above is clean, suspect the line

When you have satisfied every condition — direct to the modem, wired, background traffic silenced — and still see roughly half the advertised rate, the problem is likely outside the house rather than inside it. At this stage, work through the following.

  • Power-cycle the modem or ONT. Unplug it, wait a full minute, plug it back in. A stuck session that had been throttling throughput often clears here.
  • Inspect the optical connection. A loose or sharply bent fibre patch cord behind the modem introduces signal loss.
  • Compare against a different line at the same moment. Tether to a phone on mobile data and test the same site. If tethering is dramatically faster, the fixed line is clearly implicated.
  • Shared infrastructure in the building. In older buildings the in-house wiring can be the bottleneck. If the slowdown is evening-only, mention that pattern explicitly when you call.
  • Verify the plan you are actually on. It is surprisingly common to be paying for a 100 Mbps plan signed years ago while believing it is gigabit. Confirm the subscribed tier in the app or with support before anything else.

Minimum guaranteed speed: how a slow line becomes a refund

Broadband contracts in many markets include a minimum guaranteed speed. The number on the marketing material is a maximum; separately, the provider commits to a floor it will not fall below. In South Korea the three major carriers raised that floor from 30 percent to roughly 50 percent of the advertised rate in a 2021 revision of the terms. That makes 500 Mbps the floor on a 1 Gbps plan, 250 Mbps on a 500 Mbps plan, and 50 Mbps on a 100 Mbps plan.

The condition that triggers compensation

The test the terms describe is specific. Measure at least five times within 30 minutes; if 60 percent or more of those measurements fall below the minimum guaranteed speed, that day's service fee is refunded. Accumulate five or more such days in a month and you can terminate the contract without an early-termination penalty.

There is one catch worth knowing. The adjudication relies on measurements taken on the carrier's own official test page. A low number from a third-party site does not by itself trigger anything. So the practical procedure looks like this.

  1. Open your provider's official speed measurement page. Ask support or check the carrier app for the exact location, which is the most reliable route.
  2. Set up the wired, background-silenced conditions, then run five or more tests inside 30 minutes.
  3. Results are normally logged on the carrier side, but keep your own screenshots with timestamps.
  4. Once a shortfall is confirmed, the fee reduction is processed. If it is not automatic, ask support, citing your measurement history.
  5. When qualifying days reach five in one month, request termination without penalty.
  6. If the provider will not agree, escalate through the telecommunications consumer protection channel in your jurisdiction.

One important limit: the scheme is based on wired measurements. A poor Wi-Fi result will not earn a refund. If a claim is on your mind, test over Ethernet from the very beginning.

When the numbers look fine but it still feels slow

A healthy measurement alongside a frustrating experience almost always points at latency and name resolution rather than bandwidth.

  • DNS delay. Typing a domain triggers a lookup that converts it to an IP address before anything else happens. When that answer is slow, the browser sits on "connecting" for ages. Point the router or the device at a different public DNS resolver and see whether it changes.
  • Bufferbloat. Everything turns sluggish while somebody uploads a large file. Enable QoS or SQM on the router if it offers them.
  • International congestion. Domestic sites fine, one overseas service slow — that is a transit problem, not your line. A traceroute shows which hop the delay starts at.
  • Server-side problems. If a single site is slow, it may simply be that site. Compare over a mobile connection and the answer is immediate.
  • Too many simultaneous clients. In a home with dozens of IoT devices, the router's session-handling capacity hits its limit before bandwidth does.
  • Browser cache and extensions. Years of accumulated add-ons on one profile slow page loads. Compare against a private window to confirm.

What this article could not verify

Collected in one place, for accuracy, are the points left open.

  • The current terms of each individual carrier as of August 2026 were not verified one by one. The 50 percent figure reflects the position after the 2021 revision; check your own provider's current contract before filing a claim.
  • Whether the same 50 percent floor applies to 2.5 Gbps and 10 Gbps plans could not be confirmed. Subscribers on those tiers should check their specific terms.
  • Whether public regulator measurements are accepted directly in a carrier's compensation decision is unclear. Since the terms reference the carrier's own testing, running both is the safer approach if a refund is the goal.
  • Real-world Wi-Fi 7 throughput was left out of the table for lack of reliable published field measurements.
  • The exact formula each carrier uses to calculate the refund amount could not be confirmed. The size of the reduction varies by plan and by contract.

Conclusion: the sequence answers the question, not the number

Internet speed problems reduce to three sentences. First, a number measured over Wi-Fi is not evidence about your line. Only a wired test with background traffic silenced says anything about the connection you pay for. Second, do not confuse the units. If a gigabit line hands you 110 MB per second, that is close to a perfect score. Third, find the cause by eliminating segments. Wired, then direct to modem, then another device, then cable and port, then a different hour — repeat the same test through that sequence and the culprit reveals itself.

And if you have worked through all of it and a wired test still returns roughly half the advertised rate, that is not something to live with. It is a state in which the contract terms entitle you to a fee reduction and, potentially, a penalty-free exit. Five tests in 30 minutes, a 60 percent shortfall, five days in a month — those are the only numbers you need to remember. Knowing how to measure properly is, quite literally, worth money.

Frequently asked questions

Q1. How do I run an internet speed test that actually means something?

Plug the computer straight into a LAN port on the router with an Ethernet cable, stop every other device on the network, and close background updates and cloud sync before you press start. Do not judge from a single number: run at least three tests at different times of day. A result measured over Wi-Fi includes the Wi-Fi segment, so it tells you almost nothing about the line itself.

Q2. My plan says 1 Gbps but downloads peak around 110 MB per second. Is that broken?

That is a full-speed connection. The Gbps on your plan is bits and the MB/s in a download window is bytes, so divide by eight. One gigabit per second is 125 MB/s in theory, and after protocol overhead the practical ceiling sits around 110 to 118 MB/s. People often mistake this eight-fold unit gap for a fault.

Q3. Why do speed test sites give me different numbers?

Because they measure different things. Speedtest-style tools open several parallel TCP connections to well-peered servers and push the link to its ceiling. Fast.com pulls dummy video from Netflix Open Connect CDN servers, so it lands closer to what streaming actually feels like. If you are building a case for a refund with your ISP, use that ISP official measurement page instead.

Q4. Is my Wi-Fi really the bottleneck rather than my internet plan?

Very likely on a fast plan. University of Chicago researchers measured Wi-Fi and access-link throughput side by side in more than 50 homes for nearly two years, gathering over 13,000 paired samples. For households whose access link exceeded 800 Mbps, the home Wi-Fi network was the bottleneck 100 percent of the time.

Q5. The speed test looks fine but browsing still feels slow. Why?

The culprit is usually delay and loss rather than bandwidth. Page loads and game responsiveness depend on ping, jitter and packet loss far more than on the big download figure. Add slow DNS resolution, congestion on a specific server or international hop, and background traffic on the device itself, and the measurement stays healthy while the experience does not.
