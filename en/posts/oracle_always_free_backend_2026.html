Running a Free Backend Forever on Oracle Cloud Always Free

A practical playbook for Ampere A1's capacity traps and free-tier alternatives, so your side-project backend doesn't disappear

Jul 9, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: Why "free backend hosting" still matters

A personal API, a hobby bot, a learning project - none of these generate enough traffic to justify a monthly server bill, yet paying for one anyway feels wasteful. That is why developers have long hunted for an always-on free server, and one of the most durable answers has been Oracle Cloud Infrastructure's (OCI) Always Free tier. The pitch was simple: Arm-based Ampere A1 compute that never gets reclaimed once your trial ends. For a while, that made it the de facto default answer in homelab and side-project communities.

But the picture has been shifting through 2026. The free allowance was quietly cut, Out of Capacity errors have become a more common complaint when trying to spin up an instance, and more people are wondering whether they need to move to Pay As You Go (PAYG) after all. This piece cross-checks a personal developer's operating notes shared via GeekNews against Oracle's official documentation and independent tech press coverage, to lay out the real traps of running a backend on Always Free and the practical ways to avoid a surprise bill. This is current as of July 2026, and policies and figures can differ by region and over time.

1. Why Oracle Always Free is genuinely appealing

1.1 Free resources that outlive the trial

Most cloud "free trials" burn through credits over a fixed window and then either convert to paid or get deleted. Oracle's structure is different: separate from the 90-day trial credit, it designates a category called Always Free, a set of resources you can keep using indefinitely even after the trial ends. That set includes Arm-based Ampere A1 compute, Autonomous Database, and a slice of Object Storage. Being able to run an always-on server without ever entering a credit card is a combination that is hard to find among the other major clouds.

1.2 The 2026 cut - halved from 4 OCPUs/24GB

That strength has already taken a hit once. Multiple outlets reported that Oracle cut the Always Free Ampere A1 allowance in half, from 4 OCPUs/24GB RAM to 2 OCPUs/12GB RAM, effective June 15, 2026, with no public announcement - many users only discovered the change after their instances shrank or were terminated. BrainDetox covered that cut in a separate analysis, worth a read if you want the background on the reduction itself. This piece focuses on what comes after that: what to watch for when actually running a backend on the reduced allowance day to day.

2. The Out of Capacity problem - why it happens and how to handle it

2.1 Free instances are a shared, first-come pool

Always Free A1 instances are allocated from a limited capacity pool shared across a region. Unlike paid shapes, they are not guaranteed on demand, so trying to launch a new instance or resize an existing one in a popular region frequently trips an "Out of host capacity" error. This is reported especially often in high-demand regions such as US East.

2.2 Workarounds the community has settled on

These are closer to informal community habits than official solutions, but a few patterns come up repeatedly: refreshing the create page repeatedly to catch a moment when capacity frees up, launching first in a less crowded region and migrating later if needed, and remembering that a resize is treated much like a fresh launch and will fail the same way if there is no capacity. Success rates for all of these vary a lot by region and timing, and none of them are officially guaranteed. If your goal is genuine uptime, it is more realistic to pick a relatively roomy region from the start and to design a fallback path rather than betting your entire service on a single free instance.

3. Upgrading to PAYG - when and why it becomes necessary

3.1 Free Trial and Always Free are not the same thing

This is a distinction worth nailing down. Paid resources provisioned with Free Trial credits are reclaimed and deleted after a grace period once the trial ends, unless you upgrade the account. Resources tagged Always Free, by contrast, keep working regardless of trial status. There is one exception, though: Oracle's own documentation states that even an Always Free compute instance can be reclaimed if it stays idle - defined as 95th-percentile CPU utilization under 20% over a 7-day window. That means a personal API or hobby bot with sparse traffic is genuinely at risk. A common practical fix is keeping a lightweight health check or cron job running to maintain some baseline activity.

3.2 The real reasons people upgrade to PAYG

Even so, some people do upgrade their accounts to a paid plan such as PAYG or Monthly Flex. Oracle's documentation points to two main reasons: paid accounts are subject to different capacity limits than Always Free accounts, which can help somewhat with Out of Capacity issues, and paid accounts get access to customer support tickets when something goes wrong. In other words, what paying actually buys you is not more free resources, but reliability and support access.

3.3 A checklist before you upgrade

The moment you register a payment method, the risk of accidentally creating a paid resource comes along with it. Before upgrading: (1) double-check in the console that everything you currently run truly sits within Always Free limits, (2) always verify that a new region or new shape does not default to a paid option, and (3) set up the budget alerts covered in the next section at the same time you upgrade, not afterward.

4. Preventing bill shock - budgets, alerts, and hard limits

4.1 Setting up Budgets and Alert Rules

OCI lets you create a Budget per compartment and set alerts based on either Actual Spend or Forecast Spend, triggered by a percentage or an absolute amount. Oracle's own guidance recommends setting the budget to an amount you can genuinely absorb, then triggering an email alert once forecast spend hits 100%. In January 2026, Oracle also added Cost Anomaly Detection, which automatically flags unusual spending patterns as a secondary layer of defense.

4.2 The blind spot - alerts are not real-time

There is a trap worth calling out explicitly here. Budget alerts are evaluated on a 24-hour cycle. Setting one up does not mean you get notified the instant a charge spikes. If you accidentally provision a paid shape, or a traffic spike drives up outbound data costs, in the worst case you might not find out for nearly a day. Treat alerts as a safety net, not a real-time circuit breaker.

4.3 Before alerts - design so paid resources can't appear at all

A safer approach in practice is not to rely on alerts alone but to make it structurally hard to create a paid resource in the first place - through compartment policies or quotas that restrict paid shapes, and by building a habit of checking projected cost every time you provision something. Regularly opening the billing dashboard yourself, say once a week, is the simplest and most reliable complement to automated alerts.

5. Comparing alternatives - GCP, AWS, Fly.io, and Cloudflare free tiers

Oracle isn't the only option. Here's a snapshot of the major alternatives as of 2026, though each provider's free policy changes often enough that you should always verify current terms on the official page.

The takeaway: if you need a genuine always-on server, Oracle's A1 is still one of the more generous options even after the cut. If your workload is light and request-driven, a serverless free tier like Cloudflare Workers requires less upkeep and is more stable, without needing a server running around the clock at all. These aren't mutually exclusive - a common real-world pattern is putting the heavy backend on A1 and pushing light edge logic to a serverless layer.

6. What kind of project fits, and what doesn't

Good fit: low-traffic personal APIs, learning or portfolio servers, hobby bots, and early-stage side-project prototypes that can tolerate some downtime. For these, a temporary block on re-provisioning due to Out of Capacity, or a delay of a few days, is not catastrophic.

Poor fit: anything needing an SLA, production apps handling payments or personal data, or services with expected traffic growth - these can't absorb the free tier's uncertainty (capacity shortages, idle reclamation, shifting limits). For those, a cheap paid VPS or managed PaaS from day one saves more time and trust in the long run. We covered exactly this kind of failure mode, betting everything on a single cheap resource, in Lessons from a $6 VPS - whether it's free or ultra-cheap, over-trusting a single resource always exacts a price eventually.

Conclusion: a free backend is still possible, but with conditions attached

Oracle Always Free in 2026 is not as generous as it was a couple of years ago. The allowance has shrunk, Out of Capacity remains a real issue, and idle instances can be reclaimed. Even so, it retains real value as a way to run an always-on server without ever entering a credit card. To capture that value safely, though, three habits matter. First, treat Out of Capacity as the default assumption rather than an edge case, and build a fallback path. Second, understand the Always Free limits and idle-reclamation criteria precisely before considering PAYG. Third, assume budget alerts can lag by up to 24 hours, and back them with hard caps and quotas as a second layer of defense. Depending on free infrastructure isn't the mistake - over-trusting the word "free" is. If your side project is the kind that takes a long time to finish, it's also worth revisiting your completion strategy alongside your infrastructure choices.
