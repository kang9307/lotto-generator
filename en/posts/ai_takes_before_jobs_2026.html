What AI Takes Before Your Job

The Meaning Developers Are Losing - like the sophons in The Three-Body Problem, AI quietly takes flow and growth long before any layoff notice

Jul 3, 2026 Trends & Culture

Introduction: A Loss That Arrives Before the Layoff Notice

A quiet post appeared on GeekNews last week. A developer had spent more than a year working alongside AI, and wrote an essay comparing the unease that built up to Liu Cixin's novel The Three-Body Problem. It only scored 1 point, but this piece argues it asks the question the developer community will eventually have to face in the second half of 2026: will AI actually take our jobs one day, or has it already quietly taken something else, long before that?

Over the past year, this author has drifted from someone who "writes" code to someone who "directs and reviews" it. Output went up. But what's left at the end of the day feels different from before. This piece is an independent analysis of what that difference is, why it happens, and what can still be reclaimed.

1. The Sophon in The Three-Body Problem - Not Destruction, But a Slow Stop

1.1 The Device of Intellectual Blockade

In Liu Cixin's novel, the alien Trisolaran civilization does not attack Earth directly. Instead it sends sophons - protons reprogrammed as supercomputers - to disrupt humanity's particle accelerators. The goal is not destruction but containment: block fundamental physics from advancing so the civilization stalls on its own. For a long time, humanity does not notice, because on the surface everything still appears to function normally.

1.2 What AI Is Quietly Blocking

The original essay on hada.io transplants this device directly onto today's engineering floor. AI doesn't lay us off. Instead it removes the friction of wrestling with a problem - the exact friction that used to build skill. This comparison doesn't read as an exaggeration. But there is one difference from the sophon: the sophon was an external adversary, while it was our own desire for convenience that opened the door for AI.

2. Three Things AI Takes Before Your Job

2.1 Flow - Losing the Process of Thinking

Flow is the state where your sense of time disappears while you wrestle with a problem. Much of the psychological reward of good engineering comes not from the output but from that process. Yet a large share of design and decomposition work now happens inside a reasoning model instead. As flow becomes rarer, work starts to feel like an endless stream of task completion rather than craft - and recent analyses suggest this accelerates fatigue and burnout.

2.2 Growth - The Disappearing Bottom Rung

Junior developers typically build skill by wrestling with small, tedious problems on their own, failing, and fixing them again. As AI climbs that bottom rung for them, the rung itself is disappearing. A recent Stanford analysis found that employment for early-career workers aged 22-25 in AI-exposed occupations has fallen nearly 20% from its peak. The issue isn't just fewer hires - it's that the training ground where the next generation builds skill is shrinking.

2.3 Ownership - Output That Doesn't Feel Like Yours

An idea sketched out by hand still has a clear shape in memory a month later. An AI-polished proposal, by contrast, gets fuzzy in the details within a week. Knowledge acquired without trial and error doesn't stick. This is exactly what the original essay points to: the polish of the output has gone up, while the sense that it's "mine" has quietly gone down.

3. The New Identity of Orchestrator - What Has Actually Changed

3.1 From 55% Coding to Roughly 20%

Recent industry analysis suggests the average developer's share of the day spent on pure coding has dropped from about 55% to roughly 20%, with the rest filled by architecture, code review, and orchestrating AI tools. A "10x developer" in 2026 is being redefined - not as someone who types faster, but as someone who can direct multiple AI agents at once while holding the line on quality and consistency.

3.2 Conducting the Orchestra, Not Playing the Instrument

Being pushed toward the conductor's role sounds like a promotion, but it is a different kind of loss. Flow tends to come from playing - the act of building something directly - not from conducting, the act of supervising. There are growing reports of engineers who can modify the codebase they own but can no longer explain why a given change is correct. They know how to fix the code, but their intuition for why it should be fixed that way is thinning out.

4. What the Evidence Actually Shows - Two Conflicting Faces

4.1 Cracks in the Productivity Narrative

DORA's large-scale survey reports that developers who use AI heavily spend more time in flow and report higher satisfaction. UC Berkeley's study of experienced open-source developers found the opposite: seasoned developers using AI tools were actually 19% slower, because the overhead of reviewing, verifying, and correcting AI output outweighed the time saved by generation. That both findings can be true at once shows how complex this problem really is. A 2026 analysis argues the deciding factor isn't the tool itself but what organizations do with the time it saves - often absorbing it into higher workload expectations rather than rest, which produces a new kind of burnout in exactly the people who embraced AI hardest.

4.2 Cognitive Debt Building Quietly

MIT-affiliated research found that a group relying heavily on LLMs to write could not properly cite the reasoning behind text they had just written themselves. It's the same structure documented in studies of London taxi drivers, where reliance on GPS navigation weakened spatial memory. Aviation has long worried that over-reliance on autopilot erodes manual response capability in emergencies. Coding is no different: wrestling with a hard problem, failing, adjusting, and finally solving it is exactly the cycle that builds real skill. When AI lets you skip that cycle, the skill you would have built by solving it gets skipped too.

5. What Is Still Worth Protecting - A Room the Wallfacer Keeps

5.1 One Room AI Cannot Enter

In the third book of The Three-Body Problem, "Wallfacers" plan entirely inside their own minds, hidden from the sophons' surveillance. The original essay turns this into advice for today's developers: keep "a room AI cannot enter" somewhere in your thinking (flowkater.io, 2026-07-02). This metaphor is unusually practical. It isn't a call to reject AI - it's a proposal to draw a line marking where you must still think for yourself.

5.2 Three Practices Worth Applying

Three principles worth following in practice: first, sketch out the first hypothesis for a new feature or bug by hand before handing it to AI. Second, keep at least one task a week, however small, that gets finished entirely without AI. Third, never approve AI-generated code until you can explain, in your own words, why it was written that way. A related trend: universities are reviving oral exams - one of the oldest evaluation methods - to counter AI-assisted work, shifting the check from the output back to the thinking process.

Conclusion: Before the Fleet Arrives

As of July 2026, there is no conclusive evidence that AI has replaced developer jobs at scale. But the question the original essay raises still stands. Just as the sophons stalled scientific progress long before any fleet could destroy humanity, AI can take the place where we used to find flow, the rung where we used to grow, and the sense of ownership over our own output - long before it ever takes a job. This isn't a conclusion that says avoid AI. Keep using the tool, but consciously protect one room where you still think for yourself. Before the fleet arrives, there is still time to check whether we are still finding flow and still growing.
