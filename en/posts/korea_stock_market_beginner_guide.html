Korean Stock Market for Beginners - Rules from Account Opening to Your First Trade

Trading hours, tick sizes, settlement, costs and safeguards in South Korea, as of July 2026

2026-07-29 Finance & Economy

Introduction: not 「what to buy」 but 「which rules you are buying under」

Almost everything written for people starting out in the stock market answers a single question: 「what should I buy?」 How to pick a company, how to read a chart, how to read financial statements. Then the first real trade happens, and what stops you comes from somewhere else entirely. Why does an order placed at 3:40 in the afternoon fail to execute? Why does the cash not arrive the moment you sell? Why does a message about a volatility interruption appear and then nothing happens for two minutes? Why does selling ten million won of stock not put ten million won back in the account? None of these belong to the domain of company analysis. They belong to the domain of market rules.

Rules are easier to learn than companies. Company analysis has no correct answer and reasonable people reach opposite conclusions, but trading hours, tick sizes, settlement cycles and tax rates are single values written down in regulations. Learn them once and they keep working for as long as the market is open. Without that foundation, every other judgement becomes imprecise as well. An expected return calculated without taxes and commissions, a cash plan built without knowing the settlement date, a sharp move interpreted without knowing the daily price limit - each of them leads to a conclusion that is quietly wrong.

This article deals only with the market rules of the Republic of Korea. It does not discuss which stocks are good or when to buy and sell them. Instead it sets out, in order, which rules operate between opening an account, placing a first order and reaching final settlement. Everything here was checked against material from the Korea Exchange (KRX), the Financial Services Commission (FSC), the Ministry of Government Legislation and the National Tax Service (NTS) as of July 2026, and figures that could not be verified were left out rather than guessed.

One warning belongs at the front. This area changes more often than people expect. The listing-day price limit for new issues was rebuilt in 2023, short selling resumed in 2025, and securities transaction tax rates rose in 2026. A shorter settlement cycle and longer trading hours are both in progress. In other words, trusting an old explainer as written will throw your arithmetic off. Every item below records which institution published the source used to verify it, so anyone reading this later should go back to that source and confirm whether it still holds.

1. The market structure - KOSPI, KOSDAQ, KONEX and the alternative exchange

The Korea Exchange (KRX) operates three stock markets. The names circulate like brand labels, but they are in fact separate markets with different listing requirements and different trading rules. Which market a stock is listed on determines its daily price limit and the tax rate you pay when you sell it.

ItemSecurities market (KOSPI)KOSDAQ marketKONEX market
CharacterMain market, large and mid-sized companiesTechnology and growth companiesEarly-stage SMEs and venture firms only
Daily price limit±30% against the previous close±30% against the previous close±15% against the previous close
Securities transaction tax on sale0.20% (tax 0.05% + rural surtax 0.15%)0.20%0.10%
Large shareholder ownership threshold1% or more2% or more4% or more
Listing-day price limit exceptionApplies (60~400% of the offering price)Applies (60~400% of the offering price)Does not apply

The table makes one thing immediately clear. The common belief that 「KOSDAQ is riskier」 does not come from the price limit. KOSPI and KOSDAQ share the same ±30% band. The difference comes from the size, operating history and liquidity of the listed companies, and what actually differs in the rules is the listing requirements and the securities transaction tax rate. Conversely, KONEX has a narrower ±15% band but so little trading that buying or selling at the price you want can be difficult. A narrower band does not mean a safer market.

1.1 The alternative trading system as a second venue

Since March 2025 Korea has had an alternative trading system (ATS), Nextrade, operating alongside the Korea Exchange. The same stock can now be traded in two places. The practical implications for a beginner are as follows.

  • The alternative exchange keeps different hours from the Korea Exchange. Press coverage and brokerage notices describe a pre-market from 08:00 to 08:50, a main market from 09:00:30 to 15:20, and an after-market from 15:30 to 20:00.
  • Not every stock trades there. It launched with a small set of names and has been widening the list in stages, and because the eligible universe changes you should check before trading.
  • Brokerages normally route orders to whichever venue is more favourable under their best execution policy. Some also give the investor a way to choose the venue directly.
  • When you look at a quote, seeing only one venue may not reflect the whole picture. It is worth checking whether your platform shows consolidated quotes.
  • Exact operating hours, eligible stocks and order types change frequently, so the current notices from the alternative exchange and your brokerage are the authority.

The Korea Exchange is also preparing to extend its own hours. According to press reports, an after-market session is targeted for September 2026, while the pre-market session has been pushed back to the end of 2027. In other words, as of July 2026 the Korea Exchange trading day is exactly as set out in section 3 below, with a real possibility of change in the second half of the year. This item is unusually time-sensitive, so confirm it before trading for real.

2. Opening an account and getting ready - the actual sequence to a first trade

To buy shares you need a brokerage trading account at a securities firm. A bank account alone will not do it. Most accounts are opened remotely these days, but there are a few points where people get stuck, so it helps to know the order before you start.

2.1 The account opening sequence

  1. Choose a brokerage - this article does not recommend any firm. It is worth knowing, however, that the commission structure described later differs from firm to firm, and that promotional commission discounts run for limited periods.
  2. Identity verification - typically a photograph of an identity document combined with either a small transfer from a bank account in your own name or a video call.
  3. Investor profiling - questions about your investment purpose, income and experience. It looks like a formality, but it follows the suitability principle under the Financial Consumer Protection Act, and the outcome determines which products you are permitted to buy.
  4. Account opening and registration of certificates and apps - after opening, you register your login credentials with the trading app (MTS) or the desktop platform (HTS).
  5. Funding and the first order - transfer money from the linked bank account into the brokerage account, then place an order.

2.2 Where people commonly get stuck

  • Limits on opening multiple accounts in a short period - to prevent accounts being sold on for fraud and voice phishing, financial firms operate criteria that restrict remote account opening if you have opened an account at another financial institution within the past 20 business days. This is not a uniform statutory figure but an internal standard set by each institution, so it varies between firms. Trying to open several accounts at once is a common way to hit a wall.
  • Verification of the purpose of the financial transaction - the firm may ask why you are opening the account and request supporting documents. If the purpose is unclear, the account may be opened with transaction limits attached.
  • Accounts for minors - these require legal guardian consent and family relationship documents, and in many cases cannot be opened remotely at all.
  • Overseas stocks, derivatives and margin trading - these can require separate agreements or completion of prior education. None of it is necessary if you only intend to buy Korean shares with cash.
  • Opening the account itself is free, but trading always costs something. It is worth reading the cost structure in section 7 first.

An account must be opened in your own name and operated by you. Borrowing someone else's account, or lending your account to another person, can breach the Act on Real Name Financial Transactions and Guarantee of Secrecy, and if the account is used for fraud it can lead to criminal liability and restrictions on your financial transactions. This article does not describe such methods.

3. Trading hours - the regular session, single price auctions and off-hours trading

This is the item people get wrong most often. Knowing only that the market runs 「from 9:00 to 15:30」 is knowing half of it. In reality there are single price auction windows inside the regular session, and separate off-hours sessions before and after it. The current framework, as compiled by the Ministry of Government Legislation in its Easy Law Information service, is as follows.

SessionTimeExecution methodWhat to note
Order acceptance begins08:30Orders accepted onlyOrders are taken but nothing executes until 09:00
Pre-open off-hours trading at the closing price08:30~08:40Executed at the previous closeThe price is fixed at the previous close
Opening single price auctionOrders 08:30~09:00 → execution at 09:00Single priceOrders are pooled and the opening price is set once
Regular session, continuous trading09:00~15:20Real-time executionThe ordinary trading window
Closing single price auction15:20~15:30Single priceNothing executes in real time during this window
Post-close off-hours trading at the closing price15:40~16:00Executed at the day's closeThe price is fixed at the day's closing price
Off-hours single price auction16:00~18:00Single price every 10 minutesWithin ±10% of the day's close and inside the daily price limit

Two sources of confusion trip up beginners more than anything else. First, orders placed between 15:20 and 15:30 do not execute in real time. Those ten minutes exist to gather orders and determine the closing price, and execution happens once, at 15:30. The correct description is not 「it will not sell」 but 「the execution moment has not arrived yet」. Second, nothing trades at all between 15:30 and 15:40. That is the gap between the end of the regular session and the start of post-close off-hours trading.

The off-hours single price auction runs until 18:00, but volume there is far thinner than in the regular session. Your full quantity may not fill, and a small order can move the price noticeably. The price range is capped at ±10% against the day's close, and even within that range it cannot break out of the daily price limit. For a beginner, trading during the regular session is simply simpler.

Market holidays matter too. Beyond Saturdays, Sundays and public holidays, there are separately designated closures such as Workers' Day on 1 May and the final business day of the year. The Korea Exchange publishes the calendar annually, so checking it once at the start of the year saves trouble later.

4. Orders and execution - tick sizes and the priority rules

An order specifies a quantity and a price. The price, however, cannot be any number you like. A tick size is prescribed for each price band, and orders may only be placed at multiples of it.

5,000 won to under 20,000 won10 won12,340 won allowed, 12,345 won not
20,000 won to under 50,000 won50 won34,550 won allowed, 34,540 won not
50,000 won to under 200,000 won100 won78,900 won allowed, 78,950 won not
200,000 won to under 500,000 won500 won320,500 won allowed, 320,300 won not

The trading unit is one share. There was a time when high-priced stocks could only be bought in blocks of ten shares, but today everything trades from a single share. For a handful of very expensive names even one share can be a stretch, and some brokerages offer fractional trading in response. That is not an exchange trading unit; it is a service in which the brokerage itself splits and administers the holding, so availability and terms differ from firm to firm.

4.1 Execution priority

When many orders arrive in the same stock, they execute in a defined order. Knowing this explains the familiar complaint of 「why did someone who ordered after me get filled first?」 The basis is Article 22 of the KRX Stock Market Business Regulation and Article 17 of the KOSDAQ Market Business Regulation.

  • Price priority - among buy orders the higher price comes first, among sell orders the lower price comes first. Market orders take precedence over limit orders.
  • Time priority - at the same price, the order received earlier executes first.
  • Quantity priority - in single-price phases handled as simultaneous quotes, such as the opening and closing auctions, the larger quote is allocated first.
  • Customer priority - for exchange-traded funds, exchange-traded notes and equity-linked warrants, client orders take precedence over the brokerage's own proprietary orders.

4.2 Order types and frequent mistakes

  • Limit order - you set the price yourself. If the market never reaches it, nothing executes. This is the sensible default for a beginner.
  • Market order - executes immediately without specifying a price. It is fast, but in a stock with a thin order book it can fill at a far worse price than expected.
  • Conditional limit, best limit and best-priced variants - these are modified order types offered alongside on brokerage screens. Each behaves differently, and it is better not to use one whose meaning you do not know.
  • Order validity - orders in Korean stocks are in principle valid for the current day only. Unexecuted orders vanish when the session ends and must be re-entered the next day.
  • Amendment and cancellation - possible only for the unexecuted quantity. Anything already executed cannot be undone.
  • Orders during a single price auction - an indicative execution price is displayed during the opening and closing auctions, but it is not the final price. Orders arriving just before the cut-off can change it.

5. The ±30% price limit and the listing-day exception

The Korean stock market caps how far a price can move in one day. The securities market and the KOSDAQ market allow 30 percent up or down against the previous close, and the KONEX market allows 15 percent. Reaching the top is called the upper limit price, reaching the bottom the lower limit price.

CaseReference pricePrice limitNote
KOSPI and KOSDAQ, ordinary daysPrevious close±30%Widened from ±15% on 15 June 2015
KONEXPrevious close±15%Excluded from the 2015 widening
KOSPI and KOSDAQ, listing dayOffering price60~400%Effective 26 June 2023
Off-hours single price auctionDay's closing price±10%Within the daily price limit

One misunderstanding about price limits needs clearing up before anything else. The limit is not a device that protects you from losses. It means a stock can only fall 30 percent in a day; it does not mean the fall stops at 30 percent. Two consecutive lower limit days erase half your principal. The purpose of the band is to slow abrupt moves within a single day so that people have time to think, not to cap anyone's losses.

Listing day works differently for newly listed stocks. Under the Korea Exchange rule change effective 26 June 2023, on the listing day the offering price becomes the reference price directly, with no separate opening auction, and the price limit runs from 60 to 400 percent of it. A stock offered at 20,000 won can therefore trade anywhere between 12,000 won and 80,000 won on its first day. The 400 percent ceiling is what gets repeated, but the 60 percent floor, meaning a 40 percent loss on day one, is equally possible within the rules and deserves the same weight. This change also means that the Korean retail phrase describing a doubled opening price followed by a limit-up move no longer corresponds to any mechanism that exists. The rules of IPO subscription itself are covered separately in the complete guide to IPO subscription in Korea.

6. T+2 settlement - the day the money and the shares actually move

Execution and settlement happen on different days. Not knowing this is how cash plans go wrong. As of July 2026 the settlement cycle in the Korean securities market is T+2. The day a trade executes counts as T, and ownership of the cash and the shares is finally reconciled two business days later. The Financial Services Commission also publishes a glossary entry explaining the T+2 settlement system.

  • Sell on Monday and the settlement date is Wednesday, which is when withdrawal becomes possible.
  • Sell on Thursday and, because Friday and Monday are the business days, the settlement date is the following Monday. A weekend in the middle makes the wait feel longer.
  • A public holiday in between pushes it out further. Count business days rather than calendar days.
  • Most brokerages let you buy other shares with the sale proceeds before settlement. That produces the gap people notice: repurchase is immediate, withdrawal waits until T+2.
  • The dividend record date follows the same logic. To appear on the shareholder register on the record date, settlement must be complete by then, so buying on the record date itself is too late.
  • Selling shares before settlement is permitted. You can place a sell order before the settlement date arrives.

This is another rule that may change. According to press reports, the Korea Exchange and the Korea Securities Depository (KSD) are pursuing a shortening of the cycle to T+1, with the idea of aligning to the transition timetables in the European Union and the United Kingdom. North American markets including the United States already moved to T+1 in 2024. However, as of July 2026 Korea remains on T+2, and no start date for T+1 has been fixed. Until it is, calculating on a T+2 basis is the correct approach.

7. The full cost of trading - commissions and taxes in one calculation

Buying and selling shares costs money in three ways: brokerage commission, market infrastructure fees, and the securities transaction tax and rural development surtax that apply only on the sell side. Each has a different character.

  • Brokerage commission - the intermediation fee the brokerage charges. It differs by firm and by channel (app, desktop, telephone) and is sometimes waived through promotions. This article does not compare rates between firms. Note that products advertised as commission-free can still leave the infrastructure fees below in place, so read the terms carefully.
  • Market infrastructure fees - amounts passed on to the Korea Exchange, the Korea Securities Depository, the Korea Financial Investment Association and similar bodies. They are usually included in the brokerage commission or itemised separately.
  • Securities transaction tax and rural development surtax - charged only when you sell, and levied on the entire sale amount regardless of whether you made a profit. You pay it even when you sell at a loss.
  • Capital gains tax - in principle not applied to on-exchange trades in Korean listed shares by small shareholders. Section 8 covers this separately.

KOSPI adds a 0.15% rural development surtax to a 0.05% securities transaction tax, while KOSDAQ carries a 0.20% securities transaction tax alone, and the result is identical. The old explanation that 「KOSPI is cheaper on tax」 ceased to be true for disposals from 1 January 2026. The basis is Article 8 of the Securities Transaction Tax Act and Article 5 of its Enforcement Decree, together with Article 5 of the Rural Development Special Tax Act, as compiled by the Ministry of Government Legislation in its Easy Law Information service.

Second, costs scale with the number of trades. The example above covers one purchase and one sale. Buying and selling ten times a month with the same capital means paying the transaction tax ten times. Even repeating trades with a 0% return, the account balance keeps shrinking. This is exactly where the cost of frequent trading comes from.

8. The tax structure - capital gains, dividends and aggregate financial income

Taxes on Korean shares divide into three strands: transaction tax, capital gains tax and dividend income tax. Tax law is amended often, so what follows is stated as of July 2026 and should be confirmed with the National Tax Service before you file anything.

8.1 Most individuals are outside capital gains tax

For a small shareholder buying and selling Korean listed shares on the exchange, there is in principle no tax on the gain. Tax applies only to those who meet the large shareholder definition. That test is satisfied by either the ownership ratio (1 percent on the securities market, 2 percent on KOSDAQ, 4 percent on KONEX) or the holding value (a market value of 5 billion won or more per stock), and meeting either one is enough. It is normally assessed at the end of the preceding fiscal year and applied to disposals in the following year. According to Korea's policy briefing service, the government stated in September 2025 that the holding value threshold would remain at 5 billion won per stock. This threshold has been raised and reversed repeatedly, so anyone who might fall within it should confirm the current standard directly with the National Tax Service.

8.2 Dividends and aggregate financial income taxation

  • Dividends arrive net of tax. The figure credited to your account already has 15.4 percent deducted.
  • If financial income, meaning interest plus dividends, exceeds 20 million won a year, the excess is aggregated with your other income and taxed at global income tax rates.
  • From 2026 a separate taxation regime for dividend income from qualifying high-dividend companies has been in effect. Press and tax commentary describe rates of 14, 20, 25 and 30 percent by bracket, and note that it is not automatic but must be claimed when filing the global income tax return. The eligible companies and conditions are detailed, so individual eligibility should be confirmed with the National Tax Service.
  • Overseas stocks follow different rules. Unlike Korean listed shares, they are subject to capital gains tax even for small shareholders and carry a separate filing obligation. That falls outside the scope of this article, so see the guide to investing in US stocks.
  • Securities transaction tax is payable even at a loss. It is not an income tax; it attaches to the act of trading itself.

9. Market safeguards - volatility interruptions, circuit breakers, sidecars and market alerts

When execution suddenly stops mid-session, the instinct is to suspect a system fault, but it is usually normal operation prescribed by the rules. The Korean market has several layers of machinery designed to slow abrupt moves.

MechanismScopeTriggerAction
Dynamic volatility interruptionIndividual stockDeviation of a set ratio from the last execution priceSwitches to a single price auction for about 2 minutes
Static volatility interruptionIndividual stock±10% against the previous close or the last single priceSwitches to a single price auction for about 2 minutes

Circuit breaker, level 2Whole marketDown 15% or more plus a further 1 percentage point drop from level 1All trading halted for 20 minutes, then resumed
Circuit breaker, level 3Whole marketDown 20% or more plus a further 1 percentage point drop from level 2Trading closed for the day

The trigger criteria for circuit breakers and sidecars are as compiled by the Ministry of Government Legislation in its Easy Law Information service on the basis of Articles 25 and 16 of the Securities Market Business Regulation and Article 26 of the KOSDAQ Market Business Regulation. The point most often left out is that levels 2 and 3 require an additional 1 percentage point fall from the preceding level to be satisfied at the same time.

The detailed trigger rates for volatility interruptions are set differently by market, by stock group and by time of day. Brokerage and press material indicate that during continuous trading the dynamic trigger differs between KOSPI200 constituents and other stocks, and that the threshold is lower during the closing single price auction window. Because the exact figures are prescribed in the Korea Exchange enforcement rules and can be amended, this article describes only the structure and recommends checking individual ratios against exchange regulations.

The market alert system is a warning attached to stocks whose prices have risen abnormally. It has three stages - investment caution, investment warning and investment risk - and trading can be suspended for a day if the rise continues after designation. Designation is not a verdict that 「this is a bad company」, but it does mean abnormal signals have been detected, such as trading concentrated in particular accounts or branches, so it is better to find out why before chasing the price higher.

10. Short selling - where things stand since the 2025 resumption

Short selling is borrowing shares, selling them first and buying them back later to repay the loan. It profits when the price falls and loses when it rises. Korea has banned and resumed it several times, so knowing the current state accurately matters.

  • According to the Financial Services Commission, short selling was fully resumed from 31 March 2025, and as of July 2026 that resumption remains in place.
  • The resumption came with a central electronic checking system designed to block naked short selling in advance, covering all listed stocks.
  • Corporations that sell short and the brokerages that accept their orders were placed under an obligation to maintain systems that prevent naked short selling.
  • An overheated short selling stock designation system operates, so a stock with concentrated short selling may face restrictions on short selling the following trading day.
  • Naked short selling is prohibited under the Financial Investment Services and Capital Markets Act. Selling shares you have not borrowed is unlawful and subject to penalties.
  • For an individual to take part in short selling, separate procedures and requirements such as a stock lending arrangement are needed, along with collateral and a repayment deadline.

What a beginner really needs to take from short selling is the structural risk rather than the mechanics of doing it. Buying and holding a stock ends, in the worst case, with the loss of the invested principal, whereas short selling has no ceiling on how far the price can rise, so the loss is theoretically unlimited. On top of that, borrowed stock can be recalled, so even a correct judgement can be forcibly unwound if the timing is off. This article does not encourage anyone to sell short. But understanding why the price of a stock you hold gets pressed down in certain phases does require knowing that this system exists.

Price manipulation, the use of material non-public information and fraudulent trading are crimes prohibited by the Financial Investment Services and Capital Markets Act, subject to criminal punishment and administrative fines. Matched trading between designated accounts, spreading false information, and trading on undisclosed information learned through your work all fall into this category. This article does not describe how such conduct is carried out and does not encourage it in any form. Suspected unfair trading can be reported to the Korea Exchange Market Oversight Commission or the Financial Supervisory Service.

11. Ten rules that beginners commonly misunderstand

  1. 「Trading executes continuously from 9:00 to 15:30」 - it does not. From 15:20 to 15:30 the market runs a single price auction to set the closing price, and nothing executes in real time.
  2. 「I can withdraw the cash as soon as I sell」 - you cannot. Settlement is T+2. Repurchasing is immediate, but withdrawing cash waits two business days.
  3. 「The daily price limit protects me from losses」 - it does not. It caps movement within one day, and another lower limit day can follow tomorrow.
  4. 「KOSPI is cheaper on tax than KOSDAQ」 - for disposals from 1 January 2026, the combined rate is 0.20% in both.
  5. 「No profit means no tax」 - securities transaction tax attaches to the entire sale amount regardless of profit or loss.
  6. 「Korean shares are completely tax-free」 - only capital gains for small shareholders are untaxed; transaction tax and dividend income tax still apply.
  7. 「A volatility interruption means trading has been suspended」 - it does not. The stock switches to a single price auction for about two minutes and then resumes normally.
  8. 「My order stays alive until tomorrow」 - orders in Korean stocks are in principle valid for the current day only.
  9. 「Buying on the dividend record date gets me the dividend」 - settlement must complete by the record date, so buying on the day itself is too late.
  10. 「The doubled-open-then-limit-up pattern is still possible」 - the June 2023 overhaul of listing-day reference prices and price limits means the phrase no longer describes anything that exists.

12. A final checklist before your first trade

The list below is not an encouragement to buy anything. It is a set of checks for someone who has already decided to trade and wants to avoid mistakes caused by the rules.

  • Have you confirmed which market (KOSPI, KOSDAQ or KONEX) the stock you want to trade is listed on
  • Do you know that market's daily price limit and its tax rate on sale
  • Does the price you intend to order at match the tick size
  • Have you checked whether the market is currently in continuous trading or in a single price auction window
  • If you are using a market order, have you looked at whether the order book is deep enough
  • Is the day you need the sale proceeds after the settlement date (T+2)
  • Have you checked your brokerage's commission structure and waiver conditions
  • Does the trade still leave the return you expected once commission and transaction tax are included
  • If you are investing for dividends, have you worked out the relationship between the record date and the settlement date
  • Have you checked whether your annual financial income is approaching 20 million won
  • If your holdings grow, have you checked whether you might fall within the large shareholder threshold
  • Have you checked whether the stock is currently under a market alert designation
  • Is the amount one you can lose without disrupting your daily life
  • Have you verified the rules against primary sources

Conclusion - knowing the rules does not avoid losses, it removes surprises

Knowing the rules guarantees no returns. Memorising trading hours will not reveal a good company. The reason to organise these fundamentals first is that the losses caused by not knowing the rules are the preventable kind. Panicking into a market order because you did not know about the closing auction, having no cash on the day you needed it because you did not know the settlement date, misjudging your real return because you left transaction tax out of the calculation - all of these are losses that knowledge can stop.

Three sentences compress what this article covered. First, time has a structure. There are single price auction windows inside the regular session, off-hours sessions attached before and after it, and a two-day gap between execution and settlement. Second, costs are always incurred. Brokerage commission is negotiable, but securities transaction tax goes out even on a losing sale, and from 2026 the combined rate is 0.20% in both KOSPI and KOSDAQ. Third, the market stops itself. Volatility interruptions, circuit breakers, sidecars and market alerts are designed behaviour rather than faults, and the order you fire off in panic at that moment tends to be the most expensive order you place.

And finally, this area keeps changing. The listing-day price limit in 2023, the resumption of short selling in 2025 and the increase in securities transaction tax rates in 2026 all happened within the last five years, and the move to T+1 settlement and the extension of trading hours are both under way. Parts of this article will become old information with time. That is why each item records which institution published the material used to verify it. Building the habit of checking lasts longer than memorising the numbers.
