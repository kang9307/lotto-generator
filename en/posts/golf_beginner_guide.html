The Complete Beginner's Guide to Golf - Gear, Basics, Etiquette, and Costs

Everything a first-time golfer needs to know - club sets, grip and stance, course manners, and what it really costs

Jul 5, 2026 Life & Practical

Intro: So You've Decided to Take Up Golf

Maybe a friend invited you to a weekend golf outing, or a round of screen golf at a work event got you hooked, or you simply want to pick up a new sport. Whatever the reason, starting golf means facing unfamiliar gear, unfamiliar terms, and a set of manners to follow, all at once - and that can feel overwhelming.

This guide is written so that even a complete beginner can read it in order and walk onto a course prepared: what equipment you actually need, whether to rent or buy clubs, how a golf simulator, a driving range, and an actual course differ, the basics of grip and stance, terms like par, birdie, and handicap, the etiquette and dress code expected on a course, and what it really costs, as of July 2026. This isn't meant to promote any brand or course - the goal is practical information that cuts down on trial and error before you pick up a club for the first time.

1. What Order Should You Learn Golf In?

Golf goes much more smoothly if you learn things in the right order. The commonly recommended path is: sign up for lessons → learn grip and swing basics at a golf simulator or indoor driving range → confirm real ball flight and distance at an outdoor driving range → play a round on an actual course (the field). Few people go straight to a course; most spend around three months practicing steadily before they're ready to play a full round. Rushing onto a course too soon makes things harder on you and puts pressure on the people you're playing with, so it's worth building at least the basics and etiquette first.

2. Golf Gear - the Club Set at a Glance

The rules of golf allow a maximum of 14 clubs in your bag during a round. But a beginner doesn't need all 14 from day one - a so-called "half set" of about 7 to 10 clubs is plenty to start.

2.1 Driver

The club used to hit the ball the farthest off the tee. It has a large head and a low loft. Beginners tend to do better with a driver around 10.5-12 degrees of loft and a shaft that isn't too stiff, since that combination makes it easier to get the ball airborne.

2.2 Fairway Woods and Hybrids

Fairway woods are the next-longest clubs after the driver, and hybrids blend the best of woods and irons - a larger head that's easier to hit and get airborne, which is why they're often recommended for beginners.

2.3 Irons

Used for mid-range approach shots; the lower the number, the farther it goes, and the higher the number, the shorter and higher it flies. A beginner set typically runs from a 6- or 7-iron through the pitching wedge.

2.4 Wedges

Specialized irons for short shots and getting out of bunkers. The pitching wedge and sand wedge are the essentials, and wedges carry the highest loft of any club.

2.5 Putter

Used to roll the ball into the hole on the green - and the club you'll actually use the most during a round.

2.6 Balls, Gloves, Shoes, and Bag

As a beginner you'll lose plenty of balls, so start with a generous supply of inexpensive ones - at least a dozen. A glove improves your grip, and golf shoes with spikes or spikeless soles keep you from slipping on the course. A golf bag carries your clubs, and a lightweight stand bag is the common choice if you'll be walking the course.

3. Rent or Buy - When Should You Actually Purchase?

The short answer: don't buy right away. Practice facilities and simulators almost always have rental clubs on hand, and many courses rent clubs too. It's smarter to take lessons first, learn your own swing style and what club specs (shaft flex, grip thickness, and so on) suit you, and buy afterward. In South Korea, a brand-new beginner full set realistically starts around 1,000,000 KRW, and premium brands can easily run past 2,500,000 KRW. If that's a stretch, starting with used clubs or an entry-level package set is a perfectly reasonable choice.

4. Simulator, Driving Range, and the Actual Course - What's the Difference?

The three spaces serve different purposes. A golf simulator (screen golf) lets you play indoors against a video screen. You can play in a small space, wear whatever you like, and it's relatively cheap - but it can't reproduce the feel of real turf or variables like wind. A driving range, indoor or outdoor, is where you hit into a net or an open field to refine your swing; at an outdoor range you can actually see ball flight and distance, which helps you correct your swing. The actual course (the field) is where you face every real variable - turf, bunkers, wind, slope - and it's the most expensive of the three, with the strictest etiquette and dress code. The usual learning path is to build fundamentals at a simulator or range before heading out to a real course.

5. The Basics - Grip, Stance, and the Swing

5.1 Grip

There are three main ways to hold a club. The overlapping (Vardon) grip rests the trail hand's pinky on top of the gap between the lead hand's index and middle fingers; most tour pros use it, and it suits larger hands. The interlocking grip weaves the trail hand's pinky between the lead hand's fingers, giving smaller hands a more secure hold. The baseball (ten-finger) grip keeps all ten fingers on the club, like holding a bat - it's the easiest to learn and is commonly recommended for beginners.

5.2 Stance

The basic stance is feet shoulder-width apart, knees slightly bent, and the upper body tilted forward from the hips with the back kept straight. This posture creates a stable axis for your body to rotate around during the swing.

5.3 The Swing

A swing breaks down into address (the setup), backswing (raising the club), downswing (bringing the club down), impact (the moment you strike the ball), and follow-through (the finish). Beginners are usually better off learning the feel of solid contact with a shorter swing before attempting a full one.

7. Course Etiquette, Manners, and Dress Code

Golf places more weight on etiquette than most sports. Making a mistake because you didn't know a rule is viewed very differently from ignoring it on purpose, so it pays to learn at least the following in advance.

  • Dress code: Most courses require a collared shirt and golf pants, shorts, or a skirt; jeans and gym wear are commonly banned. Rules vary by course, so it's safest to check ahead.
  • Quiet: Stay still and quiet while a playing partner is swinging.
  • Pace of play: Be ready to hit as soon as it's your turn, and be mindful not to slow down the group behind you.
  • Course care: Replace or fill divots, press down pitch marks on the green with a repair tool, and rake the bunker after your shot.
  • Cart rules: Follow cart-path rules and each course's regulations, and keep carts away from greens.

8. Cost Structure - Green Fees, Memberships, and Lessons

Golf costs break down into three main buckets: equipment, practice and lessons, and rounds.

  • Equipment: A brand-new beginner full set starts around 1,000,000 KRW, and premium sets can exceed 2,500,000 KRW. Renting or buying used cuts your upfront cost significantly.
  • Practice and lessons: An indoor simulator membership runs about 180,000-250,000 KRW a month, or roughly 500,000-600,000 KRW for a three-month package. Twice-weekly lessons add another 250,000-350,000 KRW a month.
  • Green fees: Public-style courses in South Korea cap course fees at around 199,000 KRW on weekdays and 259,000 KRW on weekends, though in practice weekday fees of 150,000-250,000 KRW and weekend fees of 250,000-400,000 KRW or more are common.
  • Caddie and cart fees: A caddie fee typically runs about 140,000-150,000 KRW per team, and cart fees run in the low six figures per team in KRW - usually split among four players.
  • Memberships: Private-club membership prices range from the tens of millions to billions of KRW, and most beginners don't need to think about this yet. It's fine to gain experience at public-style courses first and weigh membership later.

9. Tips for Your First Round

Arrive 15-30 minutes before your tee time and check in at the front desk. Bring plenty of balls since you'll lose some, and starting from the forward tees takes the pressure off. Don't obsess over your score - play "ready golf" by preparing while it's not your turn to help keep pace, and focus on staying quiet while others swing. Get those two things right and you've already won half the battle on your first round. It helps to remember that every golfer once stood exactly where you are now.

10. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. What should a beginner do first when starting golf?

Sign up for lessons before buying clubs, and learn grip, stance, and basic swing at a golf simulator or an indoor driving range first. Renting clubs is enough at the start, and most people practice steadily for about three months before heading out to an actual course.

Q2. How many golf clubs does a beginner need?

The rules allow up to 14 clubs in your bag during a round, but beginners do fine with a 7-10 club half set: a driver, a fairway wood or hybrid, a few irons, a wedge, and a putter. Add clubs one at a time as your game improves.

Q3. What is the difference between a golf simulator and an actual course?

A golf simulator (screen golf) lets you play indoors against a video screen - casual dress and lower cost, but it cannot reproduce real turf or wind. An actual course (the field) means playing outdoors under real conditions, which costs more and calls for stricter etiquette.

Q4. What golf etiquette must beginners follow?

Follow the course dress code, typically a collared shirt, stay quiet while others are swinging, be ready to hit promptly on your turn to keep pace, and repair divots and pitch marks and rake bunkers after you play out of them.

Q5. How much does it cost to start playing golf?

Starting with rental clubs and an indoor simulator or driving range plus lessons typically runs about 200,000-350,000 KRW a month. Once you move to an actual course, green fees, cart fees, and caddie fees usually add 200,000-400,000 KRW or more per round, and buying your own clubs can wait until your swing is more consistent.

11. Conclusion - Start With Rentals and Build the Fundamentals

Golf can feel like a high barrier to entry because you have to learn gear, terminology, and manners all at once. But if you start with rental clubs, build your fundamentals at a simulator or driving range, and learn the basics of grip, stance, and course etiquette ahead of time, you can genuinely enjoy your first round. I hope this guide gives you a practical head start.
