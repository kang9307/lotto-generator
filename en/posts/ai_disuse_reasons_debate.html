Deciding to Stop Using AI Coding Tools

Which reasons for quitting hold up, which ones do not, and where the line actually falls per task

Aug 16, 2026 Dev & Infra

Intro: when saying I do not use AI anymore became a position

Someone posts that line in a team channel and the thread runs long. Half the replies say they feel the same way lately. The other half say that just means you never learned to use it. The thread usually closes without deciding anything, because both sides are talking about the tool in general while their disagreement is actually about what their days are made of.

Quitting gets treated as a matter of taste, but it is arithmetic. There are only a few terms. The time it takes to bring a generated artifact to a state you would sign your name to, the time it would take to write it yourself, and the cost that is not visible today but gets billed later. Lay those three across task types and you get a clear result: in some cells quitting is obviously right and in others it is obviously expensive. Two answers, use it or do not, cannot fill that table.

This piece neither recommends quitting nor argues against it. It separates the reasons that can be checked from the reasons that are feelings wearing a different name, and explains why a decision made alone works only halfway once it enters a team.

1. Five roads to quitting - where people actually stop

The reasons people give look varied, but they collapse into five. Each one differs in how checkable it is.

1.1 Repeatedly hitting the region where verification costs more than generation

This is the sturdiest reason. When reading the output, confirming it matches intent, and checking its contracts with the surrounding code takes longer than simply writing the thing, the tool is a cost on that task. That is not a feeling. It is a stopwatch reading.

1.2 Code you do not understand accumulating in the repository

Code that works but that you cannot explain piles up. Nothing hurts today. The pain arrives six months later when that part breaks, and by then the cost exceeds the time originally saved. That axis is covered separately in the shifting economics of code, where writing got cheap and understanding got expensive.

1.3 Debugging changing character

Debugging your own code means finding where your assumption was wrong. Debugging generated code means reconstructing someone else's assumption first and then finding where it broke. One extra step, with no shortcut through it. In the 2025 Stack Overflow developer survey, 45.2% of 31,476 respondents said debugging AI-generated code takes more time than expected — a direct read on that change of character.

1.4 The role hardening from author into reviewer

Most of the day fills up with reading and judging things other agents produced. This is a job-satisfaction problem rather than a productivity one, which is exactly why numbers do not settle it. The structure of that supervision load is covered in the piece on why the human in the loop gets tired.

1.5 The upkeep cost of the tooling itself

Models change, prompts get reworked, context files need maintenance, plans get compared. None of that time ships a feature and none of it appears in any ledger. The smaller the team, the larger this share tends to be.

Of the five, 1.1 and 1.3 can be checked with a clock. 1.2 has too long a lag for an individual to confirm. 1.4 is not a measurement question at all, it is a preference. 1.5 becomes visible the moment you write it down. Reaching the same conclusion from reasons of different kinds means they should be handled differently.

2. When verification cost overtakes generation gain

The criterion itself is simple and already covered in the piece that draws boundaries by task type. What matters here is making it usable for a decision to stop.

Three variables set the break-even point.

  • Is there a grader? When a compiler, a type checker or a test says wrong immediately, verification cost approaches zero. When the only grader is a human, verification cost scales with the length of the output.
  • When does a mistake surface? Immediately is cheap. After deployment is expensive. Silently corrupting data while nobody notices makes the arithmetic impossible to perform at all.
  • Does the context live outside the code? If nothing in the repository records why this field is nullable or why these calls must happen in this order, a generator has no way to know. Output produced in that region is dangerous in proportion to how plausible it looks.

Read those three together and the break-even point turns out to depend less on model capability than on the nature of the codebase. In a repository with dense tests, strong types and clear boundaries, break-even sits far away. In a repository whose implicit conventions live only in people's heads, break-even is right in front of you. That is usually why two engineers using the same tool reach opposite conclusions.

One-line working test. When output arrives, ask whether you could merge it without reading it. If not, reading time belongs in the calculation, and once that reading time passes half the writing time, drop the tool from that task type.

3. The deferred invoice from code you never understood

What makes this one awkward is that the cost is billed in a different period than the decision. Saving thirty minutes today and spending six hours in six months is a net loss, but the two events land in different quarterly reports. Organisational metrics rarely catch it.

A few practical signals let an individual detect it.

  • The share of questions about your own merged code that you cannot answer on the spot goes up.
  • During incidents you fail to narrow the candidates and end up re-reading everything.
  • You cannot propose a refactor, because you do not know what is safe to touch.
  • The second time you modify a module takes longer than the first.

The fourth signal has the highest diagnostic value. In a healthy codebase the second visit to the same place is faster. If that direction has inverted, the fraction of the repository you do not know is growing.

These signals also appear without any tool involved. A thin handover, code that was always someone else's, or a team that is growing fast produce the same symptoms. Before concluding the tool did it, check whether the parts you wrote by hand show the same pattern. An attribution made without that comparison is an impression, not evidence.

4. The case for the other side - task types the tools clearly win

What quitting arguments often omit is that the break-even calculation runs in both directions. Where verification is cheap the gain is real, and those tasks are not a small share of the working day.

4.1 Mechanical transformation

Schema to type definitions, JSON to structs, one configuration format into another. Input maps to output mechanically and mistakes break loudly. Doing it by hand is tedious and typo-prone, while verification is one execution. Removing the tool from this cell is a straight loss.

4.2 Boilerplate

Writing the eleventh instance of a pattern that already appears ten times. The context is in the code and verification is a comparison with existing cases. The risk is that a wrong pattern gets replicated quickly. So this cell assumes the existing pattern is correct, and that judgement stays with a person.

4.3 Exploration

Finding the entry point of an unfamiliar library, narrowing candidates for an error message you have never seen, deciding where to start reading in a large repository. The output here is direction rather than code, and a wrong direction reveals itself within minutes. Cheap verification leaves the gain intact.

4.4 Test scaffolding and boundary enumeration

A person decides what to test, but enumerating cases exhaustively is machine work. People tend to test the path they had in mind, which is exactly how boundaries get missed.

These four cells share one property: grading is immediate and cheap. The cells where quitting is justified share the opposite one: grading requires a human, domain knowledge and time. So the argument was never about the tool. It is about which cells your day is made of.

6. Why an individual decision only half works inside a team

This is the largest blank in the individual case for quitting. Stop using the tools and the code you produce stays comprehensible. The volume of code you have to read does not fall by a line. If colleagues raise their output it rises.

The mechanism runs like this.

  • Faster generation raises the team's inflow of changes.
  • Review capacity is tied to headcount and does not scale at the same rate.
  • When the queue lengthens, review concentrates on whoever is most thorough, because whatever others wave through lands on that person anyway.
  • The person who quit is usually that person. The price of quitting is a heavier review load.
  • None of it shows up in any metric, because review time is rarely counted as work.

DORA's 2025 findings point the same way. Across roughly 5,000 technology professionals, AI adoption showed a positive relationship with delivery throughput and product performance but a negative relationship with delivery stability. In the report's own framing, without robust control systems such as strong automated testing, mature version control and fast feedback loops, an increase in change volume leads to instability. One person quitting does not reduce the team's change volume, which puts this problem outside the range of any individual decision.

What actually works at team level sits on the inflow side rather than on anyone's personal usage.

  • A cap on change size. Make reviewable size a convention. Anything larger gets split.
  • Provenance marking. Say when a change is heavily generated, because a reviewer should read it differently. Open source has taken a stronger form of the same principle, with Godot, SQLite and Rust each drawing explicit contribution lines.
  • Counting review time. If review does not enter sprint capacity, it stays free forever.
  • Reviewer rotation. Keep the load from pooling on one person.
  • An author explanation requirement. Before merge, the author writes the intent and the risky spots. Failing to write it is itself the signal.

7. What the empirical work says and does not say

Plenty of material gets cited in productivity arguments, but different designs yield different conclusions. Move a number without its sample and conditions and it stops being evidence and becomes decoration.

Read together the three do not contradict each other. Adoption spread wide (DORA 90%, Stack Overflow 84%), perception is broadly positive (DORA above 80%), trust did not follow (32.7% trusting against 45.7% distrusting, and 30% in DORA reporting little or none), and elapsed time measured under controlled conditions can run against perception (METR). Wide adoption and shallow trust holding at the same time is the picture the 2025 to 2026 material draws in common.

One condition must always travel with the METR number. That trial studied experienced developers in repositories they knew intimately. That is the condition in which the human baseline is highest, and therefore the least favourable one for a generator. Nothing in the trial supports expecting the same result for someone entering an unfamiliar codebase or working in a language they do not know well. The researchers wrote as much themselves.

8. What is measurable and what is not, in the US adoption argument and elsewhere

Organisation-level measurement design is covered in a separate piece, and why most commonly used metrics are wrong is covered in the one on measurement traps. Here we take only the minimum an individual needs.

Context matters for how this argument is framed. In much of the US market the debate has shifted from whether to adopt to whether mandated adoption is producing anything, and leadership-level adoption targets now sit uneasily beside the individual-contributor distrust the surveys above record. Public figures on how many organisations have rolled back or narrowed a mandate are not something this piece could verify, so treat the shape of the argument as the observation and not any specific rate.

8.1 What you can measure

  • Time to completion per task type. Averaging across mixed types shows nothing. Split by the rows of Table 2.
  • Rework count. How often the same change gets touched again after merge. First-pass rate is the substance of the gain.
  • Review round trips. Comment rounds until your change is approved. Rising numbers mean the output is not being understood.
  • Non-productive time. Time spent on tool configuration, prompt maintenance and model comparison. Unlogged, it counts as zero.

8.2 What you cannot

  • What would have happened otherwise. An individual has no counterfactual, so personal observation is always a before-and-after without a control group. Use it while admitting that.
  • Long-term capability change. Over a few months it is indistinguishable from an ordinary learning curve.
  • Code quality itself. Proxies exist, direct measures do not. Defect counts move with how hard you look.
  • Job satisfaction. Measurable, but do not convert it into productivity. They are different axes, and in a decision to stop, satisfaction is a legitimate reason on its own terms.
A four-week personal experiment. Pick the three rows of Table 2 that dominate your work. Within each row, alternate tool and no-tool per task rather than per weekday, since weekdays mix difficulty. Log only completion time, rework count and review round trips. Four weeks shows direction. Pinning down magnitude needs a far larger sample, which is not something an individual can produce.

9. Why partial use collapses, and what makes it hold

Only where I need it is a compromise that fails often. The cause is not willpower. It is that the boundary is not fixed in any state.

The collapse runs predictably. You start with boilerplate only. Then the logic beside it gets generated too. The session is open anyway, so you ask one more thing. A few days later nobody remembers where the line was. When the tool is always on and invoking it costs nothing, the boundary becomes something you re-decide every time, and deciding every time ends up being the same as not deciding.

Arrangements that hold share one property: the boundary is made of configuration rather than resolve.

  • Leave the tool off by default. If switching it on is explicit, that moment becomes the decision point.
  • Split by repository or directory. A settings file is more stable than a personal commitment.
  • Cap the size of a single output. Limiting how much arrives at once reduces merging without reading.
  • Never merge what you received verbatim. Retyping while reading leaves almost no comprehension debt. It looks slower and is faster in the lower rows of Table 2.
  • Revisit the table monthly. When the codebase changes, break-even moves with it.

That is why a full stop is easier to sustain than partial use: there is no boundary to defend. The price of that simplicity is giving up the entire gain from the top four rows of Table 2. Which is cheaper depends on how your work is composed.

10. Where contract structure moves the line

Beyond individual preference, the break-even point moves with how the work is contracted, and this is where market context changes the answer.

  • Contract and agency delivery. The moment an artifact is handed over, comprehension debt transfers to the client. The producing side never sees the cost and the receiving side cannot isolate the cause. That is the arrangement in which the second row of Table 1 gets structurally hidden.
  • Long-running maintenance. Conversely, when the same organisation carries a system for years, comprehension debt returns as its own cost. Restraint in the lower rows of Table 2 becomes a real gain.
  • Thickness of review culture. The more ceremonial the review, the more generated output passes straight through. That is not the queue problem of section 6; it is merging with no queue at all.
  • Acceptance criteria in public procurement. When acceptance is documentation-centred, the comprehensibility of the code is not an acceptance item. The gap predates any tool, but rising output volume widens it.
  • Air-gapped and regulated environments. Where outbound calls are blocked, the option set differs from the start, and the argument shifts from whether to use to what deployment shape to allow.

Summarised: the strongest justification for quitting is structural rather than emotional — whether you are the one who pays the comprehension debt. Inside that structure, restraint pays. Outside it, restraint is a personal cost with no return, which is why trying to solve the second case with individual resolve rarely works.

11. What could not be verified

At the time of writing (August 2026), no reliable primary source was available for the following. The article describes their shape without attaching numbers.

  • Official statistics on discontinuation rates for AI coding tools. Adoption surveys exist; a credible source that counts abandonment or scaling back separately was not found.
  • Independent research measuring the relationship between generated-code share and long-term defect rates under controlled conditions. Correlational observations exist; a causal treatment was not found.
  • Measurements of how a team's review load actually redistributes when one person stops. The mechanism in section 6 is an explanation, not a quantified finding.
  • Controlled studies on AI use and developer mental health. Individual accounts exist; generalisable data was not found.
  • Experiments on how break-even differs by experience level. The METR sample is restricted to experienced developers, and a controlled trial with juniors was not found.

Conclusion: write the decision down and you can undo it

Whether you stop or continue, recording the conditions the decision rested on matters more than the decision. Write the conditions and you can revisit the call when they change. Leave them unwritten and the decision becomes an identity, and identities do not move when the evidence does.

Three lines are enough.

  • What you observed, in which task type. Not it got slower, but review round trips roughly doubled on legacy changes without regression tests.
  • What would reverse this decision. Once coverage passes a threshold, once the team adopts a review convention, once that task type disappears — in that form.
  • When you will look again. Codebases and tools both change on a scale of months. There is a reason the METR trial has Early-2025 in its title. Measurements are bound to a date.

I use AI and I do not use AI are both summaries rather than judgements. Unfold the summary into a table and most people find they were already standing in both columns. Arguments run long because two people look at different cells and use the same word for it.
