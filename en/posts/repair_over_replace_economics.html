Repair or Replace - Deciding With Repair Cost and Remaining Life

From sorting the failure layer to annualized cost, parts availability windows and right-to-repair law

Aug 17, 2026 Life & Practical

Introduction: The Decision You Make While Holding the Quote

The technician comes, looks, and writes a number. Parts, labour, total. And then, almost always, a sentence follows: there is a new model out, and for that money you might as well. Five seconds later we have decided, on the strength of exactly one input. What percentage of a new unit does the repair cost?

That calculation misfires because it reads the numerator and ignores the denominator. A repair costing 300 dollars buys four more years in one case and one more year in another. That is the difference between 75 dollars a year and 300 dollars a year for an identical invoice, and the quote does not print the number that separates them. The new unit has missing numbers too: delivery and installation, disposal of the old machine, and the electricity bill difference stretched across the next decade.

This piece fills those blanks in. How to sort the failure by layer, where to check whether parts still exist, and how to put both options into the same unit so a comparison means something. It also includes the part that repair advocacy often skips: repairing is not always the right answer. Nor is this a piece about blaming manufacturers. Designs that resist repair usually resist it for a reason, and knowing the reason sharpens the decision.

1. The Four Variables That Decide It

In practice only four variables flip the conclusion. Everything else is a derivative of these.

  • Parts availability. This comes first. If the part does not exist, the other three never need computing. It is the input people check last and should check first.
  • Labour against parts cost. Some failures are cheap parts and expensive labour, others the reverse. When the parts cost dominates, the part is usually a module, and module pricing climbs toward the price of a whole new unit fast.
  • Remaining life after the fix. How many more years does this repair actually buy? This is the denominator. Two identical part swaps land differently when the rest of the machine has worn unevenly.
  • Whether the failure is consumable or structural. A worn consumable is scheduled spending. Structural damage is an announcement about the next failure. This distinction is what sets your estimate of remaining life.

The order of questions matters as much as the questions. Ask about parts first, then about the nature of the failure, and only then about money. Ask about money first and the two earlier answers get coloured by it.

2. Sort the Failure First - Consumable or Structural

Half the decision is settled by reading the diagnosis properly. The same symptom means different things depending on which layer the cause sits in. Three layers cover almost everything.

LayerWhat it looks likeRemaining life after repairDefault call
ConsumableFilters, belts, hoses, gaskets, batteries, brushes, lamps - parts designed with a short life from the startThe full design life of the body is still thereAlmost always repair. This is scheduled maintenance
ComponentPumps, motors, valves, sensors, control boards - a single part has failedDepends on how worn the neighbouring parts areRepair if the unit is under half its design life
StructuralFrame deformation, internal corrosion, accumulated water damage, refrigerant circuit leaks, immersion historyShort. The next failure is already queuedLeans strongly toward replacement

Take the part name written on the diagnosis and place it in this table. A drain pump swap is component layer, a door gasket is consumable layer, and a refrigerant leak requiring a joint to be redone is structural. When the wording is ambiguous, one question resolves it: did something else cause this part to fail? If the cause lives elsewhere, that elsewhere is the real layer.

Water immersion and overheating deserve their own treatment. A board that has been wet can be repaired at the failed point while corrosion advances quietly at another, and wiring whose insulation has been cooked by heat looks perfectly fine from the outside. For those two histories, keep the remaining-life estimate short no matter how cheap the quote looks.

3. How to Compute Total Cost - Annualize Both Sides

Repair cost and new unit price cannot be compared directly because they are priced in different units. One buys a certain number of years, the other buys a different number of years. Put both on a per-year basis and the comparison becomes real.

Annual cost of repairing = repair quote ÷ years of use gained by the repair
Annual cost of replacing = (new unit price + delivery, installation and disposal − resale value of the old unit) ÷ expected life of the new unit − annual energy bill saving
Compare the two and take the smaller. Subtracting the energy saving on the replacement side is the step most people skip.

Numbers make it concrete. The figures below are assumptions chosen to show the structure of the calculation; real amounts vary by category, model and region.

Case A - Twelve-year-old refrigerator, compressor work at 350 dollars

  • Repair: 350 ÷ 4 years (everything besides the compressor has twelve years of wear, so a longer horizon is hard to justify) = 88 dollars a year
  • Replace: (900 for the new unit + 0 installation − 0 resale) ÷ 12 years = 75 dollars a year
  • Now the energy gap. If the labelled consumption differs by 30 kWh a month, that is 360 kWh a year; at 0.15 per kWh the saving is 54 dollars a year
  • Effective replacement cost: 75 − 54 = 21 dollars a year → replacement wins

Case B - Three-year-old washing machine, drain pump at 70 dollars

  • Repair: 70 ÷ 7 years (three years used out of a ten-year design life) = 10 dollars a year
  • Replace: 600 ÷ 10 years = 60 dollars a year, and the energy gap against a three-year-old machine is negligible → repair wins by roughly six to one, so repair decisively

The repair-to-new ratios in these two cases are 39 percent and 12 percent. The half-price rule says repair in both, while annualizing splits them. What the rule misses is the denominator and the energy line.

Because the choice of years drives the outcome, run an optimistic and a pessimistic value. If repair still wins when you assume only two more years of service, that is a safe repair. If it only wins on the optimistic value, go back and verify parts availability before committing.

5. Parts Availability Windows - How Long Repair Stays Possible

The ceiling on repairability is the parts availability window. In Korea the Consumer Dispute Resolution Standards, a public notice issued by the Fair Trade Commission, set that period by product category. The table below reflects the revision of 18 December 2025 and can be checked against the text published by the Korea Consumer Agency and the Easy Law commentary. Verified as of August 2026.

CategoryWarranty periodParts availability
Refrigerator1 year9 years
Television1 year9 years
Air conditioner (cooling only)2 years8 years
Air conditioner (heating and cooling)1 year8 years
Washing machine1 year7 years
Water purifier1 year7 years
Vacuum cleaner1 year7 years
Electric rice cooker1 year6 years
Desktop and laptop1 year4 years
Mobile handset2 years (battery 1 year)4 years

What matters in this table is not the numbers but the start date. The clock runs from the manufacturing date, and where only a year or a year and month appears, the last day of that year or month counts. It does not run from the day you bought it.

  • If you bought a display unit or a discounted carryover model, part of the window was already spent when you carried it home
  • The same model name can have different remaining windows depending on production run. The label on the back or side carries the manufacturing month
  • Four years for laptops and phones looks short, but component generations turn over quickly in those categories and the inventory economics differ from large appliances
  • The window is a floor, not a promise in either direction. Parts do not vanish the day it expires, and an unpopular model can run dry on a specific part well before it

So in practice, instead of memorising the table, call the service centre with the model name and manufacturing month and ask whether that specific part is in stock. That question comes before the quote.

6. How Unrepairable Gets Built - The Design Logic

Repair gets harder for reasons, and most of those reasons are trade-offs made for something else. Understanding the structure lets you predict which products will resist repair.

Discontinued parts

Parts inventory is capital sitting in a warehouse. Restarting a small production run means pulling the tooling and stopping a line, and dividing that fixed cost across a few hundred units pushes the unit price of a single part toward the price of a finished product. Discontinuation is therefore less about stinginess than about the unit economics of small batches. The flip side is useful: models that sold in volume keep parts available longer. Buying the mainstream model rather than the unusual variant is a repairability decision.

Module-level replacement

Designing so that a board carrying ten small components is swapped whole, rather than diagnosing and replacing each of the ten, shortens diagnosis and raises first-visit resolution rates. Measured as service quality, that is an improvement. The cost is that a single failed resistor bills you for the board. Repair time falls, repair price rises.

Adhesive assembly

Screws and clips occupy volume and puncture seals. Where a design needs a higher ingress rating, a thinner profile and drop resistance, adhesive wins on the engineering merits. Without that choice, current waterproofing and thicknesses would not exist. The price is that opening the device requires heat and dedicated tools, and the seal has to be re-established afterwards.

Software parts pairing

Components are cryptographically matched to the body, so an unregistered part triggers limited functionality or a warning. For fingerprint sensors and cameras, where security and calibration are genuinely at stake, there is a case for it. The friction appears when the same mechanism extends to genuine parts fitted outside authorised channels. This is precisely why recent regulation aims at that point rather than banning the design.

Put together, regulation tends to touch three things: making parts obtainable, keeping part prices from being high enough to make repair pointless, and forcing repair information to be published. Prohibiting particular design methods is not the approach taken.

7. Right to Repair - What Has Actually Been Legislated

Right to repair is no longer a slogan; it is text in statute. The following reflects what is confirmed as of August 2026.

European Union

  • Directive (EU) 2024/1799 was published in the Official Journal on 10 July 2024, and the resulting obligations apply from 31 July 2026
  • The categories in scope include household washing machines and washer-dryers, dishwashers, refrigerating appliances, vacuum cleaners, electronic displays including televisions and monitors, mobile phones, cordless phones and tablets, and servers
  • The length of the spare parts supply obligation is set per category in conjunction with the ecodesign regulations, running longer for large appliances and shorter for phones and laptops
  • The framework also carries the intent that parts must not be priced so high that repair becomes economically pointless

The French index system

  • A repairability index has been mandatory since January 2021. It runs on a scale of 0 to 10 and is built from five axes: documentation, ease of disassembly, spare parts availability, spare parts pricing and category-specific criteria
  • It began with five categories - smartphones, laptops, televisions, front-loading washing machines and lawnmowers - and from 4 November 2022 added top-loading washing machines, dishwashers, vacuum cleaners and pressure washers
  • A durability index introduced by decree on 5 April 2024 is progressively replacing the repairability index. It applies to televisions from January 2025 and front-loading washing machines from April 2025, keeping repairability criteria while adding reliability and commercial guarantee factors

United States

  • The work has happened at state level rather than federally. Electronics repair laws have been enacted in New York, Minnesota, California, Oregon and Colorado, with two in Colorado
  • Connecticut is scheduled for 1 July 2026 and Texas for 1 September 2026
  • Oregon SB 1596 is regarded as the first law to address parts pairing directly, with Colorado taking a similar approach. Enforcement of the Oregon provision was deferred to 2027
  • The New York and California statutes are silent on software verification gates, which critics identify as the gap in those regimes

Korea

Korea has a statutory basis as well. The Act on Promotion of Transition to a Circular Economy Society, a full rewrite of the former Framework Act on Resource Circulation, took effect on 1 January 2024 and introduced grounds for supplying repair parts and information, with a clause on informing consumers about repair support applying from 1 January 2025.

The strength of that provision should be read accurately, though. It is framed as a duty to make efforts, and its scope is limited to producers and importers of manufactured goods covered by the Consumer Dispute Resolution Standards that carry a parts availability window of three years or more. That is structurally different from the EU directive, which imposes the repair obligation itself and attaches sanctions. The Korean regime leans toward securing parts, and does not yet effectively guarantee self-repair information and tool access.

8. How Big the Environmental Argument Really Is

The claim that repairing is greener is half true, because two emissions streams occur on different timelines.

  • Manufacturing emissions happen once and are already spent. The longer the item serves, the thinner they spread per year. This is the ground repair stands on
  • Use-phase emissions accrue for as long as the item runs. Where the efficiency gap between old and new is wide, the difference here more than offsets the manufacturing cost of a new unit

So the fork narrows to a single question. Does this item consume energy while you use it?

  • Items that do not - furniture, bags, shoes, bicycles, hand tools, clothing. Repair wins here nearly always, because no saving exists for a replacement to recover
  • Items used intermittently - microwaves, hairdryers, vacuum cleaners. Short run times mean the efficiency gap barely registers in the total. Leans to repair
  • Items running around the clock - refrigerators, always-on filtration and heating equipment. This is where replacement can win environmentally
  • Seasonal peak loads - air conditioners and boilers. Run hours are limited but instantaneous draw is high, so a wide efficiency gap pays back quickly

Using a refrigerator as the example, the payback logic works like this. If the labelled consumption gap between old and new is large, a few years of operation offsets the manufacturing emissions and everything after that is net gain. If the efficiency difference is slim and the old unit still runs well, replacing it adds fresh manufacturing emissions with no recovery - the worst combination available. A study on the optimal household refrigerator replacement policy covering life cycle energy, greenhouse gas emissions and cost works through the same structure. The exact payback period varies with the product, usage pattern and grid mix, so a single universal number does not exist.

Checking it is straightforward. Compare the monthly consumption printed on the energy label of the new unit with the figure on the old unit label or its manual. If the label is long gone, the model number will find the specification sheet. Multiply the difference by twelve and by the per-kWh rate on your bill, and the annual saving drops straight into the formula in section three.

9. What to Check at Purchase to Regret Less Later

Repairability is not decided when something breaks. Most of it is fixed at the moment of purchase. Restricting the list to what can be checked in a shop:

  • Manufacturing month. Since the parts window runs from that date, it is the start line for how long repair stays possible. A deeply discounted carryover model should have that spent time priced in
  • The parts availability window for that category. Check the category classification, because two products that look like the same appliance can sit in different categories with different windows
  • Distance to the official service network. Whether on-site repair is available or the unit has to be shipped in changes the real cost of an identical fault. On islands and in remote areas this alone can flip a decision
  • Self-repair support. Whether service manuals are published, individual parts are sold, and tool loan programmes exist. Where they do, consumable-layer costs drop sharply
  • Consumable formats and prices. Whether filters and belts are proprietary or generic, and what the annual consumables bill comes to. For many categories this drives total cost more than the purchase price
  • Module integration and software locks. Whether board, display and battery come as one piece or separate, and whether a part swap needs registration or triggers feature limits. Separable and unlocked means small faults bill small
  • Energy label and monthly consumption. Today it is a bill line; later it becomes an input to the replacement decision. Keeping the label figure with the manual pays off in five years
  • Sales volume. Models that sold well keep parts stock and repair know-how alive longer. The mainstream configuration beats the exotic one on repairability

Products sold in Europe may carry a repairability or durability index, and that labelling does not apply to models sold in Korea. Still, checking whether an equivalent model from the same manufacturer scored on those indices gives a reasonable read on the design philosophy.

10. The Line on Self-Repair

Any article about repair has to draw one line, and it is drawn by safety rather than by cost.

Work that belongs to qualified technicians
1. Circuits holding high voltage. Components such as microwave oven capacitors retain charge after the plug is pulled. Electric shock does not require the power to be on.
2. Lithium-ion batteries. Puncture, compression and deformation can lead to thermal runaway. A cell that has already swollen is hazardous simply to remove.
3. Gas-fired equipment. Installation and inspection of boilers and combustion appliances carry licensing requirements, and incomplete combustion produces carbon monoxide.
4. Sealed refrigerant circuits. Handling refrigerant and working on the pipework requires certification and recovery equipment.
5. Safety-critical systems. Vehicle braking and steering, lifting equipment, earth leakage protection circuits.
This is not a line you cross by learning a technique. Have it diagnosed by someone qualified.

Conversely, anything the manual documents as a procedure is generally safe territory: cleaning filters and dust bins, consumables reachable with ordinary tools, clogged drains, battery covers held by screws, firmware updates. The test is simple. Does the manufacturer manual describe it as a user task? Following a video through a disassembly that the manual does not cover risks falling outside warranty coverage, and a poor reassembly creates a new fault. For the five areas above, assume self-repair is not an option and put a qualified technician rate into the repair line of the calculation.

11. What Could Not Be Confirmed

  • The category useful-life schedule annexed to the Consumer Dispute Resolution Standards exists, but the per-category figures were not reproduced here. If depreciation calculations require them, consult the annex directly
  • Actual dates on which manufacturers exhaust parts stock and standard labour rate tables by category in Korea are not published. The notice period is a floor and differs from real inventory, and the amounts in this article are assumptions shown to illustrate the calculation
  • Member state implementing legislation under the EU directive and the per-category parts supply durations tied to the ecodesign regulations need to be checked individually
  • Enforcement practice after the deferral of the Oregon parts pairing provision has not yet accumulated observable cases

Conclusion: The Habit of Writing In the Denominator

The quote prints only the numerator. The denominator is ours to write. How many more years does this repair buy, and how many years will the new one serve while saving how much annually? Write those two numbers down and the half-price rule stops being necessary.

The order holds too. Ask whether the part exists. Sort the failure into consumable or structural. Annualize the money. Add the energy gap last. Asked in that sequence, it also becomes obvious that this is not a decision to make in five seconds while a technician waits.

And one thing deserves honest acknowledgement: repairing is not always right. Swapping an old always-on machine for one that is substantially more efficient can be the correct call on both cost and carbon. Replacing something that still works with something barely more efficient satisfies neither argument. A preference for repair is not a substitute for arithmetic. Do the calculation, then follow it.

The last piece is the next purchase. A few minutes spent on manufacturing month, parts window and consumable formats widens your options five years from now. Choosing repairable things is the most reliable repair strategy there is.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I replace once the repair costs more than half of a new unit?

It is a widely used shortcut, but it drops remaining life out of the equation and therefore fails often. The same 50 percent points in opposite directions on a three-year-old unit and a twelve-year-old one. Convert both options into annual cost: divide the repair quote by the years of use it buys, then divide the new unit price minus resale value by the expected life of the new unit and subtract the annual energy saving.

When does the spare parts availability period start counting?

In Korea it runs from the manufacturing date, not the purchase date. Where only a year or a year and month is printed, the last day of that period counts. Clearance stock, display units and carryover models can already have burned one or two years of the window. Under the Consumer Dispute Resolution Standards as revised on 18 December 2025, refrigerators and televisions carry nine years, air conditioners eight, washing machines and water purifiers seven, and laptops and mobile handsets four.

Is repairing always the greener choice?

No. The dividing question is whether the item consumes energy while in use. Furniture, bags and hand tools should almost always be repaired, because no efficiency saving exists for a replacement to recover. An old refrigerator running around the clock can be worth replacing when the efficiency gap is wide. Replacing a working unit whose efficiency is barely better serves neither cost nor carbon.

What does right-to-repair law actually require today?

In the EU, Directive (EU) 2024/1799 was published on 10 July 2024 and applies from 31 July 2026 across washing machines, dishwashers, refrigerating appliances, vacuum cleaners, displays, phones, tablets and servers. In the US the work is state by state, with laws in New York, Minnesota, California, Oregon and Colorado, and Connecticut and Texas dates in 2026. Korea has a partial basis through the Act on Promotion of Transition to a Circular Economy Society, framed as a duty to make efforts.

How far can I safely repair something myself?

As far as the manufacturer manual describes as a user task - filters, belts, hoses, dust bins, clogged drains, screw-held battery covers. Outside that line sits qualified work: high-voltage components such as microwave capacitors that hold charge after unplugging, lithium-ion batteries, sealed refrigerant circuits, and gas-fired boilers and combustion appliances. That boundary is about fire, electric shock and carbon monoxide, not about cost.
