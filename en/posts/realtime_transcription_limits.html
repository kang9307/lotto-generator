Answering Before the Sentence Ends - Why Real-Time Transcription Is Harder Than Transcribing a Recording

A recording lets you hear what comes next and fix what came before. In real time you cannot

Introduction: the words have to appear before the meeting ends

Captions scrolling under a live meeting and a transcript file landing twenty minutes after it ends are not the same product. From the outside both are turning speech into text, so they get filed as one feature. From the inside they share fewer parts than you would expect.

In August 2026 Google released two speech-to-text models together: gemini-3.5-transcribe-live for real-time streaming and gemini-3.5-transcribe for pre-recorded audio. The first attaches to the Live API and the second to the Interactions API, according to the published announcement. The same document lists automatic detection across more than 85 languages, word-level timestamps, custom vocabulary, and live language switching.

This article is not a tour of that release. The fact that the name split in two is the subject. When one company ships a streaming version and a recorded version in the same generation, it is saying the two jobs are not one problem.

Here is the judgement up front. In live transcription the result is usually decided not by model quality but by a design choice: when to commit. That one decision sets the latency, the rate at which sentences get chopped, the odds that speaker labels swap, and ultimately whether the output can be used as a meeting record. What follows walks through what the constraint actually changes. How to choose a tool and what to ask a vendor is covered in what to check before picking a transcription tool, so this is a piece about constraints rather than shopping.

1. Can you hear the end and fix the beginning

Transcribing a recording starts with the whole file in hand, and the advantage is enormous. English shows it too: the sound ice cream and I scream are the same acoustic event, and only what follows settles it. Korean is worse, because case markers and endings attach to stems, so word boundaries themselves move depending on what comes next.

Live has no next yet. And the text already emitted has already been read. Undoing it means letters changing on screen, which is a direct cost to the reader: the eye stops and re-reads what it just took in.

The published figures follow the same split. The announcement lists an average word error rate of 4.0% for streaming and 2.6% for non-streaming, while attributing both to measurements by the third party Artificial Analysis. On the multilingual FLEURS benchmark the top-language averages are 5.50% streaming and 5.04% non-streaming. In either set the streaming side is worse. Not because the model is weaker, but because it gave up the chance to fix the beginning.

Do not transplant these numbers into your own meeting room. FLEURS is a parallel speech dataset covering 102 languages with roughly 12 hours per language, built on top of the FLoRes-101 machine translation benchmark. That is read sentences, not six people talking over each other. A word error rate is defined by which dataset, which speakers and which noise conditions produced it.

2. Waiting longer means guessing better - latency and accuracy are one dial

Streaming recognisers generally listen a little ahead. Before committing to the current sound they wait a few hundred milliseconds and judge it together with what follows. The wait buys accuracy, and the wait is the delay. Latency and accuracy are not two dials but one.

Experiments show the coupling directly. A Google research paper on streaming end-to-end recognition added separate penalties for ending an utterance too early and too late and reported an 8.0% relative word error rate reduction together with a 130ms reduction in 90th percentile latency on a voice search test set. With a second-pass rescorer combined, the paper reports 18.7% relative reduction and 160ms. There is a reason those two numbers appear in the same sentence.

So asking only about accuracy is half a question. It always needs the words at what latency attached. And what a user actually feels is not the model's number alone.

  • Audio buffer and chunk size. How often the device hands over sound, and how much goes per send
  • Network round trip. With a server-side setup this is often the largest share
  • Commit policy. Whether provisional text is shown first or only finalised text appears
  • Rendering. Screen refresh and scroll handling land in perceived delay too

Once the use case is fixed, the cut point mostly follows. For captions a person reads live, latency wins; slightly wrong but readable beats correct and late. For a draft meeting record, latency is close to irrelevant, so turn the dial toward accuracy. For voice commands, latency is the usability, and where a misheard command is destructive, adding a confirmation step is more reliable than swapping models.

3. The moment you declare the speaker finished

A live system has to announce at some point that this person has finished speaking. But silence does not mean finished.

  • Thinking. Recalling a number or choosing a word can leave more than a second of nothing
  • Breathing. A pause inside a long sentence is not a terminator
  • Silence filled by fillers and noise. An um, or a keyboard, erases the silent stretch entirely
  • Trailing off. Speakers who drop their volume at the end make the boundary blurrier

Set the threshold short and sentences break in half. The severed remainder enters as a separate utterance, and the context between them is gone. A system that also responds starts answering a sentence that has not finished. Set it long and the reply lags, so the other person restarts and the speech overlaps. Either way the user concludes that the tool cannot hold a conversation.

A single fixed silence threshold rarely covers this. People differ, and the same person differs by situation. That is why the paper above trains the decision with different penalties for cutting early and cutting late instead of leaving a hand-picked constant in the code.

There is room on the operator's side as well. Whether the end-of-speech wait can be tuned, whether provisional text is exposed before commit, whether a new onset cancels a previous commit. A tool that leaves none of these open gives you nothing to adjust in a room where everyone speaks differently.

4. People do not speak in sentences

What surprises people opening a transcript for the first time is not the errors. It is that the words are right and it still cannot be read.

Real speech runs like this. So the file, no, the whole folder, if we just move the whole folder that works, right? Oh but the, the permissions on it. Nothing here is misheard. That is how the person spoke. While talking we abandon a started sentence and back up, correct mid-phrase, repeat ourselves and leave endings hanging. Listeners tidy it up in real time, which is why nobody notices.

So transcription splits in two. Verbatim writes down everything heard, repetitions and fillers included, and belongs wherever how something was said is the point. Clean transcription drops the abandoned run-ups, deletes fillers and stands the sentences up; it is for documents meant to be read. The announcement putting self-correction handling, filler removal and automatic punctuation on its feature list is aimed at the clean side. That means it is treated as explicit work rather than incidental polish.

The problem is that clean transcription is structurally disadvantaged live. Tidying requires the utterance to be over. Whether to delete the file gets decided only after no, the whole folder arrives, and by then the earlier phrase is on screen. So live captions tend to stay close to verbatim, with the tidying done in a second pass afterwards.

One thing gets missed here. A cleaned transcript is a different document from the original. It reads better and it is no longer what was said. Where the wording itself is the issue, as in interview quotes or fact checking, that gap is not something to wave through.

5. Who said it is a separate problem

The substance of a meeting record is not the sentences but who decided what. A perfect transcript with swapped speaker labels is worth close to nothing. Diarization is a separate pipeline from recognition, and the conditions that break it are different too.

  • Overlapping speech. In conversations full of backchannels and interruptions, overlapped stretches bury one side by construction
  • Similar voices. Two people with close pitch and cadence are hardest to split on short turns
  • Far-field microphones. Distance and reverberation smear the per-speaker characteristics
  • An unknown headcount. Knowing the number in advance helps a lot, and meetings gain people midway

The difficulty gap is visible in public evaluation. The Third DIHARD Diarization Challenge report, covering 11 domains, notes that diarization has improved markedly for two-party interactions while stating that for domains such as web video the problem remains far from solved. Broken out by domain, the harder ones including meetings, web video and restaurant sat around a 35~45% median diarization error rate under track 1, while six domains built on one or two participants came in below 10%.

Live adds one more constraint. Approaches that gather per-speaker characteristics and cluster them work best with the whole file, whereas a live system has to decide, the moment a new voice appears, whether this is someone heard before or someone new. Defer and the label arrives late; rush and one person splits into two.

Reading the announcement in that light, it states accurate attribution for up to three speakers and marks anything beyond that as experimental. Most working meetings have more than three.

6. Accuracy breaks in predictable places

Open a handful of transcripts and the errors are clearly not spread evenly. The same places give way almost every time.

6.1 Proper nouns and jargon

Personal names, company names, product names, internal acronyms. These are absent or rare in training data, and worse, context cannot recover them. Jon and John sit in identical surroundings. However well a language model builds a sentence, nothing inside that sentence tells it which one to pick.

The cost is also uneven. A misplaced preposition does not stop anyone reading, but a wrong name makes the document unusable. Word error rate, being an average, weighs the error that matters most in practice the least.

6.2 Feeding context hints, and where that stops working

The standard answer is a vocabulary hint, and the announcement lists custom vocabulary recognition among its features. Supply a word list in advance and candidates get pulled toward it. The effect is real and it is not a cure.

  • Long lists backfire. Similar sounds get dragged onto a listed word, creating errors that did not exist
  • Live means knowing in advance. You cannot populate the list once the meeting has started
  • The list itself can be sensitive. Put unreleased product names and client names in it and where that list travels becomes a new question

So the practical order tends to be this. Loading the attendee list, the agenda and the usual acronyms as hints before the meeting comes before swapping models. It costs three minutes and name errors drop visibly.

6.3 When changing the microphone beats changing the model

When a complaint about transcription quality arrives, the cause sits on the input side more often than people expect. One microphone in the middle of a table serving six people means the signal is already ruined before recognition begins.

  • Distance. Speech-to-noise ratio degrades fast as the talker moves away
  • Reverberation. Rooms full of glass and whiteboard send the sound back overlapped
  • Stationary noise. HVAC and projector fans seem quiet and never stop
  • Band limiting. Audio that crossed a phone line already had its frequency range cut

If only one rule of thumb survives, make it this. Listen to 30 seconds of the raw audio from the bad stretch yourself. If a person cannot make it out, a different model usually will not either. What is available there sits on the input side: per-speaker microphones or headsets, and moving the microphone.

7. When two languages share one sentence

Korean workplace speech mixes English terms in at the level of single words. A sentence like deploy this and cut me a rollback branch, with only the verbs in Korean, occurs dozens of times a day. The difficulty is three-layered. A design that puts a language identifier up front can only split at utterance or sentence level, while real mixing happens one word at a time, so there is nowhere to cut; Korean is agglutinative, so an English stem carries Korean endings and one word contains two languages; and whether to write Docker or its Korean transliteration is settled by house style rather than by the model.

That is why the HiKE benchmark, built to measure Korean-English mixing, labels mixing level in three tiers of word, phrase and sentence. The same paper reports that many multilingual speech recognition models do not initially perform well on code-switched utterances. Knowing several languages separately and hearing two of them in one breath are different abilities.

Live piles one more constraint on top. The decision to switch languages has to be made before that word finishes. With a recording you can hear the whole sentence, realise that was English, and rewrite backwards. Live has no rewind. The announcement listing live language switching as its own feature also says that this does not come along for free but is handled as a separate problem. Attempts to target that boundary at model level are covered further in the piece on a Korean-English bilingual speech model.

8. May you record - a checklist instead of a verdict

From here it stops being a technical question, and this is where internet advice does the most damage. One-line summaries along the lines of if you are a party to it you may record circulate widely, and a decision should not be made on that line. Consent requirements differ by jurisdiction, and within one jurisdiction different rules attach depending on context.

What can be established is narrower. Article 3(1) of Korea's Protection of Communications Secrets Act prohibits recording or listening to a conversation between other people that is not open to the public, and case law published on the national law information portal reads the provision as aimed at a third party who was never part of that conversation. In the United States, 18 U.S.C. §2511(2)(d) provides that it is not unlawful for a person not acting under colour of law to intercept a communication where that person is a party to it, or where one of the parties has given prior consent, unless the interception is made for the purpose of committing a criminal or tortious act.

Stopping there matters. Those provisions do not establish that a meeting you attend may be recorded. Separate things attach. Recording collects other people's voices and statements, so a lawful basis for processing personal data is needed; HR and labour contexts bring internal rules and collective agreements; medical and counselling settings carry their own confidentiality duties; and a client contract or the other organisation's policy may prohibit it outright. In the United States state law is applied alongside the federal statute, and when attendees sit in different states which law applies is itself the first question.

So what this article can offer is not a conclusion but the list of questions.

9. Where the voice goes and how long it stays

Audio carries both what was said and who said it, and that split is reflected in the rules. Article 4 of the GDPR defines personal data as any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person, and biometric data as personal data resulting from specific technical processing relating to physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics that allow or confirm unique identification. What to take from that is that the processing changes the character of the thing. Turning a voice into text and extracting voice characteristics to identify a person start from the same file and are different acts.

A meeting recording also holds more than your own data. Other attendees are in it, and so are the names and circumstances of third parties mentioned in passing. In practice this is the side that causes trouble more often.

Axis to confirmWhat specifically to ask
Processing locationDoes it end on the device, stay in-region, or cross a border
Storage in transitDoes the streaming leg persist anywhere, or only pass through
RetentionHow many days for the audio and the transcript each, and what is the default
Training useIs it used to improve models, is there an opt-out, is it on by default
Sub-processorsDoes another vendor sit in the middle, and is the list published
Access and loggingWho internally can open it, and is that access recorded
Scope of deletionDoes a deletion request reach summaries, indexes and embeddings

Live shifts the axis. A recording only needs one decision about where to upload the file, whereas streaming is on the network for the whole meeting. The question changes from is it deleted afterwards to which hops does each segment pass through.

The last row leaks most often. Derivatives routinely outlive the original. The audio is purged after 30 days while the transcript sits in a document store, the summary in a chat thread, and the search index and embeddings somewhere else again. A deletion request aimed only at the original effectively deletes nothing.

10. A transcript is an index, not the record of truth

Automatic transcription makes mistakes. Even a very good transcript is a mostly correct document rather than an accurate one. The trouble is human habit: once words are on a screen we treat them as a record, which is to say as fact.

The danger zones are fairly narrow.

  • Negation. Decided to proceed and decided not to proceed sound close and mean the opposite
  • Numbers. Amounts, dates and version numbers flip on one syllable, and context will not protect them
  • Conditional clauses. Lose the if and an assumption becomes a decision
  • Speaker labels. Correct sentences under the wrong name make the whole document false

So the principle collapses into one line. The original audio is the record of truth, and the transcript is an index back into it. Seen that way, word-level timestamps are a safety mechanism rather than a convenience, which is how the announcement putting them on the feature list reads. A transcript with no route back cannot be verified, and a document that cannot be verified eventually gets trusted by nobody.

Translated into operating rules:

  • Decisions, amounts, dates and personal names circulate only after a human has checked them
  • Leave a header saying generated automatically, not reviewed. That one line prevents a great deal later
  • When quoting, include the timestamp so the reader can go back to the source
  • Where a dispute is plausible, do not treat the transcript as evidence. That is the original's job

Live captions add one more. Provisional text appears and then changes. The sentence people read during the meeting may differ from the sentence in the final transcript. If a judgement turned on what was on the screen at that moment, what was on the screen no longer exists anywhere.

What could not be confirmed

  • Measured latency distribution. The announcement supports the phrase sub-second and a 70% improvement in time to final transcription over the previous generation. Under what conditions it was measured, and whether the network is included, could not be cross-checked
  • Composition of the 4.0% and 2.6% figures. The third-party attribution is stated in the document, but which set of datasets that average covers, and the speaker and noise conditions, could not be confirmed
  • Korean-only performance. Only the count of 85 or more languages and the FLEURS top-language average are available. Per-language figures could not be cross-checked
  • Data handling and retention policy. The announcement contains no statement on retention periods or training use. That has to be confirmed separately in the terms of each delivery route
  • Real accuracy beyond three speakers, and public preview pricing and quota terms. Anything above three is only marked experimental, and pricing was out of scope for this article

Conclusion: the split name says the most

What will last from this release is not a figure after the decimal point. It is that a streaming version and a recorded version shipped under separate names on separate APIs in the same generation. That fork explains every section above. Whether you can fix the beginning sets the accuracy, the punctuation, the speaker labels, and finally how the output must be handled.

When choosing a tool, invert the order. Do not start by comparing accuracy figures; write down your latency requirement first. Captions a person reads live, a record read later, or a command that must respond immediately. Once that is settled, the numbers worth reading narrow, and so do the numbers you can ignore.

One thing is worth doing today. Run 30 minutes of your own meeting twice, once live and once as a recorded file, then mark where the two outputs diverge. That list will look almost exactly like the section headings here: the wrong names, the broken sentences, the swapped speakers, the mangled English terms. That single set of marks tells you more about an adoption decision than ten of someone else's benchmarks.
