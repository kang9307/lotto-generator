Answer Engine Optimization Is Not a Standard - Sorting Evidence From Folklore

What actually changed and what did not - the fixes worth making today and the ones that cost more than they return

2026-08-09 IT & Technology

Introduction: the premise hidden inside 「we dropped SEO」

The sentence 「we dropped SEO and built answer engine optimization instead」 has become common through 2026. The sentence has force, and the observation underneath it is not wrong. Search result pages really are shifting from lists of links toward answer surfaces.

The problem is what comes next. That sentence almost always resolves into a specific instruction: strip out your existing SEO work and bolt on an AI-shaped structure. The evidence supporting that instruction is nowhere near as sturdy as the confidence with which it is delivered.

Start with one fact. On 15 May 2026 Google published a guide to optimizing for generative AI features into Search Central documentation, and it addressed the terms AEO and GEO head-on. The conclusion reads: From Google Search's perspective, optimizing for generative AI search is optimizing for the search experience, and thus still SEO.

In the same year Bing moved in the opposite direction. Its rewritten webmaster guidelines put GEO in by name, and in February it opened an AI citation tracking report in Bing Webmaster Tools as a public preview. Two search engines are running opposite vocabulary strategies on the same phenomenon.

This article neither endorses nor dismisses the drop-SEO-for-AEO pitch. Instead it sorts claims into three buckets: verified against official documentation and primary sources, industry folklore that circulates widely on thin evidence, and claims that have not been verified at all. On top of that sort, it lays out what a blog or documentation owner can safely touch today and what will cost more than it returns.

One thing worth saying up front. In this field the expensive mistake is not inaction. It is following unsupported advice and breaking something that was working. That has actually been measured, and section 8 covers it.

1. Where AEO and GEO sit - vocabulary, not standard

1.1 What the two terms point at

AEO (Answer Engine Optimization) is used to mean optimizing to appear in answer engines. GEO (Generative Engine Optimization) is used to mean optimizing to be cited inside a generative engine's answer. In practice the two get used interchangeably, and even among the people who insist on the distinction, the definitions do not match.

The important thing is where they sit. Neither is a specification in the sense that a W3C recommendation or a schema.org vocabulary is. No standards body defined them, and no group of search engines jointly adopted them. There is no specification document and no criterion for judging compliance. These are marketing terms that spread.

Of the two, GEO at least has a traceable origin. A paper presented at KDD 2024 proposed the term and shipped a benchmark called GEO-bench alongside it. AEO does not have even that. Which means 「we followed the AEO standard」 is, read literally, a sentence produced in the absence of any standard to follow.

1.2 What 「not a standard」 does not mean

Here is where the misreading happens. Saying the vocabulary is not standardized is not the same as saying the phenomenon is fake. Pages really do get cited on answer surfaces, and that citation path really does behave differently from the click path.

What the absence of a standard actually means is narrower: the burden of proof sits with whoever is making the claim. With no reference document, the phrase 「AEO best practice」 carries no weight of its own. The primary evidence that remains is what each search engine and model provider wrote in its own documentation. So most of the rest of this article is the work of reading those documents.

2. Google's written list of things you do not need

2.1 What you do not have to build

Google's generative AI optimization guide has an unusual shape. It spends more space on 「you do not need to」 than on 「you should」. That proportion is itself evidence of how much bad advice is circulating. The items the document explicitly marks unnecessary are these.

  • Separate machine readable files: You don't need to create new machine readable files, AI text files, markup, or Markdown to appear in Google Search. The llms.txt family falls here. The document's framing is that such files neither help nor hurt
  • Special schema: Structured data isn't required for generative AI search, and there's no special schema.org markup you need to add.
  • Chunking content into fragments: There's no requirement to break your content into tiny pieces for AI to better understand it. The stated reason is that the systems can handle multiple topics within a page
  • Rewriting prose for machines: You don't need to write in a specific way just for generative AI search. Synonyms and general meaning are understood, so exact keyword matching is not required

Those four are not a random assortment. They are, almost exactly, the deliverable list sold by a large share of AEO consulting.

2.2 What the documentation says not to do

One passage goes further than 「unnecessary」. Fan-out queries — building a separate page for every variant question a user might ask — are classified by Google as a spam policy violation under scaled content abuse. Some of what gets sold as AEO tactics is not merely unhelpful; it is sanctionable.

The 「get more mentions」 advice deserves the same caution. Google's documentation warns against pursuing inauthentic mentions. The logic that a brand appearing in many places causes AI systems to recommend it is a textbook case of reading correlation as causation.

2.3 The actual eligibility condition

So what does it take to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode? The answer in the documentation is startlingly short. The page must be indexed, eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet, and compliant with the Search technical requirements. The AI features page adds a line: There are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, nor other special optimizations necessary.

There is one more clause worth quoting. Even when a page meets every requirement and complies with every policy, the documentation states directly that crawling, indexing, and serving are not guaranteed. That sentence is rarely quoted by the people selling AEO.

2.4 Why structured data stays anyway

This is the easiest place to get confused. 「Structured data is not required for generative AI」 is not the same as 「structured data can be removed」. The same document recommends continuing to use it as part of an overall SEO strategy, because rich result eligibility depends on it.

The value of structured data comes from presentation in search results, not from AI citation. Correcting your reason for doing something and deleting the work are entirely different acts. The most common self-inflicted wound in this space is the chain 「I added schema for AI」 followed by 「AI does not need schema, so I am removing it」.

3. Bing went the other way - same phenomenon, different vocabulary

3.1 The side that put GEO in the official text

Bing rewrote its webmaster guidelines so that they cover how content appears both in traditional results and in Copilot's AI-generated answers. In the process it named GEO explicitly, framing it as focused on content eligibility for grounding and reference in AI responses. It attached a caveat in the same breath: GEO does not guarantee citations, just as SEO does not guarantee rankings.

It is explicit about display controls too. NOARCHIVE prevents content from being used in Copilot responses and grounding results, and there is an attribute for specifying which text Bing may display or cite.

Then in February 2026 the AI Performance report arrived in Bing Webmaster Tools as a public preview. Microsoft described the release as an early step toward GEO tooling inside Webmaster Tools. The company did not just accept the vocabulary; it attached instrumentation to it.

3.2 How to read the gap between the two positions

On the surface this looks like a contradiction. Overlay the actual guidance, though, and the two are closer than the vocabulary suggests. Both stacked AI eligibility on top of existing crawl and index. Both say nothing is guaranteed.

What differs is language and tooling. Google refuses to name it a separate discipline and folds the measurement into its existing performance report. Bing accepts the separate term and opened a citation-specific metric. For a practitioner the meaningful difference narrows to one line: only one of the two currently offers an official window into whether a citation actually happened.

4. AI crawlers have three switches - training, search, user request

4.1 Training and search are different doors

The most common misconception lives here. 「I do not want AI training on our content」 and 「I do want to be cited in AI answers」 are different requests, and the major providers have already split their crawlers by purpose. Blocking them as one bundle closes doors you did not intend to close.

OpenAI splits four ways. OAI-SearchBot handles visibility in ChatGPT search; block it and the site does not appear there. GPTBot crawls content that may be used to train generative foundation models. ChatGPT-User is the path taken when a person asks for a specific page, and because it is not automated crawling, robots.txt rules do not apply to it. OAI-AdsBot verifies advertising landing pages and is not used for training.

Anthropic splits three ways. ClaudeBot collects content for model training, Claude-User handles web access when a user asks a question, and Claude-SearchBot explores the web to improve search result quality. Each is controllable individually in robots.txt, each subdomain needs its own configuration, and the documentation notes that blocking by IP address is discouraged because it prevents robots.txt from being read at all.

On Google's side, Google-Extended limits training and grounding use while search visibility control remains with robots.txt directives aimed at Googlebot. One directive does not decide both questions.

4.2 Getting the trade right

Reading the table as a set of operations, the branches come out like this.

  • Block only training crawlers: excluded from model training, search and citation paths stay open
  • Block search crawlers too: the citation path closes, and the clicks that came through citations close with it
  • Block everything: the site becomes non-existent on AI answer surfaces
  • The user-request path: often classified as individual user action, so robots.txt may not govern it
  • Block the search engine crawler itself: traditional search and AI visibility vanish together. This is the hardest choice to reverse

Compressed to one line: blocking a crawler is a copyright decision and a distribution decision at the same time. The point of the table is to stop you deciding both at once — and to prompt you to leave a comment in robots.txt explaining why each switch is set the way it is.

5. What the 40 percent lift really measured

5.1 Where the number came from

The figure quoted most often in GEO sales material is 「up to 40 percent visibility lift」. It traces to the KDD 2024 paper. Once you know the experimental design, the number means something quite different from how it is used.

The design: for each query, the top five Google results are handed to GPT-3.5-turbo and answers are generated. One source is then modified under one of nine alternative strategies and compared against its original. The metrics are the share of answer words attributed to that source, a position-adjusted version that discounts passages appearing later, and a subjective impression scored by a model.

The value closest to 40 percent comes from the quotation-addition strategy, where position-adjusted word count rose from 19.3 to 27.2 — roughly 41 percent in relative terms.

5.2 What was never measured

What matters is what the number does not mean.

  • The probability of being retrieved into context was not part of the experiment. The five documents were already placed there at the start
  • So the value is not an absolute increase but a redistribution among five documents. When one share rises, another falls
  • Whether any of it produced clicks or visits was not measured
  • It was obtained on a 2023-era model over the search results of that time
  • It came from a simulator, not from a live answer engine

A 2026 critical survey of the GEO literature takes this distortion on directly. It notes that the 40 percent figure is often recast as a general promise of ranking highly in ChatGPT, when it describes a relative visibility gain in a simulator in which five documents have already been placed in context, and then states flatly: This result does not mean that 40% more readers will click, nor that a page will gain 40% in retrieval probability.

The same survey delivers a broader verdict: no reviewed technique shows a stable, longitudinal, cross-platform causal effect on organic discoverability or downstream behavior.

5.3 Visibility is a chain, not a quantity

The survey offers a decomposition that is unusually useful in practice. Visibility is not one metric but a chain: probability of retrieval → probability of entering context → probability of citation → prominence within the answer → how much of the content is actually absorbed → faithfulness → the eventual behavioral and economic outcome.

The key point is that these terms are independent of one another. A high conditional probability of citation does not compensate for a low probability of retrieval. Most AEO tactics operate only on the back half of the chain, while what is actually missing on most sites lives in the front half.

One more caveat belongs here. Being cited does not mean the content was actually used as support. One measurement found that only about half of the sentences in generative search responses were fully supported by their cited sources, and a follow-up study classified a double-digit percentage of atomic claims as insufficiently supported. Citation count is a marker of exposure, not of trust.

6.2 How to handle the folklore column

Reading the folklore column as 「do not do this」 would be a mistake. Putting a summary at the top and phrasing subheadings as questions is good writing for humans. Thin evidence means you cannot claim a causal link to AI citations. It does not mean the practice is harmful.

A practical dividing line looks like this.

  • If the change makes the page better for a human reader, do it even on thin evidence. There is no downside
  • If it makes the page worse for a human and you are doing it for machines, demand evidence
  • If the only evidence produced is vendor marketing material, hold
  • If official documentation explicitly says it is unnecessary, skip it. All that remains is maintenance cost
  • If official documentation says it is a violation, skip it regardless of the promised upside

7. Why 「SEO is dead」 does not hold

7.1 The documentary refutation

The strongest rebuttal is not statistical; it is in the documentation. To appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, a page must be indexed and eligible to be shown in Search with a snippet. Traditional search eligibility is the precondition for AI visibility. A page that fails at SEO fails at AEO automatically, and there is no way to reverse the order.

Bing has the same structure. Grounding and citation eligibility sit on top of the existing crawl and index. Both engines stacked an AI layer above the search layer; neither removed the search layer. For 「we dropped SEO」 to be technically coherent, an unindexed page would have to be citable in AI answers, and neither engine's public documentation describes such a path.

7.2 What the numbers show

At the same time, the click decline is real. Pew Research Center measured a click-through rate of 8 percent on searches where an AI summary appeared versus 15 percent where it did not. Only about 1 percent clicked a link inside the AI summary itself. Users were also more likely to end the browsing session when an AI summary was present.

There are numbers on the other side too. Referrals from AI platforms are growing very quickly, but the absolute volume remains small next to traditional search — a high-quality, low-volume channel, in current shape. Google states in its own documentation that clicks arriving via AI Overviews are higher quality in terms of time spent on site. That claim is not in conflict with the observation that total clicks fell; the remaining clicks getting better and the click count dropping can both be true at once.

Read together, the picture is this. Clicks on short informational queries are shrinking. Queries where the user must eventually reach a page to finish the job — comparison, purchase, local, transactional — are not. So the accurate question is not 「is SEO dead」 but 「what share of my inbound traffic comes from queries a short answer can replace」. Sites where that share is high are genuinely being hit; sites where it is low barely notice. That is why directly contradictory field reports circulate in the same month.

8. What to touch today and what to leave alone

8.1 Start with what costs you

There is a reason for inverting the order: this side has actual empirical support.

Among the evaluations cited in the 2026 critical survey, one result stands out. Body-only optimization reduced average top-20 presence by roughly 9 percent, and top-10 presence after reranking by 16 percent. In another benchmark, only three of 54 method-domain combinations were significantly positive.

Read it this way. Rewriting body copy for AEO can degrade performance at the retrieval stage. You reach for the back of the chain and break the front of it. That measurement is the evidence behind the claim in the introduction that the expensive mistake is breaking something that worked.

8.3 The one-sentence test

When a proposed change is hard to judge, there is a single question to ask: does a human reading this come out better off? If yes, thin evidence costs you nothing. If no, demand evidence — and very few current tactics survive that demand.

This test has a side benefit. It does not wobble when a search engine edits its documentation. A checklist built on algorithm guesswork has to be rewritten every six months; 「is this better for a reader」 never needs rewriting.

9. Without changing measurement you cannot change judgment

9.1 What is visible right now

  • Bing Webmaster Tools AI Performance: total citations, average cited pages per day, the grounding queries AI used to find the content, and per-page citation activity. It started as a public preview in February 2026 and spans Copilot, AI-generated summaries in Bing, and select partner integrations
  • Google Search Console: traffic arriving via AI Overviews and AI Mode is counted inside the existing performance report. The total is visible, which also means it cannot be separated out
  • Referrer-based observation: visits from AI platforms can be distinguished by referrer, but in-app browsers and copy-paste paths strip the referrer and land in direct traffic
  • Log-based observation: server logs let you count AI crawler visits by user agent string. That tells you nothing about citation, but it does tell you about access

9.2 Why asking the chatbot yourself is not evidence

The most common self-diagnosis is 「I asked the chatbot and we did not show up」. Several things make that observation unusable as evidence.

  • Repeat the same question and the cited source set shifts substantially; measured agreement across repeats has come in below half
  • Personalization, region, and timing change the result
  • Discarding responses in which no search or citation occurred introduces selection bias
  • Letting a model grade its own output contaminates the evaluation; risk peaks when the same model writes the rewrite, the response, and the score
  • When many sites run the same tactic simultaneously they interfere with each other, because visibility is redistributive by nature

The minimum discipline is: test across multiple engines, at multiple times, with three to five paraphrases of the question, measuring pipeline stages separately. And never restructure a site on the strength of a single query.

10. The answer differs by language market

10.1 The search landscape is not uniform

Everything cited so far is Google and Bing documentation. But actual inbound traffic arrives from different places depending on the language market. In English-language markets Google dominates, with Bing mattering more than its raw share suggests because of the Copilot and grounding API surface built on it. Perplexity and ChatGPT search sit alongside as distinct referral sources with their own crawler policies.

Other markets diverge sharply. Korean-language traffic leans heavily on Naver, which attaches its own AI summary surfaces to results; there is no basis for assuming Google's control directives apply there. Mainland Chinese-language markets are different again, since access to Google and Bing is itself restricted, so those documents cannot be transplanted at all.

10.2 So what do you do

  • Identify your top inbound channels first, then read that channel's official documentation before anything else
  • If a channel has no official documentation, record that absence as a fact. Do not fill an empty document with guesses
  • Do not mix actions aimed at Google and Bing with actions aimed at regional engines in one table
  • On a multilingual site, assume each language edition has a different inbound structure
  • Before copying a tactic that worked in one market into another, reread that market's engine documentation

11. What could not be confirmed

Items where evidence was not secured, collected in one place rather than hedged throughout.

  • Bing's handling of AI-specific files: no official statement was found describing how Bing treats files like llms.txt. That is why the table says only 「not named as a requirement」
  • Regional engines' AI citation policies: the citation criteria and control directives used by Naver, Baidu and similar engines could not be confirmed at the level of published webmaster documentation
  • The figures in the source post: the claim that 52 percent of web traffic comes from AI agents, and the parse-success figure rising from 38 to 96 percent, come with no derivation, measurement scope, or reproduction procedure
  • Citation selection algorithms: no provider has published how its engine selects which sources to cite
  • The independent effect of structured data: no public experiment was found that isolates schema by controlling other variables
  • Causal paths to revenue: no public measurement was found tracing AEO or GEO tactics past citation into conversion or revenue
  • Long-run trends: no primary statistics covering multi-year, industry-level traffic change since AI summaries launched were found. Only single-point measurements exist

Frequently asked questions

Q1. Are AEO and GEO official standards?

No. Neither is a specification defined by a standards body; both are vocabulary that grew and spread inside the industry. Google documents that optimizing for generative AI search is optimizing for the search experience and thus still SEO, while Bing went the other way and wrote GEO into its guidelines by name. Because two engines apply different vocabulary to the same phenomenon, 「AEO best practice」 carries no inherent authority. Each engine's own documentation is the only primary evidence.

Q2. Do I need special markup or an llms.txt file to be cited in AI answers?

Not for Google Search. The documentation states you do not need new machine readable files, AI text files, or Markdown, and that there is no special schema.org markup to add. It also says there is no requirement to chunk content and no need to write in a particular style for generative AI search. Structured data is still worth keeping, with one correction: the reason is rich result eligibility in ordinary Search, not AI citation.

Q3. What happens if I block AI crawlers?

It depends on the switch, because the crawlers are split by purpose. Block only the training crawler and you leave training data while search and citation paths stay open. Block the search crawler too and the citation path closes along with the clicks it produced. The path used when a person explicitly asks the assistant to open a page is not classified as automated crawling, so robots.txt rules may not apply. Decide the copyright question and the distribution question separately.

Q4. Is the 40 percent visibility lift figure trustworthy?

Not as commonly quoted. It describes a relative gain in one document's share inside a simulator where five documents were already placed in context. Retrieval probability was never part of the experiment, and clicks and visits were never measured. It is also redistributive — one source gaining share means another loses it. The 2026 critical survey states directly that the result does not mean 40 percent more readers will click, nor that a page gains 40 percent in retrieval probability.

Q5. So can I stop doing SEO?

The opposite. Appearing in AI Overviews or AI Mode requires that the page be indexed and eligible to be shown with a snippet. Lose search eligibility and you lose AI eligibility with it. Bing layered grounding and citation eligibility on top of existing crawl and index as well. Both engines stacked an AI layer above the search layer rather than replacing it. What changed is which query types still produce clicks, not the foundation.

Conclusion - five steps to apply today

Compressed into an execution order, the article comes out like this.

  • Step 1 — audit the precondition. Check crawl and index state and snippet eligibility. If this is blocked, every other task is meaningless
  • Step 2 — set the crawler switches deliberately. Decide training, search, and user-request separately, and leave a comment in the configuration file explaining why
  • Step 3 — open a measurement window. Enable the report where a citation-specific metric exists, and read the aggregated one knowing that it is aggregated
  • Step 4 — delete from the leave-alone list first. If AI-specific files or fragmented pages already exist, undo those before adding anything new
  • Step 5 — spend the remaining time on content. First-hand experience and a distinct point of view are the axis both engines name explicitly, and the only axis that does not go out of fashion

One last thing this article did not do. There is no 「AEO checklist」 here, because the currently available evidence does not support building one. What exists is a line between what has been confirmed and what has not, and that line must be redrawn every time a search engine edits its documentation.

「We dropped SEO」 is appealing because it is simple. And the faster simple answers circulate in a field, the more there is to gain from reading it apart. This is one of those moments.
