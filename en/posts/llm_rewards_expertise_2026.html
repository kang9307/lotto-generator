Why the Same Model Gives Different Results - It Is Verification, Not Prompt Craft

If you cannot spot a wrong answer, every second you saved turns into a loss

Introduction: same subscription, same model

The person next to you uses the same model you do. Same interface, same plan. Yet their output goes straight into the meeting and yours needs another pass. Plenty of writing explains that gap as a difference in prompt wording. That explanation is half right.

Here is the judgment up front. What separates the results is not how the request is written but whether the returned answer can be recognized as wrong. Scoping a request, attaching context, specifying a format — those are techniques, and they can be picked up in days. Recognizing a confidently wrong answer on the spot belongs to people who have spent time in the domain. And where that ability is missing, there is a range in which every minute saved converts directly into loss.

Concluding that only experts benefit would be equally wrong. There are task types where beginners gain far more, and that is not an impression but a result from randomized experiments. The two claims are not in conflict. The dividing line runs through tasks, not people.

This article draws that line. Which direction to redesign a career in is a separate subject, covered in Redesigning Your Expertise for the AI Era. What follows is only the mechanism that splits outcomes when two people use the same tool today.

1. Four things people who know the domain do differently

Put two people's inputs for the same goal side by side and the differences show up in four places.

1.1 They narrow the request

The difference between "write some marketing copy" and "B2B checkout abandonment screen, under 40 characters, no numerals, informative rather than apologetic in tone, three options." The second works better not because it is polite but because it shrinks the space of answers the model can choose from. Given a wide space, a model returns the average. The average is not wrong; it is just unusable.

One common misreading here: this is not advice to write longer requests. The reason a knowledgeable person's request narrows naturally is that they already know which conditions drive the outcome. The list is in their head, so it lands in the request. It is not the product of trying to write a good prompt.

1.2 They select the context

More context does not simply mean better output. Attach a pile of loosely related documents and the model gets pulled toward whatever stands out. The difference is not volume but selection. Someone who knows which constraint can flip the result on this particular task attaches that one thing.

1.3 They specify the output format

Give it as a table, as JSON, as a runnable script, as a checklist. This looks like preference and is actually a verification device. A table makes the blanks visible. Runnable code settles the first round of judgment by running it. Prose dissolves the wrong part into a sentence where nothing catches the eye.

1.4 They recognize a wrong answer

This one differs in kind from the other three. Those are techniques; this is an ability. And it is the ability that produces the other three. You can only narrow a request once you know what drives the outcome, and you can only select context once you know which constraint matters.

It matters most because of how models fail. A model writes a wrong answer in the same register as a right one. The signal humans give off when unsure — hedging, shorter sentences, visible strain — is simply absent. So the filter cannot be a feeling that something is off. It has to be knowing the fact.

Look at the right-hand columns and one thing is shared. Every request from the knowledgeable side has already decided how the answer will be verified. Asking for SQL means intending to recompute. Asking for clause numbers means intending to open the contract.

2. The axis is verification - where speed turns into loss

The real return on handing work to a model comes out like this.

net gain = time to do it yourself − (time to ask + time to read + time to verify + cost of a wrong answer getting through × probability it gets through)

The first three terms are usually small and predictable. The last one is the problem. When it exceeds the sum of the others, the whole expression goes negative. And that term does not shrink as models improve, because the more plausible the output, the higher the probability of passage.

2.1 The experiment where perception and measurement had opposite signs

The sharpest demonstration is METR's randomized trial. Sixteen experienced open-source developers worked through 246 real tasks in mature repositories they had maintained for years, with AI tool access randomized per task. The window was February to June 2025, and the tooling was mostly Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5 and 3.7 Sonnet.

  • Forecast before starting: AI would make them 24 percent faster
  • Self-report after the work: 20 percent faster
  • Measured completion time: 19 percent longer

The takeaway is not that AI makes people slower. The sample was 16 people on a specific tool configuration, and the repositories were codebases they knew intimately. The takeaway is that perception and measurement can point in opposite directions. That single line invalidates most of the "we got N times faster" numbers circulating inside organizations, because nearly all of them are collected by self-report.

2.2 That study should not be taken at face value either

For balance, the other side. In February 2026 METR published follow-up results together with a decision to change the experiment design. In the new run the original cohort came out at −18 percent and newly recruited participants at −4 percent, with confidence intervals of −38 to +9 percent and −15 to +9 percent respectively. Crossing zero means the direction is not established.

The recruiting problems were reported by the researchers themselves. Developers increasingly declined to participate under a condition that required working without AI even with compensation on offer, and 30 to 50 percent of participants reported steering away from tasks they did not want to do unaccompanied. When people run several agents at once, timing measurement itself becomes shaky. The researchers state plainly that these selection effects mean their results likely understate the real productivity gains.

So the conclusion narrows to this. Whether AI makes people slower cannot be stated as a single number yet. What has been observed and is not in dispute is that people were slower while feeling faster, and that property survives tool generations. Time spent verifying does not register in memory; the moment generation finishes does.

How far a machine can carry the judging is a separate subject, laid out in What a Machine Can and Cannot Verify in an LLM Answer.

3. The other direction - work where beginners gain more

"Only experts benefit" is an overstatement. The evidence pointing the other way is larger and older.

A study of 5,179 customer support agents tracking the staggered rollout of a generative AI assistant found issues resolved per hour up 14 percent on average. That average split sharply by group. Novice and low-skilled agents gained 34 percent while experienced, high-performing agents barely moved. The researchers describe a mechanism in which the tool spreads the practices of the better agents to everyone else.

An experiment on mid-level professional writing pointed the same way. With 453 college-educated professionals randomized and half given access, time fell 40 percent, rated quality rose 18 percent, and the performance distribution compressed. The people who had been weaker gained more.

The tasks in both experiments share something, and that is what forms the rule.

  • The band of acceptable answers is narrow — support replies and internal memos have a largely settled shape
  • Good examples already exist in volume — the model has absorbed that distribution
  • Checking is cheap — the customer reacts immediately, and a draft can be judged by reading it
  • Mistakes are recoverable — it is one reply or one memo

So the accurate phrasing is not "experts benefit" but this. Where verification is cheap, beginners gain more; where verification is expensive, only people who know the domain gain at all.

The list of work that pays off today with shallow domain knowledge is long.

  • Drafts that kill the blank page — even a rough structure makes editing cheaper than starting cold
  • First-pass translation and style editing — verified directly against the source
  • Summarizing long documents — the original is in hand, so only suspicious passages need rechecking
  • Format conversion — table to JSON, meeting notes to action items
  • Generating search terms in an unfamiliar field — the point is acquiring the vocabulary that field uses
  • Reading somebody else's code — explanations of flow can be checked by running it
  • Asking for the same idea explained differently — it locates where your own understanding stalled

4. Domains split on verification cost, not difficulty

The value to look at when deciding how much to hand over is not how hard the task is. It is what it costs to discover that the answer is wrong.

4.1 The cheap side has a trap too

"You can just run the code" holds only conditionally. Running is not the same as correct. In code without tests, execution output is an impression rather than a verification. Asking the model to write both implementation and tests makes it subtler still: those tests are written against the implementation, so they inherit its misconceptions. Deciding the tests first, as a person, and delegating only the implementation reduces that problem.

4.2 On the expensive side, citations do not replace checking

Legal research is one of the rare areas where this has been quantified. An evaluation of commercial legal research tools across 202 queries found one product returning inaccurate answers more than 17 percent of the time and another more than 34 percent. The context matters more than the ratios. Both products attached supporting sources, and both were marketed with claims of being free of hallucination. The researchers concluded that providers' claims were overstated.

So on the expensive side, the presence of a link is not grounds for comfort. What has to be checked is not that the citation exists but whether the quoted sentence is actually in that document. Skip that step and citation formatting becomes a device that raises the pass rate for wrong answers.

5. Six requests that lower the cost of checking

These are chosen so they work without deep domain knowledge. Each is one line appended to the request.

RequestWhat appears in the outputWhat it filters out
Give it in a runnable formCode, SQL, formulas, spreadsheet expressionsArithmetic errors, metrics defined differently than intended
Attach source links, and say so when there are noneA checkable listDocuments that do not exist, and content that is not in documents that do
List three conditions under which this answer is wrongLimits of applicabilityAnswers carried into situations with different premises
Leave the intermediate artifactsAssumptions, steps, working tablesThe state where you cannot locate the point of divergence
Give two answers under different premisesThe point where they splitOverconfidence in a single answer
Define success criteria firstA scoring rubricThe habit of accepting output on impression

The third is unusually cheap. Models are better at naming the weaknesses of their own answer than at producing the answer. And this request works even when the user does not know the domain, because all it requires is checking whether any of the three named conditions applies to the current situation.

The sixth is about ordering. Anthropic's prompt engineering overview puts defining success criteria and an evaluation method ahead of touching the prompt at all. The same document adds a caveat that pays off in practice: not every problem is a prompt problem, and latency and cost are often improved more easily by changing the model. There are plenty of situations where one run on a different model beats twenty rounds of rewording.

Hardening this at team level with eval sets and regression testing is covered in Managing Prompts Like Code, and if what you want is a catalogue of individual techniques, Advanced Prompt Techniques is the faster route.

6. Three places where expertise becomes a liability

The claim that expertise is rewarded holds only conditionally. There are three places where the condition breaks.

  1. When the knowledge is stale. If the standard in your domain changed recently and you missed it, you will reject the model's correct answer. Expertise stops being a filter and becomes an obstacle
  2. When the request is over-narrowed. Pinning every condition to your own habitual approach cuts better approaches out of the candidate set. The tool ends up reproducing familiar answers faster
  3. When it is used outside the capability boundary. This is the expensive one

The third has been measured. In an experiment with 758 consultants, performance improved on tasks inside the range of what the AI does well, but on tasks outside that range the group using AI was 19 percentage points less likely to reach a correct solution. The researchers described AI capability as forming a jagged rather than smooth boundary. The weight of that result comes from the population: not beginners, but a selected professional cohort.

The METR participants were experienced too. People who had maintained their own repositories for years produced a result opposite to what they perceived. Expertise filters wrong answers, but it does not help much with judging how far the tool itself can be trusted. Those are two different abilities.

About the source of the claim. The essay that prompted this article, arguing that LLMs reward expertise, is written from cases and observation rather than statistics. Its author added a note acknowledging as reasonable the objection that the argument may be self-serving. The direction is broadly right, but it should not be treated as data, and the two experiments above attach immediate qualifications to it.

What domain knowledge is worth in career terms is a different question, handled in The Real Moat Is Domain Expertise, Not Coding, and the gap between benchmark scores and actual behavioral quality sits in The Blind Spots of LLM Evaluation.

7. What happens when you hand AI to junior staff

The support-agent study is an organizational story, not an individual one. Junior staff moved down the learning curve faster, and the shape of good answers spread through the organization. That is the upside.

The problem is misplacement. Put someone who cannot verify onto work where verification is expensive and errors pass quietly. Those errors are usually found late, by someone else, in a far more expensive state. So the placement criterion should be the verification-cost column from section 4, not seniority.

7.1 The bottleneck moves to review

Double one junior engineer's output and you double the reviewer's load. Install the tools without preparing for that and total team throughput drops. This is the most common shape of "we adopted AI, why is nothing faster." Three responses are available.

  • Shrink the unit handed over — fix the size of a single review
  • Expand what a machine can judge — tests, static analysis, format checks
  • Do not hand over work with expensive verification in the first place

The organizational pattern where budgets rise while throughput stays flat is covered in The Collective LLM Delusion at Work.

7.2 Do not measure by survey

METR's sign reversal applies directly to organizational metrics. Ask "what percent faster has AI made you" in a survey and you may get a number pointing the wrong way. There are better values to collect.

  • Lead time per task — actual elapsed time from start to merge or approval
  • Rework rate — the share of delivered output sent back
  • Review queue length — before and after adoption
  • When defects are discovered — the later the discovery, the more verification is leaking

7.3 The shrinking learning path

The ability to verify grows out of producing wrong answers yourself and then finding out why they were wrong. If a model always writes the draft, that path narrows. This concern has no long-term tracking data behind it yet. What is available now is deliberately reserving some tasks that juniors do not hand over, and how large that reserve should be cannot be set as a number because there is no evidence for one.

8. Five things you can measure this week

  1. How many answers did you reverse last week after judging them wrong? Zero means one of two things: the tasks really were easy, or you did not catch anything
  2. Which row of the section 4 table is your main task? If it is a lower row and you are handing over broadly, narrow the scope first
  3. What share of the do-it-yourself time does verification take? Past half, it is time to change how you delegate. Format specification and counterexample requests come first
  4. Of the sources the model offered in the last month, what fraction did you actually open? A low fraction combined with factual research is a danger zone
  5. For a team, how has the review queue changed since adoption? If it grew, the bottleneck moved; that is not the tool being bad

All five produce a value within a week. And those values separate whether the model needs to change or the scope of delegation does. It is usually the latter.

9. What could not be confirmed

The following were not used as figures or assertions because no primary source could be pinned down.

  • An experiment measuring the path from domain expertise to output quality directly. The studies cited compare effects by skill level; none verifies the mechanism by which expertise operates through the prompt
  • The direction of developer productivity as of 2026. METR's follow-up confidence intervals include zero, so the direction is unresolved
  • The gain split between higher- and lower-ability groups in the consultant experiment. It could not be checked against the primary document, so only the 19 percentage point figure was carried over
  • The share of total working time spent on verification. No published aggregate was found
  • The long-term effect of delegating drafts on verification ability. No longitudinal data exists
  • Error-rate differences in non-English domains. No public comparison against English-language evaluations was found

Conclusion: build the verification path before changing the model

Compress the reason two people using the same tool get different results into one sentence and it comes out like this. The winner is not the person who receives answers well but the person who judges them well. The knack for narrowing a request is a by-product of that judgment, not its cause.

So the first thing to touch in practice is not the prompt. It is deciding, for each piece of work you intend to hand over, how it will be checked. Work with no answer to that question is not yet work you can hand over. That single criterion resolves most placement decisions without memorizing the table in section 4.

At the same time, the framing where only experts benefit is worth discarding. That people with shallow domain knowledge gain more on cheap-to-verify work has been observed repeatedly across experiments, and drafting, translation, summarizing and format conversion are safe to hand over today. What needs separating is tasks, not people.

Two things to keep. Pick one piece of work to delegate this week and write the verification method in a single line first, and append list three conditions under which this answer is wrong to the end of your requests. Both cost almost nothing, and the first week catches more than expected.
