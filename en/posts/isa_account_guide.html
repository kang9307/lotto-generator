The Complete Guide to Korea's ISA (Individual Savings Account)

Account Types, Eligibility, Contribution Limits and Tax Benefits Explained

Jul 24, 2026 Finance & Economy

Introduction: What to Know Before You Open an ISA

You may have heard Korea's ISA described as an "all-purpose account." In practice, the ISA (Individual Savings Account) is a tax-advantaged account designed to hold deposits, funds, domestic listed stocks, and ETFs together while saving on taxes up to a set threshold. Treating it as unconditionally advantageous, however, invites misunderstanding. Which products you can hold differs by account type, there is a mandatory holding period, and the tax-free benefit itself has a cap.

This article covers the ISA's structure and types, eligibility and limits, the outline of its tax treatment, and strategy and cautions at maturity. In particular, it makes clear that proposed reforms to raise the contribution and tax-free limits have been under discussion since early 2026, but as of July 2026, when this article was written, the relevant legislation had not completed the National Assembly's process and remained unconfirmed. This article is for informational purposes and does not recommend any specific financial product. Responsibility for actual investment decisions and tax handling rests with you, and exact rates and limits should always be reconfirmed with your financial institution and tax authority materials at the time.

1. What Is an ISA?

An ISA lets you hold a mix of financial products - deposit-type instruments, funds, domestic listed stocks, ETFs, and structured products - together in a single account. Unlike a regular account, where each product is taxed separately, an ISA is commonly described as netting gains and losses across the products held inside it, then applying tax exemption up to a set threshold on that net profit, with a lower separate tax rate applying above the threshold. That netting-and-threshold structure is the account's central differentiator.

2. The Three ISA Types: Brokerage, Trust, and Discretionary

2.1 Brokerage Type

The brokerage type lets you directly select and trade domestic listed stocks, ETFs, and bonds yourself. It is the type most commonly mentioned for recent new sign-ups, generally suited to investors who want to pick their own stocks or ETFs.

2.2 Trust Type

The trust type lets you choose among various products - including term deposits - and instruct the institution to execute them. Its distinguishing feature, often cited, is that it can be run with deposit-type products, unlike the other types.

2.3 Discretionary Type

The discretionary type hands management over to the institution's professionals, who charge a separate management fee for the service. It is often mentioned for investors who would rather not make hands-on allocation decisions themselves, though the fee's drag on returns is worth weighing.

3. Eligibility, Contribution Limits, and the Mandatory Holding Period

Generally, resident individuals with income can open an ISA, and accounts have historically been split into a general type and, for lower-income individuals (including certain farming and fishing households), a type that receives a larger tax-free allowance. Under the existing framework, all three account types are reported to carry a 3-year mandatory holding period, along with annual and cumulative contribution caps, with unused annual room typically carrying forward to the next year. The exact income thresholds and dollar figures depend on the law in effect, so it is safest to confirm with the current notice from the institution where you plan to open the account.

4. How the Tax-Free and Separate-Taxation Structure Works

The ISA's central benefits are gain-loss netting and the tax-free and separate-taxation treatment. Because tax is calculated on the net profit after netting gains and losses across the products held in the account, a loss in one holding can offset a gain in another before tax applies at all - a structure commonly cited as the key difference from an ordinary account. A set threshold of that net profit is reported to be tax-exempt, with the amount above the threshold taxed at a separate rate lower than standard interest and dividend income tax rates. The tax-free threshold has historically been designed larger for the lower-income type than the general type. The exact threshold amounts and separate tax rate depend on the law in effect when you hold the account, so treat any figures here as directional and verify with current official sources.

5. What Products Can Go Into an ISA?

The available range differs by account type, but commonly cited eligible holdings include term deposits and similar deposit-type products, domestic bonds and funds, domestic listed stocks (brokerage type), ETFs, and structured products. There is also a range of products that cannot be held in an ISA - for instance, directly buying foreign stocks through the account is generally not possible - so confirm in advance whether the specific holding you want is actually eligible under the ISA type you plan to open. This is not a recommendation of any specific product, nor a guarantee of returns.

6. Strategy at Maturity: Closing the Account or Transferring to a Pension

Once the mandatory holding period ends, you generally have the option to close the account and withdraw the funds, or extend the maturity and keep the account running. A frequently cited strategy here is transferring to a pension account. Moving funds from a matured (closed) ISA into a pension savings account or an IRP within a set window is reported to provide an additional tax deduction - based on a portion of the transferred amount, up to a cap - on top of the regular pension-account deduction limit. Note, however, that holdings inside the ISA typically need to be liquidated to cash before the transfer, and missing the deadline by even a day causes the transfer to be treated as an ordinary pension contribution, forfeiting this additional benefit. Confirm the exact deadline and deduction cap with the law in effect and your institution's guidance.

7. ISA vs. a Regular Account: Trade-offs

A regular brokerage or bank account typically taxes each product separately with no gain-loss netting, whereas an ISA is generally regarded as tax-advantaged because it nets gains and losses within the account and applies tax exemption and separate taxation up to a threshold. On the other hand, an ISA locks up funds for the mandatory holding period, and its annual and cumulative contribution caps can themselves become a constraint for investors with substantial spare capital. It is commonly discussed as reasonable to size your ISA versus regular-account allocation based on how much spare capital you have and how long you can tolerate having funds tied up.

8. Cautions: Early Withdrawal and the Netting Trap

Closing the account early, before the mandatory holding period is met, is frequently noted to forfeit tax benefits or trigger clawback of benefits already received. Putting all your spare funds into an ISA without considering situations where you might urgently need cash is not advisable. Also worth understanding: while gain-loss netting is generally a benefit, it cuts both ways - a large gain in one holding can be offset by a loss in another, which can make how much of the tax-free allowance you actually end up using play out differently than expected.

9. 2026 Policy Changes Under Discussion - Still Unconfirmed

Reporting since early 2026 has described proposals to raise the ISA's annual contribution limit from roughly 20 million KRW (cumulative 100 million KRW) to around 40 million KRW (cumulative 200 million KRW), and the tax-free threshold from roughly 2 million KRW (general type) / 4 million KRW (lower-income type) to around 5 million KRW / 10 million KRW respectively, alongside a proposal to let certain high financial-income taxpayers, previously excluded, open a so-called domestic-investment-type ISA. However, as of July 2026, when this article was written, this reform package had not fully completed the National Assembly's tax-law amendment process, and whether and when it takes effect remained unconfirmed. Treat these figures only as the "direction under discussion," and before opening or funding an account, always verify the confirmed status through the Financial Services Commission, the National Tax Service, or your institution's latest notice.

10. Conclusion: How to Approach an ISA

An ISA offers a clear tax advantage, but it comes bundled with type-specific differences, a mandatory holding period, and contribution caps. First, decide among the brokerage, trust, and discretionary types based on whether you want to pick your own holdings, run things mostly through deposits, or hand it to a professional. Second, contribute only spare funds, on the assumption that money may be tied up for at least three years. Third, at maturity, weigh closing, extending, or transferring to a pension account based on your situation. Fourth, treat the 2026 reform discussion as unconfirmed reference information until an official announcement lands.
