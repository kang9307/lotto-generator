Getting Started with Google Sheets — What Excel Users Should Decide First

Google Sheets for Excel Users: What to Decide Before Your First Sheet

Aug 16, 2026 Tech & IT

The features take half an hour. The decisions before them are the problem

If you already know Excel, Google Sheets feels familiar within about thirty minutes. There are cells, formulas still start with an equals sign, and SUM and VLOOKUP are right where you expect them. Which is why a walkthrough of which button to click is not really what anyone needs. Clicking teaches that part on its own.

What is genuinely hard to undo later has nothing to do with features. It is which account created the file, where the file lives, and who received the link. All three get decided in the two seconds it takes to create a new sheet, and all three come back months later in the shape of a departure, a handover, an audit or a leak. Settle them properly at the start and everything else can be picked up as you go.

This article covers those decisions and the differences that every Excel user runs into at least once. The behaviour described here follows Google's official help documentation and is current as of August 2026. One deliberate omission: there are no menu names or click paths here. Google reshapes the editor interface continually, and an item that sat in one menu yesterday can sit under another today. Searching by the name of a setting outlasts memorising where it used to be.

Which account you use is the first fork in the road

This is the most common mistake and the one discovered latest. You are in a hurry, you create the file under whichever account happens to be signed in, and you start using it for work. That account is now the owner.

  • Work material created under a personal account cannot be reclaimed by the company. The moment responsibilities change, the handover hangs on a single shared link. If that person revokes sharing or closes the account, it ends there.
  • Personal material created under a work account disappears when you leave. Once an organisation suspends the account, files owned by it become inaccessible. Personal budgets and career notes end up on company accounts more often than you would guess.
  • Transferring ownership is less free than people assume. Depending on organisational policy, transfers to outside accounts may be blocked entirely. Assuming you can move it later means discovering the block on the day you need to move it.
  • Multiple signed-in accounts make the same link behave differently. The address carries a number such as /u/0 or /u/1, and a link that carries someone else's number shows you a no-access screen. Nothing is wrong with the file; you are simply opening it as the wrong identity.
  • Organisation accounts often restrict external sharing. If a sheet is meant to be seen by an outside partner, confirm that sharing is permitted before you build it. Finding out afterwards means rebuilding it somewhere else.

The test is short. Does someone need to keep using this sheet after I am gone? If the answer is yes, do not start it on a personal account.

My Drive or a shared drive — this is where ownership is settled

With the account chosen, the next question is location. Google's own documentation frames the difference in terms of who owns the file: files in a shared drive belong to the team instead of an individual, and even if members leave, the files stay exactly where they are. Files in My Drive are tied to the person who made them. This is less a feature comparison than a decision about whose file it is.

  • Personal records belong in My Drive. A shared drive is a space the organisation can reach into, which is not where private material should sit.
  • Anything that will ever be handed over belongs in a shared drive. The single property of surviving a personnel change outweighs every convenience argument on the other side.
  • Creating it in the right place beats moving it later. A move keeps the link working, but sharing and permissions are recalculated under the new location's rules, and it is easy to miss who quietly lost access in the process.
  • Whether shared drives exist for you depends on the subscription tier. Personal accounts have no such concept at all. If you are on a work account and the option is absent, that is an edition or administrator question, not a bug.
  • Build the folder structure first. Sheets multiply into dozens quickly. Deciding where new files land is far cheaper than tidying up afterwards.

Four differences from Excel that actually matter

Comparing feature lists is not useful. In practice only four differences cause real incidents.

1. There is no save button, and version history takes its place

Edits apply as you make them, so there is nothing to press. Version history carries the job of undoing instead. Three documented points are worth knowing. Browsing earlier versions requires edit permission on the file, named versions are capped at fifteen per spreadsheet, and some changes never appear in edit history at all — row and column modifications, cell formatting changes and values updated by formulas among them. That last one is why the belief that the log captures everything is dangerous. Create a named version yourself at moments that matter.

2. Simultaneous editing is the normal state, not an exception

In Excel a shared workbook was a special mode. Here it is simply Tuesday. When two people edit the same cell nothing locks; the later input wins. That makes actions like sorting, filtering and inserting rows — the ones that move other people's screens — something you do not fire off casually. The other person experiences the row they were reading jumping somewhere else.

3. The unit is a link, not a file

You are not attaching something to an email; you are granting access to a document that keeps changing. The contents continue to move after you send it, and the wrong permission means the recipient edits your original. When you deliver a final version, export a PDF or a spreadsheet file rather than sending the link.

4. The function sets overlap without matching

Most familiar functions are present. But a few exist only here, and a few tools such as Power Query exist only on the other side and have to be solved differently. If you plan to go past formulas into real automation, the complete Google Sheets automation guide continues into Apps Script and form integration, and the AI-assisted angle sits in the Excel and Google Sheets AI automation guide.

Settings worth fixing on day one

There is not much to configure at the start. This is close to the whole list.

  • Set the locale. The official documentation states that adjusting locale and time zone changes the spreadsheet default currency, date and number formatting. The setting belongs to the file, so every collaborator sees it the same way no matter where they are. Most mismatched dates and currencies begin here.
  • Set the time zone. It defines what NOW and TODAY return and what timestamps mean. In any sheet that tracks deadlines, getting this wrong makes everything downstream wrong.
  • Check the functions language. The same settings area lets you choose the language function names are written in. When it differs, a formula pasted from a colleague comes back unrecognised.
  • Look at the calculation settings. Recalculation timing and iterative calculation live here. Anything relying on a deliberate circular reference needs the latter enabled first.
  • Understand the autocomplete behaviour. Entering similar values down a column produces suggestions. Convenient, but values you did not intend do get committed, so read the suggestion before accepting during bulk entry.
  • Turn on offline access in advance. Per the documentation this requires Chrome or Microsoft Edge with the dedicated extension, does not work in private browsing, and allows only one account per browser profile. Large files will not sync, and administrators can disable the feature for an organisation. Realising this at the airport is too late.
  • Rename the file immediately. Once ten untitled spreadsheets pile up, even search will not save you.

Three functions Excel does not have — ARRAYFORMULA, QUERY, IMPORTRANGE

Learning these three is the point where Sheets stops being Excel-in-a-browser and becomes a different tool. The definitions below follow the official function reference.

ARRAYFORMULA — stop dragging formulas down

The documented definition is that it enables the display of values returned from an array formula into multiple rows and columns, and the use of non-array functions with arrays. Pressing Ctrl+Shift+Enter while editing a formula wraps it automatically.

=ARRAYFORMULA(B2:B1000 * C2:C1000)

Written once in a single cell, the rows below fill themselves. Nothing has to be dragged down again when the data grows, which pays off most in sheets where several people keep appending rows. The catch is that array formulas cannot be exported. That is exactly why a workbook downloaded as an Excel file can arrive with its calculated column empty.

QUERY — write a query inside a cell

The official description is that it runs a Google Visualization API Query Language query across data. The syntax is QUERY(data, query, [headers]) and supports select, where, group by, order by and pivot clauses.

=QUERY(A1:E, "select B, sum(D) where C = 'Done' group by B order by sum(D) desc", 1)

Two things trip people up. Inside the query string, columns are referenced by column letters such as A, B and C, not by their header text. And the documentation notes that when a column mixes types, the majority data type determines the data type of the column. A few stray text entries in a numeric column will silently drop those values, so when a query looks wrong, inspect the source column's types first.

IMPORTRANGE — pull a range from another document

The syntax is IMPORTRANGE(spreadsheet_url, range_string).

=IMPORTRANGE("https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/SHEET_ID", "Sales!A1:D")

The first time two documents are connected, a #REF! appears with a prompt to allow access, and values only arrive once that is granted. This is the privacy-relevant part. Per the documentation, once approved the destination spreadsheet can import from any part of the source, that access persists until the user who granted it is removed from the source, and it counts toward the sharing limit. Writing A1:D in the formula does not narrow the approval to those four columns.

Why anyone with the link is dangerous

The documentation describes this setting as anyone who has the link being able to use the file without signing in. People read it as safe because of the belief that such files do not appear in search results, but those are two separate claims.

  • Links get copied. One forward in a chat and control is over. There is no realistic way to pull it back.
  • No record of who opened it exists. Since it opens without signing in, there is nothing to attribute an access to.
  • Combined with editor access it is the worst case. Anyone holding the link can change your original, and viewers cannot even inspect version history, because browsing versions requires edit permission.
  • It gets switched on for convenience and never switched off. That is by far the most common path to an exposed sheet.

When the sheet contains personal data

A sheet holding names, contact details, addresses, account numbers or dates of birth — even one row of them — cannot be treated like any other document. It stops being a settings question and becomes a responsibility question.

  • Do not use anyone with the link. Name the individuals who need access and revisit that list on a schedule. In most cases it still contains people who left and partners whose engagement ended.
  • Keep only the fields you need. Every column in the sheet will eventually be read by someone. Identifying details that the purpose does not require are safest never copied in at all.
  • Download, print and copy restrictions prevent mistakes, not leaks. The documentation confirms owners can control those actions, but the screen is still visible and a screenshot is still a screenshot.
  • Use expiry dates on external sharing wherever they are available. The documentation states that the expiration date feature is only available for eligible work or school accounts. Its absence on a personal account is expected, not a fault.
  • Do not pull it in with IMPORTRANGE. As covered above, approval is granted per document. Approving a source that holds personal data exposes the whole document.
  • Distribute a summary rather than copies of the original. Move the figures that are actually needed into a separate sheet and share that one. Very often the job gets done without granting access to the source at all.
  • Clean up on the day someone changes roles. Plans to do it in a batch later are not kept.

The places beginners get stuck

Some symptoms give no hint of their cause. These five account for most of them.

SymptomCauseWhere to fix it
The total differs from the values on screenFormatting changes display only; the stored value is unchangedUse ROUND to change the value itself
A number is skipped by SUMIt is stored as textLeft alignment is the tell. Convert with VALUE
Month and day are swapped in datesThe locale follows another countryThe file's locale setting
Pasting dragged formatting along with itA plain paste carries values, formulas and formattingUse paste values only
My filter changed someone else's screenA filter applies to the whole documentUse a filter view instead
  • Cell format and stored value are different things. Displaying two decimal places does not round the underlying number, which is why adding up what you see can disagree with the computed total. Rounding the value itself takes a formula.
  • Numbers stored as text disappear quietly. This is common in data exported from other systems or copied off a web page. A leading apostrophe or an invisible space is usually behind it.
  • A date showing as a five-digit number is not a fault. Dates are stored as numbers and the format merely presents them as dates. Restoring the date format brings it back. If the mechanism interests you, the piece on Excel serial values and the 1900 leap year bug goes through it in detail.
  • Use the paste options deliberately. Values only, formatting only and formulas only are separate choices. Pasting plainly from someone else's table drags colours, borders, conditional formatting and validation rules across, overwriting the rules already in place.
  • Filters and filter views are not the same thing. The documentation explains that when you add a filter, anyone with access to your spreadsheet will find the filter too, whereas a filter view applies only to your view and several people can hold different filter views at once. It also notes that with view-only permission you can still create a temporary filter view that only you can use. On any shared sheet, make filter views the default.
  • Whole-column references are convenient and heavy. Formulas pointing at A:A accumulate and slow the whole document. Bound your data ranges, or compute only the span you need with ARRAYFORMULA.
  • Lookup functions behave almost exactly as in Excel. The VLOOKUP family transfers directly, so there is nothing new to learn. Error types and alternatives are collected in the complete VLOOKUP and XLOOKUP guide, and if aggregation is the unfamiliar part, the practical PivotTable guide explains the same concepts.

What this article does not claim

The following are left open here. They vary with time, account tier or organisational policy, or can only be confirmed on the actual screen.

  • Menu names and click paths are omitted deliberately. Google keeps rearranging the editor. Searching by the name of a setting outlasts a memorised route.
  • Which subscription tier unlocks which feature is not settled here. Some capabilities, such as expiry dates, are documented only as available to eligible work or school accounts, and the precise boundary has to be read in the administrator console.
  • Why a button is missing from your screen cannot be judged from outside. The feature may not exist for your account, or an administrator may have turned it off — the documentation confirms offline access can be restricted that way.
  • Row, column and cell limits and performance ceilings are not quoted. These numbers have changed over time, and printing one here would leave it wrong.
  • Whether a particular Excel file converts losslessly depends on the file. Macros and some formatting and functions shift during conversion. Keep the original and test against it.
  • Legal judgements about handling personal data are outside this article's scope. What is covered here is settings and habits; which safeguards a given dataset requires is a matter for the relevant authority's guidance and professional advice.

Frequently asked questions

Q1. Can I open an Excel file in Google Sheets and keep working as before?

Most tables and formulas open fine. Macros do not carry over — automation here is Apps Script, not VBA, so it has to be rebuilt. Conditional formatting, validation rules and some functions can shift during conversion, so keep the original file and open a copy to compare. Exporting the other way, remember the documented limit that array formulas built with ARRAYFORMULA cannot be exported.

Q2. There is no save button. Can my work be lost?

Edits apply live and version history is what you roll back to. But edit permission is required to browse versions, and named versions are capped at fifteen per spreadsheet. Some changes, such as row and column edits and cell formatting, never appear in edit history, so create a named version yourself whenever you need a dependable point to return to.

Q3. Is it safe to share a sheet with anyone who has the link?

The documentation describes it as anyone with the link being able to use the file without signing in. Not showing up in search and not being openable are different claims. Links get forwarded, cannot be recalled and leave no record of who opened them. For sheets holding personal data, do not use it — name the people who need access instead.

Q4. Which functions exist in Google Sheets but not in Excel?

Three stand out. ARRAYFORMULA displays values returned from an array formula across multiple rows and columns and lets non-array functions work with arrays. QUERY runs a Google Visualization API Query Language query across data. IMPORTRANGE imports a range from another spreadsheet and requires an allow-access step on first connection. All three are defined in the official function reference.

Q5. Why do my dates display incorrectly or turn into plain numbers?

Check the locale first. The documentation states that changing locale and time zone changes the default currency, date and number formatting, and that setting belongs to the file, so every collaborator sees it identically. A file built under another country's locale reads month and day in the opposite order. Dates appearing as five-digit numbers is separate — the cell format was switched to a number while the stored value is still a date.

Wrapping up

There is genuinely little to relearn in Google Sheets. Account and location determine ownership, version history replaces saving and needs edit permission to read, locale rides along with the file and applies to everyone equally, sharing opens a door rather than handing over a copy, and ARRAYFORMULA, QUERY and IMPORTRANGE do the Excel jobs by other means. Those five carry the rest.

Before you start, settle two questions. Does someone need to keep using this sheet without me? And does it contain information about people? If the first is yes, do not create it on a personal account. If the second is yes, do not turn on link sharing. Almost everything that cannot be undone later begins at one of those two points.
