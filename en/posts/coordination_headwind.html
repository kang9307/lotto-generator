Why Adding People Does Not Add Output - How Coordination Cost Outgrows Headcount

What grew is not hands but pairs that have to agree. Connections grow with the square of headcount

2026-09-02 Trends & Culture

When work falls behind, the usual move is to put people on it. More hands should mean more output. Then a few months later the headcount has doubled and what ships has not, and sometimes it is worse than before. The explanations that show up at that point are almost always about people. The wrong hires. Poor chemistry. Not enough management.

This piece sets those explanations aside, because the same thing happens when everyone is capable and diligent. The cause sits in the arithmetic. Headcount goes up one at a time, but the number of connections those people have to keep aligned goes up faster. The time that difference consumes does not vanish. It comes back as meetings, documents, approvals and waiting. What follows is how that invoice is calculated, and where the structural part ends and the choices begin.

This is a general account of how organization size and coordination cost interact, not an assessment of any specific company, institution or team. Every number in the text is either computed from a formula or a model value taken from a cited source. None of them are measurements of a real organization.

1. The trigger - a headwind nobody is blowing

In early September 2026 a 171-page English slide deck circulated in a Korean developer community. The title is Coordination Headwind, and the subtitle says organizations are like slime molds. A slime mold is a single-celled collective with no brain and no command center, yet it solves mazes and covers food efficiently. The place where Alex Komoroske picks up that comparison sits earlier than any prescription. The observation comes first: much of what happens inside an organization happens not because anyone ordered it but because individual behaviors stack.

The argument is simple. Situations arise in which nothing moves even though every person involved is good at their job, works hard and cooperates, and the cause of that is not a personal defect but a property that emerges from the system. That is why the word headwind fits. Nobody is blowing a headwind, and holding the same speed into one means pushing harder.

The direction the deck points toward is being loosely coupled while tightly aligned, and thinking like a gardener rather than a builder. Rather than restate that prescription, this article looks at the arithmetic sitting underneath it, because prescriptions land differently in different organizations while the arithmetic is the same everywhere.

2. Connections grow faster than people

If n people are in a room and each of them has to align at least once with everyone else, the number of pairs to be aligned is n(n−1)/2. That formula is not organizational theory. It is a combination: the number of ways to pick two people out of n.

The same arithmetic appears in two classic texts of this field, for opposite purposes. Melvin E. Conway wrote in Datamation in April 1968 that elementary probability theory tells us that the number of possible communication paths in an organization is approximately half the square of the number of people in the organization. Fred Brooks wrote in chapter 2 of The Mythical Man-Month in 1975 that if each part of the task must be separately coordinated with each other part, the effort increases as n(n−1)/2. Conway used it to explain why an organization's structure ends up carved into what it designs. Brooks used it to explain why throwing people at a project backfires.

The column worth staring at is not the last one but the third. That doubling the headcount quadruples the pairs is the famous part, but what an individual actually feels is not the total number of pairs. It is the number of pairs in front of them, which is n−1, and that grows in direct proportion to headcount. Someone who used to align with four people in a team of five now aligns with nineteen in a team of twenty. That is time subtracted from output.

  • The formula is an upper bound for the case where everyone has to align with everyone. In a real organization not every pair is active, so the actual load is lower
  • Which means the useful question is not how many pairs exist but which pairs are actually live. The count of live pairs is the organization's real coordination cost
  • Conversely, narrowing the interface lowers the load without changing the pair count at all. Most structural work is work on that interface

3. Brooks's law - what the original says and what it does not

It gets quoted constantly and quoted in full almost never. The sentence reads: Oversimplifying outrageously, we state Brooks's Law: Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later. The first two words carry weight. The author attached an admission of outrageous oversimplification to his own law, immediately before stating it, and the version that circulates has usually had that clause cut off.

The original sets two conditions. One is that the project is already late. The law says nothing about adding people to a project that is on schedule. The other is that the work does not partition cleanly. Brooks drew a separation between tasks that speed up when you add people and tasks that do not, no matter how many you add. His example of the second kind was childbirth: assign as many people as you like and it still takes nine months.

The same chapter splits the cost of a new person in two. Training is the cost of bringing someone up to speed on the technology, the goals and the plan. It is proportional to the number of people added and is largely paid once. Intercommunication follows the pair count above and keeps billing until the project ends. Whether adding people works comes down to which of those two terms dominates.

  • Where it works — the work genuinely partitions, the piece the new person will own is already defined, and enough time remains to amortize the training cost
  • Where it does not — the deadline is close, nobody has decided what the new person will own, and deciding that consumes the time of the people already there
  • The exception clauses that usually travel with the law, such as it is different if you add them early or it is different if they already know the codebase, come from later literature rather than from the book itself
  • Turned into something usable: before adding a person, write down in one sentence the piece they can finish alone. If you cannot write it, it is not time to add them

4. What actually grows when headcount grows

Coordination cost is an abstract phrase, but the invoice is concrete. Add people and the following five things grow, and all five grow faster than headcount does, each for a different reason.

What growsWhy it outpaces headcountThe visible symptom
Meeting attendeesAs more people are affected, it gets harder to justify not inviting someone. Anyone invited once tends to stay invitedAttendance rises while the number of people who speak stays flat
DocumentsWhat used to be said out loud has to be written once it crosses a certain size. Anything written becomes something to maintainTwo or more documents covering the same thing are in circulation
Approval stagesOne incident adds one stage, and added stages rarely come back outApprovers pass things through without asking about the content
WaitingAs dependencies grow, the length of each wait is set by the other side's queue. You cannot shorten it at your own paceSomething is marked in progress and has not moved in days
HandoffsFiner division means ownership changes hands more often, and each change drops part of the contextThe same question comes up again at every stage

The hardest row is approval stages. The other four are visible when they grow, but approvals have a rationale when added and none when removed. Adding one has an incident behind it; removing one requires a guarantee that no incident will follow. Nobody can give that guarantee, so the count moves in one direction.

  • Meetings are easier to fix by cutting attendees than by cutting the meeting. The price is set by the number of people, not by the existence of the meeting
  • Documentation cuts coordination cost more by removing duplicates than by adding pages. Two copies of the same thing generate a new conversation about which one is right
  • For approval stages there is little to do beyond setting an expiry date at the moment you add one. Adding is the only point at which removal is negotiable
  • Handoffs are their own metric. How many times one item changed hands is a number you get simply by counting, and most organizations never count it

5. The point where waiting outgrows working

When you work alone, elapsed time and working time are nearly the same. Add a second person and a handover gap appears between them, and the length of that gap is set by the other person's situation, not yours. The more people are involved, the more of these gaps there are, and past a certain point most of the elapsed time lives in them.

There are two shapes. If several people have to sign off in sequence, the waiting is the sum of the gaps: three stages of one day each is three days. If several people have to be in the same room, the waiting is not a sum but the calendar of the latest available person. Four of five free tomorrow and one free next week means next week. The first shape shortens by removing stages; the second shortens by removing required attendees. Confuse them and you work on the wrong side.

That said, long waits are not exclusively a headcount problem. Queues piling up behind a sped-up upstream stage, and waiting times that climb sharply as utilization rises, hold regardless of how many people there are. That mechanism is covered separately in what actually speeds up when you speed up a non-bottleneck. What this article looks at is the case where the cause is added headcount.

  • Waiting is not time nobody is working, it is time spent on something else. That is why it looks busy from the inside and stalled from the outside
  • That mismatch is the main reason coordination cost hides. Measured as individual utilization, it shows up nowhere at all
  • To see waiting you have to measure per item. Measured per person, waiting always comes out near zero

6. Where probabilities multiply

The model in the deck runs like this. For something to happen, each person involved has to actually put in their part. Even if each of them is 95 percent likely to do so, if ten people all have to, the overall probability is 0.95 multiplied ten times over, roughly 60 percent. The deck uses this as the reason success probability falls faster than linearly as more people are needed.

People requiredEach at 95%Each at 99%Each at 90%
385.7%97.0%72.9%
577.4%95.1%59.0%
1059.9%90.4%34.9%
2035.8%81.8%12.2%

How to read the table matters. These values did not come from surveying organizations. They are a demonstration of how multiplication behaves. In a real organization the participants are not independent of one another. One person joining pulls others in, and one person dropping out gives everyone else a reason to drop out too. Where the correlation is positive the real number lands above the table; where people are watching each other and deferring, it lands below.

One thing in the table does not move, though. However good you make each person, the total falls as the number of required participants rises. Twenty people at 99 percent each still comes to 82 percent. There is a ceiling on what raising individual reliability can do, and above that ceiling the only lever left is reducing how many people are required. That is the point where the deck says to loosen the coupling.

  • This multiplication applies when agreement has to be unanimous. Under majority rule or delegation the formula changes
  • So the first question in cutting coordination cost is not about meeting format but whether this decision really needs everyone to agree
  • A structure with veto rights in several places is the worst case of this formula. Every additional participant multiplies the total down
  • By contrast, in a structure where one person decides and the rest are informed, this term disappears entirely. The quality of the decision then rests on that one person

7. When splitting works and when it does not

If the pair count is the problem, the fix looks obvious: split. Break twenty people into four teams of five and the pairs inside teams come to ten each, forty in total, far fewer than the original 190. The arithmetic is correct. It is only correct when nothing crosses between the teams.

In practice the interfaces remain. If they are wide, the pairs inside teams went down while cross-team coordination appeared, leaving the total flat or higher. And cross-team coordination is more expensive than the internal kind. The people are not co-located, priorities differ, and when they disagree the matter has to climb to a shared manager to be settled. That is exactly what the deck means when it notes that the cost of resolving disagreement rises sharply with organizational distance.

So for a split to actually pay off, four things have to hold together.

  • The interface is narrow and stable — what passes between pieces is clearly defined and that definition does not change often. An interface that moves monthly makes splitting a net increase in coordination
  • Each piece is verifiable alone — you can tell whether your piece is right without assembling the whole. If you always need the whole, the pieces are pieces in name only
  • Data and permissions divide along the same lines — if several teams must edit the same records, the line has been drawn but there is still one team
  • Decisions finish inside the piece — the one most often missing. Split the work while keeping decision rights above and the total coordination is unchanged while handoffs increase

8. Conway's law - what gets built resembles who built it

Why that last condition matters so much was already explained in 1968. Conway's thesis: organizations which design systems (in the broad sense used here) are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations. An organization that designs a system ends up producing a copy of its own communication structure. He called the relationship a homomorphism, writing that there is a homomorphism from the linear graph of a system to the linear graph of its design organization.

The same paper has a more operational line: the very act of organizing a design team means that certain design decisions have already been made, explicitly or otherwise. Forming the team is itself already several design decisions. And: Every time a delegation is made and somebody's scope of inquiry is narrowed, the class of design alternatives which can be effectively pursued is also narrowed. Each delegation that narrows someone's scope also narrows the set of designs anyone can realistically reach.

The claim has been cited for decades, and it has also been tested. Alan MacCormack, Carliss Baldwin and John Rusnak, in Research Policy volume 41 issue 8 (pages 1309–1324, 2012), compared software products serving the same function but built by organizations of very different shapes, measuring the coupling between components. The result supported the hypothesis: the product from the loosely coupled organization was significantly more modular.

  • The operational implication is that there is no separate architecture meeting. The meeting where you decide how to divide the teams is already the architecture meeting
  • It runs the other way too. If the structure you want is settled, one route is to shape the organization that way first. That is a hard decision to reverse
  • Where structure and organization are misaligned, coordination cost attaches permanently at every seam, because a person has to bridge the gap each time
  • Conway's own paper does not end in a prescription for how to organize. It lands closer to knowing that organizing narrows the options while you do it

9. Where the feeling of speed disappears

Ask individuals after a headcount increase and most say things are better than before. They are right. Someone who used to watch ten things now watches three. Measured inside their own stage, handling time went down. Meanwhile the lead time measured from outside is flat or longer. The two impressions are not contradictory, they are measuring different intervals.

Individual speed is measured only while hands are on the work. Coordination cost is incurred mostly while hands are off it: after handing something over until the other side picks it up, after asking until an answer comes, after submitting until approval lands. None of those intervals appear in anyone's performance metric. That is how you end up with every stage posting good numbers while the whole thing slows down.

Headcount adds one more term on top. More people means more output, and more output means more things that need to be checked. If review and approval do not scale with headcount, a queue forms there. The effect usually arrives in this order: a clearly faster first few weeks, then review waiting starts to grow, then a few months later the overall lead time is back where it was.

  • Individual productivity metrics and total throughput metrics routinely fail to improve together, because they measure different things
  • Pushing harder on the individual metric at that point makes it worse. The queue grows faster and the waiting hides inside people's hands where it cannot be seen
  • The same arithmetic applies when you add agents rather than people. More participants means a larger coordination term either way. That version is in the piece on the orchestration tax

10. Actually pricing a meeting

Everyone says meetings are expensive and almost nobody writes down the price. The calculation is not hard. A meeting is priced in person-hours, not hours, and the time attached before and after has to be in the total.

Person-hours consumed by one meeting
  = attendees × (meeting time + prep time + recovery time)

  prep time     : reading the agenda and pulling materials together
  recovery time : getting back into the interrupted work
                  (varies by person, so measure your own for a few days)

Monthly cost of a weekly recurring meeting
  = the value above × 4.3      (average weeks per month = 52 / 12)

Share of one person's week spent on coordination
  = (sum of all meeting hours they attended) / (weekly working hours)

e.g. 8 attendees, 60 min meeting, 15 min prep, 20 min recovery, weekly
  per session : 8 × 95 min = 760 min = 12.7 person-hours
  per month   : 12.7 × 4.3 = 54.6 person-hours

The point of the calculation is not to abolish meetings. A meeting is a tool that collapses the sequential waiting described earlier into a single slot, and there are places where it is worth the price. The point is to hold them with the price known. Once the price is written down, it usually turns out that the thing to change in most organizations is not the number of meetings but the number of attendees. Remove two people from the same meeting and a quarter of the cost is gone.

  • Changing the invitation criterion from people who should know to people who will take part in the decision here cuts attendance noticeably. Knowing is what records are for
  • Recovery time varies a lot by person and also by where in the day the meeting sits. A 30-minute meeting in the middle of the morning splits the whole morning
  • Recurring meetings outlive the person who created them, and then nobody knows why they exist. Writing down once a quarter why this meeting exists is enough

11. What async and documentation reduce, and what they do not

Discussions of coordination cost tend to land on async work and documentation. Both are real, but they reduce different things, and treating them as one thing produces the wrong expectations.

Async reduces the hours several people are held in the same slot. Turn something that needed eight people in a room for an hour into fifteen minutes each and the person-hours drop from eight to two. The total volume of information has not dropped, and may have risen. And if the matter requires a decision, the waiting is multiplied by the number of round trips. Something that takes three exchanges to settle takes three days async and twenty minutes on a call. Async works well for transmission and badly for negotiation.

Documentation reduces repeated transmission. Explaining the same thing ten times becomes writing it once and having it read ten times, which turns the cost from one-to-one into one-to-many. But writing is not a one-time cost. Documents go stale, and stale documents generate fresh conversations to confirm them. So the real effect of documentation is decided by how much you throw away, not how much you write. The same rule applies to material produced from outside. The structure in which the deliverable stays while the reasoning behind it leaves is covered in what external consulting leaves behind.

  • Some things neither of them touches: the number of approval stages, the points where opinions genuinely diverge, and how far decision rights are scattered. Those are structural, not a matter of format
  • If lead time got longer after moving to async, the matter was probably negotiation rather than transmission. Counting the round trips shows it immediately
  • Any decision to add documents is better made together with who retires them and when. Unspecified, nobody throws anything away

12. The real reason small teams are fast

That small teams move fast is widely accepted, and the reasons usually attached to it are about people: only strong people remain, engagement is higher, there is more ownership. Those explanations are hard to falsify, and explanations that are hard to falsify usually conceal something else.

What they conceal is the arithmetic above. Five people means ten pairs to align; twenty means 190. Put the same people into a team of twenty and the capability is unchanged while the speed drops. Small teams are fast not because their members are special but because there are fewer people to reach agreement with. That is a statement about counting, not about ability, so swapping people does not fix it. How far the phrase talent density is actually supported is covered separately in what talent density actually means.

That does not make small teams universally fast either. If the last of the four conditions above is missing, that is, if the team cannot finish its own decisions, then coordination simply moves outward. Ten pairs inside and five approval stages outside still leaves lead time determined elsewhere. In that case, cutting headcount changes nothing.

  • The advantage of a small team only materializes alongside the range of decisions that team actually owns, not from the headcount itself
  • So there is one question to ask whenever you make a team small: what can this team finish without asking anyone outside it
  • If the answer is close to nothing, that is not a small team but a large organization cut into small pieces. Fewer pairs, same waiting
  • Individual ability operates on top of this structure. Where the structure is blocked, differences in ability barely show up in the result

13. Some work has to be big

Read this far and the conclusion tilts toward staying small. That prescription is not universal, and used as though it were, it generates other costs quietly. Some work genuinely requires scale.

Anything needing round-the-clock coverage requires more people than there are shifts. Work where audit or control rules mean the same person cannot hold two roles requires the headcount to be divided. So does work spanning multiple regions and languages, or a maintenance surface wide enough to need rotation. And of the two types Brooks separated, work that genuinely partitions does speed up when you add people. Being frugal with headcount there is not saving, it is delay.

The staying-small prescription has another limit. Keeping the organization small does not remove coordination, it moves it outside the organization — into outsourcing contracts, supplier negotiations and integrations with other companies. Coordination that has moved outside is usually more expensive than the internal kind, because the interface is written into a contract and takes time to change, and because there is no shared manager to escalate a disagreement to.

  • Compressed into one test: write down the pairs the new headcount creates next to the waiting that headcount removes, and compare them
  • People added to remove waiting generally work. People added to increase output depend on whether the work partitions
  • In work that requires scale, coordination cost is not something to eliminate but a budget line. Try to eliminate it and a necessary check goes missing, then returns as an incident
  • If you decide to grow, define the interfaces at the moment of growth. Defined later, they are defined after they have already widened

14. What to measure

Conversations about coordination cost go in circles for one main reason: both sides argue from impressions. Impressions form inside your own stage and coordination cost lives between stages, so one side saying it got faster while the other says it got slower is the natural outcome. Counting five things ends that conversation.

lead time        = completion time − time the request was accepted
touch time       = sum of time actually worked on it in each stage
wait time        = lead time − touch time
flow efficiency  = touch time / lead time × 100  (%)

approval stages  = number of sign-offs one request must clear
handoffs         = times ownership changed (by person, and by team)
people involved  = number of people who touched the item at least once

How to compare
  Take equal periods before and after the point where headcount
  changed and compare all five. A single reading means nothing.
MetricWhat a rise in it meansCommon trap
Lead timeActual speed as seen from outside. The final standard for improvementThe average hides the distribution. Read the median and the upper band alongside it
Flow efficiencyThe lower it is, the more waiting dominatesTouch time collected by self-report comes back overstated
Approval stagesThe floor of structural waiting. Each stage stops the item at least oncePeople argue that pro-forma approvals should not count, but they cost the same time
HandoffsThe number of points where context falls offChanges of hands within a team frequently go uncounted
People involvedA proxy for the coordination cost attached to that itemDecide up front whether observers count before you start

The cheapest two to start with are approval stages and handoffs. Neither requires timing anything, and opening the last ten completed items gives you both. Flow efficiency needs timestamps, so it takes preparation. How to observe the flow itself, and which queue to count, is set out in the earlier article.

  • Before measuring, write down what will count as improvement. Decided afterwards, the flattering number gets chosen
  • Treat it as a signal only when two or more of the five move in the same direction. One moving alone is usually a change in how it was measured
  • The effect of a headcount change needs at least a full quarter to read. The improvement of the first few weeks generally reverts
  • Use these numbers to evaluate people and they break immediately. The waiting hides inside individual hands and the measurement itself dies

What I could not verify

  • The deck's public page is a script-rendered flipbook, so I could not read it as displayed and instead confirmed the content from a text extract of the same document. For that reason I have not cited page numbers and have not reproduced its sentences verbatim
  • The exception clauses usually attached to Brooks's law, such as it is different if you add them early or it is different if they already know the codebase, were not confirmed in the book itself. What I confirmed in the original is the late-project condition and the partitionability condition
  • For what percentage coordination cost actually represents at a given organization size, I could not find any tally that publishes its sample and its measurement method together. Every figure in the text is therefore computed from a formula or a model value from a cited source
  • The ceiling of roughly 150 people often attached to discussions of organization size is not used here. A 2021 reanalysis in Biology Letters recomputing the same data reported 95 percent confidence intervals so wide — 3.8 to 520.0, and 2.1 to 127.7 — that no single number can be specified
  • The prep time and recovery time in section 10 are not observations but values each reader has to measure. The 15 and 20 minutes in the example are arbitrary figures used to show the calculation and are not survey results

Summary

Output failing to keep up with headcount is a problem of arithmetic, not of ability. Hands grow as n while pairs to align grow as n(n−1)/2 — ten pairs at five people, 45 at ten, 190 at twenty. The number of counterparts one person faces is n−1, so it rises in direct proportion to headcount. That time does not disappear; it is billed as meetings, documents, approvals, waiting and handoffs. Where unanimity is required, probabilities multiply on top: ten people at 95 percent each comes to 60 percent.

So adding people works only conditionally. The original sets two conditions — not already late, and work that genuinely partitions — and splits the cost of a new person into training paid once and intercommunication billed to the end. Splitting works when the interface is narrow, each piece is verifiable alone, data and permissions divide along the same lines, and decisions finish inside the piece. Drop the last one and you get pieces in name only with more handoffs. And the decision about how to divide the organization is simultaneously a decision about the structure of what it builds.

What remains is two questions. Does this decision really need everyone to agree, and which is larger, the pairs the new headcount creates or the waiting it removes. Answering them takes five numbers rather than impressions: lead time, flow efficiency, approval stages, handoffs, people involved. The last two are available this afternoon by opening the ten items closed today. That is where the workable part starts.
