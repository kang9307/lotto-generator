How to Choose Energy-Efficient Appliances: Ratings, Running Cost, Savings

From reading efficiency rating labels to total cost of ownership, standby power savings, and rebate programs

Jul 8, 2026 Life & Practical

Introduction: One Appliance, a Decade of Electric Bills

When you shop for a new refrigerator or air conditioner, it's easy to glance past the yellow rating label stuck to the front. But a major appliance is something you'll own for anywhere from five to fifteen-plus years, drawing power every single day. You pay the purchase price once, but the running cost shows up on your electric bill for as long as the appliance lives. This guide, current as of July 2026, walks through how to read energy efficiency rating labels properly and how to choose appliances using a total-cost-of-ownership mindset.

This is not a guide to specific brands or models. Instead, it covers principles that apply no matter where you shop: how to read rating labels, how to understand running-cost figures, what to check for each major appliance category, how to calculate total cost of ownership, everyday savings habits, when replacement pays off, and how rebate programs generally work. Exact rating criteria and rebate amounts change constantly by country and by year, so before buying, always check the latest information from your country's official energy agency - the Korea Energy Agency in Korea, ENERGY STAR/DOE in the US, or your country's energy labeling authority in the EU.

1. How to Read Energy Efficiency Rating Labels

1.1 Korea: A 1-to-5 Grade System

Korea applies an energy consumption efficiency grade system to major appliances like refrigerators, air conditioners, washers, and TVs, ranking them from Grade 1 (most efficient) to Grade 5 (relatively less efficient). The grade is not an absolute power-draw number - it's a relative ranking within a similar product category and capacity range. That means Grade 1 for a large refrigerator and Grade 1 for a compact one can have very different absolute power consumption, so you should check the annual energy consumption in kWh and the estimated monthly electricity cost printed alongside the grade, not just the grade number itself. Because the government periodically tightens minimum efficiency standards and rebalances the grade distribution, a model that was Grade 1 a few years ago might be reclassified as Grade 2 or 3 under today's criteria.

1.2 Labels Abroad: The EU and the US

Labeling formats differ once you look at international brands. The European Union simplified its once-complicated scale - which had climbed up to A+++ - back down to a clean A-to-G scale starting in 2021. In that process, many products kept the same real-world performance but received a lower letter grade under the new scale; a refrigerator once rated A+++ might now show up as a C. The United States combines the government-certified ENERGY STAR mark (issued by the EPA and Department of Energy) with the yellow-and-black EnergyGuide label, which shows an estimated annual operating cost range with an arrow marking where the specific model falls. That's a fundamentally different way of reading a label compared with Korea's 1-to-5 grades or the EU's A-to-G scale. Bottom line: grading systems and reference years differ by country, so directly comparing grade letters or numbers across countries is not accurate.

2. Understanding Annual Running Cost and Power Consumption Figures

There's a number on the label that arguably matters more than the grade itself: the annual energy consumption (kWh/year), and the estimated annual or monthly electricity cost it translates into. The grade is only a relative ranking within a product category, but the kWh figure is an absolute value, which makes it far more useful for comparing products of different sizes and features. A large refrigerator's Grade 1 model, for example, can still draw more absolute power than a mid-size refrigerator's Grade 3 model.

To estimate your real cost, multiply the annual kWh figure on the label by your actual electricity rate. Rates vary widely by country, region, and usage tier, so the estimated cost printed on the label is only a reference average - plug in your own bill's rate for an accurate figure. In places with tiered or progressive pricing, like Korea, a household that already uses a lot of electricity may see even bigger savings from switching to a high-efficiency appliance than a simple calculation would suggest.

3. What to Check for Each Major Appliance

3.1 Refrigerators: The One Appliance That Never Stops

A refrigerator is the only major appliance that runs 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, so a single grade difference shows up in your cumulative electric bill more than for any other appliance. Larger capacity and more doors generally mean higher absolute power draw, so avoiding an oversized unit relative to your household size is itself a meaningful saving. Leaving enough clearance for heat dissipation behind the unit and keeping it away from direct sunlight or heat sources also helps keep actual consumption close to the labeled figure.

3.2 Air Conditioners: A Short but Intense Season

Air conditioners run for a short season but at high load, concentrated in summer. If the cooling capacity is undersized for the room, the unit has to run longer to reach the target temperature, which can end up using more electricity than a properly sized unit with a lower grade - so matching capacity to room size sometimes matters more than the grade itself. Inverter models modulate output smoothly after reaching the target temperature, rather than cycling on and off like fixed-speed models, which usually means lower running costs for extended use.

3.3 Washers and Dryers: Water and Heat Combined

Washing machines often carry a water-usage rating alongside the energy rating, so check both figures together. Dryers, in particular, are among the highest power-draw appliances in a typical home. Heat pump dryers are generally known to consume significantly less power than conventional resistance-heater dryers, so if you dry loads frequently, it's worth checking which technology a model uses before buying. Whether you use a combined washer-dryer unit or separate machines also affects your overall consumption pattern.

3.4 TVs: Screen Size and Display Technology Matter

TV power consumption varies significantly with screen size and display technology (OLED, mini-LED, and so on). Even at the same screen size, actual power draw can swing widely depending on how high you set the brightness, so adjusting brightness and picture mode toward an eco setting can lower real-world consumption even between two TVs with the same label grade. TVs are also a classic standby-power draw even when turned off, which brings us to the next section.

4. Upfront Price vs. Running Cost: The Total Cost of Ownership View

High-efficiency models generally cost more upfront. This is where total cost of ownership (TCO) becomes useful: instead of comparing purchase price alone, add the purchase price plus the electricity cost over the expected usage period, minus any rebate you receive.

The calculation is straightforward. Multiply the label's annual kWh figure by your electricity rate to get the annual running cost. Multiply that by the appliance's expected years of use to get lifetime running cost. Add that to the purchase price to get total cost of ownership. Comparing this figure between a lower-efficiency and a higher-efficiency model tells you the break-even point - how long it takes for the price premium to be recovered through lower running costs.

Under this framework, the longer an appliance runs, the faster a high-efficiency model reaches its break-even point. For appliances like refrigerators that run around the clock, or air conditioners used heavily in summer, even a meaningfully higher purchase price is often recovered within a few years of running-cost savings. For appliances used infrequently, chasing the top grade may not pay off as clearly. The right answer varies by product, so running this calculation yourself is more reliable than trusting marketing copy.

5. Saving Through Standby Power and Everyday Habits

Even after you turn an appliance off with its power button, if the plug is still in the wall, it often keeps drawing a small trickle of electricity - this is called standby power (also known as phantom load). Standby power is estimated to account for roughly 5% to 10% of a typical household's total electricity use, and older appliances, as well as always-connected smart devices with network or standby features, tend to draw more.

The practical habits are simple: unplug devices you're not using, or switch off a power strip with a physical switch to cut several devices at once. Low-frequency secondary devices may each seem trivial, but they add up when there are several around the house. That said, it's realistic to exempt devices that need to stay on, like a refrigerator, or ones where restarting is genuinely inconvenient, like a home router.

6. When Replacement Actually Pays Off

There's a judgment call between "it still works, so I'll keep using it" and "it's inefficient, so I should replace it." Generally worth watching for: first, repeated repair costs that add up to a substantial fraction of a new unit's price. Second, an aging unit with a power-draw gap that has grown large compared with current new models - a refrigerator or air conditioner over ten years old, even if it was compliant when purchased, often falls into a lower grade by today's standard. Third, heavy usage hours where the TCO break-even point calculated earlier has already passed in favor of replacement.

On the other hand, if a unit hasn't broken down, sees infrequent use, and the efficiency gap with new models isn't large, there's little upside to rushing a replacement. Discarding a perfectly functional appliance early isn't automatically the "greener" choice either, once you factor in the resource cost of manufacturing and disposal. Whether to replace is a call best made against your own household's situation.

7. Rebates and High-Efficiency Refund Programs, in General

Many countries run programs to encourage purchases of high-efficiency appliances. Korea, through the Korea Energy Agency, has run programs refunding a percentage of the purchase price for top-grade appliances, with expanded terms for vulnerable households and larger families in some rounds. The US often layers federal and state rebates, tax credits, and utility-specific rebates on top of each other, and European countries run their own subsidy and tax-incentive programs.

The catch is that these programs commonly end early once funding runs out, and the refund percentage, cap, and eligible grade often change year to year. That is exactly why this guide avoids citing specific dollar amounts or percentages as if they were fixed. Before buying, always check the current terms - eligible grade, refund percentage, required documents, and application deadline - on your country's official energy agency website (the Korea Energy Agency's high-efficiency refund program page in Korea, ENERGY STAR and your state energy office in the US, or the responsible ministry in your EU member state).

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. How much does electricity cost differ between a top-rated and a bottom-rated appliance?

It is hard to generalize, but even appliances of the same size and features often show a noticeable difference in power consumption between the highest and lowest grades. The gap matters most for appliances that run long hours, such as a refrigerator running around the clock. For an accurate comparison, multiply the annual kWh figure on the label by your local electricity rate.

Q2. Do energy efficiency labels differ by country?

Yes. Korea uses 1-to-5 grades, the EU uses an A-to-G scale rescaled in 2021, and the US combines the ENERGY STAR mark with the yellow EnergyGuide label showing an estimated cost range. Because formats and criteria vary, avoid comparing grade letters or numbers directly across countries.

Q3. Is a pricier high-efficiency appliance always worth it?

Not necessarily. For long-running, long-lifespan appliances like refrigerators and air conditioners, the higher upfront cost often pays off. For infrequently used appliances, it can take much longer to recoup the premium. Estimate the break-even point based on expected usage and your electricity rate.

Q4. How much can I actually save by reducing standby power?

Standby power is estimated at roughly 5% to 10% of a typical household's electricity use, though this varies by appliance and habits. Unplugging unused devices or switching off a power strip can produce a noticeable reduction, but the exact savings depend on how many devices you own and their idle draw.

Q5. Should I always replace an old appliance with a new one?

Not necessarily. If a unit still works reliably, weigh its remaining lifespan and current power draw against the efficiency gap with a new model. Units with frequent repair costs, or old, long-running refrigerators and air conditioners with high power draw, tend to reach the point where replacement pays off sooner.

Conclusion: Look Past the Grade Number to Total Cost of Ownership

Choosing energy-efficient appliances comes down to three things. First, check the annual kWh figure and estimated running cost printed alongside the grade, not just the grade itself. Second, judge by total cost of ownership - purchase price plus running cost over the full usage period - rather than purchase price alone. Third, combine small habits like managing standby power with external support like rebate programs.

Grading systems and rebate terms keep changing by country and by year. This guide summarizes general principles as of July 2026; before making an actual purchase decision, always re-check the current official label information and program details for your country.
