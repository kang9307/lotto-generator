Who Makes the Automation List - What Makes Work a Target and Which Seat Stays Off It

Work with standard inputs, measured output and cheap error checking gets named first. Apply those same conditions to the seat that writes the list and you see structure, not a punchline

Aug 30, 2026 Trends & Culture

When automation comes up, most people go straight to the list. Is my job on it? How far down? But the list does not fall out of the sky. Somebody picks the items, sets the order, and leaves the exceptions in place. What this article looks at is not the contents of the list but the way the list gets made.

The conditions come first. Whether a piece of work becomes an automation candidate is decided by the nature of the work, not by the rank of the person doing it. There are three conditions, and candidacy requires all three at once. Then comes the actual argument. Apply the same three tests to the seat that writes the list, see what happens, and read the result as structure rather than as a joke at somebody's expense.

This is a general observation, not an assessment of any particular company or occupation. Decision-making structures and the distribution of accountability differ from one organization to the next, so actual judgment follows your own organizational rules and the law and standards that govern your field.

1. The trigger - one gesture thrown back

In late August 2026 a story moved fast through developer communities. There were reports that a company had cut engineering headcount and filled the gap with AI, and shortly afterward an open-source repository that split executive functions across eight specialist agents and bound them into a single voice drew attention. Strategy, finance, people, legal, operations, marketing, product and board communications, released under Apache 2.0.

One factual point needs separating out here. The dramatic version that spread most widely, in which laid-off developers built this as revenge, has no published basis. The repository's own public material does not claim that origin. What travelled, in other words, was not a confirmed event but the shape of a story. A gesture was thrown back, and people understood the shape immediately without needing it explained.

So this article does not use that repository as evidence that executive work is replaceable. The existence of software that imitates a job is not proof that the job can be replaced. That code exists and that a seat can be filled by it are entirely different claims. The repository story ends here and is not cited again as support anywhere below.

What remains is the reaction. Why did the shape land without explanation? To answer that, start with the conditions.

2. Three conditions that make work a candidate

Which work moves into tools first is not sorted by job title. It sorts at the level of individual tasks, where three properties overlap.

  • The input is standardized - you can count in advance what arrives, and the list of exceptions is broadly closed. If what comes in is new every time, no rule can be written for it
  • The output is measured - whether it went well leaves a number behind. A number allows comparison, and comparison is what puts a person and a tool on the same axis
  • Errors are cheap to catch - the time and cost of noticing a mistake are small. This is the condition most often left out of the assessment

Writing in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in 2015, David Autor described the territory where computers substitute for people as routine, codifiable tasks. The same essay points the other way as well: automation substitutes for labor but also complements it, raises output in ways that increase demand for labor, and commentators tend to overstate the extent of machine substitution. Meeting the conditions means a task moves early, not that it disappears.

The third condition deserves more weight than it usually gets. Tools bring down the cost of producing, but the cost of checking does not fall in step. The smoother the output looks, the fewer clues awkwardness gives you, and errors become correspondingly harder to spot. Pick candidates on the first two conditions alone and you arrive, some months later, at a review burden that grew quietly while nobody was counting it.

Rank never appears in that table. That is the point. Slices inside a single job title land on opposite sides, and the ordering often inverts what an org chart would suggest. Which is why sentences of the form this profession is going away or this profession is safe tend to miss. The unit that moves is not the profession.

3. Who writes that list

Lists do not assemble themselves

Somebody decides which tasks go up as candidates. Normally that happens in a seat that holds the budget and the org chart at the same time. And there is a regularity worth noticing here. The seat that writes the list generally does not appear on it.

Read that purely as self-interest and it is entertaining but empty. Apply the three conditions to that seat directly, and the same outcome falls out without assuming any conspiracy at all.

What happens when you apply the conditions

  • Is the input standardized? No. What arrives at a deciding seat has a different shape every time, and working out what the problem even is forms part of the job
  • Is the output measured? Attribution is blurry. The effect of a good decision shows up quarters later, tangled with the market, the people, the timing and the competition. There is no clean way to separate the decision's share
  • Are errors cheap to catch? No. What would have happened under a different decision does not exist. The raw material for a scorecard is missing

Three noes. So the seat does not reach the list. That much is a structural explanation, and it holds.

One qualification has to follow immediately. Failing the conditions is not proof that the work is valuable. It only means the work is not measured. Glue those two together and the discussion goes wrong fast, because unmeasured work includes both excellent work and work nobody would miss, and the conditions cannot tell them apart.

The regularity also does not apply only upward. Anywhere in an organization, seats with blurry output drop off the list and seats with sharp output land on it. Internal communications, cross-team coordination, interpreting internal policy. The principle that nature decides rather than position holds here just as firmly.

4. The paradox measurability creates

An uncomfortable symmetry appears. The better you can show your results in numbers, the more easily you become the subject of replacement-cost arithmetic. Arithmetic requires something calculable. Once your contribution is organized, that organization serves evaluation and comparison equally well. The same figure travels in two directions.

Which means two pieces of advice usually handed out together are not pointing the same way at all. Quantify your contribution so it is visible and do not stand where you are easy to replace collide head-on. The familiar warning is that a measure stops being a good measure once it becomes a target. Here the point goes one step further. Measured means comparable.

Visibility of resultsStandardization of processHow the list treats itWhat actually happens
SharpStandardizedCalculated firstTools attach quickly and the remaining role shifts toward review
SharpNon-standardCalculable but hard to movePartial automation stalls, and the number survives as a management metric
BlurryStandardizedFound lateInvisibility buys time, then the whole block moves at once
BlurryNon-standardNever reaches the listNothing visibly breaks when it goes, so it shrinks by other means

Do not read that last row as a safe zone. Absent from the list means uncalculated, not protected. Work that is not calculated is harder to defend when budgets contract, because there is nothing to point at.

So is the conclusion hide your numbers? That is bad advice. A state in which nobody knows how much your work amounts to hurts you and the organization both. The real response sits elsewhere. Say first, yourself, what the number means. If you can explain how far the metric tracks the actual work, where it stops tracking, and what judgment and coordination sit behind the figure, the number will not be read as a single cell in a comparison table.

5. Visible work and invisible work

Coordination, holding context, relationships. This kind of work is unnoticeable while it is happening and becomes evident only once it stops. What is usually called Polanyi's paradox applies here: we know more than we can tell. Work that cannot be written down does not appear in a specification, and what is not in a specification does not reach a candidate list.

That much is a familiar argument, and being familiar it leans one way. Ending at invisible work is precious is only half right. A great deal of invisible work could disappear without loss. Handoffs that exist because departments are split, reconciliation that exists because systems are split, confirmation calls that exist because a rule is ambiguous. These look like work but are really interest payments on design debt. When a tool clears them away, that is tidying rather than loss.

The test separating the two is simple. When the work vanishes, does cost pop out somewhere else? If it does, it was real work. If nothing happens at all, it was friction. The judgment is only available after the fact, but at least you know what to watch.

  • Did meeting time in another team grow after the person left
  • Did the same question start repeating across several channels
  • Did decisions begin escalating one level up more often
  • Did reasons for missed deadlines start naming waiting periods nobody knew about
  • If nothing changed at all, that seat was most likely friction the structure produced

6. Why the same joke keeps coming back

If that is the rule, start the automation at the top is not a new line. It returns in a fresh costume every time the tooling changes. Read only as resentment it leaves nothing behind. Read as a demand for consistency, it acquires content.

The demand says this. If you have built criteria for choosing automation targets, apply the criteria to the seat that built them. If you want an exception, say the grounds for the exception out loud. That is all.

And grounds do exist. The three conditions from section three are the grounds. Deciding work has non-standard input, blurry attribution and expensive error checking. It fails the conditions, so it does not reach the list. That is a coherent explanation.

The trouble is that the explanation is almost never given. Criteria become documents on the way down and stay unwritten on the way up. Absent an explanation, people conclude there are no grounds. That conclusion comes back in the form of a joke.

Which makes the joke a compressed question rather than an answer. What it aims at is not a person but the absence of an explanation. Answer it and the joke loses its force. Leave it unanswered and the same shape returns with the next tool.

7. Accountability - a signature does not divide

One question has to be reframed here. Is the thing machines cannot do judgment, or is it bearing responsibility?

Judgment has already moved a long way. Recommendation, classification, prioritization, drafting, flagging risk. Tools produce results in all of those seats. What has not moved is attaching a name to the result.

Regulation already carries this distinction in plain sentences. Article 14 of the EU AI Act requires high-risk systems to be designed so that they can be effectively overseen by natural persons during the period in which they are in use. It further requires that the person assigned oversight properly understand the system's capacities and limitations, correctly interpret its output, be able in a given situation to decide not to use the system or to disregard, override or reverse the output, and be able to interrupt it so that it comes to a halt in a safe state. What the article regulates is not the capability of the tool but the position of a person.

Korean law shows the same structure from another angle. Decisions about workforce reduction come with procedure and signature attached. Article 24 of the Labor Standards Act requires an urgent managerial necessity for dismissal on managerial grounds, obliges the employer to make every effort to avoid dismissal, and to set reasonable and fair criteria and select those affected accordingly. The same article requires notifying the workers' representative of the avoidance measures and the criteria at least 50 days before the intended date of dismissal and consulting in good faith, and reporting to the Minister of Employment and Labor above a certain scale.

The reason for citing that provision is not to evaluate any decision. It is to show that a decision comes with requirements, consultation and a reporting party attached. Article 393(1) of the Korean Commercial Act places execution of company business such as the disposal and transfer of important assets and large-scale borrowing under a resolution of the board of directors, and paragraph 2 makes the board responsible for supervising the directors' performance of duties. Large decisions are designed from the start as the record of a collegiate body rather than the output of one person.

So for a machine made this decision to hold, the machine would have to evidence that the requirements were met, take part in consultation, stand as the reporting party, and leave a name in the minutes. Current institutions are not built that way. Before it is a limit of technology, it is a question of attribution.

8. Why the automation decision itself resists automation

Three things ride on the decision about what to automate.

  • Reversal is expensive - once people have left and the org chart has changed, restoring the previous state is effectively a fresh hiring round
  • Interests conflict - the side being cut and the side remaining do not share an interest, and mediating between them is the content of the decision
  • Errors are slow and costly to confirm - the consequence of choosing the wrong targets surfaces quarters later, and usually under a different name

Those three invert the candidate conditions from section two precisely. So the automation decision never nominates itself. Self-reference breaks. Not because anyone blocks it, but because of the shape of the conditions.

There is one more piece. The first step of any automation is always writing the work down. Tools attach only once you can state in sentences what the input is, what success is and what failure is. Deciding seats are almost never asked to produce a specification of their own work, and work nobody is asked to write down does not enter step one.

A practical implication falls out of that observation. Once work is documented, it moves closer to candidacy. Reading this as avoid documentation would be a mistake, since that harms the organization and eventually the person too. The point is to know that documentation and automation are attached, and to set the order yourself. What to write down first, and what to design alongside the writing, is usually within the writer's control.

9. How this reaches an individual

Advice to become someone who cannot be replaced is unexecutable. A person is too large a unit. Converted into something you can actually do, it becomes this. Split your work into slices and write down the properties of each slice.

Take what you actually did last week, break it into five or six chunks, and fill three columns for each. Half a day is about the right size for a chunk. Too large and every column reads it depends; too small and the count becomes unmanageable.

Combination of the three columnsHow to read itWhat to do next
Standard + measured + cheap to checkThe slice that moves firstRather than slowing the tool down, decide first which slice the freed time goes into
Standard + measured + expensive to checkThe tool arrives but the review staysSet your own standard for what gets checked and at what density
Non-standard + measuredThe most ambiguous positionGet ready to explain where the metric and the actual work diverge
Non-standard + not measuredOff the list, but also without groundsWrite one line on where the cost would surface if it disappeared

The last row is the heart of the table. Unmeasured slices never reach the candidate list, but nothing visibly breaks when they go either. That is a different kind of exposure, not safety. Uncalculated work is harder to defend than calculated work.

And this table is not a forecast. It shows the order in which slices move, and order is not outcome. Slices that meet the conditions strongly often sit unchanged for years, and some grow in volume after a tool attaches to them. Autor's point about substitution and complementarity running together applies here too.

The question of how skill is acquired belongs to After the Stuck Time Disappeared, and the sense of a professional identity coming loose belongs to When a Whole Profession Stops Believing in Its Own Career. This article looked at neither of those axes but at the structure of being named.

10. What an organization should look at honestly

What automation moves is not the work but the location of review and accountability. As production time falls, the bottleneck shifts toward checking, and that shift is usually assigned to nobody. The bottleneck movement itself is covered in more detail in When Engineering Practices Move Into Other Professions. Here, three things the list-making side can do.

  • Write down the grounds for the list - why this task is a candidate and that one is not, in terms of the three conditions. Unwritten, people read the list as rank. They read it that way even when rank had nothing to do with the selection
  • Run the list-making seat through the same table - a result of not applicable is fine. What matters is not the conclusion but the record that the test was applied. The joke in section six appears exactly where that record is missing
  • Count the added review burden - write down, in hours, who now has to check more and how much more. Uncounted, that burden lands quietly on whoever stayed

One more thing belongs alongside those. Part of the cost automation saves should be spent on keeping the ability to reverse. The slack to return to the previous way of working when a tool stops, when the output turns out wrong, when the direction has to change. A decision you cannot reverse is not a decision but a stake on the table.

Summary

What makes a task an automation candidate is the nature of the work, not rank. Three conditions: standardized input, measured output, cheap error checking. The last of the three is the one most often skipped, which is why review burden is what remains after the move.

Apply the same three conditions to the list-making seat and all three come back no. So that seat drops off the list. This is a structural explanation and it is accurate, but failing the conditions does not mean the work is more valuable. It means it is unmeasured. From there a paradox follows: the better you can show results in numbers, the more easily you become the subject of arithmetic.

The reason start the automation at the top keeps returning is not resentment but the absence of an explanation. Criteria become documents on the way down and stay unwritten on the way up. Answer the question and the joke loses its force.

What an individual can do is not to become irreplaceable but to split the work into slices and know the properties of each. What an organization can do is write down the grounds for the list, run the list-making seat through the same table, and count the added review burden. What automation actually moves is the location of review and accountability, and a signature still does not divide.
