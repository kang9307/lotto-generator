Introduction: Why You Should Understand Bonds

When you start studying investing, the first word you meet is "stocks," and the second is almost always "bonds." Yet many people freeze when asked to explain what a bond actually is. Stocks come with an intuitive story, you own a piece of a company, but bonds arrive trailing unfamiliar terms like face value, coupon, yield to maturity, and duration, which feel like a wall at the entrance. In reality, bonds start from an idea we all already know from daily life: lending money and collecting interest.

Bonds form one of the largest asset classes in the world, no less significant than stocks. They are a core channel through which governments and companies raise money, and many investors use them to lower portfolio volatility and build a steady stream of income. The goal of this article is not to recommend or discourage bond investing, but to help beginners understand bonds accurately enough to judge for themselves, by laying out the concepts, the differences from stocks, the key terms, the types, the relationship between rates and prices, how to start, and the risks. No specific securities or products are mentioned, and the principles discussed are general and not region-specific. Korea-specific rules worth checking before buying bonds there, such as tax, deposit insurance, and retail government bonds, are gathered separately in section 6.4.

1. What Is a Bond - A Certificate of a Loan

A bond is, in a word, an IOU you receive as proof that you have lent money. Issuers such as governments, municipalities, public agencies, and companies issue bonds when they need funds, borrowing from investors. The investor who buys the bond becomes a creditor who lent the money; the issuer pays interest on a set schedule and, at maturity, returns the principal originally borrowed.

Suppose an issuer offers a bond with a face value of 1,000, a coupon rate of 4% per year, and a 3-year maturity. Buy it and you receive 40 in interest each year, and at maturity in three years you get your 1,000 principal back. The structure looks like a bank deposit, but a bond differs in one decisive way: it can be bought and sold in the market before maturity, and its price rises and falls in the process. That very feature, a changing price, turns a bond from a simple deposit into an investable asset.

1.1 The Three Promises of Every Bond

Every bond essentially makes three promises. First, how much it will repay (face value). Second, how much interest it will pay (coupon rate). Third, when it will repay (maturity). Read those three accurately and you already understand half of the bond's skeleton. The other half is about how the price is determined when the bond trades in the market, and at the heart of that is "interest rates."

2. Bonds vs Stocks - What Is Different

The fastest way to place bonds in context is to set them side by side with stocks. Both live under the umbrella of "investing," but their natures are fundamentally different.

AspectStocksBonds
Your statusOwner (shareholder)Creditor who lent money
Return structureDividends + price gainsFixed interest + principal at maturity
VolatilityGenerally higherGenerally lower
Priority in defaultLast in lineAhead of shareholders
UpsideTheoretically unlimitedUsually capped at fixed interest

The key differences boil down to three. First, status differs. A stock means owning part of a company; a bond means lending money to it. Second, the return structure differs. Stocks have no upper limit because prices and dividends can grow as a company thrives, while a bond's promised interest is fixed, limiting the return but making it predictable. Third, repayment priority differs. If an issuer goes bankrupt, bondholders have the right to be paid before shareholders. That seniority is why bonds are generally classified as safer than stocks.

"Safer" does not mean "no losses." A bond can still lose principal if the issuer defaults, and its price falls before maturity when rates rise. Bonds merely tend to be less volatile than stocks; they are not risk-free assets.

3. The Key Bond Terms You Must Know

Bonds are an asset with a high wall of jargon. But master just the five below and you can read a bond's documentation and tell good bonds from poor ones.

3.1 Face Value (Par Value)

Face value is the baseline amount printed on the bond, the principal the issuer promises to return at maturity. Crucially, face value is not the same as the bond's market price. A bond can trade above (at a premium) or below (at a discount) face value, and its price keeps changing while it is alive. Face value is simply the reference point for calculating interest and for repayment at maturity.

3.2 Coupon Rate

The coupon rate is the fixed annual interest paid as a percentage of face value. The term "coupon" comes from the old practice of clipping a coupon off a paper bond to claim interest. What matters is that once the coupon rate is set at issuance, it does not change until maturity. This "fixed interest" is the starting point of the rate-price inverse relationship described later.

3.3 Maturity

Maturity is the point when the issuer promises to repay the principal. Maturities range from under a year (short-term) to 30 years or more (very long-term). Generally, the longer the maturity, the more sensitively the price reacts to rate changes, and the higher the rate tends to be as compensation for that uncertainty.

3.4 Yield to Maturity (YTM)

Yield to maturity is the annualized total return you earn if you buy a bond at today's market price and hold it to maturity. Where the coupon rate is a fixed rate based on face value, YTM is the "real return" that also reflects the difference between the price you actually paid and the face value, plus the time remaining. Buy a bond below par and its YTM is higher than the coupon rate; buy above par and it is lower. When comparing the returns of different bonds, always look at YTM, not the coupon rate.

3.5 Duration

Duration is a sensitivity measure of how much a bond's price moves when rates change. Put simply, it tells you roughly "by what percentage the price moves for a 1 percentage-point change in rates." A bond with a duration of 5, for example, would fall about 5% if rates rise by 1 point and rise about 5% if rates fall by 1 point. Longer maturities tend to have larger durations, so long-term bonds swing far more with rate moves.

3.6 Credit Rating

A credit rating is a grade that rating agencies assign to an issuer's ability to repay principal and interest. Higher ratings imply lower default risk and usually carry lower yields, while lower-rated bonds offer higher yields to attract investors. Remember that this higher yield is not free; it is compensation for default risk.

4. The Inverse Relationship Between Rates and Prices - The Core Principle

This is the most important part of understanding bonds, and the most counterintuitive. When market rates rise, bond prices fall; when rates fall, bond prices rise. The two move in opposite directions, like a seesaw.

4.1 Why They Move in Opposite Directions

The key lies in the coupon being "fixed." Suppose you hold a bond paying a 3% coupon, and market rates jump to 5%. Newly issued bonds now pay 5%. Who would buy your 3% bond at full face value? No one. For your bond to sell, its price must drop so that the buyer ultimately earns a return (YTM) near 5%. That is why rising rates push existing bond prices down. Conversely, if rates fall to 1%, your 3% bond becomes precious; buyers appear even at a premium, and its price rises.

In one line: a bond's interest is fixed, but the world's interest rates change. The "price" adjusts to close that gap, and that is the essence of the rate-price inverse relationship.

4.2 Longer Duration Means a Bigger Shock

For the same rate move, the size of the price swing differs from bond to bond, and duration decides that size. A long-term bond with a high duration has many cash flows far in the future, so it reacts much more sensitively to rate changes. Hence, when rates rise, long-term bonds fall more; when rates drop, they gain more. For this reason, some investors shift toward shorter-duration bonds when they expect rates to rise, to cushion the blow. That said, this is just a general explanation, and accurately predicting the direction of rates is hard for anyone.

Recent records show that rates really do move. The Bank of Korea base rate was cut to 2.50% on May 29, 2025, then raised twice, to 2.75% on July 16, 2026 and to 3.00% on August 27, 2026 (Bank of Korea base rate history, checked 2026-09-29). This record says nothing about where rates go next, and the base rate is not the same number as the yields on bonds traded in the market. It simply confirms this section's premise: interest rates are not fixed.

5. Types of Bonds - It Depends on Who Issues Them

The nature of a bond's risk and return changes greatly depending on "who issued it." Knowing the main types makes it easier to choose a bond that fits your goals.

  • Government bonds: Issued by national governments, generally regarded as having the highest credit quality and very low default risk. Yields tend to be lower, but they offer good liquidity and high stability, serving as the benchmark of the bond market.
  • Corporate bonds: Issued by companies to raise funds. Because they rely on the issuing company's financial strength, they carry more default risk than government bonds and typically offer higher yields as compensation. The range is wide, from safe high-grade corporates to risky high-yield ("junk") bonds.
  • Municipal bonds: Issued by state and local governments to fund public projects such as roads, schools, and water systems. Backed by tax revenue they tend to be low-risk, but carry slightly more credit and liquidity risk than government bonds.
  • Central-bank instruments: In some markets, central banks issue short-term securities to manage the money supply. These function as monetary-policy tools and trade mostly at short maturities.
As you move from government bonds to corporates and on to low-rated high-yield bonds, the offered yield climbs, but so does default risk. "A high yield" can be a signal that "the risk is high," and the two should always be viewed together.

6. Starting With Bond ETFs and Funds - A Realistic Entry Point

Once you grasp the theory, how do you actually begin? You can buy individual bonds directly, but for many beginners the more realistic starting point is bond ETFs and bond mutual funds.

6.1 What a Bond ETF Is

A bond ETF holds many bonds in a single basket and lists it on an exchange to trade like a stock. Buying even one share gives you exposure spread across dozens to hundreds of bonds, lets you start small, and allows real-time trading for convenience. Where individual bonds often have large minimum trade sizes and require checking each one's details, a bond ETF is frequently cited as a route for beginners to gain diversification and convenience at once.

6.2 Individual Bonds vs Bond ETFs

The two have clear trade-offs. An individual bond offers the predictability of receiving promised interest and principal if held to maturity, but it is hard to diversify and concentrates the default risk of a single issuer. A bond ETF, by contrast, is broadly diversified and easy to trade, but has no fixed maturity, so its price keeps moving with the market and there is no certainty of repayment at maturity. Neither is simply better; it depends on your goals and time horizon.

6.3 What to Check When Choosing a Bond ETF

  • Tracked index: Check what kind of bonds make up the index it follows (government or corporate, and which maturity band).
  • Average duration: The longer the duration, the more the price swings with rates. See whether it matches your risk tolerance.
  • Expense ratio: Lower running costs mean a smaller long-term burden.
  • Credit quality of holdings: A higher average credit rating is safer but tends to come with lower yield.
One important misconception: a bond ETF is not a "deposit-like, principal-protected safe product." When rates rise, the bond ETF's price falls too. Long-duration bond ETFs in particular can post surprisingly large losses during periods of rising rates.

6.4 Rules to Check When Buying Bonds in Korea (Korea-specific)

The concepts are the same everywhere, but taxes and safeguards differ by country. Below are facts an individual in Korea should know before buying bonds or bond ETFs and funds, each checked against a primary source on 2026-09-29. These rules can change with legislation, so check again before buying.

  • Tax on interest: Bond interest is interest income, and Article 129 of Korea's Income Tax Act sets the withholding rate on ordinary interest income at 14%, shown as 15.4% once local income tax is included. The National Tax Service states that gains from trading securities such as stocks and bonds are not financial income and therefore are not subject to comprehensive financial-income taxation, which applies when annual financial income such as interest and dividends exceeds 20 million won. How distributions from bond ETFs and funds are taxed depends on the product's structure, so check the prospectus.
  • Not covered by deposit insurance: A bond is not a deposit. The Korea Deposit Insurance Corporation lists bonds issued by banks and securities firms, repurchase agreements (RP), and subordinated bonds of savings banks among non-protected products, and says it does not protect financial investment products such as funds whose payouts vary with performance. A bond's safety comes from the issuer's ability to repay, not from deposit insurance.
  • Retail government bonds for individuals: These savings-type Korean government bonds, which only individuals can buy, were first issued in June 2024. According to Korea's finance ministry, an individual with a dedicated account (one account per person) can buy from 100,000 won up to 200 million won per year, in 5-, 10-, and 20-year maturities. Principal and interest are paid in a lump sum at maturity, with an add-on spread over the coupon and annual compounding, and if held to maturity, interest on purchases of up to 200 million won in total is taxed separately at 14%. Early redemption can be requested one year after purchase, but then the add-on spread, compounding, and tax benefit do not apply. Unlike ordinary bonds that can be sold midway, the product is designed around holding to maturity.
  • You can check credit ratings yourself: The rating shown when you pick a corporate bond on a brokerage's over-the-counter bond screen is a rating agency's opinion, and the original rating reports are available through the Financial Supervisory Service's credit rating disclosures. A rating reflects the judgment at the time of assessment, so check the latest rating date before buying.

7. The Risks of Bond Investing - Seeing It in Balance

Bonds are often introduced as safer than stocks, but they are by no means free of risk. Here are three risks every beginner must know.

7.1 Credit Risk

This is the risk that the issuer fails to pay promised interest or principal, the so-called default risk, and the most direct cause of loss in bond investing. Government bonds carry very low credit risk, but the lower a corporate bond's rating, the greater its credit risk. Remember that a high yield is precisely the compensation for that risk.

7.2 Interest Rate Risk

This risk stems from the rate-price inverse relationship explained earlier. When rates rise, the market price of bonds you hold falls. Hold to maturity and you receive the promised principal, but if you must sell before maturity, or you hold a product with no maturity like a bond ETF, that price drop can turn into a real loss. The longer the duration, the greater this risk.

7.3 Liquidity Risk

This is the risk of not being able to sell at the price you want, when you want. Heavily traded government bonds have good liquidity, but some corporate or low-rated bonds trade thinly, so a forced quick sale may have to go through at an unfavorable price. Bond ETFs trade on an exchange and are relatively liquid, but if the underlying bonds are illiquid, prices can gap widely during market stress.

Beyond these, there is inflation risk, where rising prices erode interest income, and call risk, where the issuer buys the bond back before maturity. Underestimating risk by leaning on the "safe asset" image of bonds is exactly the attitude beginners should guard against most.

8. The Role of Bonds in a Portfolio

Many investors use bonds less as a standalone bet and more as a portfolio "cushion." Stocks and bonds often tend to move in different directions, so when stocks wobble, bonds can absorb part of the shock and lower overall volatility. This is why "diversifying between stocks and bonds" is so often cited as the classic starting point of asset allocation.

Bonds also generate a steady cash flow through their fixed interest. That is why bonds come up so often for investors approaching retirement who want less volatility, or those who want regular income. Still, stocks and bonds do not always move in opposite directions; in certain periods they fall together. Diversification is a device to reduce risk, not insurance that eliminates loss.

9. The Steps to Start Investing in Bonds - A Conceptual Outline

Here is the actual flow of getting started, at a conceptual level. This is not a recommendation of any specific brokerage or product, but general steps.

  1. Set goals and horizon: Decide whether the aim is steady income or cushioning portfolio volatility. Your time horizon becomes the basis for choosing duration.
  2. Direct vs indirect: Decide whether to buy individual bonds or diversify through bond ETFs and funds. Beginners typically start with ETFs and funds.
  3. Choose a risk level: Compare credit ratings and risks to decide between safety with government bonds or more yield with corporates.
  4. Check the key metrics: Review the tracked index, average duration, expense ratio, average credit rating, and YTM.
  5. Review and maintain: Reassess weightings as the rate environment changes, while keeping the discipline not to react to every short-term price move.

10. Common Misconceptions Among Beginners

Finally, here are the traps bond beginners most often fall into. This part connects most directly to actual losses.

10.1 "Bonds Are Always Safe"

This is the most common and most dangerous misconception. Bonds merely tend to be less volatile than stocks; they can still lose value through issuer default, rising rates, or a lack of liquidity. Low-rated bonds touting high yields are risky in exact proportion to that yield.

10.2 "Even If Rates Rise, Bonds Stay Put"

When rates rise, the market price of the bonds you hold falls. Hold to maturity and you get your principal, but a product with no maturity like a bond ETF turns that price drop straight into a valuation loss. Without understanding the rate-price inverse relationship, you can meet a surprising loss in a long-term bond ETF you bought believing it was safe.

10.3 "A High Coupon Means a Good Bond"

Choosing a bond by its coupon rate alone is risky. The real return must be measured by YTM, which also reflects the purchase price, and a high coupon is often paired with a low credit rating. Look at risk and return together, not just the headline interest rate.

11. A Self-Check Checklist for Beginners

Before your first bond investment, here are questions to ask yourself, organized as a checklist. Only when you can answer every item are you ready to take a step.

  • Goal: Do I want steady interest income, or do I want to cushion portfolio volatility?
  • Issuer: Who is the issuer of this bond, and at what credit rating?
  • YTM: Did I check the real return (YTM) rather than the coupon rate?
  • Duration: Did I estimate how much this bond (or ETF) would swing when rates change?
  • Risk: Do I understand and accept credit, rate, and liquidity risk?
  • Amount and horizon: Am I using money I can afford to lose, with a maturity and duration that fit my time horizon?
This checklist is not a tool that hands you answers; it is a tool that makes you ask questions. If filling in the answers reveals something you do not know, that is exactly the point at which to study more or consult a professional.

12. Conclusion - Learn the Instinct of the Lender

Bond investing is ultimately about judging "to whom, how much, at what interest, and for how long" you will lend. If stocks are the perspective of a partner betting on a company's growth, bonds are the perspective of a creditor weighing whether a promise will be kept. The face value, coupon, maturity, YTM, duration, and credit rating covered here, along with the inverse relationship between rates and prices, are all tools to support that judgment.

Three suggestions to close. First, understand the terms precisely. Do not confuse coupon rate with YTM, and use duration to gauge interest rate risk. Second, do not underestimate risk by leaning on the "safe" image. Bonds clearly carry credit, rate, and liquidity risk. Third, begin with diversified bond ETFs and funds, but always check the tracked index, duration, and credit quality. To repeat: this article is for educational purposes only and is not investment advice. All investing carries the risk of losing principal and interest, every decision and its responsibility rest with the investor, and you should consult a professional before any actual decision.
