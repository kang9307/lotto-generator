Why RAG Is Simple and Still Hard - The Structure Is Two Lines, the Problem Is Everything Around It

Retrieve and paste is the whole of it. The hard parts are what to cut and how, and what the system should say when it finds nothing

Aug 27, 2026 Dev/Infra

The Structure Is the Simple Part. Everything Around It Is Not

If you built a demo in three days after hearing that RAG is just retrieve-and-paste, and a month later the answers are still subtly wrong, the retriever is usually not the problem. The cause is not one badly ranked chunk but the reason that chunk was cut and indexed the way it was. And that reason is written down nowhere in the code.

A recent piece in the Lighthouse Newsletter, RAG is Simpler Than You Think, drew discussion on GeekNews. Its argument runs like this. Many teams bolt on embeddings and a vector index from day one, when most problems dissolve with full-text search plus query rewriting. The author sorts retrieval systems into six shapes and estimates that roughly 60 percent need only full-text search and rewriting, 25 percent need a hybrid approach, 10 percent need full pre-embedding, and only the remaining 5 percent call for something custom. The closing warning is against building a 5 percent solution for a 60 percent problem.

I agree with half of it. The structure is indeed simple. But that simplicity does not come from how you pick a retrieval method. What is genuinely simple is the skeleton of RAG, and what is hard sits outside the skeleton. What goes in, where it gets cut, and what gets said when nothing is found. Switch the retriever from full-text to vector search and all three of those survive the change untouched.

The Minimal Structure - Find Pieces by the Question, Paste Them Into the Prompt

A language model carries only the parameters frozen at the end of training. Your internal wiki is not in there, neither is the policy revised yesterday, nor the support ticket that arrived this morning. To stop it from speaking about things it does not have, you fetch the raw material for the answer from outside before the answer is written.

So there are only three moves. Turn the question into something searchable, pull back a handful of ranked candidate pieces, and concatenate instructions, pieces, and question for the model. The structure Lewis and colleagues named in 2020 in Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks is ultimately this. The idea is to use frozen parametric memory alongside memory that lives outside, and the paper reported that the result is more specific and more factual language.

What matters is that no particular store appears anywhere in that definition. A few lines that stringify query results already constitute RAG. A vector index is not a required component of the structure but one implementation of the second move.

The reason newcomers find RAG hard is vocabulary, not architecture. Chunking, embeddings, reranking, hybrid, query rewriting are all names for options inside the first and second moves, not separate layers. That is genuinely all of it, and it is also exactly as far as the original article's claim of simplicity reaches.

So Why Is It Hard - The Difficulty Is Before and After Retrieval

Retrieval algorithms have already been built by other people. Term-frequency ranking or approximate nearest neighbors, you call a library and you are done. Projects almost never collapse there. They collapse on three questions no library answers for you. Where do we cut the documents, what do we index, and how do we know that what came back is right.

What those three share is that all of them are domain judgments. Whether to cut a contract by clause or by sub-clause, and whether to keep a retired policy document in the index, is not something retrieval technology decides. And when those judgments are wrong, the symptoms are outwardly indistinguishable from a bad retriever. That is why so many teams spend their time swapping retrievers.

Only the last row is a swappable part. The axis the original article works on, full-text versus embeddings, is that single row. This does not make choosing well on that row pointless. It means that choosing only that row leaves the three rows above it exactly as they were.

What to Index - The Second Question Ends Here

Index scope is a data decision, not a technical one. Whether to store only the conclusions of a meeting or the whole transcript, whether to flatten tables inside attachments into text, whether drafts sit alongside final versions. The most dangerous item in that list is the expired document. A retriever does not distinguish last month's retired rule from this month's rule. If anything, because the two look alike, they surface side by side near the top.

So there is a minimum set of facts worth storing next to every chunk at index time.

  • Source document and original location - without this, citing evidence in an answer cannot even begin
  • Validity period and revision date - the basis for filtering retired copies or weighting the newest one
  • Access permission - if retrieval does not know permissions, RAG becomes the highest-performing data leak path in the organization
  • Document type - the same sentence carries different weight in a rule, a case note, or a draft

These four are the hardest things to bolt on later. Leaving even an empty slot for them in the first design cuts the later cost substantially.

Chunking Decides the Outcome - Where You Draw the Boundary

Chunking is cutting documents into retrieval units. But the unit you cut is also the unit of evidence the model will see. However accurate retrieval is, it means nothing if the chunk itself does not contain the answer. Chunking is therefore not preprocessing but the design step that sets the ceiling on answer quality.

The symptoms at the two extremes are opposites.

  • Cut small and a chunk loses its own context - the 「in this case」 of 「in this case an exception applies」 stays in the previous chunk
  • Cut small and a table's header row separates from its data rows, leaving chunks that are bare numbers
  • Cut small and conditions split from conclusions, producing chunks that read as the opposite of their meaning
  • Cut large and unrelated prose mixes into one chunk, blurring its meaning vector into an average
  • Cut large and even a few top hits eat the context window quickly
  • Cut large and users struggle to see which sentence the citation actually refers to

So this is not the problem of choosing a size number but of deciding where the boundary goes. Good boundaries usually already exist in the document. Clauses and sub-clauses, heading hierarchy, a single table, one FAQ entry. Slicing mechanically by character count is bad not because the size is bad but because it ignores those boundaries.

Two corrections are common. One is overlap. Pushing the tail of each chunk into the head of the next reduces loss near boundaries, but storage and retrieval costs rise and near-duplicate chunks fill the top of the ranking together. The other is prepending the parent heading to each chunk as a header. It makes a chunk able to say on its own which document and which section it belongs to, and it costs so little that it is worth trying first.

One more thing. If document types are mixed, a single chunking rule will not cover them. Code, tables, and long prose all have different natural boundaries.

What Embedding Search Actually Does - It Finds Similar, Not Correct

Embeddings turn text into vectors and retrieval measures distance between those vectors. So what comes back is the chunk closest in meaning, not the chunk that answers the question. Most of the time those two coincide, which is why it looks like it works. Knowing where they stop coinciding is the more useful thing in practice.

  • Negation - refunds are available and refunds are not available sit extremely close in the meaning space
  • Identifiers and codes - product codes and error numbers are strings never seen in training, so vectors carry no distinguishing features
  • Numbers and dates - thirty dollars and three hundred dollars, 2024 and 2026, point in nearly the same direction as vectors
  • Antonym pairs - approved and rejected, new and cancelled appear in the same contexts and separate weakly

That is precisely where lexical matching is strong. The original article concludes that exact keyword matching beats semantic understanding for proprietary terms, and the conclusion follows straight from first principles. If the word itself is right there, there is no reason to detour through meaning.

Where lexical matching fails is equally clear. It fails when the words users type differ from the words the documents use. Users write cancel and the contract says termination of agreement. Users write dead and the incident report says unresponsive. Closing that vocabulary gap is the value embeddings genuinely earn.

The two are not competitors but tools with opposite failure modes. That is why the two-stage shape of casting wide and choosing narrow is so common. Cheap retrieval grabs dozens of candidates and only the top few get reordered by the heavier method, which is the same shape the original article recommends. Adding query rewriting on top converts conversational questions into something closer to search terms and closes the vocabulary gap once more. The price is one extra model call in the latency budget, and when rewriting goes wrong the cause is now spread across two stages, which makes debugging harder. There is also a branch that changes the shape of the knowledge itself instead of touching the retriever, and that approach and its benchmarks are covered separately in Retrieval as Reasoning - What the LLM-Wiki vs RAG Benchmarks Show.

The Real Problem Is When Nothing Is Found - Manufacturing I Do Not Know Is Hard

RAG works acceptably for anyone when retrieval succeeds. The character of a system is settled at the moment retrieval fails. And a language model never returns empty-handed. Sentences come out even with no evidence, and they come out in exactly the same tone as when evidence existed. From the user's side there is no way to tell the two apart.

The worse case is when retrieval did return something and it is irrelevant. Irrelevant context is more dangerous than empty context. The model tries to treat what it was given as evidence, so out comes an answer that diligently interprets the wrong chunk. Such answers even arrive with citations attached, which makes them look more trustworthy rather than less.

So I do not know does not appear by itself. It has to be manufactured. Four controls are used, in ascending order of strength.

  • Retrieval score floor - if the top chunk scores below threshold, skip the model call and answer immediately that nothing was found. The simplest control and the most effective
  • Fixed output shape - pin down in the prompt that the answer must be a plain statement of absence when the supplied material lacks it. Works with no evidence, weak with irrelevant evidence
  • Mandatory citation - require every sentence to mark which chunk it came from, then filter out unmarked sentences downstream
  • Post-generation check - verify with a separate call that each claim in the answer really appears in the supplied chunks. Expensive, but it pays in domains where a wrong answer costs a lot

Whatever control you pick, the price is the same. A system that often says it does not know looks like a system that cannot answer. The threshold is a dial that trades accuracy against answer rate, and where it sits is decided by the domain. Where a wrong answer costs more than no answer, meaning billing, contracts, and regulated rules, turn the threshold up. For exploratory question answering or drafting assistance it can come down.

If I leave one judgment behind, it is that this dial is the second thing to attach when building a new RAG system. The first is the evaluation set in the next section.

Where Evaluation Belongs - Retrieval Quality and Answer Quality Are Separate

When a wrong-answer report arrives, the cause is one of two things. Either the needed chunk was not retrieved, or it was retrieved and the model read it badly. Measure both with a single satisfaction metric and there is no way to know which side to fix.

Three things to watch on the retrieval side.

  • Top-k recall - did the chunk containing the answer land inside the top k. This is the first number to look at
  • Rank of the correct chunk - it came back, but in what position. A low rank leaves room for reranking or query rewriting
  • Actual usefulness of top k - how many of the k retrieved were useful to the answer. If it is low, the chunk count can come down

Three on the generation side.

  • Groundedness - is every claim in the answer present in the supplied chunks. A sentence from outside is a dangerous sentence
  • Answer relevance - faithful to the evidence, but did it answer what was actually asked. These two move independently
  • Abstention accuracy - did it say no for questions with no answer, and did it avoid abstaining on questions that had one

Building a minimum evaluation set is lighter than it sounds. Collect 50 to 100 real user questions and have a person mark, for each question, the document chunk that contains the answer. It does not need to be perfect. All it has to tell you is whether a chunking change made things better. Without that set, every improvement becomes a comparison of impressions. You get stuck in a loop of changing chunk size, throwing a few questions at it, and deciding it feels better.

The set must deliberately include questions the documents cannot answer. Abstention is measurable only when those exist. Many systems collapse right there, and without those items in the set they ship without anyone noticing the collapse.

Where Cost and Latency Actually Come From

Three axes generally write the bill.

  • Chunks placed in the prompt - going from 5 to 20 raises input tokens accordingly, and that increase repeats on every single request
  • The reranking stage - rescoring dozens of candidates is often heavier than the retrieval itself
  • Re-indexing - every document update means re-embedding, and swapping the embedding model means rebuilding everything

Chunk count is the axis most often misread. It feels as if accuracy should rise with volume, and it does not. Liu and colleagues reported in 2023 in Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts that performance is highest when relevant information sits at the beginning or end of the input and degrades noticeably when it sits in the middle, and the same tendency appeared in models designed for long contexts. Adding more chunks does not substitute for choosing chunks well.

Re-indexing cost is the item most often missing from an initial design. An index looks like an asset you build once, but in an organization whose documents change often it is effectively a standing operational cost.

For latency, cutting chunk count first, then shrinking the reranking candidate pool, then adding a cache is a sound order. In a service where the same questions repeat, a query cache pays off more than any other optimization. The original article puts full-text latency under 10 milliseconds, embedding search in the 200 to 500 millisecond range, and a query rewriting call at roughly 0.001 dollars per request. The absolute numbers will move with data volume and infrastructure, but the direction, that lexical retrieval is lighter than vector retrieval, falls straight out of how the two methods work.

When RAG Is Not the Answer

RAG is a way to supply knowledge, not a way to change behavior. Most misapplications split along that line.

  • When the corpus is small enough to fit entirely - for a 30-page internal policy, pasting all of it is more accurate and easier to debug. Position bias still caps how far this scales
  • When what you need is format, tone, or a classification standard - that is the territory of examples and instructions, or fine-tuning, not retrieval. Trying to fix writing style through retrieval usually fails
  • When the answer lives in system state rather than documents - stock levels, order status, and balances are query results, not chunks. Fetching real values through tool calls is the right shape, and turning them into indexed documents guarantees stale answers
  • When the answer is a computation - totals, durations, and conversions do not arrive by retrieval. However well you rank evidence, arithmetic is a separate problem
  • When the question space is narrow and answers are fixed - a well-maintained response matcher is more accurate and far cheaper

The places where RAG clearly fits are equally clear. Documents are numerous, they change often, questions are open-ended, and answers must show their evidence. That last condition on its own settles the decision, because knowledge injected by fine-tuning cannot tell you which document and which clause an answer came from. If citation is a requirement of the work, there is effectively one option.

The Order to Start In

Follow the order and each step proves the need for the next one. Skip it and you build the complicated version first, then never learn whether it was needed.

  1. Collect 30 to 50 questions the system must answer from real users. Without these, the remaining decisions have no reference point
  2. Mark the ones whose answers are not in the documents. That becomes your abstention test set as is
  3. Cut documents at natural boundaries. Tuning character counts can wait
  4. Start with the simplest retrieval available. Whatever full-text search you already have is enough
  5. Measure top-k recall. If it is low here, changing models will not improve answers
  6. Attach the score floor and the not-found response. This comes before work on accuracy
  7. Then layer on embeddings, reranking, and query rewriting one at a time, remeasuring with the same set after each

The point of the order is to produce evidence that complexity is needed before adding complexity. That is the same spirit as the original article's rule about not growing the structure until data proves the need, and on that part there is no disagreement.

The claim that the hard part is outside retrieval is not entirely bad news. Outside is largely territory a person who knows the domain can judge, and it lasts far longer than a library choice. Retrieval methods will have changed into something else in two years, but the judgment about where documents should be cut, the decision about what to say when nothing is found, and the evaluation set will all still be in use. That makes it fairly clear where investment belongs.

What Could Not Be Confirmed

  • The 60-25-10-5 distribution in the original article is presented as the author's experiential estimate, with no sample or derivation method published. Useful as a rough sense of direction, but not a figure to cite as evidence
  • The latency numbers and per-request cost in the article likewise come without stated measurement conditions. Different data volumes and infrastructure could shift them by an order of magnitude
  • General optimal values for chunk size and overlap ratio vary by document type and question type, so there is no basis for a universal recommendation
  • The right setting for an abstention threshold likewise depends on the per-domain cost of a wrong answer and cannot be stated as a number

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a vector database to start with RAG?

No. Nothing in the definition of RAG requires a vector index. If full-text search already exists in your system, measuring top-k recall with it first is the right order. If that number is low, the cause is more likely chunking or index scope than the retrieval method, and adding a vector index at that point buries the problem one layer deeper.

What is the right chunk size?

There is no general answer, and boundaries matter more than size. Using the boundaries a document already has, such as clauses, heading levels, a single table, or one FAQ entry, is almost always better than slicing by character count.

Is fixing the prompt enough to make a model say it does not know?

Prompting alone is not enough. It works reasonably well when no context is supplied, but poorly when retrieval returned something irrelevant. The stronger control is a score floor in the retrieval stage, which skips the model call entirely and answers that nothing was found when the top chunk falls below threshold.

Retrieval looks fine but answers are still wrong. Where do I look?

If the correct chunk is inside top-k and the answer is wrong, the cause is on the generation side. Too many chunks may have buried the evidence, ordering may have pushed the important part into the middle of the input, or the prompt may permit inference beyond the evidence. If the correct chunk is not in top-k at all, swapping models changes nothing.

Context windows are long now. Why not just paste the whole corpus?

If the corpus is small enough to fit, that is more accurate and easier to debug. Length does not improve things indefinitely, though, and information placed in the middle of the input is reported to be used poorly. Once documents are numerous and change often, selecting chunks stays the better option.
