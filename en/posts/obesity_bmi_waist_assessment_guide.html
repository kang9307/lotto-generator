Where BMI Alone Gets It Wrong - Reading Waist Circumference Alongside It

Why Asian thresholds sit lower than WHO ones, why waist circumference is a separate measurement rather than a redundant one, why Japan reverses the male and female cut-offs, and how a few centimetres of tape placement change the verdict - a guide to reading the two numbers together

2026-08-07 Health & Wellness
Before we start: this article is general health information about how to read obesity-related measurements. It does not replace medical diagnosis, prescription or treatment. Every number below is a classification threshold set by a professional society or public agency, not a target proposed for any individual. This article does not encourage weight loss and does not steer anyone toward a particular weight or body shape. What your numbers mean and what to do next belongs to a clinician who knows your history and medications. Adolescents in growth, people who are pregnant or breastfeeding, older adults, and anyone under treatment for a chronic condition cannot apply general adult thresholds directly.

Introduction: the same number gets a different verdict abroad

Suppose a checkup report shows a BMI of 24.5. The clinic leaflet in Seoul calls it pre-obesity. A calculator hosted in the United States calls the same figure normal. Neither one is broken. They are applying different tables, and both are correct inside their own system.

Take one step further and the problem grows. BMI is computed from exactly two values, weight and height. Nothing in the formula distinguishes weight that comes from muscle from weight that comes from fat, or fat spread evenly across the limbs from fat packed inside the abdomen. Two people with the same BMI can carry very different metabolic risk.

This article is not about how to calculate BMI. A BMI calculator settles that in a second. The hard part comes afterwards: which table the number belongs to, where judging by that number alone goes wrong, and how to overlay a second number, waist circumference, on top of it. Every threshold quoted below is current as of August 2026, and thresholds move when societies and governments revise their guidelines.

1. What BMI measures, and what it cannot

1.1 An index with only two inputs

BMI is body weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. At 1.70 m and 70 kg, that is 70 ÷ (1.70 × 1.70) ≈ 24.2. The calculation ends there.

That simplicity is the point. It needs nothing but a scale and a stadiometer, it costs nothing, and it does not swing much between observers. That is why it appears in national statistics everywhere. For describing how a population is distributed and tracking change over time, nothing else is as cheap and as reproducible.

The trouble is that the strength is also the limit. When only two values go in, everything else stays outside. Body fat percentage, where the fat sits, muscle mass, frame size and fluid status never enter the equation at all.

1.2 The societies say so themselves

This limitation is written into the guideline documents. It is part of the guidance, not a criticism of it.

  • The Korean Society for the Study of Obesity states that measuring BMI alone has limitations in accurately reflecting body fat mass, especially in athletes or those with muscle loss.
  • The same guidance says classification should account for age, sex, race, body fluid status and muscle mass.
  • Waist circumference is presented as the measurement that corrects errors in BMI in patients with low muscle mass, in older adults, and in those with weight-loss-inducing diseases.
  • The clinical obesity framework published by an international commission in January 2025 points the same way: do not settle the question on BMI alone, and pair it with an anthropometric measure that reflects adiposity.

The summary is straightforward. BMI was designed as a first-pass screening index. It was never designed to deliver a final verdict on an individual. Reading the checkup number that way uses the tool outside its design.

2. Four situations where BMI alone misleads

BMI does not diverge from reality at random. The pattern is predictable, including the direction of the error.

2.1 Plenty of muscle - risk overstated

  • Muscle is denser than fat. Someone with a long training history, or an athlete in a strength-based sport, can land in an obesity band while carrying little body fat.
  • Here BMI reports more risk than exists. It is the first example societies reach for when they list the limits of the index.
  • If waist circumference sits below the threshold in this case, that points toward lean mass rather than fat accumulation. It is a signal, not a conclusion, and body composition assessment and a clinical visit settle it.

2.2 Low muscle with abdominal fat - risk understated

  • This is the opposite error and, in practice, the more common one. When body weight is in the normal range but muscle mass is low and fat is concentrated around the abdomen, BMI quietly reads normal.
  • Korea's Ministry of Health and Welfare has issued public guidance built on exactly this combination, telling people at a normal weight to check waist circumference first.
  • Reading BMI alone and stopping there means the check never happens. This is the practical reason the article insists on a second number.

2.3 Body composition shifting with age

  • Muscle mass tends to decline and fat proportion to rise with age. Body weight can hold steady while the composition behind it changes.
  • BMI then displays the same figure it displayed twenty years earlier, but the figure no longer describes the same thing.
  • This is the background to waist circumference being named as the correction for BMI error in older adults.

2.4 Increased body fluid

  • With oedema, ascites, or fluid retention driven by a condition or a medication, body weight stops reflecting fat mass at all.
  • That is why fluid status appears explicitly on the list of factors to consider during classification.
  • In that situation the answer is not to read a table but to have the underlying cause assessed clinically.
The error runs both ways

Discussions of BMI usually stop at the muscular-athlete example. The case that actually gets missed sits on the other side: a BMI inside the normal range that nobody looks at twice. BMI can overstate risk and it can understate it. Without a second measurement, you cannot even tell which direction it has gone.

3. National and international thresholds were never the same

This is where online calculators start disagreeing with each other. Three tables, side by side.

BMI (kg/m²) Korea - KSSO WHO international WHO Asia-Pacific
Below 18.5 Underweight Underweight Underweight
18.5 - 22.9 Normal Normal (18.5 - 24.9) Normal
23.0 - 24.9 Pre-obesity (overweight) Overweight
25.0 - 29.9 Class 1 obesity Overweight (pre-obesity) Obesity (25 and above)
30.0 - 34.9 Class 2 obesity Obesity class 1
35.0 - 39.9 Class 3 obesity (35 and above) Obesity class 2
40.0 and above Obesity class 3

One line from the table is enough. A person with a BMI of 26 has class 1 obesity under Korean criteria and is overweight under WHO international criteria. The body is unchanged; the verdict moves a full band. The same goes for 23.5, which is pre-obesity in Korea and comfortably inside the normal range internationally. The US CDC, for reference, uses the international layout: overweight from 25, class 1 obesity from 30, class 2 from 35, class 3 from 40.

Why do Asian populations use lower lines? Because risk arrives earlier. At an identical BMI, people of Asian descent tend to carry a higher body fat percentage and more upper-body fat, and obesity-related disease risk begins climbing at a lower BMI. The WHO Western Pacific Region issued a separate Asia-Pacific classification in 2000 for that reason, and the American Diabetes Association shifted its screening trigger in 2015 so that adults of Asian ancestry are considered for diabetes screening from a BMI of 23 rather than 25.

The Korean threshold has not gone unchallenged either. A proposal to raise the diagnostic line from 25 to 27 has been floated, and the Korean Society for the Study of Obesity concluded that it was more appropriate to anchor the criteria to the onset of comorbidities rather than to mortality, and kept 25 in place. As of August 2026 the left-hand column above is what applies in Korea.

One thing to check when you use a calculator

Every calculator returns the same arithmetic. What differs is the label printed underneath. Most international services attach WHO wording, so a figure that counts as pre-obesity under Asian criteria can come back marked normal. Look past the number to the line stating which standard was applied. The BMI calculator on this site reports results against the bands used in Korea.

4. Why waist circumference is needed - where the fat sits

4.1 The same amount of fat means different things in different places

Body fat divides broadly into subcutaneous fat under the skin and visceral fat packed between the organs inside the abdominal cavity. Two people can carry the same total and differ in what that total means metabolically.

  • Visceral fat sits inside the abdomen, so neither body weight nor BMI reveals whether it is there. It moves independently of the visible total.
  • Waist circumference is used as a measure that tracks visceral fat, and it is regarded as predicting cardiovascular risk better than BMI in some respects.
  • It needs one tape measure and no special equipment. Covering the weak point of BMI at almost no added cost is why guidelines are built around using the two together.
  • Japan went further and worked backwards from a visceral fat area of 100 cm² to derive the waist circumference used as its national screening line. The logic of treating waist as a proxy for visceral fat is written into the design of that threshold.

4.2 Where the abdominal obesity line sits

The Korean Society for the Study of Obesity defines abdominal obesity as a waist circumference of 90 cm or more in men and 85 cm or more in women, presented as the point where the prevalence of metabolic disease starts to rise. In inches that is roughly 35.4 and 33.5.

The point worth holding onto is that this is a classification line, not a target. Nothing changes abruptly in the body between 89 cm and 91 cm. A single line is drawn where risk begins to separate meaningfully in population data, and lines like that get revised.

5. Thresholds differ by country, and Japan reverses the sexes

Here is why you cannot lift a threshold table off a foreign website and apply it to yourself. Waist criteria vary by country, and the male-female relationship itself flips.

Standard Men Women Measurement site Notes
Korea - KSSO 90 cm and above 85 cm and above Midpoint between lowest rib and iliac crest Included in the national health checkup measurement panel
Japan - specific health checkup (metabolic syndrome) 85 cm and above 90 cm and above Umbilical level Derived to correspond to a visceral fat area of 100 cm². Sexes reversed relative to Korea
China - WS/T 428-2013 90 cm and above 85 cm and above Standard measurement site Men 85-90 and women 80-85 are classed as pre-central obesity
WHO - Europid thresholds 94 cm and above / 102 cm and above 80 cm and above / 88 cm and above Midpoint between lowest rib and iliac crest The first value marks increased risk, the second substantially increased risk

The Japanese row stands out. The female threshold is higher than the male one. That is not a typo. Japan derived its cut-offs by working backwards from the waist circumference corresponding to a visceral fat area of 100 cm² in each sex, and women tend to carry more subcutaneous fat at the same visceral fat area, so the corresponding waist value comes out larger. The reversal falls out of the fact that the starting point was visceral fat area rather than waist circumference itself.

  • Always establish whose standard a line belongs to. A figure of 85 cm for a man is the abdominal obesity threshold in Japan and comfortably below the line in Korea.
  • Apply WHO Europid thresholds to an Asian population and a large share falls below the line. Separate criteria exist precisely because body shape and fat distribution differ.
  • A threshold table and a measurement site travel as a pair. Reading a Korean table with a navel-level measurement can shift the verdict.

6. A few centimetres of tape placement change the answer

Memorising thresholds precisely is worthless if the measurement wanders. Waist circumference is easy to move by several centimetres between attempts.

6.1 Where to place the tape

WHO and the Korean Society for the Study of Obesity specify the midpoint between the lowermost rib and the top of the iliac crest. The landmark is bone, not the navel, and that matters: navel position varies between people and moves within the same person with posture and body shape.

As noted above, Japan's specific health checkup measures at umbilical level instead. The two sites can differ by several centimetres in the same person. Decide which table you are reading, then measure at the site that table specifies.

6.2 Posture, breathing and timing

  • Stance: feet about 25-30 cm apart, body weight distributed evenly between them.
  • Breathing: measure after breathing out comfortably. Holding an inhale or pulling the stomach in changes the value.
  • Tape tension: loose enough that it does not compress the skin. Pulling it tight reads smaller than reality.
  • Recording: to the nearest 0.1 cm. If you want a trend, do not round.
  • Fixed timing: abdominal distension after eating shifts the value. Choose one consistent moment, such as after waking and using the bathroom, and measure under the same conditions every time.

6.3 Common mistakes that ruin the number

  1. Pulling the stomach in - the most frequent one. The result reads smaller than reality.
  2. Measuring while holding a breath in - the ribcage lifts and the circumference changes.
  3. Measuring at the narrowest point of the torso - that is not the specified site. It is the tailoring measurement, not the clinical one.
  4. Measuring at the trouser waistband - waistbands vary by garment and by body shape.
  5. Measuring over thick clothing - bare skin or a thin layer only.
  6. Letting the tape sit at an angle - it has to stay parallel to the floor. A tape that dips at the back reads larger.
  7. Measuring right after a meal or a large drink - that day's digestion goes straight into the number.
  8. Measuring at a different time and a different site each round - the records then cannot show a trend at all.

7. Reading the two numbers as a pair

BMI and waist circumference do not substitute for one another. Overlaid, they show which direction to look next.

Combination What it suggests What a clinician reads alongside it
Normal BMI + waist below threshold Both measures inside range. What neither measure captures remains a separate question Other checkup values and the trend across years
Normal BMI + waist above threshold Normal body weight with fat concentrated abdominally. The state BMI alone would never have flagged Blood pressure, fasting glucose, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol
BMI in obesity band + waist below threshold Includes cases driven by lean mass. The cell where BMI alone most often misreads Body composition assessment, training history, other checkup values
BMI in obesity band + waist above threshold Both measures pointing the same way Metabolic markers generally and any existing comorbidity

The way to read the table is through the third column, not the labels. These combinations are signposts for what to check next, not diagnoses. A waist above the line does not establish any condition, and a normal BMI does not establish that nothing is wrong. They acquire meaning next to blood pressure, glucose and lipid values. The diagnostic frame that assembles those is metabolic syndrome, and waist circumference enters it as one of the components.

8. How far the secondary measures can be trusted

Several other measures come up alongside BMI and waist circumference. Here is which body proposes which line, and what gets in the way in practice.

Measure Proposed threshold Source Practical catch
Waist-hip ratio (WHR) Above 0.90 in men / above 0.85 in women WHO expert consultation report (2008) Requires two measurements, and hip circumference is the less reproducible of the pair
Waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) 0.4-0.49 healthy / 0.5-0.59 increased central adiposity / 0.6 and above high NICE guidance, United Kingdom Disputed for adolescents, and awkward at extremes of height
Visceral fat area 100 cm² and above The value Japan used to derive its waist thresholds Needs imaging or dedicated equipment, so it is not a self-measurement
Body fat percentage Published values differ by organisation - Home impedance scales swing with hydration, making absolute comparison unreliable
  • Waist-to-height ratio wins on simplicity. Divide waist by height and you are done, and it compresses into one sentence: keep your waist under half your height. That line comes from UK guidance, though, while classification in Korea still runs on absolute waist circumference.
  • Waist-hip ratio has a long history but doubles the measurement burden. The measure included in Korea's national checkup panel is waist circumference.
  • Piling on measures does not sharpen the verdict by itself. Waist-hip ratio has earned a partial exception, though: as section 11 shows, there are cells where it disagrees with the BMI band more often than waist circumference does, and the evidence that it repays the extra measurement is accumulating. Even there the principle holds. Measuring one well-defined index accurately beats collecting several that wobble.

9. Where these numbers already sit in a national checkup

Both measures are already being taken every year in Korea. The measurement panel of the national health insurance checkup includes height, weight, waist circumference and obesity index. Nothing extra has to be requested.

  • The numbers are already printed on the report. What usually goes wrong is not that they were skipped but that nobody reads that box.
  • More useful than any single year is the trend across years. Several years of values taken at the same facility in a comparable way is already a usable dataset.
  • Waist circumference is meant to pair with the rest of the report. Fasting glucose, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol and blood pressure have to be present for the interpretation to close.
  • How to read the report as a whole is laid out item by item in reading health checkup results.

10. The criteria themselves are moving - clinical and preclinical obesity

In January 2025 an international commission proposed rebuilding the definition and diagnostic criteria of obesity. It has not been transposed into national guidelines as such, but it will keep appearing in future revision debates.

  • The core move is a split between clinical obesity and preclinical obesity. The former is a state in which excess adiposity has already produced organ dysfunction or limits on daily activities; the latter is excess adiposity without those manifestations but with raised future risk.
  • Diagnosis runs in two stages. First an anthropometric step confirms excess adiposity, then a clinical step establishes whether organ dysfunction or activity limitation is present.
  • At the anthropometric step the commission recommends moving beyond BMI alone and pairing it with waist circumference, waist-hip ratio or waist-to-height ratio. That is exactly the concern behind reading two numbers together.
  • The proposal has not yet been written into national practice guidelines. As of August 2026, classification in Korea follows the Korean Society for the Study of Obesity criteria.

The direction of travel is away from settling the question on BMI alone. That does not mean BMI is being discarded. Its place as a cheap, reproducible first-pass screen stays; what changes is that the job of assessing an individual gets shared with other measures.

11. The study that took the comparison head-on

Everything so far has been about what the guidelines recommend. That leaves the obvious question of whether anyone has gone at the data directly and asked which measure sorts people better. A paper released online on 11 August 2026 in the Journal of the American College of Cardiology, volume 88 issue 6, takes that question on. It is titled Risk Reclassification Beyond BMI by Waist Circumference and Waist-to-Hip Ratio Across 9 Cardiovascular Outcomes, a pooled analysis of 15 NIH and NHLBI cohorts assembled as the Cross-Cohort Collaboration. First author Zeina A. Dardari; corresponding author Michael J. Blaha of the Johns Hopkins Ciccarone Center.

  • Scale: 259,388 adults with no coronary heart disease at baseline, median follow-up 20.0 years.
  • Nine outcomes: myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, total coronary heart disease, total cardiovascular disease, coronary heart disease mortality, cardiovascular mortality and all-cause mortality.
  • What it examined: how many people land in a different box when a population already split by BMI category is re-sorted by waist circumference and waist-hip ratio (WHR), and how later event rates differed for the people who moved.

What stands out first is that disagreement between the two measures is not the exception. The four-box combination set out in section 7 turns out to hold these shares of an actual population.

BMI band Re-sorted by waist circumference Re-sorted by WHR
Normal weight waist on the high side 5% WHR on the high side 18%
Overweight waist on the high side 39% WHR on the high side 40%
Obesity waist on the low side 9% WHR on the low side 45%

In the overweight band four people in ten also carry central adiposity, and in the obesity band nearly half land on the low side once WHR is the yardstick. People in the normal or overweight bands whose waist-based measures ran high showed 15% to 50% higher risk across most of the nine outcomes. The editorial accompanying it in the same issue is titled Tipping the Scale Away From Body Mass Index? for that reason.

What the study showed, and what it did not

What it showed is reclassification: re-sort a BMI-defined population by a waist-based measure and groups with different later risk pull apart. What it did not show is causation. This is a pooled analysis of observational cohorts already collected, not a trial that randomly assigned participants and then reduced one group's waist circumference. The sentence "shrink your waist and your risk falls by that much" does not come out of this study. That is a question for intervention trials, and this study is not in that seat. Differences observed here can carry unmeasured or unadjusted factors along with them.

  • The result does not move any national threshold. As of August 2026 classification in Korea still follows Korean Society for the Study of Obesity criteria.
  • The pooled cohorts are largely American. What adjustment would be needed before carrying the finding to Asian populations falls outside the study's scope.
  • None of this article's practical advice changes. Large reclassification supports the case for recording both numbers under fixed conditions; it does not hand anyone a new target value.

12. What this article could not confirm

The dangerous failure in an article about thresholds is an unverified number sitting among verified ones. The following could not be pinned down, and so no figures for them appear in the body text.

  • The funding source of the JACC paper in section 11 could not be confirmed. The press materials carry no statement of it and the article itself is public only as far as the abstract, so the sponsor and the declared interests could not be checked against the original. Unconfirmed, it does not get written into this article.
  • Whether an intervention that lowers a waist-based measure actually lowers risk is not covered here. The section 11 study demonstrated reclassification in observational data; the effect of intervening is a question for separate trials.
  • Whether the Korean Society for the Study of Obesity issues its own domestic thresholds for waist-hip ratio or waist-to-height ratio could not be confirmed from the published guideline summaries. The figures quoted above come from WHO and from NICE in the UK.
  • Which visceral fat area the Korean waist thresholds were derived against could not be confirmed. The country confirmed to have used 100 cm² explicitly in threshold design is Japan.
  • Whether China's standard measurement site is identical to the WHO landmark could not be verified against the original specification, so the table says only standard measurement site.
  • Separate criteria for children and adolescents, pregnancy, and older adults fall outside this article. Adult thresholds cannot be transferred to them.
  • Thresholds get revised. Everything here is current as of August 2026 and will change as societies and governments update their guidance.

Frequently asked questions

Q1. Does a BMI of 25 always mean obesity?

It depends entirely on which table you apply. Under Korean Society for the Study of Obesity criteria, 25 and above is class 1 obesity and 23 to 24.9 is pre-obesity. Under WHO international criteria, 25 to 29.9 is overweight and obesity begins at 30. The same number lands in obesity in one system and overweight in the other. Asian criteria sit lower because, at an identical BMI, body fat percentage and upper-body fat tend to run higher and obesity-related disease risk rises at a lower threshold; the American Diabetes Association moved its diabetes screening trigger to a BMI of 23 for people of Asian ancestry for the same reason. Beyond that, BMI takes only two inputs, so it carries no information about where fat sits. Rather than settle the question on one number, read waist circumference alongside it and let a clinician who knows your history make the call.

Q2. I lift regularly and carry a lot of muscle, but my BMI reads high. How should I interpret that?

This is the textbook case where the structural limit of BMI shows through. The formula has no term that distinguishes fat mass from lean mass. Muscle is denser than fat, so someone with a long resistance-training history can land in an obesity band while carrying little body fat. The Korean Society for the Study of Obesity states plainly that BMI measured alone has limits in reflecting body fat mass, particularly in athletes and in people with muscle loss. The first thing to check is waist circumference. A high BMI paired with a waist below the threshold points toward lean mass rather than fat accumulation. That is a signal rather than a conclusion, and confirmation belongs to body composition assessment and a clinical visit.

Q3. Should waist circumference be measured at the navel?

That depends on the country. WHO and the Korean Society for the Study of Obesity specify the midpoint between the lowest rib and the top of the iliac crest, which is a bony landmark rather than the navel. Japan measures at umbilical level in its specific health checkup programme. The two sites can differ by several centimetres in the same person, so the table you consult and the site you measure at have to be a matched pair. Applying a navel-level number to a Korean or WHO table can shift the verdict. For technique, stand with feet about 25-30 cm apart with weight evenly distributed, measure after breathing out comfortably, keep the tape loose enough not to compress the skin, and record to the nearest 0.1 cm.

Q4. My BMI is in the normal range but my waist is not. Does that matter?

A normal body weight with fat concentrated around the abdomen is worth looking at separately, and it is the combination BMI is most likely to miss. Korea's Ministry of Health and Welfare has issued public guidance built around exactly that point, telling people at a normal weight to check waist circumference first. Waist circumference tracks visceral fat and covers ground that BMI does not in cardiovascular risk prediction. Korean criteria set abdominal obesity at 90 cm or more for men and 85 cm or more for women. A normal BMI combined with a waist above that line is meaningful information in a clinical setting. It is not a diagnosis on its own, though, and it has to be read next to blood pressure, fasting glucose and lipid values.

Q5. When should I measure my waist, and is daily measurement fine?

Holding the conditions constant matters more than the raw number. Abdominal distension after a meal shifts the value, and so does the time of day. Pick one fixed moment, such as after waking and using the bathroom, and always measure at the same site in the same posture so the records are comparable. Daily measurement is not harmful in itself, but day-to-day movement is mostly noise from hydration and digestion. If the point is to see a trend, once a week is plenty. If frequent checking starts to feel like pressure or turns into a compulsion, reduce how often you measure and raise it with a clinician.

A number does not stand in for a person

Every figure in this article is a classification threshold drawn from population data, not a target any individual is expected to reach. If measuring and recording starts to drive anxiety or compulsion, or if your sense of control around eating begins to wobble, that itself is a reason to talk to a clinician. Extreme restriction and rapid weight loss are not ways of managing a measurement.

Conclusion

BMI keeps its place as a first-pass screening index because it is easy to compute and can be taken anywhere. But the index accepts only two values. How much body fat there is, and where it sits, live outside the formula. So it overstates risk when muscle is abundant and understates it when body weight is normal while fat is concentrated abdominally. The error runs in both directions, and without a second measure you cannot tell which way it has gone.

The thresholds themselves differ by country, and that has to be read too. As of August 2026, Korea treats 23 as the start of pre-obesity and 25 as class 1 obesity, while WHO international criteria put overweight at 25 and obesity at 30. For waist circumference, Korea uses 90 cm for men and 85 cm for women, Japan uses 85 cm for men and 90 cm for women with the sexes reversed, and the WHO Europid thresholds are different again. That is why a foreign calculator's verdict does not transfer cleanly onto your own body.

Measurement matters as much as the threshold. Waist circumference is taken against a bony landmark, after breathing out comfortably, with the tape loose enough not to compress the skin, and recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm. Pull the stomach in or measure at the narrowest point of the torso and the resulting number matches no threshold table at all.

So the practical task is small. Get the number from a BMI calculator, measure waist circumference under fixed conditions and record it alongside, and set both next to the year-by-year values already sitting in your checkup reports. What the combination means is then a conversation with a clinician who can see your blood pressure, glucose and lipid values at the same time. These measures are signposts pointing somewhere, not a diagnosis.
