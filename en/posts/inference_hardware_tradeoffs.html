What Actually Decides Inference Hardware - Bandwidth, Latency, and Ecosystem

The bottleneck in your workload decides, not the headline multiplier

August 20, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: why to stop at the tokens per second column

Build a comparison sheet for inference hardware and your eye lands on one cell every time. Tokens per second. A big number looks good, a small one looks disappointing. Yet this is exactly the kind of figure that tells you nothing without its conditions. Which model, at what precision, with how many concurrent requests in flight, with how much of the context window filled — change any of those and the same box produces numbers that differ by multiples.

Choosing hardware, then, is not a matter of picking the largest number in the table. It is a matter of deciding first what the bottleneck in your own workload is. Once the bottleneck is settled, the candidate list shrinks on its own. Evaluate spec sheets without knowing it and every candidate looks plausible for some reason or another.

Announcements that package an entire rack as a single inference system keep arriving. They are enjoyable to read, but too many conditions are omitted to transfer them straight into a purchase order. This article is not here to recommend or criticize any product. It lays out which axes actually separate inference hardware choices, what each approach gains and gives up, and in what order to read announcements like these.

The memory arithmetic of whether a model fits on one card is covered in Fitting a Large MoE on One GPU - From Memory Math to Where It Breaks 2026, and model selection for personal machines in Local LLM Hardware Selection Guide 2026 - Find Models That Actually Run on Your PC, so neither is repeated here. The focus here is the axes of the choice itself.

1. The starting point: training and inference are different problems

Both involve the same model, so they sound like the same hardware conversation. Their requirements barely overlap.

Training is work that waits for you. You push data through in large batches and it does not matter whether it takes days, as long as it finishes. Nobody sits counting seconds in front of a screen. So the virtues of a training box are simple: run large batches for a long time, without interruption, as cheaply as possible.

Inference is different. Behind every request sits a person, or another system keeping a person waiting. Three things are in play at once here.

  • Latency — how long until the first character appears after a request is sent, and how long until the response is complete
  • Throughput — how many requests are absorbed in the same span of time
  • Unit cost — what a single token or a single request costs

These three pull against each other. Allocate generous resources per request to cut latency and throughput falls while unit cost rises. Pack the machine densely to drive unit cost down and latency grows. A triangle that did not exist in training appears in inference.

The nature of the cost differs too. Training happens once every few weeks and lands in a project budget, while inference is operating expense that flows out for as long as the service is alive. So a training box is closer to a problem of running one job well even if you have to rent the capacity, and an inference box is closer to a question of how much you intend to burn over several years. That single difference is enough to route the two decisions through different approvers inside the same organization.

2. Where the bottleneck is: bandwidth versus compute

This is where inference hardware discussions most often go wrong. The top line of a spec sheet is usually operations per second, yet while tokens are being produced one at a time, what is actually scarce is memory bandwidth rather than compute.

The reason is structural. For a language model to produce the next single token, the weights that computation needs have to be read out of memory. And when the following token is produced, the same weights are read again. There is no arrangement where one read yields many tokens. With only one request in flight there is nobody to share that read cost with, so the compute units spend most of their time waiting for data to arrive.

NVIDIA describes this stage by saying that the speed at which weights, keys, values and activations are transferred from memory dominates the latency rather than how fast the computation actually happens — in other words, a memory bound operation. The stage that processes the input prompt in one pass has the opposite character. Because the full extent of the input is already known, the work parallelizes into large matrix by matrix operations, and compute capability becomes the limit.

In practice the conclusion is short. If your workload has few concurrent requests and cares about response speed, look at memory bandwidth first; if it feeds long documents and asks for short answers, compute capability carries more weight. When both are mixed, it is better not to guess which one consumes most of the time without measuring. That ratio differs between services, and even between features inside one service.

3. Prefill and decode want different machines

The two stages above have common names: prefill, which processes the input in one pass, and decode, which appends tokens one at a time. The fact that these two compete for resources inside one box is what makes inference infrastructure hard to design.

3.1 The two stages consume different resources

Prefill is compute intensive and takes longer as the input grows. Most of the time to first token is lost here. Decode is memory intensive and takes longer as the output grows. The gap between one token and the next is decided here.

So even within a single request, the weight of the two stages changes completely with the ratio of prompt length to response length. A request that feeds a long document and asks for a one-line summary, and a request that answers a short question with a long essay, place different kinds of load on the machine even with the same model. When both arrive at one cluster, tuning for either one costs the other.

3.2 KV cache, the third item

So that decode does not recompute every preceding token at each step, intermediate results are kept in a cache. That eats memory separately from the weights. In the form NVIDIA presents it, the total size is batch size × sequence length × 2 × number of layers × hidden size × bytes per element. What matters here is not the exact value but the property that it is proportional to two variables at once.

  • Double the concurrent requests and the cache doubles; double the context length and the cache doubles as well
  • So long context and high concurrency cannot both be demanded. Memory runs out first
  • However comfortably the model weights fit, once the cache consumes the remaining space, concurrency is capped right there

Calculating capacity for the weights and forgetting the cache is a common mistake. Any capacity figure produced before you have fixed a real concurrency level and a target context length is only half a figure.

The third column is the one to study. No approach gets stronger for free. Give up generality and you gain efficiency; give up efficiency and you gain options. So the question to answer before asking what is fastest is what you are willing to give up.

A one-line filter. If the models you serve are likely to change within the next two years, weight flexibility; if the model and the traffic are already fixed and the scale is large, weight efficiency. If you cannot make that call yet, you are not yet at the stage of buying hardware.

5. Latency and throughput cannot both be captured

The metrics need pinning down first, because people use the same words for different things and comparisons fall apart. Using the definitions from NVIDIA's inference benchmarking documentation:

  • Time to first token — from submitting a query to receiving the first token. Request queuing, prefill and network latency are all included
  • Inter-token latency, or time per output token — the average time between consecutive tokens, with the first token excluded from the calculation
  • Tokens per second and requests per second — system-wide throughput. The former is total output tokens divided by the end-to-end time between the first request and the last response; the latter is the average number of requests successfully completed in a one second period

The key point is that the third and the second move in opposite directions. As the same documentation states, when the number of concurrent requests increases, total system tokens per second rises while tokens per second per user falls as latency increases.

The reason already appeared in the previous section. Enlarging the batch lets several requests share one read of the weights. NVIDIA also explains that the simplest way to improve utilization and effectively throughput is batching, since multiple requests use the same model and the memory cost of the weights is spread out. The machine as a whole gains, while individual requests queue.

The point is that the three rows point at three different configurations. Serving all three from one cluster means separating the workloads and routing them to pools with different settings. There is no point where a single configuration satisfies all three goals.

6. The software ecosystem is the real constraint

One question never gets answered no matter how long you stare at a spec sheet: does the model I want to serve actually run on that hardware. Theoretical performance is only performance when software exists to extract it.

More layers are involved than people expect.

  • Serving engine — the layer handling continuous batching, queue management and cache reuse. Without it, things collapse the moment concurrency arrives
  • Compute kernels — the actual implementations of attention and matrix multiplication. When a new attention variant appears, the kernel has to land before the model runs
  • Quantization formats — whether published weights exist in that format, and whether the hardware accelerates it
  • Distributed execution — the sharding strategy and communication library used when a model spans several devices
  • Observability — a profiler that shows where the time goes when things are slow. Without it, tuning becomes guessing

These layers are the real barrier for new entrants because each of them has to be refilled every time a new model arrives. It is not a job that ends once the hardware is built; it is standing work that has to keep pace with the model ecosystem.

A useful gauge is the support surface of a widely used open source serving engine. The platforms the official vLLM installation documentation covers, for example, are NVIDIA CUDA, AMD ROCm and Intel XPU accelerators, plus CPUs on x86, ARM, Apple silicon and IBM Z. Hardware beyond that attaches as plugins living outside the main repository, as the documentation itself states. Absence from that list does not mean something is unusable, but it does mean that the integration cost of stepping off the standard path lands somewhere.

6.1 Software questions to ask before adopting

  • Does your current model family already run in production on that hardware — production, not a demo — and how many days does a newly published model typically take to arrive there
  • How many lines of existing code have to change to move over? Is it a matter of swapping an endpoint address
  • Is there a profiler that shows the cause when something is slow? Without one, all tuning is guesswork
  • When something breaks, is the place to ask a public repository or one named contact? And do quantized weights have to be converted yourself, or can published builds be used

7. The conditions attached to announced figures

Take this week's news as material. Cerebras announced CS-4, a rack scale inference system. The point is not to introduce the product but to look at the posture with which numbers in announcements like this should be read.

Here are representative values from the official page, carried over with their conditions. All are first-party claims.

  • Up to 30x faster inference than GPUs — the configuration of the compared GPU system, the model, the precision, the batch size and the context length are not specified on the page
  • More than 1,000 tokens per second on models exceeding 10 trillion parameters — a model size condition is attached, but the model name, the precision and the concurrency are not stated
  • Up to 10x more throughput per watt than CS-3 — a generational comparison, with no measurement workload named
  • Wafer to wafer interconnect latency of 2 microseconds — a specification figure
  • Power delivery placed 0.5 millimeters from the processor — presented as a structural contrast against roughly 50 millimeters on conventional GPU boards
  • First shipments beginning this quarter — no specific date given

And the same page carries this qualifier: performance comparisons are based on third-party benchmarking or internal testing, and observed inference speed improvements versus GPU-based systems may vary depending on workload, configuration, date and models being tested. That sentence already tells you how to read the numbers above it. Copying a figure whose condition dependence the manufacturer itself declared, without those conditions, makes a stronger claim than the source did.

7.1 First-party claims and independent measurement are different objects

Without separating the two, no comparison holds. A first-party claim generally shows an upper bound under conditions the vendor selected; an independent measurement generally shows the conditions a user actually meets. They measure different things to begin with.

Independent measurement has its own reading rules. Artificial Analysis, which continuously measures and publishes the performance of public inference services, states that what it measures is API endpoint performance. Testing runs from a virtual machine in one cloud region; output speed is defined as the average number of tokens received per second after the first token is received, and time to first token as the seconds between sending a request and receiving the first token of the response. It also notes that time to first token is sensitive to server location because it includes network latency, and that a single testing location may advantage or disadvantage certain providers.

In other words, such figures are not the maximum capability of the hardware but what that service produced on that day in that configuration. The two kinds of number answer different questions, and neither substitutes for the other.

8. Build versus API, and where most teams belong

A question follows naturally at this point. So should we buy? For most teams, not yet. Three axes decide it.

8.1 Fixed cost and utilization

Building your own is a trade that takes on fixed cost to lower unit cost. The trade only pays when utilization is high. A box used a few hours a day still burns depreciation, power, floor space and staff time while it sits idle. Conversely, when traffic is spread evenly around the clock and the scale is large, the same trade turns favorable.

  • A large gap between peak and normal load argues against building. You buy for the peak and idle the rest of the time. Predictable, flat traffic flips the same trade the other way
  • A hybrid exists as well: take base load on your own hardware and spill the peaks to an external API
  • Hybrids do mean two response profiles, so quality validation and caching strategy end up needing two versions

8.2 Data requirements

Sometimes regulation, not price, makes the decision. If data cannot leave your boundary, unit cost comparisons lose their meaning. In that case you are not building because it is cheap but because there is no other option. Even then, scale is a separate judgment. Starting from the smallest configuration that satisfies the requirement is the better move.

8.3 People and time

The line most often omitted is operations staffing. Serving engine upgrades, model swaps, incident response and capacity planning all become somebody's standing job. Compare hardware cost alone and leave this out, and the estimate diverges from reality.

  • Launch the service on an external API first and observe the real shape of the traffic and token consumption
  • Only once monthly spend is meaningful and the pattern is stable, run the build calculation — adding power, floor space, network, maintenance and staff cost to the hardware price
  • If the break-even computed that way does not arrive within a year, it is usually still early

9. What rack scale integration signals

What deserves attention in this announcement is the direction rather than the specifications. The trend of selling a rack rather than a single accelerator as the unit is becoming pronounced. Power conversion, liquid cooling and I/O are integrated into one package, with the stated design intent of cutting deployment time.

For a buyer, that flow cuts both ways.

  • What you gain — less assembly and validation burden, and a configuration guaranteed to perform from the start. You do not get to repeat cabling and cooling mistakes
  • What you lose — less room to mix and match components. Partial expansion and partial replacement lose freedom
  • What newly attaches — rack level power and cooling become preconditions. If an existing machine room cannot meet them, the facility becomes the problem before the hardware does
  • What changes on the books — as the minimum purchase unit grows, small pilot deployments become harder to arrange

Rack scale integration is therefore a story about purchase units and facility requirements before it is a story about performance. Reading such an announcement, the thing to check before the multiplier is whether your machine room can accept that rack. 10. A checklist for reading announcements like this

Order matters. Work down the list and most announcements resolve around the third item.

  1. Is the figure first-party or independent? Find the qualifier at the bottom of the page first
  2. Are model, precision, batch and context all stated? Miss any one and the multiplier is of unknown conditions
  3. What is it compared against? The previous generation, a competing product, or an unoptimized reference configuration
  4. Is it a latency figure or a throughput figure? And does it correspond to the prefill to decode ratio of your workload
  5. Does the software support surface include your model and your serving engine
  6. Can your facility meet the power, cooling and floor space requirements? If not, the facility becomes the problem before the hardware
  7. Does the minimum purchase unit match your scale? Is a pilot possible
  8. Is there supply and an exit? What is the lead time, and is there a disposal path in a few years
  9. How many months of external API usage does the same money buy? And how much is traffic likely to change over that span

The performance multiplier appears only in the second line. The other eight lines are what actually decide adoption.

11. What could not be confirmed

Nothing here was measured on any machine directly. Every figure in the article is a value written in published documentation, and the following could not be confirmed this time.

  • The price and absolute power draw of CS-4. No monetary figure or absolute rated power appears on the official page
  • The comparison configuration behind the 30x claim. Which GPUs, how many, and on what software stack is not stated
  • The measurement conditions behind 1,000 tokens per second. Model name, precision, concurrency and context length are not given
  • Independent measurements of CS-4. Within what was checked here, no published third-party measurement was found
  • On-chip memory capacity and bandwidth figures for the wafer. The page referenced here does not carry them
  • Shipping dates and regional availability paths. Beyond a quarter level mention, no specific schedule exists

The comparison table in section 4 has the same character. It organizes the structural properties of each approach; it does not rest on measurements of four approaches placed side by side under identical conditions. That is why every multiplier in the body carries a note that it is a first-party figure. A multiplier stripped of its conditions is not a value you can copy forward.

Conclusion: measure the workload first, then look at hardware

Three things actually separated the choices. Whether the bottleneck is bandwidth or compute, whether the goal is latency or throughput, and whether your model actually runs on top of it. The top line of a spec sheet answers none of the three directly.

So it is better to reverse the order. Instead of picking a box and fitting the workload to it, write down the prompt length, the response length, the concurrency and the target response time as numbers first. Once those four are settled the candidate list falls to two or three on its own, and from there software support and facility requirements finish the decision.

Announcements like rack scale integration are material for that judgment, not the conclusion of it. Every time a new structure appears, the thing to check is the same. What did this design give up in order to gain what, and is that happen to be the thing you can afford to give up.
