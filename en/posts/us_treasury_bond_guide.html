Why a Safe Asset Still Loses Money - US Treasuries, Rates, Duration and FX

Low default risk and a stable price are two different claims. This piece pries them apart with arithmetic

2026-08-01 Finance

Introduction: what the word safe actually points at

US Treasuries get called safe assets. Yet among people who have actually bought them the opposite story circulates: they opened the account and found a negative number. The two statements look contradictory but they are describing different things. Safe is about whether the issuer pays what it promised. A loss is about what the market pays for the paper today. Those were never the same axis.

The argument of this article fits in three lines.

  • Saying Treasuries are safe means default risk is low, not that the price does not move. Credit risk and market risk exist independently of one another
  • Losses mostly happen when you sell before maturity. Hold to maturity and you receive the promised cash flows in nominal terms
  • The size of that loss is set by duration. The same 1 percentage point rate move hits a 30 year more than eight times harder than a 2 year. And an investor based in Korean won carries the exchange rate as one more axis

This piece follows that arithmetic all the way through. Everyone has read the sentence about rates up meaning prices down, but almost nobody puts a number on how far down. So a calculator was written from scratch, run, and the output moved into tables. If you need the basics of bonds first, start with bond investing for beginners and corporate bond investing. Nothing foundational is re-explained here. This article drills into one thing: how the price of a US Treasury moves.

1. Why a safe asset loses money - credit risk versus price risk

Start by splitting the vocabulary. There are broadly two ways a bondholder loses money, and the causes and the responses are completely different.

  • Credit risk. The issuer fails to deliver interest or principal as promised. This is the axis people mean when they call Treasuries safe, and here safe is the market's assessment of the issuer's capacity to pay
  • Price risk, or market risk. The issuer is perfectly fine but the value quoted in the market today has fallen. Treasuries trade daily, so they have a price, and that price keeps moving with market yields
  • The two axes are independent. A bond whose issuer has never wobbled can still see its price drop a long way. The reverse also happens: credit deteriorates while the rate environment holds the price up

One distinction decides everything from here. Price risk is only realised when you sell.

  • Hold to maturity. Bonds have a maturity date, and on that day the issuer repays the face amount. Whatever the price printed in between, in nominal terms the promised coupons and principal arrive. The unrealised loss on the statement stays a number that passed by
  • Sell early. You sell at that day's market price. This is where an unrealised loss becomes a realised one. Almost every loss discussed in this article travels that route
  • But holding to maturity is not a cure-all. Getting the nominal principal back is not the same as the purchasing power of that money holding up. Rising prices shrink the real value of the same sum
  • And holding requires being able to hold. If cash is suddenly needed, you cannot wait for maturity. In that case the hold-to-maturity plan only ever existed on paper

So the sentence about a safe asset producing a loss is not a contradiction. It is what happens when one word covers two axes. The issuer was safe; the price on the day you sold was below the price on the day you bought. Why did that price fall? The next section is the arithmetic.

2. Why rates and prices move in opposite directions - the arithmetic

A bond's coupon is fixed at issue. That single fact produces everything else.

Say you bought a bond with a face value of 100 dollars and a 4.00 percent annual coupon. It pays 4 dollars a year until maturity. If newly issued paper starts paying 5.00 percent, nobody buys the 4 dollar stream at 100. The coupon is contractually fixed and cannot be raised. The only thing that can move is the price. It falls until, at that lower price, the total return to maturity lines up with what the new bond offers, and a trade clears there.

The formula that finds that point is a sum of present values. US Treasuries pay every six months, so the timeline is cut into half-year steps.

3. Duration - why the same 1pp hurts differently by maturity

The table in section 2 shows different maturities reacting by different amounts to the same rate move. The number that measures the difference is duration.

The intuition behind duration is the centre of gravity of when you get your money. A 30 year bond returns its 100 of principal thirty years out. Converting that distant 100 into today's value is highly sensitive to small changes in the discount rate. On a 2 year the principal is right there, leaving the discount rate little room to matter. Hence longer maturity, more rate sensitivity.

  • Macaulay duration. The average time to recovery, weighting each cash flow by its present value. The unit is years
  • Modified duration. Macaulay divided by (1 + yield to maturity / payments per year), which converts it into the percentage price move per 1 percentage point change in yield
  • Convexity. Duration is a tangent drawn on a curve, so it misses on larger moves. Convexity is the second order term that corrects that miss
  • Lower coupons mean longer duration. Less cash comes back along the way, pushing the centre of gravity further out
MaturityMacaulay durationModified durationConvexityExact price change at +1pp
2 year1.942 y1.904 y4.62-1.88%

30 year17.728 y17.380 y420.81-15.45%

One line deserves attention. The 30 year's duration is 17.38 years, not 30. Maturity and duration are not the same quantity. Coupons arriving every six months pull the centre of gravity forward. Reasoning that a 30 year must move 30 percent per 1pp is simply wrong arithmetic; the real answer is roughly half that.

The block above makes two points at once.

  • Duration is an approximation. On the 2 year the error is 0.02pp and can be ignored, but on the 30 year it widens to 1.93pp. Trusting the approximation alone paints the outcome worse than it actually is
  • Adding convexity nearly fixes it. On the 30 year, minus 17.38 percent tightens to minus 15.28 percent against an exact minus 15.45 percent
  • Equal moves up and down are not symmetric. On the 30 year a 1pp fall is plus 19.69 percent while a 1pp rise is minus 15.45 percent. The sum is a positive 4.24
  • That asymmetry is convexity itself. Price and yield are related by a curve that bows, not a straight line. The longer the maturity the larger the effect
  • Do not read this as an argument that long maturities are therefore better. It is a directional difference within one maturity, not a ranking between maturities

Same ten year maturity, but the zero coupon reacts by minus 9.32 percent while the 6 percent coupon moves minus 7.35 percent. The more cash comes back along the way, the further forward the centre of gravity sits and the shorter the duration. So the idea that equal maturity means equal risk does not hold. Maturity and coupon have to be read together.

4. What happens if you hold to maturity - pull to par

Section 1 said that holding to maturity delivers the promised money in nominal terms. That too can be checked by calculation. Assume yields rise and then simply sit there, and let only time pass.

--- 30 year, immediately after a +1.00pp rise, yield unchanged thereafter ---
purchase price               100.00 (per 100 face)
price right after +1.00pp     84.55
sell now                     -15.45  (-15.45%)
hold to maturity             coupons 2.00 x 60 + face 100 = 220.00 nominal

--- the price converges to par even with the yield unchanged ---
  30y remaining     price   84.55
  25y remaining     price   85.82
  20y remaining     price   87.45
  15y remaining     price   89.53
  10y remaining     price   92.21
   5y remaining     price   95.62
   2y remaining     price   98.12
   1y remaining     price   99.04
  0.5y remaining    price   99.51

That table is the structure of holding to maturity. Even with yields never retracing at all, the price climbs from 84.55 back to 100. The remaining term shrinks, so there is less time left to discount. This is called pull to par, and it is why unrealised losses shrink naturally with time.

  • But this is a story about nominal amounts. Whether 100 dollars in thirty years buys what 100 dollars buys today is a separate question
  • And an opportunity cost remains. The fact that you were tied to a low coupon while higher coupon paper was being issued does not disappear
  • Holding to maturity presupposes being able to not sell. If living expenses and the maturity date do not line up, the strategy does not apply
  • A structure without a maturity does not deliver this convergence in the same way. Section 8 returns to that

5.1 How inflation linked securities are built

TIPS work on a fundamentally different mechanism. The structure as TreasuryDirect describes it is this.

  • The principal moves. The official wording is that the principal, called par value or face value, of a TIPS goes up with inflation and down with deflation. So rising prices raise the principal and falling prices lower it
  • Fixed rate, variable payment. TIPS pay a fixed rate of interest every six months until they mature, but that fixed rate is applied to the adjusted principal, so the cash amount received varies
  • There is a floor at maturity. If the principal is equal to or lower than the original amount, you get the original amount. Even if deflation pushes the principal below where it started, at maturity you receive the original amount
  • Terms are 5, 10 and 30 years. The official phrasing is that TIPS are sold for a term of 5, 10 or 30 years
  • Note that the floor applies at maturity. It is not a guarantee that an interim sale price will be at or above the original amount. Sold early, TIPS carry full price risk

One common misreading is worth correcting. TIPS are not a bond that cannot lose when prices rise. The principal is indexed, but the price the market puts on the security responds to real yields. Just as a nominal bond swings with nominal yields, TIPS swing with real ones. The axis changed; price risk did not vanish.

6. The second axis for a won-based investor - the exchange rate

Every calculation so far has been in dollars. Somebody who earns and spends in Korean won carries one more axis. The bond result and the currency result multiply together.

The formula is simple.

won return = (1 + dollar return) x (1 + FX change) - 1

  FX change = exit rate / entry rate - 1
  (+) means the dollar-won rate rose, the won weakened, the won amount grew
  (-) means the rate fell, the won strengthened, the won amount shrank

--- won return matrix (all values assumed) ---
USD return      FX +10%      FX +5%       FX 0%       FX -5%      FX -10%
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
  +5.0%        +15.50%     +10.25%      +5.00%      -0.25%       -5.50%
  +3.0%        +13.30%      +8.15%      +3.00%      -2.15%       -7.30%
   0.0%        +10.00%      +5.00%       0.00%      -5.00%      -10.00%
  -3.0%         +6.70%      +1.85%      -3.00%      -7.85%      -12.70%

--- the FX move that exactly cancels a dollar gain (breakeven) ---
  USD +5.0%  needs FX -4.76% to end flat in won
  USD +3.0%  needs FX -2.91% to end flat in won
  USD -3.0%  needs FX +3.09% to end flat in won

Look at the upper right diagonal of the matrix. A 5 percent dollar gain becomes minus 5.50 percent in won if the exchange rate falls 10 percent. The bond call was right and the won account still shows a loss. Breakeven sits at minus 4.76 percent on the currency. In other words a 5 percent dollar gain only absorbs a 4.76 percent currency decline.

The reverse works identically. Take the 10 year's minus 7.79 percent from section 2 and layer the currency on top.

--- 10 year at +1.00pp (USD -7.79%) multiplied by the FX move ---
  bond -7.79%   FX +10%   gives   won basis    +1.43%
  bond -7.79%   FX  +5%   gives   won basis    -3.18%
  bond -7.79%   FX   0%   gives   won basis    -7.79%
  bond -7.79%   FX  -5%   gives   won basis   -12.40%
  bond -7.79%   FX -10%   gives   won basis   -17.02%

Same bond, same rate move, and yet the won result ranges from plus 1.43 percent to minus 17.02 percent. The first line matters most. The bond lost 7.79 percent and the won account is positive. On the last line the bond loss has been more than doubled.

  • Currency both hides and amplifies bond results. Looking only at the won account number, you cannot tell which of the two caused what
  • So the two axes have to be recorded separately. Unless the dollar amount and the applied rate at entry and exit are logged apart from each other, there is no way to reconstruct afterwards what was right and what was wrong
  • Currency moves have no predetermined direction. This article forecasts nothing about exchange rates. The table only shows what the arithmetic does given a currency move
  • Hedging changes the structure. A hedged structure reduces the currency axis and introduces a hedging cost line item instead. Neither is declared better here; knowing which one you actually hold comes first
  • Short maturities do not shrink the currency axis. Duration falls with maturity, but the exchange rate multiplies the entire position regardless. A four week bill carries 100 percent currency exposure

That last point is the one most often missed in practice. If somebody went short on the curve to be safe and found their won-based volatility was higher instead, the currency axis was usually still there. Duration does not reduce exposure to the exchange rate.

7. Tax - for Korean residents, verified and unverified

This section is written for Korean residents and reports only what was verified on National Tax Service material. Overseas investment tax in general is covered in more depth in overseas fund investing and US stock tax for Korean residents, so this stays short and sticks to what touches US Treasuries directly.

7.1 What the NTS material confirms

  • Interest on foreign bonds is interest income. The NTS lists, as interest income within financial income, interest on deposits held at overseas financial institutions and trust income received abroad, together with interest and discount received on bonds issued by foreign corporations
  • Without Korean withholding, aggregation is unconditional. The NTS wording is that interest income not subject to withholding inside Korea is unconditionally subject to comprehensive taxation
  • The 20 million won threshold has exceptions. It states that unwithheld financial income, giving foreign source interest and dividend income as the example, is aggregated even at or below 20 million won
  • Withholding rates for residents. The NTS specifies 25 percent on non-business loan gains and 14 percent on other interest income
  • Securities trading gains are not financial income. The guidance states that trading gains on securities such as shares and bonds are not financial income and therefore not subject to comprehensive financial income taxation

7.2 What is left flagged as needing confirmation

  • Whether local income tax is included. The NTS page consulted presents 14 percent and 25 percent without stating whether local income tax is included. This article therefore does not assert an effective rate
  • Whether foreign bond trading gains are capital gains. The wording above only reaches not subject to comprehensive financial income taxation. Whether another income category applies is not on the material consulted
  • How currency gains are treated on their own. How a currency gain on the bond itself is handled, and which category a gain at the point of converting sale proceeds falls into, is not on the material consulted
  • How the foreign tax credit applies. Treatment where US withholding occurred was not verified for this article
  • Differences by holding structure. How direct holding and holding through a fund or exchange traded product diverge in tax treatment is outside what was verified

Tax outcomes depend heavily on individual circumstances. Income composition, residency determination, account type and holding structure all change the conclusion. Even the items marked as confirmed above are reproductions of the quoted guidance and do not mean the same result applies to your situation. For an actual filing, follow NTS guidance and a tax professional.

8. Buying directly versus buying through a fund or ETF - structural differences

Access to US Treasuries splits broadly into holding individual bonds directly and holding through a product that bundles many of them. This article does not say which is better. It only lays out how the structures differ, because the advantage depends on holding period, cash flow plans, tax position and cost, and lands differently for each person.

ItemIndividual bonds held directlyThrough a fund or exchange traded product
MaturityExists. You are repaid face value on the dayDepends on the structure. Perpetual forms are common, and dated forms exist too
Pull to parAs in section 4, time pushes the price back toward face valueIf holdings are rolled continuously the portfolio duration is maintained, so the same convergence is hard to expect
Getting money out earlyBefore maturity you must sell into the marketYou redeem or sell. The exit route differs
The duration you knowOne number, for the bond you boughtA weighted average of holdings. It has to be looked up in the product material
DiversificationOne line. The maturity ladder is yours to buildMultiple maturities are already mixed in
CostTransaction related costsTransaction costs plus ongoing charges such as fees
TaxMay diverge by holding structure. See the needs-confirmation items in 7.2

The most consequential row is pull to par. The property from section 4, where the price returns to 100 without yields retracing, requires a maturity to exist inside the structure. Where holdings are continuously rolled to maintain a target duration, waiting for a return to face value cannot be applied the way it is with an individual bond.

  • This is a difference in properties, not a ranking. Diversification and convenience are gained and the maturity mechanism is given up
  • Conversely an individual bond gains a maturity while concentrating in one line and leaving the ladder for you to design
  • Either way you have to know the duration of what you hold. That number is the direct input into the calculations in section 3
  • If holding through a product, checking whether it has a maturity and whether it is currency hedged in the product material is the first step

9. Verification procedure - the yield curve and official data

Everything so far has been about structure. Actually checking anything requires knowing where to look and at what.

9.1 How to read a yield curve

A yield curve puts maturity on the horizontal axis and yield on the vertical, connecting bonds from one issuer in order of maturity. Reading it goes like this.

  • Read the whole curve, not one point. There is no single number called the US interest rate. Each maturity carries its own yield, and what matters is which segment sits against your duration
  • Yield is another expression of price. A point on the curve rising means bonds in that segment got cheaper. The arithmetic from section 2 applies directly
  • A change in shape is not a parallel shift. Every calculation here assumed a parallel shift. In practice short and long ends often move by different amounts, and then the results by maturity differ from the tables
  • This article makes no forecast from the curve. Interpretations that read the future from its shape exist, but the curve is used here only to check where the yield on your own maturity segment currently sits

9.2 Where the official data lives

  • Terms, types, interest. The US Treasury's TreasuryDirect marketable securities pages. The table in section 5 was verified there and on the TIPS page
  • Daily yields. The US Treasury publishes Daily Treasury Par Yield Curve Rates (interest rate statistics page). That page could not be reached while this article was being written, so the published tenors and the derivation methodology were not verified. Nothing from it is quoted here, and the numbers must be checked by going there yourself
  • Time series. A public database in the US Federal Reserve system provides Treasury yield time series. That too was unreachable during writing, so no value from it is quoted in this article
  • Tax. For Korean residents the National Tax Service is the primary source. The wording quoted in section 7 comes from there
  • Important. This article asserts no level of interest rates as of 2026-08-01. The 4.00 percent and similar figures in the text are assumed inputs used to expose the arithmetic

9.3 Pre-purchase checklist

  1. Identified what is being bought. Bill, Note, Bond, TIPS or FRN
  2. Checked the maturity. And checked whether that maturity fits the cash flow plan
  3. Checked the duration. Duration, not maturity. If through a product, found the weighted average portfolio duration in the material
  4. Computed the price at a 1pp rate rise. And confirmed that number is survivable
  5. Ran the 2pp and 3pp scenarios too. On a 30 year that is minus 27.68 percent and minus 37.42 percent under the assumed conditions
  6. Confirmed the ability to hold to maturity. If an early sale is possible, the convergence in section 4 has to come out of the plan
  7. Checked currency exposure. Whether the structure is hedged, and if so where the cost shows up
  8. Ran a minus 10 percent currency scenario. Applied the matrix in section 6 to the expected return
  9. Checked the maturity structure. An individual bond has one; for a product, checked whether the structure is dated
  10. Checked costs. Transaction costs and ongoing costs, separately
  11. Checked tax. The needs-confirmation items in section 7 were taken to NTS guidance or a professional individually
  12. Built a record keeping habit. Decided to log the dollar amount and the applied rate separately
  13. Checked current yields against official data. At the issuer and the statistics publisher, not a blog or a summary article

10. The calculation code - run it yourself

Every number in this article came out of the functions below. It is an honest implementation that discounts semiannual cash flows directly, and it needs no special library.

const F = 2; // coupon payments per year (US Treasuries pay semiannually)

// theoretical price: present value of coupons plus present value of face
function price(face, cpn, ytm, years) {
  const n = Math.round(years * F);
  const c = face * cpn / F;
  const y = ytm / F;
  let p = 0;
  for (let t = 1; t <= n; t++) p += c / Math.pow(1 + y, t);
  return p + face / Math.pow(1 + y, n);
}

// Macaulay duration: average time to recovery weighted by present value, in years
function macaulay(face, cpn, ytm, years) {
  const n = Math.round(years * F);
  const c = face * cpn / F;
  const y = ytm / F;
  let pv = 0, w = 0;
  for (let t = 1; t <= n; t++) {
    const v = c / Math.pow(1 + y, t);
    pv += v; w += v * (t / F);
  }
  const vf = face / Math.pow(1 + y, n);
  pv += vf; w += vf * (n / F);
  return w / pv;
}

// modified duration = macaulay / (1 + ytm / F)
// approx price change = -modified x rate change + 0.5 x convexity x rate change^2
// won return = (1 + dollar return) x (1 + FX change) - 1

// check: price(100, 0.04, 0.05, 30) gives 84.55, matching section 2

One caution. These functions compute a theoretical price. Real trades add accrued interest, bid-ask spreads, settlement conventions and tax, so the screen will not match exactly. Even so, it is more than enough to gauge which way and how far a rate move pushes the price.

12. Summary in a handful of lines

  • Safe means the issuer pays. It does not mean the price stays still. The two axes are independent, and the negative number on the statement is almost always the second one
  • Losses are realised when you sell. Hold to maturity and the price converges to par. In the calculation the 30 year fell to 84.55 after a 1pp rise, then returned to 92.21 with 10 years left and 98.12 with 2. But that is a nominal story and it requires being able to not sell
  • Duration sets the size of the loss. On the same 1pp the 2 year is minus 1.88 percent and the 30 year minus 15.45 percent. More than eight times. Look at duration, not maturity
  • A won-based investor carries one more axis. Duration does not shrink it, and the exchange rate multiplies the whole position. Minus 7.79 percent on the bond can be plus 1.43 percent or minus 17.02 percent in won
  • So the first question is not whether to buy but what duration and what currency exposure you are holding. Without those numbers the calculation cannot even begin

# US Treasuries — duration and FX tables, plus a pre-purchase checklist

> Source: braindetox.kr — Why a Safe Asset Still Loses Money
> Written: 2026-08-01
> Informational purposes only. Not a product recommendation. Not a rate or FX forecast. Decisions and outcomes are your own responsibility.
> Tax items are written for Korean residents.

## 0. Assumptions (behind every number below)

- Face 100 · coupon 4.00% per year · 2 payments per year · initial market yield 4.00%
- Price at issue = 100.00 (coupon = market yield)
- **These are assumed inputs to expose the arithmetic, not actual market rates**
- Yields are assumed to shift the whole curve by the same amount (parallel shift)

## 1. Duration table

| Maturity | Macaulay(y) | Modified(y) | Convexity | Exact at +1pp |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2y | 1.942 | 1.904 | 4.62 | -1.88% |

| 30y | 17.728 | 17.380 | 420.81 | **-15.45%** |

- Lower coupon means longer duration (10y: 0% coupon → 9.804y, 6% → 7.705y)

## 3. Pull to par when held (30y, +1pp then yield unchanged)

| Remaining | 30y | 20y | 10y | 5y | 2y | 0.5y |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Price | 84.55 | 87.45 | 92.21 | 95.62 | 98.12 | 99.51 |

- The price converges to par even if yields never retrace
- But this is a **nominal** amount and requires **being able to not sell**

## 4. Won-based return (the FX axis)

won return = (1 + dollar return) x (1 + FX change) - 1

| USD return | FX +10% | FX +5% | FX 0% | FX -5% | FX -10% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| +5.0% | +15.50% | +10.25% | +5.00% | -0.25% | -5.50% |
| +3.0% | +13.30% | +8.15% | +3.00% | -2.15% | -7.30% |
| 0.0% | +10.00% | +5.00% | 0.00% | -5.00% | -10.00% |
| -3.0% | +6.70% | +1.85% | -3.00% | -7.85% | -12.70% |

- Breakeven: a +5.0% dollar gain only absorbs **-4.76%** on the currency
- The 10 year's -7.79% spreads to **+1.43% ~ -17.02%** once FX is applied
- **Duration does not shrink FX exposure.** Even a four week bill is fully exposed

## 5. Types (as verified on US Treasury pages)

| Type | Terms | Interest |
|---|---|---|
| Bills | 4 to 52 weeks, five options | Sold at a discount, face at maturity |
| Notes | 2, 3, 5, 7, 10 years | Every six months |
| Bonds | 20-year terms stated as now offered as well | Every six months |
| FRN | Two years only | Every three months |
| TIPS | 5, 10, 30 years | Every six months, fixed rate on adjusted principal |

- TIPS principal moves with inflation, and **the original amount is the floor at maturity**
- But that floor applies at maturity; **an interim sale price is not guaranteed**

## 6. Calculation code (runs as is in Node or a browser)

```js
const F = 2;
function price(face, cpn, ytm, years) {
  const n = Math.round(years * F);
  const c = face * cpn / F;
  const y = ytm / F;
  let p = 0;
  for (let t = 1; t <= n; t++) p += c / Math.pow(1 + y, t);
  return p + face / Math.pow(1 + y, n);
}
function macaulay(face, cpn, ytm, years) {
  const n = Math.round(years * F);
  const c = face * cpn / F;
  const y = ytm / F;
  let pv = 0, w = 0;
  for (let t = 1; t <= n; t++) {
    const v = c / Math.pow(1 + y, t);
    pv += v; w += v * (t / F);
  }
  const vf = face / Math.pow(1 + y, n);
  pv += vf; w += vf * (n / F);
  return w / pv;
}
// modified duration = macaulay(...) / (1 + ytm / F)
// check: price(100, 0.04, 0.05, 30) gives 84.55
```

## 8. Pre-purchase checklist

- [ ] Identified the type (Bill / Note / Bond / FRN / TIPS)
- [ ] Checked the maturity and it fits the cash flow plan
- [ ] Checked **duration, not maturity**
- [ ] Computed the price at +1pp and it is survivable
- [ ] Ran the +2pp and +3pp scenarios too
- [ ] Confirmed the ability to hold to maturity (otherwise drop pull to par from the plan)
- [ ] Checked whether it is currency hedged, and where the cost lands if so
- [ ] Applied a -10% FX scenario to the expected return
- [ ] If through a product, checked **whether the structure is dated**
- [ ] Checked transaction costs and ongoing costs separately
- [ ] Took tax questions to NTS guidance or a professional individually
- [ ] Decided to log the dollar amount and the applied rate **separately**
- [ ] Checked current yields at the official publisher

## 10. Do not

- Treat maturity and duration as the same thing (the 30 year's duration is 17.38 years)
- Read safe asset as meaning the price does not move
- Judge a bond call by the won account number alone (FX is mixed in)
- Assume a short maturity also shrinks currency exposure
- Apply an individual bond's pull to par to a holding through a product
- Put unverified tax treatment into a calculation as though it were verified
