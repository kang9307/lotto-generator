How Successful Companies Go Blind

Competence blindness - why the capabilities that built an organization stop being visible to it

Jul 13, 2026 Trends & Culture

Introduction: Why the Fish Lost Its Eyes

In the dark limestone caves of Mexico live tetra fish whose eyes have completely degenerated. The genetic blueprint for sight is still there, but in an environment with no light, eyes pay no rent. Over millions of years, the fish simply lost any reason to keep them. A recent essay that made the rounds on GeekNews (hada), Ian Reppel's "How Successful Companies Go Blind", carries that natural-history metaphor straight into organizations. He calls the phenomenon competence blindness - the exact capability that carried an organization to success becomes, after that success, invisible to the organization itself.

This piece takes the hada topic and the original essay as a starting point, but builds its own analysis on that frame. Rather than summarize the source, it asks why the metaphor holds up against established management thinking, which existing concepts it touches, and what organizations in 2026 should watch for and prepare.

1. What Competence Blindness Actually Is

1.1 Not invisible - unrecognized

Competence blindness is not a case of capability disappearing. It is, more precisely, an organization losing the sense to identify and reward that capability. The careful design decisions, the thorough code reviews, the habit of weighing risk first that once saved the company - these can still live inside someone's head. The problem is that the organization's evaluation systems, promotion criteria, and hiring interviews no longer look for them, let alone find them.

1.2 Growth bends the bar

Reppel describes hiring during rapid growth this way: "headcount targets bend the bar" (Ian Reppel). Under pressure to fill seats quickly, an organization first lowers the bar slightly, then mistakes that lowered bar for the new normal. A few generations later, no one left in the building even remembers what the original bar was.

2. Why Success Itself Causes Blindness

2.1 Outcome metrics obscure the source

Successful organizations inevitably refine their processes and metrics. The trouble is that those metrics mostly measure outcomes - revenue, shipping velocity, user counts - not the source capability that produced them: design judgment, domain knowledge, the instinct to spot risk early. Over time, organizations start confusing the ability to manage a metric with the underlying capability itself. If the metric looks healthy, the assumption is that the capability must be too.

2.2 Bureaucratization and the "vestigial trait"

As organizations scale, decision-making shifts from individual judgment to process approval. That shift is, to some degree, unavoidable for economies of scale. But in the process, careful engineering or deep domain judgment gets reduced to a formality needed to pass through the process, and loses the chance to actually be exercised. In Reppel's phrasing, such capabilities linger like a vestigial trait - present, but no longer expressed as function.

2.3 The inertia of success

Success breeds repetition. The belief that a formula that worked once will work again seeps into the whole organization. That inertia is comfortable - and the comfort itself is the problem. In a stable environment there is little need to exercise sharp judgment, and a capability that goes unexercised gradually blurs out of the organization's field of view.

3. What Management Theory Already Knew

3.1 Core rigidities

In 1992, Harvard Business School's Dorothy Leonard-Barton addressed the same paradox from a different angle in "Core Capabilities and Core Rigidities: A Paradox in Managing New Product Development." She argued that an organization's core capabilities - technical systems, skills, managerial systems, and values - can convert directly into core rigidities once the environment changes, drawing on case studies of 20 product-development projects across five firms including Ford and Hewlett-Packard. If competence blindness explains capability becoming invisible, core rigidities explain that same capability actively working against the organization. They are two symptoms branching from one root.

3.2 The innovator's dilemma

Clayton Christensen's 1997 book "The Innovator's Dilemma" is the better-known cousin concept. His core insight is that good management itself can be the cause of decline. The more diligently a management team listens to its existing customers, the more precisely it will miss disruptive technologies that look unimpressive at first and the fringe customers who want them. The overlap with competence blindness is clear - both theories say organizations go blind not because they became incompetent, but precisely because they kept doing what once made them competent.

3.3 The success trap

Recent research from IESE Business School explains the success trap from a different angle. Contrary to the usual assumption, organizations fail to innovate not for lack of ability but for lack of motivation. Employees who expect that leadership will retreat to the core business if a new bet does not show results quickly simply hold back from committing fully in the first place. Add that lens to competence blindness and the picture completes itself - fading capability is partly a structural problem, but it is also a rational choice made by people who have learned that exercising the capability will not matter anyway.

4. Warning Signs Inside an Organization

4.1 The quiet exit of key people

The earliest detectable sign of competence blindness usually shows up in exit interviews. Senior people who feel the organization no longer needs their judgment leave quietly. The trouble is that when they leave, so does the tacit knowledge they carried - why a design was built a certain way, which failures had already been survived.

4.2 Neglect of maintenance and foundational systems

Shipping new features is visible and gets rewarded; paying down technical debt or cleaning up pipelines is not. The result is more and more systems that only their original author can understand and operate. This is not a lack of capability - it is neglect that happens because the organization stopped putting a price on that capability.

4.3 Drift toward short-term metrics

When quarterly targets, OKRs, and dashboard metrics become the only language of decision-making, judgment, intuition, and experience - all hard to quantify - get pushed aside as "unprovable." Capability that cannot be proven eventually gets pushed out of promotion and compensation criteria too.

4.4 Erosion of the hiring bar

As Reppel points out, a hiring bar lowered during a rapid growth phase becomes the next generation's "normal." A few years later, once people hired against that lowered bar become the interviewers, the organization loses the eyes that could even recognize what the original bar looked like.

5. How to Prevent It

5.1 Preserve tacit knowledge explicitly

Mentorship programs, design-decision logs, and structured knowledge handoffs before someone leaves sound like clichés, but they are among the few prescriptions with proven effect. The key is capturing "why we did it this way" in human language, not just in code or documentation.

5.2 Make capability visible independent of outcomes

Evaluation systems need to credit not just "what got built" but "what judgment prevented from happening." Designs that averted incidents, decisions not to ship - these never show up in a metric, but they are the organization's real skill. Building this explicitly into promotion and leveling criteria is the most direct way to slow the onset of blindness.

5.3 Invest long-term in small experiments

In the same spirit as IESE's prescription, small teams with real budget authority and decision-making autonomy become the space where overlooked capability can be tested and rediscovered. That only works if leadership backs it with consistent, credible commitment - otherwise people default to the cynicism that things will revert to normal anyway.

6. What It Means for Individual Careers

The most comforting line in Reppel's essay comes at the end: "swim elsewhere, and your sight may return" (Ian Reppel). That applies to individuals just as much as organizations. If the organization you're in right now cannot recognize your carefulness, your depth, your instinct for risk, that does not necessarily mean the ability itself is gone. It may simply be going unrewarded in this particular environment. That is why changing jobs or teams sometimes works not as "new stimulation" but as the recovery of sight you thought you had lost. The reverse is also worth checking periodically - whether your own judgment is quietly dulling inside a comfortable environment.

7. In the AI Era, Organizational Capability Can Blur in a New Way

As of July 2026, this discussion gains a new layer. As generative AI takes over drafting, code generation, and first-pass analysis, organizations increasingly judge only the quality of the output without checking whether the underlying thinking that used to produce it is still intact. Recent HR-focused surveys found that a majority of respondents worry that repeated delegation to AI could erode original thinking to some degree, and in response some companies have started piloting "AI-free" judgment assessments. Interestingly, a concern also raised in the hada discussion is that large language models could accelerate convergence of organizational thinking, speeding up competence blindness itself. AI has dramatically sped up how fast output gets produced - but whether an organization keeps recognizing and cultivating the judgment behind that output remains an entirely separate question.

Conclusion: The Illusion of Still Seeing Clearly

The most dangerous part of competence blindness is that the organization does not even know it has gone blind. Revenue and metrics can still look healthy, and that illusion lets the problem sit unaddressed for far longer than it should. The capability that built success does not maintain itself. It survives only when the organization keeps consciously choosing to recognize it, name it, and reward it. As of July 2026, with AI accelerating how fast output gets produced, this is exactly the moment for organizations to open their eyes again and look at what the source capability actually was.
