Putting AI Authorship in the License - Where Permission and Disclosure Come Apart

A license grants rights. It is not a document for reporting how the code was made. Where the two come apart once you merge them

2026-09-02 Dev & Infra

Suppose you open a repository and the LICENSE file says this. Some or all of this software may have been written by an AI, and the author may not have read, reviewed or understood it. For a reader that sentence is useful. It settles, at minimum, how hard you should be looking.

What deserves a second thought is why the sentence is in the license file at all. A license is where a rights holder writes down that this much of my rights may be used on these conditions. Reporting how something was made is a different kind of act, and overlaying the two in one file makes several joints slip. This piece goes through those joints clause by clause.

This article compiles publicly available license texts and the published positions of public bodies. It is general information, not legal advice. Conclusions in copyright and contract turn on the jurisdiction and the specific facts, so a matter that requires an actual decision should go through a lawyer, or in the Republic of Korea the Korea Legal Aid Corporation. Provisions and agency positions are stated as of September 2026, and nothing here is a recommendation to adopt or avoid any particular license.

The trigger - a disclosure written in the shape of a license

In early September 2026 a document billed as a license for code its own author never read circulated in Korean developer forums. The source is a single public Gist, the file is named LICENSE, and the title reads VibeCoded AI-Slop License v1.0. The register is openly comic. Its schedule of damages includes lost weekends and existential doubt, and the last line says nobody cares.

The form, however, is not comic. Permission, provenance, quality, support, warranty, liability and a closing clause: it has the full skeleton of a permissive license. Nothing in the text states the author's purpose, so purpose is not something I will judge. The scope here is what this document does and fails to do. How a project should set policy when contributions contain AI work is covered in a separate article; the question here is narrower. Can a license compel disclosure?

What the text actually says

There are seven numbered clauses. It helps to sort them first by what kind of sentence each one is.

  • 1. Permission — use, copy, modify, merge, publish, distribute, sublicense, sell, and train an AI on it are all allowed. No permission need be sought, no attribution need be given, and modifications need not be published
  • 2. Provenance — the software may have been written partially, substantially or entirely by AI, and it lists that the author may not have written, read, reviewed, understood, tested or run it. It closes by stating that no representation is made that the software reflects the intent, skill, knowledge or conscious participation of any particular human being
  • 3. Quality — provided with no expectation of quality. The examples run to unnecessary abstractions, imaginary APIs, duplicated code, and comments describing code that no longer exists
  • 4. Support — there is none. Someone may read an issue or review a pull request, but neither is promised
  • 5. Warranty — provided as is, as generated, with all faults, disclaiming merchantability, fitness for a particular purpose, non-infringement, correctness, security and more
  • 6. Liability — no liability for any claim, damages or data loss. It adds that if you deployed it to production without reading it, that was your decision
  • 7. Final Clause — a declaration that nobody cares

What repays attention is the grammar of clause 2. The word must never appears. Everything is may have and is not made. That difference determines the rest.

The clause that looks like disclosure is a disclaimer

Sentences in a license come in roughly three kinds. Permission, saying what you may do; conditions, saying what you must do in exchange; and disclaimers, saying what is not being guaranteed. Breaking a condition undermines the basis of the permission, so conditions have force. Disclaimers are the opposite. They ask nothing of the user; they narrow the scope of the grantor's own assertions.

Clause 2 belongs to the third kind. The sentence that AI may have made this creates nothing for the user to comply with. It does not ask whoever takes the code to label it as AI-made, and it does not ask a redistributor to carry the sentence along. It is closer to: I never claimed this code reflects human craft.

The difference matters. A duty can be breached, and a breach puts the use outside the scope of the permission. A disclaimer has nothing to test. So contrary to the impression that a license has been made to force AI use into the open, what the document actually did was narrow its own side of the warranty.

The disclosure vanishes at the first redistribution

The sharper problem is the collision with clause 1. Widely used permissive licenses keep their own text alive by making it a condition that the notice ships with every copy. The MIT License says the above copyright notice and this permission notice shall be included in all copies or substantial portions of the Software. That one line is why the MIT disclaimer follows the code through ten redistributions.

The document in question has no such condition. Clause 1 instead states explicitly that attribution is not required, and nothing obliges anyone to ship the license alongside. The moment someone drops this code into their own project and redistributes it, the sentence about AI having written it has no vehicle to travel in. It never reaches the point where disclosure matters most: the person meeting this code two or three hops downstream.

# MIT — the line that makes the notice travel
The above copyright notice and this permission notice shall be
included in all copies or substantial portions of the Software.

# VibeCoded AI-Slop License v1.0 — no counterpart condition
You do not need to provide attribution.

Look at the two cells in bold. It is not that a license cannot carry a notice duty. Apache 2.0 Section 4(b) and GPL 3.0 Section 5(a) already make it a condition to say what you changed. But that duty attaches to the act of modification. It binds what the licensee did, not how the original author produced the work. That distinction drives the next section.

Permission and disclosure sit on different layers

Permission deals in rights. Who may do what, and what they owe in return. Disclosure about provenance and quality deals in facts. How this thing was made and how much of it was checked. The first can only be written by the rights holder and it binds the user. The second can be written by anyone who knows, and binds no one.

Overlay them and three things slip. The addressee differs — a license condition points at the user, while provenance is a fact about the code. The lifespan differs — a license runs for the term of copyright, whereas review status changes with the next commit, and the license file does not update itself. The unit differs — a license covers the whole repository, while provenance varies by file and by function.

None of the three makes the document void. They simply stop it from achieving its purpose. Disclosure exists so that a reader can adjust judgement, and placed where the addressee, lifespan and unit all fail to match, it gives nothing to adjust with.

The prior question - is there a copyright to license

Here the order inverts. A license can only be granted by someone who holds a right. So the phrase putting a license on AI-generated code only works once you have checked whether there is a right to attach.

The American position is comparatively crisp. In Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability, published on 29 January 2025, the U.S. Copyright Office wrote that where AI merely assists an author in the creative process its use does not change the copyrightability of the output, while at the other extreme, if content is entirely generated by AI it cannot be protected by copyright. On prompts, the Office concluded that given current generally available technology, prompts alone do not provide sufficient human control to make users of an AI system the authors of the output; prompts function as instructions conveying unprotectible ideas.

The courts point the same way. On 18 March 2025 the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit decided Thaler v. Perlmutter (No. 23-5233), holding that the Copyright Act of 1976 requires all eligible work to be authored in the first instance by a human being, and affirming the refusal of a registration that named a machine as author. The reach of that ruling should be read narrowly, though. The panel expressly declined to address whether the person who built and used the machine was its author, because that argument had been waived before the agency. It answers whether a machine can be an author, not what happens when a person works through one.

And the Copyright Office already requires disclosure — in the registration process rather than in a license. The registration guidance published in the Federal Register on 16 March 2023 (88 Fed. Reg. 16190) provides that where a work contains more than a de minimis amount of AI-generated material, the applicant should disclose that and briefly describe the human author's contribution. Zarya of the Dawn (decision of 21 February 2023) is the worked example: the human-authored text and the human selection, coordination and arrangement were covered, while the images generated by an image model were excluded.

How the Republic of Korea reads it

In Korean law the starting point is in the statute itself. Article 2(1) of the Copyright Act of the Republic of Korea defines a work as a creative production expressing human thoughts or emotions, and Article 2(2) defines an author as the person who creates a work. Reading those provisions, the guide published by the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism and the Korea Copyright Commission states that an output produced by an AI rather than a human is not itself recognised as a work.

The same guide widens the frame in its next sentence. Where human creativity has been added to an AI output through modification, augmentation, editing or arrangement, that part can be recognised as a work. And although the output itself cannot be registered, a compilation of AI outputs can be registered where there is creativity in the selection and arrangement. Conversely, trivial alterations such as fixing typographical errors or resizing are not treated as creative human involvement.

The registration procedure has the same shape as the American one. The guide directs applicants to set out in detail, in the description of the work on the application form, which parts are AI output and which parts the applicant creatively added. Here too, disclosure attaches to a procedure rather than to a license.

The last two rows explain something. Registration is not a condition for the right to exist. So most public repositories never register, and the disclosure duty that attaches to registration never reaches them. That is why a blank space is left for anyone who wants to record the fact somewhere. The license file is the most conspicuous spot in that blank, and the three slipped joints above are exactly where it sits.

No right means nothing to grant

Put the last two sections together and a structural tension appears. The document keeps a Copyright (c) [YEAR] [AUTHOR] line at the top, and two clauses later states that no representation is made that the software reflects the conscious participation of any particular human being. A line asserting a right and a line disclaiming human contribution sit in the same file.

Jumping from there to therefore the license is void would be wrong, for two reasons. First, real code does not fall wholly on one side. A person sets the requirements, revises prompts across several attempts, picks what to keep, splits it into files, and patches it. Much of that is conduct that can land inside the protected zone under the names selection, arrangement and modification. Second, in a fully permissive license a weak rights basis does not cut against the user. If there is no right to grant, there is equally no basis to restrain anyone. Where a shaky rights basis really bites is in the reciprocal copyleft family, and the duties that arise at the moment of distribution are handled in a separate piece.

What remains is this. The question is not whether the license is void, but that the document records, in its own words, that its rights basis is thin. If anyone later tries to assert rights over this code, the least helpful evidence will be clause 2, which they wrote themselves.

Warranty disclaimers are already everywhere

On warranty and liability alone there is almost nothing new here. MIT states that the software is provided as is, without warranty of any kind express or implied, specifically excluding merchantability, fitness for a particular purpose and non-infringement. Section 7 of the Apache License 2.0 provides the work on an as is basis without warranties or conditions of any kind, and Section 8 limits liability. Section 15 of the GNU GPL 3.0 occupies the same position.

What clause 5 of the document adds is breadth of phrasing. It stacks as is, as generated, as vibecoded, and extends the disclaimed qualities past correctness, security, reliability, maintainability and performance to basic competence. The list of protected parties reaches prompt authors and model providers.

  • Wider phrasing does not widen the disclaimer by the same amount. Whatever mandatory law does not allow is limited regardless of wording, which is why most licenses attach the qualifier to the maximum extent permitted by applicable law
  • The effect of a disclaimer is a question of the particular contract and jurisdiction, not something the license wording settles on its own
  • So the impression that this is stronger than existing disclaimers is largely produced by sentence length
  • The substantive difference was on the distribution side instead. As the table above showed, this document carries no notice retention condition

What 「we did not read it」 adds, and what it does not

So what does the author may not have read it add to an ordinary disclaimer. The honest answer is that there is no verified case showing what changes legally. I found no court decision on this document and no record of a dispute involving it. Still, the two directions one can guess at are worth noting, because they run opposite to each other.

  • The favourable direction — it pre-emptively lowers implied expectations about quality. Against a claim of I assumed this had been reviewed, there is room to say the basis for that assumption was explicitly removed
  • The unfavourable direction — it puts on record, in the author's own hand, that no review took place. Where circulating unreviewed code is the point at issue, that document can be read as evidence of state of mind
  • Which one prevails depends on the context of distribution. A personal repository published for nothing and a deliverable supplied under contract do not sit in the same place even carrying identical wording
  • One thing is certain. Attaching a disclaimer does not make liability vanish in every situation. That is not peculiar to this document; it is the common limit of every disclaimer

When an unverifiable condition goes into a license

Whether someone read the code cannot be checked from outside. Fortunately this document does not ask for it. It only says we did not read it either, so there is nothing to enforce. The trouble arises with conditions running the other way.

  • Do not use this to train an AI model
  • Do not merge AI-generated code into this project
  • Mark derivative works with the fact that AI was used
  • Warrant that a human reviewed it

These face the same problem in reverse. Compliance cannot be checked from outside, because there is no reliable way to determine from the code alone what produced it. The side effects come in three forms.

  • Unverifiable breach becomes routine. A condition nobody can tell is being observed leaves no trace when it is not, and a condition that leaves no trace soon hollows out
  • It becomes hard to clear legal review. An organisation with an approval process struggles to accept a component carrying conditions whose performance cannot be evidenced
  • It does not sit well with existing licenses. Clauses adding further restrictions create problems in combination with the GPL family, and whether they satisfy the open source definition is a separate question

The practical answer has been to convert the condition into something verifiable. Instead of do not use AI, the form becomes what the submitter warrants under their own name. The tool cannot be checked, but a human signature can.

Disclosure still does something

None of this should read as disclosure is useless. Lacking legal force and doing nothing are different things. What disclosure actually does is lower the reader's cost of judgement.

If a reviewer knows a change leaned heavily on tooling, they look in different places. The mistakes common in hand-written code and those common in generated code are of different kinds. Calls to functions that do not exist, plausible-looking configuration keys, exception handling that has the shape but not the substance — those cluster in the second group. One line of information helps decide where to look first.

Adjusting trust from a signal is not unique to code. The way readers use a whiff of AI to revise their judgement shows up in prose as well. But that is convention rather than rule, and for something to become convention it has to attach in a consistent place.

Where disclosure belongs

The approaches that have taken hold share a trait. Rather than attaching once to the whole repository the way a license does, they attach to a unit of change and are read at the moment of review.

The most settled example is the commit trailer. Linux kernel documentation, in Documentation/process/coding-assistants.rst, states that AI agents must not add Signed-off-by tags and that only humans can legally certify the Developer Certificate of Origin. The human reviews all AI-generated code, ensures compliance with licensing requirements, adds their own sign-off, and takes full responsibility. Tool involvement is recorded in a separate trailer.

Subject: [PATCH] net: fix use-after-free in foo_release()

    ... description of the change ...

    Signed-off-by: Jane Doe <jane@example.com>
    Assisted-by: LLM coccinelle sparse

The format is Assisted-by: LLM [TOOL1] [TOOL2], where the bracketed items are optional entries for specialised analysis tools such as coccinelle, sparse, smatch or clang-tidy. Basic tools like git, gcc, make and editors are not listed. Notice how this design avoids the three slipped joints. The addressee is the history rather than the user, the lifespan is pinned to that commit, and the unit is a single change.

The inventory side, though, has not settled. SPDX added an AI profile and a dataset profile in 3.0 and CycloneDX has extensions covering machine learning components, but these lean toward describing models and datasets as parts. I could not confirm any basis for saying a standard field exists to record that a tool wrote this function in this file.

One more thing. Using a license that is not on the list means automated tooling cannot assign it an identifier. An SPDX expression can only express such a license as a user-defined reference beginning with LicenseRef-.

# A license on the SPDX License List
SPDX-License-Identifier: Apache-2.0

# A license not on the list — only expressible as a custom reference
SPDX-License-Identifier: LicenseRef-VibeCoded-AI-Slop-1.0

The law does demand labelling, but of someone else

A requirement to label AI-generated output already exists in law. What differs is who bears the duty.

In the Republic of Korea, the Framework Act on the Development of Artificial Intelligence and Establishment of a Foundation of Trust, known as the AI Framework Act, took effect on 22 January 2026. Article 31 sets out transparency duties. Paragraph 2 requires an AI business operator providing generative AI, or a product or service using it, to indicate that the result was generated by generative AI. Paragraph 1 requires prior notification when such a product or service is to be provided, and paragraph 3 requires notice or labelling in a form users can clearly recognise where the output is hard to distinguish from reality.

The European Union's AI Act has the same structure. Article 50(2) requires providers of AI systems generating synthetic audio, image, video or text content to ensure the outputs are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated. The obligation does not apply to the extent the systems perform an assistive function for standard editing or do not substantially alter the input data or its semantics. These provisions apply from 2 August 2026.

The first row is the point. The law places the labelling duty on whoever provides the generating system. An individual who produced code with such a system and pushed it to a repository is not a direct addressee. So the blank in personal repositories remains, and attempts to fill it with a license follow. The diagnosis that a blank exists is right. Only the choice of putting a license in it slips.

What the consumer of the code should actually check

For a user, the license file is one line on a checklist. What answers has this code been reviewed usually lives elsewhere.

  • The shape of the commit history. If one commit dumps several thousand lines and almost nothing follows, no trace of a human working through it in parts was left
  • Whether tests actually run. Not the presence of files but recent execution records. Automation in place with an unbroken run of passes is the substantive signal
  • Whether issues get answers. Even in a project that declares no support, a maintainer's responsiveness shows in the history
  • Whether declared dependencies exist. A package name that does not exist does not merely fail to install. It invites someone to register that name first, as a supply chain foothold
  • Whether tooling can identify the license. If it is not on the list, scanners can only express it as a custom reference, so an organisation with an approval process needs a human decision
  • Which layer it goes into. There is no reason to apply the same bar to a throwaway script and to a component on a payment path

Notice that none of these asks directly whether AI made it. What you actually want to know is not the identity of the tool but how much a human looked at it. The tool cannot be verified; traces of review can.

What I could not verify

  • Whether the author of the original text intended it for real use or as a joke is not stated in the document. This article does not judge intent and treats only what the document does
  • I could not find any court decision or dispute involving this license. The two directions in section 10 are inferred from the structure of the clauses, not verified conclusions
  • The statutory text of Article 2 of the Korean Copyright Act could not be opened directly because the article page on the national law information portal is frame-based, so I relied on the wording quoted in the guide published by the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism and the Korea Copyright Commission
  • I did not examine the specific labelling methods and exceptions that Article 31(4) of the Korean AI Framework Act delegates to Presidential Decree. How they apply to a code repository would need separate interpretation
  • I could not confirm any concrete official interpretation of how Article 50 of the EU AI Act applies to source code
  • I could not confirm whether the SPDX 3.0 AI profile or the CycloneDX extensions have settled on a standard field for marking AI authorship at the level of code. I have recorded only that both standards carry AI-related profiles
  • I found no reliable primary data on how often non-existent package names appear in AI-generated code, so no figure is given

In summary

Three sentences. First, the provenance clause of the document in question asks nothing of the user. It is a disclaimer rather than a duty, and because another clause says attribution is not required, it disappears at the first redistribution. Second, granting a license requires holding a right, and the rights status of AI output splits by jurisdiction and circumstance. The United States has concluded that what is entirely AI-generated is not protected, and the Republic of Korea recognises only the parts to which human creativity was added. Third, which is why disclosure has settled into history, procedure and regulation.

The document circulated anyway because the diagnosis is accurate. Unreviewed code moves in volume, and the burden falls not on whoever pushed it but on maintainers and the people consuming it as a dependency. Saying that imbalance in one page is the document's achievement. The problem is not the diagnosis but where the prescription was placed.

And in whatever form it is written, a disclosure alone does not reverse that imbalance. What does is what a person warrants under their own name, and the procedure that makes it checkable. The tool will remain unverifiable. The signature will not.

To restate: this article is not legal advice. The effect of a license, the reach of a disclaimer and the ownership of AI output all turn on jurisdiction, contract and specific facts, and nothing written here is a determination that can be applied directly to a particular matter. If a real decision is needed, consult a lawyer — in the Republic of Korea, the Korea Legal Aid Corporation — and an organisation should obtain internal legal review.
