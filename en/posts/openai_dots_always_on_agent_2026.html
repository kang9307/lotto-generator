This article covers the Dots announcement OpenAI made on September 29, 2026. The official announcement page could not be opened, so the article relies on the GeekNews summary and on wording quoted in Unite.AI and MarkTechPost coverage. No figure is invented, and sentences relayed by coverage are labeled as such.

What was announced - Dots, an always-on agent

Dots are always-on agents that OpenAI built on GPT-6 Astra. Unlike a chatbot that answers a question and stops, each dot has its own cloud computer and browser and keeps several projects moving at once. The central sentence of the announcement is that the work continues after the user disconnects. Instructions can be given not only in ChatGPT but also in Slack and Teams, and context is described as carrying over wherever a task started.

The examples the announcement offers are plainly work-shaped. Turning recurring customer feedback into tested pull requests. Rerunning a research analysis each time new data arrives. Revising launch materials when scope changes. Updating an enterprise sales proposal as requirements shift. Preparing clips and show notes from interview transcripts. In one early test, a dot noticed that a user had forgotten to invoice a publication, prepared the invoice, and sent it after the user approved.

The interest of this article is not the feature list but what to look at when granting permissions to an agent that is always on. A tool that works only while someone sits at the chat window produces its mistakes in front of a person. A tool that runs while nobody is present leaves its mistakes only as records. That is why, even within one announcement, the sentences about limits, rules and data are the material for an adoption decision rather than the sentences about features. Those sentences are read here in four groups.

  • Connected apps. Being able to reach more than 4,000 apps also means the reachable surface is that wide.
  • Read-only scope. What matters is where the boundary lies between restricted and unrestricted actions.
  • Approval rules. The point is who approves, when, and how far a user can change those rules.
  • Data use. Whether work content feeds model improvement is worded differently for each plan.

Why being always on changes the permissions discussion lies on the time axis. In a tool someone watches, approval sits naturally inside the work flow. In an agent that runs while the user is logged off, approval requests either pile up to be handled later or, if the rules are too loose, pass without approval. Either way the cause of an incident is the design of the permissions, not the capability of the model. That is why the same model can carry very different risk depending on how the rules are written.

One more point concerns the nature of the sources. The limits and rules quoted here are wording from a product announcement and its coverage. An announcement shows design intent, but whether actual behavior matches the wording is for the adopter to verify by use. For that reason each section closes with questions that the wording alone cannot answer.

The safety side of agents in the same family was treated on another axis in the article on an OpenAI autonomous agent reaching government sites and sending images, namely the limits of consent and control. This article does not repeat that axis and concentrates on the limits and rules that this announcement puts forward directly.

First, connected apps - 4,000+ versus the apps actually connected

The reported wording says a dot connects to more than 4,000 apps through the plugin ecosystem. That number is closer to the ceiling of what can be connected; the number of apps one user's dot actually reaches is however many that user has connected. The announcement says a dot reaches the apps the user has connected along with its own cloud computer. There is also an explanation that the user's laptop stays separate from the dot unless the user connects it.

Read as permissions, the connected-app question leaves two things. One is the unit of connection. Whether connecting an app can be limited to reading, or brings the whole account permission of that app along, differs by app and the announcement does not say. The other is the manner of sign-in. According to coverage, a dot signs in with saved passwords without exposing them to the model. That the model does not see the password is a separate question from what can be done with that credential.

Wider connections widen what a single instruction can touch. The case of turning customer feedback into a pull request, for example, ties three connections into one flow: reading a support tool, modifying a repository, and running tests. In that flow permissions are granted app by app, yet the result merges into a single task. So when reviewing the connection list, it is right to look not only at the risk of each app but at the paths that appear once they are tied together.

  • Nature of the number. More than 4,000 is the size of the plugin ecosystem, not the number a single account uses. For an adoption review, the benchmark is the number of apps actually used.
  • Per-app rules. The GeekNews summary says allow, approve and block rules can be set per app. Whether they can be split by action type inside one app cannot be told from the source wording.
  • Kind of account. Connecting a personal and a work account to the same dot may let one rule apply to both. Separating the work account before connecting is the safer course.
  • Route for disconnecting. What happens to that app's information in the context and memory a dot has already received after a connection is cut is not in the announcement.

Second, the read-only scope - proactive research versus assigned work

The phrase read-only is easy to take as reassurance, so its scope needs to be read exactly. The reported sentence concerns proactive research, the stage in which a dot gathers material on its own without an instruction from the user. Tools in that stage are restricted in code so they cannot send messages, change content in connected apps, or control a browser or computer. The emphasis is that the restriction lives in code rather than in a prompt instruction. Even if the model tried to disobey, the tool does not support the action.

Everything outside that falls under other rules. When a user assigns work, a dot can send email or change files, and coverage reports that a separate system called Auto-review steps in before such actions. Auto-review checks whether the planned steps match the user's instructions, custom rules and safety requirements. The controls that enforce it are said to sit outside the environments a dot can change. In short, read-only is a restriction on the proactive research stage, and the safety of assigned work is handled by approval rules and Auto-review.

  • Restricted actions. Sending messages, changing connected-app content, controlling a browser or computer. Blocked by code in the proactive research stage.
  • Unrestricted actions. Write actions in work the user assigned. Approval rules and Auto-review apply here.
  • Movement of research results. The data wording also notes that information from proactive research notes can enter later conversations or tasks.
  • A question for the adopter. The range of material readable in the research stage, meaning which data in which apps, has to be defined separately.

Third, approval rules - custom rules and fixed confirmation points

Approval rules have two layers. The first layer is the built-in rules, which decide when a dot acts on its own and when it asks for approval. The second is custom rules. A user can allow a specific action, require approval for it, or block it. According to coverage, however, custom rules cannot remove mandatory confirmations, handoffs or core safety requirements. There is a floor under the direction of loosening the rules.

Where that floor lies can be seen in the reported fixed confirmation points. The table below carries the wording as reported.

Fixed confirmation pointReported contentCan a user rule override it
Password changesMandatory handoffNo (falls under mandatory confirmations and handoffs)
Transfers between financial accountsMandatory handoffNo
Permanent deletionConfirmation required each timeNo
Installing unrecognized softwareConfirmation required each timeNo
Card purchases on merchant sitesApproval requiredNo

All five items in the table are actions that are hard to reverse or that move money. Conversely, actions not on this list, such as sending email or editing files, remain in the area that Auto-review and user rules govern. The invoice example in the announcement shows the same structure: the dot prepares the invoice by itself, and sending passes through the user's approval. Where the user places the points that require approval sets the size of the autonomy.

The character of the fixed points also deserves notice. All five are actions that are hard to undo once executed or that move money. That the design lays a floor under such actions reads as meaning that even if user rules are loosened widely, the worst paths stay closed. It does not mean that every action outside the list is safe, however. Actions absent from this table, such as sending email or changing sharing settings, can have larger effects on an organization, and that judgment falls to the organization's own rules.

  • Checking the defaults. The right order is to confirm as a list which actions the default rules allow automatically, then layer user rules on top.
  • Approval delivery route. Whether approval requests arrive in ChatGPT, Slack or Teams, and whether that route can be seen in time, governs real control.
  • Scope of an allow rule. The wording must be checked on whether a rule allowing one action applies to the whole app or only to specific targets.
  • Monitoring. Coverage says monitoring can pause or stop a dot when a safety concern is detected. The criteria and notifications are not in the announcement.

Fourth, data use - where the wording differs by plan

The data wording splits by account type. According to coverage, OpenAI said it does not use content from ChatGPT Business, Enterprise or Edu workspaces to train models by default. On personal accounts, the model-improvement setting decides whether a dot's conversations and work may be used. The word default matters here. A sentence saying content is not used by default leaves room to differ by setting or contract, and its scope is fixed not by the announcement but by the plan's terms and admin settings.

Two more pieces of wording attach. Models are not trained directly on proactive research or its notes, but information brought from that research into an eligible conversation or task may be used depending on settings. And a dot receives memories and recent conversation context from ChatGPT, while conversations with a dot can contribute to ChatGPT memory. Individual dot memories currently cannot be viewed or edited, and resetting a dot reportedly deletes its conversations, saved memories and scheduled tasks together. The combination of memory that cannot be seen and cannot be edited is a fact to record before adoption.

In reading data wording, whether something is used for training must be separated from whether it is stored. The sentence saying content is not used for training concerns use for model improvement; whether work content is stored as a dot's memory or conversation record is a different matter. According to coverage, memories and scheduled tasks stay inside the dot and disappear only on reset. What meshes with an organization's retention policy and deletion-request procedure is the latter, so it is better to set the two questions side by side and get an answer to each.

  • Workspace wording. The sentence that Business, Enterprise and Edu content is not used for training by default has been reported. The exact scope is confirmed in the official announcement and the terms.
  • Personal accounts. The model-improvement setting decides. A setup that entrusts work material to a personal account's dot should check this setting first.
  • Where Pro sits. Whether Pro counts as a personal plan or has separate wording is not confirmed in this article's sources. It is not filled in by interpretation.
  • Crossing of memory. Memory and context pass between a dot and ChatGPT. Whether information deleted on one side remains on the other is not in the source.
  • Age. Dots are reported as not yet available to users under 18.

No extra cost and always on - where conditions attach

The rollout terms stack three conditions: plan, region and timing. According to coverage, the rollout began on September 29, 2026; Pro users receive it in markets excluding the European Economic Area, Switzerland and the UK, and Business Premium users in all supported ChatGPT regions. The rollout is gradual and may take several days to reach an account. Enterprise, Edu and Healthcare are an admin-enabled beta, off by default. Whether these terms apply to the organization is the first thing to confirm.

The no-extra-cost sentence has boundaries too. What is included is the first dot. Conversations with a dot do not count toward ChatGPT usage limits, but tasks a dot starts or manages in Codex or ChatGPT Work count as usual. Adding more dots, or growing output through speed and monthly work volume, is mentioned as a future capability. Whether the phrase always on means there is no usage limit appears in none of the sources.

  • What is included. The first dot. The conditions for additional dots and output scaling appear in the sources only as future capabilities.
  • Distinction of limits. Conversations are outside the limits, while Codex and ChatGPT Work tasks are inside. Felt limits vary with which side the work flow touches.
  • The first month. Usage in the first month after launch is reported as not counting against plan allowances. The basis after that is not in the sources.
  • Where they are created. Dots currently cannot be created on mobile or mobile web, as reported. Text messaging is mentioned only as a limited beta for US Pro users.

Adoption order - starting from narrow permissions

Turning the four groups above into a procedure gives an order that starts narrow and widens. What the source wording permits is a possibility, and how far to allow it is the adopter's decision. What follows is a confirmation order that follows directly from the wording. Each step moves on only after the result of the one before it.

  • Step 1, eligibility. Confirm that your plan and region fall within the rollout terms and, for the Enterprise family, that an admin activation procedure exists.
  • Step 2, connected-app list. List the apps to connect to the dot by job unit, and split those needing read access only from those needing write access.
  • Step 3, start with proactive research. Begin in the proactive research stage, where writing is blocked, and see what the dot reads and what it proposes.
  • Step 4, write approval rules. Decide allow, require approval or block per action. Fixed confirmation points cannot be overridden, so concentrate on actions outside them.
  • Step 5, check the approval route. Decide the channel where approval requests arrive and who responds, and confirm that a request waits safely when that person is absent.
  • Step 6, compare data wording. Compare your plan's terms and admin settings for model-improvement use, memory sharing and the scope of deletion.
  • Step 7, keep records. Leave the decisions above and the dates of confirmation in a document. Announcements change and so do restrictions.

What these steps share is that they separate granting permission from checking permission. The announcement supplies the list of what the agent can do, and the organization makes the list of what it may do. The narrower the gap between the two, the fewer approval requests and the larger the gain from automation, but the decision to narrow the gap should come after the checks above are finished.

Last comes the question of a baseline. Wording right after launch is a description of the current state, not a settled contract. Among the limits quoted here, that dots cannot be created on mobile, that memories cannot be viewed, and that they are not offered to minors are all written in the present tense. The dates of confirmation in an adoption document are therefore a baseline, not a formality. When behavior changes later, that date and that wording are the only starting point from which to compare what has changed.

What could not be verified

  • The official OpenAI announcement page. Access was denied for both the English and Korean versions, so the article depends on the GeekNews summary and quotations in two news reports. Wording in coverage may differ from the official sentences.
  • Usage limits for always-on operation. No source has wording on limits or a daily task ceiling.
  • The data-use wording that applies to Pro accounts. The sources give only workspace wording and the personal-account setting.
  • The list of the 4,000-plus apps and the granularity of per-app permissions. Whether app-level allow, approve and block reach down to action types cannot be told.
  • The full list of default rules and the range of actions Auto-review steps in on. Beyond the five fixed confirmation points there is no published list.
  • The usage-counting basis after the first month and the price of additional dots.
