The Model Got Better and Working With It Got Harder

Capability and collaboration fit are different axes — the four kinds of friction, the incentive mismatch, and how to manage the switch

Aug 16, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: the score went up and the day got longer

The morning after switching to a new version, you give it the same task and the output comes back twice as long. Two files you did not ask about have changed, and there is a sentence up front explaining how it interpreted the request, except that interpretation is subtly not what you meant. The code runs. The benchmark numbers clearly went up. And you are noticeably more tired than yesterday.

This feeling is not new. Every time a model generation turns over, a post in this exact shape appears within days. One went up on August 15. The author wrote that the new version leads on benchmarks but that earlier versions were better to work alongside, and pointed at three things those versions did: they stopped and asked when intent was unclear, they did not make assumptions without checking, and they did not rewrite the author's plan on their own judgment.

This article is not here to rule on whether that observation is correct, and it is not a quality verdict on any particular model. The question underneath it is the interesting one. How is it structurally possible for a model to get more capable while the experience of collaborating with it gets worse? This is not a one-time story about one changeover. It recurs in the same shape at the next one. Recurring phenomena have recurring causes, and knowing the causes means not being surprised from scratch every time.

How to design the act of handing work over — goal, context, authority boundaries, checkpoints, undo — is covered separately in delegating to AI. Here the design is assumed to exist, and the only question is what happens when the tool side changes underneath it.

1. Why better and harder is not a contradiction

The two statements sound like they collide because we treat 「good」 as one axis. It is actually two.

The first axis is capability: how much of a hard problem the system solves when left alone. That is largely what benchmarks measure, and it tends to rise with each generation.

The second axis is collaboration fit: the total cost of a loop with a person inside it. This one includes behaviors beyond raw skill. Does it stop where it does not know? Is the output a size a human can actually review? Does it leave the plan you wrote alone? Does it answer today's request in roughly the shape it answered yesterday's?

The two axes correlate but are not the same axis. So these combinations genuinely occur:

  • Capability up, fit up — the ideal case, and what most generation changes aim for
  • Capability up, fit down — better alone, more tiring together. This is the case this article is about
  • Capability down, fit up — a smaller, faster model that is easier to live with on a bounded task. Common in practice

What matters is that the second combination can be a trade rather than a defect. A disposition to push all the way through on its own scores as a strength on autonomous-execution tasks and registers as friction on tasks where a human interrupts. The same disposition gets a different name depending on where it lands.

And the axis has more than one direction. The opposite complaint is just as steady: it asks too often, nothing gets done because it keeps checking, it makes you press approve twenty times. That complaint reliably shows up around tools tuned toward asking. The two complaints are opposite ends of one dial. So resolving this as 「asking is always better」 is half right at best. The correct dial position differs by task type.

2. The four kinds of friction and where each comes from

「It is harder to work with」 gets said as one lump, but pulled apart it contains four different things. Different causes need different responses, so identifying the type is the first practical step.

The first three have tool behavior as the cause; the last has your own assets going stale. But they are hard to tell apart by feel, because all four register identically as 「more work than yesterday」. Respond without separating them and you fix the wrong thing — adding stop conditions when the problem is a stale prompt, or rewriting the prompt when the problem is scope.

3. Proceeding without asking — when the direction of autonomy shifts

The first type gets named most often, and is also the easiest to misread. 「More autonomy」 is usually said as a compliment, but autonomy is a quantity with a direction, not just a magnitude.

More execution autonomy and more interpretation autonomy are completely different stories. Execution autonomy means walking the intermediate steps toward a fixed goal on its own. Interpretation autonomy means settling the goal itself. The former is generally welcome; the latter is generally friction. The three things the original author pointed at — asking, not assuming, not rewriting the plan — are all constraints on the latter.

3.1 Ambiguity is a constant, not a thing to remove

This is why 「just write clearer requests」 only half works. Ambiguity in real work does not come only from the requester being lazy. Some things cannot be decided at request time: things that settle only after opening the existing code, things where both ways work and team convention decides, and things the requester themselves does not know until seeing a result. That is ambiguity that cannot be removed in advance, by definition.

So the requirement for a working tool is not 「make ambiguity disappear」 but 「what does it do on encountering ambiguity」. There are three options: stop and ask, assume and proceed while surfacing the assumption, or assume and proceed silently. The third is the worst, and not because the result is wrong — it is because the moment of discovery is late.

3.2 Cost climbs steeply with the point of intervention

  • Caught at the plan stage before work starts — one or two minutes to read and correct
  • Caught at an intermediate artifact — partial discard, losses bounded to that stretch
  • Caught at the finished output — the whole thing must be reread, plus the separate cost of locating where it diverged
  • Caught after commit or deploy — the undo becomes larger than the original work

That curve is what makes a single clarifying question valuable. Asking spends 30 seconds of your time; one wrong commitment spends 30 minutes. Conversely, if asking becomes too frequent, 30 seconds becomes twenty times 30 seconds, and then not asking is the cheaper policy. Which side is cheaper depends on the undo cost of the task, not on the virtue of the tool.

3.3 So where do you set the dial

  • Cheap-to-undo work — refactoring on a branch, first drafts. Better to proceed without asking
  • Expensive-to-undo work — schema changes, outbound calls, data deletion. Checkpoints are required
  • Work with open forks — stretches where design choices remain. Take the plan first
  • Work where you know the answer — give the acceptance criterion as a command. Asking becomes unnecessary

4. Verbosity is not a matter of taste, it is review cost

Verbosity looks like the pettiest of the complaints and is far from the cheapest in practice, for one reason. Review cost attaches to the size of the artifact. Deliver the same change as 200 lines and 200 lines must be read; if only 30 of them are the real change, the other 170 hide it.

A few shapes get reported repeatedly in community threads: more comment than code, the summary repeated three times, documentation files generated that nobody asked for. These are individual user reports rather than measured values, so they are not numbers to cite. That the shapes repeat, though, is a signal worth noting.

Structurally, this direction is not strange. It is hard to grade 「covered everything」 and 「wrote at length」 as cleanly separate signals. When the two blur, writing long costs nothing, and behavior that costs nothing survives. The inertia of safe answers stacks on top: attach every condition and caveat and no grader docks you much.

  • Put the output shape into the acceptance criteria — 「changed-file list and diff only」, 「code without commentary」
  • If explanation is needed, fix its location — the last three lines of the reply, not inline comments
  • Block byproducts through authority boundaries — no new files, no writes outside a named path
  • Record change size as a metric — how the diff line count for the same task moved across the version boundary

5. What training rewards versus what real use wants

There is one explanation that ties the earlier types together. Only what is graded improves. And a large part of collaboration fit sits where grading is hard.

5.1 Binary grading gives no credit for asking

The paper Why Language Models Hallucinate, published by Kalai and colleagues in September 2025, locates the persistence of hallucination in the grading structure. The argument is simple: if an answer of 「I do not know」 is scored as the same zero as a wrong answer, then in expectation always guessing is the better strategy. The paper calls this a structure that penalizes uncertainty, and its proposed fix is not to add new hallucination evaluations but to change the scoring of the existing benchmarks that already dominate the leaderboards.

The argument extends past hallucination without modification. Asking is a form of abstention. On a scoreboard that gives abstention no credit, abstaining behavior has no room to grow. Benchmark tasks have one settled answer and nobody to ask, so asking falls out of the measured set entirely. What is not measured can degrade without anyone noticing.

5.2 Where verifiable rewards work and where they do not

Tasks whose correctness a machine can judge — do the tests pass, does the arithmetic check — give a clean learning signal. Skill on those tasks rises fast. The problem is that the shape of that task set differs from real work.

  • Benchmark task: requirements fully specified. Real request: about half of them missing
  • Benchmark task: one settled answer. Real request: several are correct and one of them is your team's convention
  • Benchmark task: done in one shot. Real request: three or four round trips
  • Benchmark task: nobody to ask. Real request: the requester is sitting right there, and reviewing the process costs as much as the result

All four say the same thing. Real work has a person in it, and the cost of having a person in it does not enter a benchmark score. That gap reopens each generation not because benchmarks are bad but because benchmarks must be reproducible, and reproducibility requires removing the person. For a wider view of what benchmarks miss, see behavior quality that knowledge benchmarks cannot show.

5.3 There is a second objective in play

The original author named another pressure: that a development direction aimed at systems capable of improving themselves favors a disposition to judge and push forward independently. That is the author's conjecture, and stated as one. The logic of the direction is still worth following. If running long without human intervention is the objective, then stopping to ask a human is a deduction against that objective. A disposition optimized for autonomous execution and one optimized for a loop with a human are not the same thing.

6. Adaptation cost — prompts are not the only thing that ages

A good share of the discomfort in the days after a switch comes not from the tool getting worse but from your own assets going stale. That part is not fixed by tool improvement. It is paid off in time and labor.

6.1 Prompts are fossil beds of old failures

Open an instruction file you have used for a long time and roughly half of it is defensive clauses. Do not create files on your own, do not repeat the summary, do not write explanation outside the code block — nearly all of those were added after a specific failure at a specific time. When that failure mode disappears, the clause stops doing work. It does not get deleted, because verifying that it is safe to delete costs something.

What remains is not harmless. It consumes instruction budget while buying nothing, and it dilutes the relative weight of the rules you add later. A model change is the moment to shorten prompts, not lengthen them.

6.2 The cost to a team is a multiple of the cost to a person

  • Individual — a few instruction files, snippets, and a mental sense of how much can be handed over
  • Team — shared convention files, review checklists, onboarding docs, the practices you used to wave through in code review
  • Product — output parsers, post-processing rules, prompt templates, eval sets, cost models and latency budgets
  • Organization — approval gates, audit criteria, risk classifications, incident response procedures
  • Common to all — human calibration. The sense of 「it will get this right without being told」 is recalibrated wholesale

The last item is the quietest and the most expensive. People learn a tool's failure modes and place their review points near them. When the failure modes move, the review points stay sitting where they no longer catch anything. That does not surface until an incident does.

6.3 Practical notes

  • In the first week, do not edit instructions — just record differences. Editing on two or fewer observations turns coincidence into rule
  • When you do edit, try deletion before addition. Remove one defensive clause; if the result is unchanged, that clause was already dead
  • Product-side assets are harder to move than prompts. Where lock-in accumulates is laid out layer by layer in what you cannot move is prompts, eval sets and embeddings
  • Put adaptation cost on the team schedule as a line item. Left off, that time comes out of somebody's evening

6.4 Where should expectations be pinned

Part of the fatigue in a changeover comes from pinning expectations to the wrong objects. Sort out what to pin them to and the same change shakes far less.

  • Do not pin to — a specific version's voice and formatting. Nothing guarantees it survives to the next release
  • Do not pin to — the efficacy of prompt wording. Knowing that a phrasing worked well is version-local knowledge
  • Do not pin to — the range of 「it will handle this much on its own」. That value has to be re-measured each time
  • Do pin to — acceptance criteria and checkpoints. Tests passing, output shape, change scope, and the places where undo cost spikes stay put when the tool changes
  • Do pin to — the regression case set. Drawn from your own work, so it keeps working regardless of version
  • Do pin to — authority boundaries. What must not be allowed holds independently of tool maturity

The rule is simple. Do not pin expectations to things attached to the tool; pin them to things attached to the work. The first three vanish with the version; the last three survive several generations.

7. Three axes for managing a transition — pin, shadow, regress

Handle 「it feels worse」 as an emotion and nothing remains; handle it as a procedure and it becomes an asset for the next changeover. The minimum configuration is three things.

Pinning is the mechanism that lets you decide when the change happens. Use an identifier pointing at a specific snapshot instead of an alias that always points at the newest release, and behavior does not shift on a morning you did not choose. Anthropic's model deprecation documentation splits the lifecycle into active, legacy, deprecated and retired, and states a minimum of 60 days notice before retirement for publicly released models. Those 60 days are the transition budget. Without pinning, that budget does not exist.

Shadowing is the mechanism that lets you see the difference. Send the same input to both versions and lay the outputs side by side, and what was an impression turns into concrete differences. Look at ten places where the two outputs differ and they usually collapse into three or four types.

Regression checking is your own standard. Benchmarks measure someone else's work; a regression set measures yours. You do not need to invent cases. Collect 20 to 50 real requests from the past few weeks, mixing ones that went well with ones that did not, and that is the set. Building eval sets and setting pass criteria for nondeterministic output are covered in change, verification and rollback design for production prompts.

8. Is perception evidence — and what should be measured

The statement 「it is worse than before」 cannot be verified or refuted as it stands. That does not make it ignorable. Perception usually points precisely at something; it is just that the something is occasionally not what the speaker named.

8.1 A case where perception ran opposite to measurement

The randomized controlled trial METR published in July 2025 addressed this directly. Sixteen experienced developers, averaging five years on their own repositories, worked through 246 tasks, and the results split into three layers. The forecast before starting was a 24 percent speedup, self-assessment afterwards was a 20 percent speedup, and actual measurement was 19 percent slower. Perception ran opposite to measurement. METR published follow-up data in February 2026 and noted that it had changed the experiment design.

Reading that result as 「therefore user perception is worthless」 is half right. The direction of divergence in that study was overestimation. The perception under discussion here is a negative assessment in the other direction. An opposite direction is no guarantee that divergence is absent, nor that it is present. Only one conclusion follows. Perception can diverge either way, so settling the direction requires measurement.

8.2 Why community reaction is not a sample

  • Self-selection — satisfied users do not write posts. Only dissatisfied ones enter the record
  • Change bias — what changed is visible and what stayed the same is not, so improvements can register as friction too
  • Different task mixes — a disposition that is friction for one person is an advantage for another, and the more polished someone's instruction files are the harder staleness hits
  • Early window — the first days after a switch mix in the human learning curve and do not separate from tool factors

None of that makes community signal worthless. Repeated reports of the same shape tell you the type. Unusable as numbers, usable for deciding what to measure. An anecdote is material for a hypothesis, not grounds for a conclusion.

9. Why this recurs at every changeover

If this were a one-off, building a procedure would be overkill. But the pattern recurs, for three reasons, and none of them is going away soon.

First, benchmarks measure with the person removed. They have to, to stay reproducible. But collaboration fit is exactly the property that appears only when a person is in the loop. Something structurally excluded from measurement cannot be expected to improve by accident each generation.

Second, user assets are version-dependent. Prompts, conventions and review habits are all assembled against one version's failure modes. The more finely assembled they are, the larger the loss at a switch. That is where the paradox comes from: the people who worked hardest at using the tool well hurt most at a changeover.

Third, improvement has more than one direction. Getting better at autonomous execution and getting better at looping with a human partially conflict. Which one gets pushed harder can differ by version, and to users that difference feels like a personality change.

So the practical conclusion is not 「which model is good」 but 「how do we absorb the change」. The former question goes stale in six months; the latter stays valid. 10. What could not be confirmed

  • No public quantitative data measuring the collaboration fit of a specific version could be confirmed. The type classification here organizes the shape of reported symptoms; it is not a measurement result
  • Figures mentioned in community threads such as comment ratios or response length increases are individual reports, and the measurement conditions are not public, so they are not cited as numbers
  • Which training choices led to which behavioral changes has not been published. Section 5 follows general logic from public discussion of grading structures, not any model's training record
  • The optimal frequency of clarifying questions differs by task type, and there is no basis for a general answer
  • The METR trial targeted tools from the first half of 2025 and does not transfer directly to the current generation. It is cited here only for the point that perception and measurement can diverge

Conclusion: friction is information, but what it is information about has to be measured

A model getting better while collaborating with it gets worse is logically possible and does happen. Capability and collaboration fit are different axes, and the second one is structurally hard to catch on a reproducible scoreboard. So dismissing the feeling as 「users failed to adapt」 and declaring it as 「the model got worse」 are both premature.

Three things are worth keeping. First, split friction into types. Proceeding without asking, scope expansion, verbosity and stale assets need completely different fixes. Second, pin expectations to things attached to the work. Acceptance criteria, checkpoints, regression sets and authority boundaries survive versions; prompt wording and tone go with them. Third, make the transition a procedure. Pin, shadow, compare against a regression set. Build it once and the next changeover does not start from surprise.

And finally, whichever way this generation moved on that axis, the next one moves again. There is no way to stop that. The only available move is lowering the cost of absorbing it, and that is a question about our preparation rather than about the tool.
