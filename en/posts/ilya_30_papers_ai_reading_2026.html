Ilya Sutskever's 30 Papers: How to Actually Read the AI Papers That Matter

What a reading list said to have been given to John Carmack teaches about the order and mindset for learning AI

Jul 9, 2026 Tech & IT

Intro: Why "30 Papers" Is Making the Rounds Again

An old story resurfaced recently on GeekNews (news.hada.io). Once upon a time, Ilya Sutskever, then a co-founder of OpenAI, is said to have handed legendary game developer John Carmack a list of papers, saying that learning them would cover 90 percent of what mattered. The site 30papers.com, built around this story, organizes that list (or what is said to be that list) with plain explanations so beginners can actually work through it.

What makes the site interesting to me is not the list itself but the question it revives: why does understanding AI properly still require reading papers at all? In an era where you can just use a finished product like ChatGPT or Claude, is there really a reason to read a 2012 image-recognition paper or a 2017 transformer paper? As of July 2026, rather than reproduce the original list, this piece reconstructs the big themes this curation covers in my own words, lays out a practical way for beginners to actually approach these papers, weighs the value and limits of this kind of curation, and adds a few tips from a Korean learner's vantage point.

1. What Is "30 Papers" - Source and Reliability

Before getting into the content, one thing needs to be said plainly: this list is not something Sutskever himself officially published. Everything below should be read with an "as far as is known" caveat.

1.1 The Carmack-Sutskever Story

As far as is known, the story starts around 2020, when John Carmack privately asked Sutskever for a reading recommendation. Carmack is the game-engine developer who co-founded id Software and later pivoted toward deep learning and AGI research. The story became public in February 2023, when Carmack posted on social media hoping Sutskever would publish the list openly. In other words, this list is not something Sutskever officially released - it is Carmack's recollection, reassembled by the community. Even the count varies by source, reported as "about 30" or "about 40" papers.

1.2 30papers.com and Its Derivatives

Despite this uncertainty, several communities have worked to reconstruct and organize the list. 30papers.com is one such effort, pairing each paper with plain-language explanations to make it more accessible to beginners. Similar attempts exist as GitHub repositories, blog series, and study notes, and the fact that the exact items included vary slightly from version to version is itself evidence of the list's uncertain provenance. Rather than treat that as a problem, I think it is more accurate to understand this as something closer to a community-negotiated canon than one person's precise curation.

2. The Big Themes This List Covers - Reading It as a Map, Not a Table of Contents

Instead of reproducing the original list, let me reconstruct the flow this curation covers into four broad chunks.

2.1 Laying Deep Learning Foundations - The Image-Recognition Revolution

The earlier part of the list generally covers the moment deep learning broke through practically, around 2012. This includes the episode where a convolutional neural network exemplified by AlexNet overwhelmed prior methods in an image-recognition competition, and the subsequent problem of training even deeper networks reliably. The core question in this stretch is why deep networks suddenly started working at all.

2.2 Sequential Data and Memory - From RNNs to Sequence-to-Sequence

The second chunk shifts from images to sequential data such as text or speech. It covers how recurrent networks like LSTMs implement something like memory, and how architectures that transform one sequence into another became the basis for tasks like translation and summarization. This stretch is the background needed to understand why today's language models grew out of predicting the next token sequentially.

2.3 Attention and Transformers - The Root of Modern LLMs

The third chunk carries the most practical weight in the whole list. The 2017 paper that introduced the attention mechanism broke past the sequential-processing limits of RNNs, leading to the transformer architecture, which computes the relationships among all pairs of words in a sentence at once. Virtually every modern large language model, including GPT, Claude, and Gemini, stands on this architecture. If a beginner is short on time, my view is that this stretch is the one section not to skip.

2.4 Scaling and Generalization - Why Bigger Tends to Be Better

The final chunk is relatively theoretical. It covers papers that examine, from a computational-complexity and information-theory angle, why models generalize to new inputs rather than simply memorizing training data, along with the scaling perspective that performance improves predictably as model and data size grow. This stretch is not something you need for writing code today, but it is the background knowledge behind why building bigger models is a rational strategy in the first place.

3. A Practical Approach for Beginners to Actually Read These Papers

Even with the list in hand, knowing where to start is a separate problem. Here is the approach I actually recommend.

3.1 Prerequisites First, in Order

Reading papers in random order is a recipe for frustration. The order I recommend follows the four chunks above: CNN foundations, then RNNs and sequences, then attention and transformers, then scaling and generalization. With just a basic sense of linear algebra and probability - matrix multiplication and probability distributions, roughly - the first two chunks are manageable, and that experience builds the confidence needed for the third chunk.

3.2 Implement in Parallel - Don't Just Read

Reading a paper and moving on rarely makes the concept stick. Pairing reading with something like computing attention scores in a short piece of code, or training a small CNN on a tiny dataset, makes understanding click far faster. Annotated versions that walk through a paper alongside its code have also become more common recently, and using them narrows the gap between the equations and the implementation.

3.3 Use Community Explanations as a Stepping Stone

Papers are written in a compressed, conference-submission style that is hard to parse on a first read. Reading blog explanations from well-known researchers and engineers alongside the original text speeds up comprehension considerably. This is exactly the role a site like 30papers.com plays. That said, relying only on explanations risks missing details you can only get from the original - experimental design choices, or the authors' own acknowledgment of limitations - so it is better to treat explanations as an entry point and still check the original text.

4. The Value and Limits of This Kind of Curation

4.1 The Value: A Compressed Map

For a beginner overwhelmed by the sheer number of AI papers out there, having a set of roughly 30 said to be selected by a credible figure saves a great deal of time on its own. Even if the list's exact provenance is uncertain, the fact that the papers it actually contains do trace the key inflection points of deep learning is something multiple independent write-ups of the list agree on.

4.2 The Limit: A Map Weighted Toward the Early 2020s

The clearest limitation is timing. As far as is known, the list's original form took shape around 2020. That means it barely reflects the alignment techniques (like RLHF) that exploded after 2022, mixture-of-experts architectures, or the recent wave of autonomous agents and reasoning models. This list is an excellent map for understanding how modern LLMs got here, but it is not an up-to-date map of how they work right now. Anyone working through it should plan to separately catch up on recent developments once they finish.

5. Tips From a Korean Learner's Perspective

5.1 The English-Language Barrier

Most of the papers on the list exist only in English, and translated versions, where they exist, vary widely in quality. For the first pass or two, I recommend getting a feel for the abstract and conclusion through a summary, then checking the key equations and figures against the original text.

5.2 Lean on Study and Practice Communities

Working through this alone is harder than doing it with a Korean AI study group or online community reading the same list together - completion rates go up. Study groups that share implementation code in particular have the natural effect of enforcing the read-and-implement pairing mentioned above.

5.3 Applying It to Career and Research

Korean AI job interviews commonly ask candidates to explain the transformer architecture or how attention works. Properly digesting just the third chunk - attention and transformers - builds enough of a foundation to answer that kind of question. That said, in practice, knowing the real-world performance and cost trade-offs of current models matters more than the papers themselves, so it is realistic to treat studying these papers as building foundational strength rather than an end in itself.

Conclusion: Three Things You Can Start Today

The list's exact provenance cannot be verified, but the value it holds as a learning map still stands as of July 2026. Here are three things I would recommend doing.

First, build a study plan around the four chunks - CNN foundations, RNNs and sequences, attention and transformers, scaling and generalization - and if time is short, make the attention-and-transformers stretch the priority.

Second, don't just read papers - pair them with short code implementations, and use explanations as an entry point without skipping the original text.

Third, once you finish the list, be sure to separately catch up on the last few years of alignment, agents, and reasoning-model developments. "30 Papers" is a great starting point, not a finish line.
