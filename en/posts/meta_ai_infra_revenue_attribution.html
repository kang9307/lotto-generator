The original does not disclose absolute revenue figures or specific confidence intervals, and this article does not estimate them. The question — why the value of infrastructure never reaches the ledger

When a model gets better and revenue rises, the gain is booked to the team that built the model. The cluster that trained it, the foundation layer that kept serving stable, the platform that made experiments run twice as fast — none of these appear in the ledger. That is why infrastructure teams face the same question at every budget review: so how much did revenue rise because of you?

The post that Meta's analytics organization published on 11 September 2026 is an attempt to answer that question with a number. Its authors are Tong Wu, Yang Zhang, Maggie Chen, Brad Hedges, Konstantin Golyaev, Gniewko Lubecki and Paolo Massimi. To state the conclusion first: for seven infrastructure platforms across the ML stack, they estimated from observational data how much more revenue a model launch that adopted a platform produced than one that did not, and found that the training-scalability platform, with lower adoption, produced roughly twice the incremental revenue per launch of the highly adopted foundation layer.

What matters in this article is not the number two. It is the procedure that led to it — what was taken as the unit, what was controlled, what was checked, what was assumed — because that is the part another organization can take away. The sections below follow that procedure in order.

  • The unit is the launch, not the team. The launch is where a comparison group arises and where revenue attribution already lands.
  • Controls were chosen only among things that affect both adoption and revenue. Not that four is enough, but that this is the criterion for choosing.
  • The comparison group was screened for technical applicability first. Skip that step and no amount of statistical refinement afterwards keeps the comparison clean.

Why random assignment is impossible

The effect of a product feature is measured with an A/B test: split users at random, give the feature to one side, observe the difference. That method does not work for infrastructure. The reason the original gives is simple: model launches and engineers cannot be randomly assigned to platforms. Which team uses which platform is decided by that team's needs and judgement, and the choice itself is entangled with the outcome.

The direction of the entanglement is easy to guess. A team building a large model is more likely to use the training-scalability platform, and large models have larger revenue potential to begin with. The observation that "launches on the platform had higher revenue" could then be the platform's effect, or it could be that strong launches chose the platform. Unless the two are separated, the contribution of infrastructure is over- or underestimated.

The second difficulty is the path of value. Infrastructure's value flows through other teams' work. The platform team does not produce revenue directly; the launches that model teams build on top of it do. Each extra link in the chain of attribution makes measurement harder.

So many organizations use substitutes instead. None of the three is an answer.

  • Adoption rate. How many teams use something is not how much they need it. A mandated standard has 100% adoption and no defined effect.
  • User surveys. The answer to "did it help" measures the relationship with the platform team, not revenue.
  • Cost saved. How much compute time was cut is a cost line. What the budget review asks about is the revenue side.

Unit and sample — about 258 launch proposals, two halves

The unit of analysis is the individual model launch proposal — not the team and not the platform. There were about 258 proposals in one half-year, and two consecutive halves were combined to double the sample. Each proposal carries which platforms it adopted, how much revenue the launch contributed, and the values of the confounders listed below.

ItemValue
Unit of analysisIndividual model launch proposal
Sample sizeAbout 258 in one half-year; doubled across two consecutive halves
ScopeSeven infrastructure platforms across the ML stack
Types named in the originalProduction model-foundation layer (high adoption) and training-scalability platform (lower adoption, used for larger models)
Outcome variableIncremental revenue per adopting launch (absolute amounts not disclosed)
Published and authors2026-09-11, seven authors at Analytics at Meta

Choosing the launch proposal as the unit solves two things at once. First, for each platform a comparison group of adopting and non-adopting launches arises naturally. Second, in an organization that already attributes revenue at the launch level, no new outcome variable has to be built. Conversely, where those two conditions are absent, the same design does not hold.

Method — the assumption that extracts a causal effect from observational data

Without random assignment, reading the relationship between adoption and outcome as causal requires an assumption. The one the original states is selection on observables: the factors that decide whether a launch adopts a platform are observed in the data, and once they are controlled, the adopting and non-adopting groups become comparable.

The strength and the weakness of this assumption sit in the same place. The strength is that estimation is possible without an experiment; the weakness is that an unobserved factor affecting both selection and outcome distorts the estimate. If something like a team's ambition pushes both platform choice and revenue, and it is not in the data, it is wrongly counted as the platform's effect. That is why which variables were controlled becomes the heart of the method.

  • The comparison group is the non-adopting launches of the same period. Launches that did not use the platform stand in for the counterfactual — what would have happened had they used it.
  • The estimate is incremental revenue per adopting launch. Not the platform's total contribution, but the average amount one adoption added. Multiply by the adoption rate to get the total.
  • The uncertainty is larger than in an A/B test. The original states that the confidence intervals are wider than in a randomized experiment, without giving their width.

The four confounders controlled

The original names four control variables. Alongside each is the bias it prevents.

ConfounderDefinition in the originalBias if left uncontrolled
Compute capacityGPU allocationLaunches that received more resources both use the platform and earn more, so the platform gets the credit
Human capital investmentTeam size, seniorityStrong teams' tendency to adopt new platforms first is counted as an effect
SeasonalityModel production cycleIf peak-season launches cluster on one platform, the timing effect gets mixed in
Model complexityModel size and complexityLarge models use the training-scalability platform and earn more anyway, so the effect is double-counted

All four are things that decide adoption and also influence revenue. That is the definition of a confounder and the criterion for choosing controls. A variable that influences adoption but not revenue need not be included; one that influences revenue but not adoption improves precision without changing the bias.

Result — the training-scalability platform at roughly twice the foundation layer

The results for the two platforms the original names are interesting in opposite directions.

  • The training-scalability platform had lower adoption and was used for larger models. Even after controlling for the confounders, its incremental revenue per adopting launch was roughly twice that of the foundation layer. A platform used rarely, but working strongly where it is used.
  • The production model-foundation layer had high adoption. But the revenue difference visible before controls was mostly selection: strong launches had simply used the layer, and the share by which the layer made launches stronger was small.
  • The other five platforms have no individual results in the original. Only the fact that the same method was applied to all seven is stated.

Placing the two results side by side shows what the method is for. By adoption rate alone, the foundation layer is the more important platform — everyone uses it. But adoption is not effect. Whether everyone uses something and whether using it changes anything are different questions, and causal inference separates the latter. In the room where the next budget is allocated, the number needed is the latter.

At the same time, the unit of the "twice" must not be forgotten. It is a value per adopting launch. Because adoption is low, converting to total contribution could reverse the order. The original does not disclose totals, and this article does not compute them.

Reporting this result to leadership means presenting two numbers together: the effect per adoption and the adoption rate. The first says how much the platform does when it is used; the second says how much it is used. The training-scalability platform is high on the first and low on the second, so the budget direction becomes getting more launches to use it. The foundation layer is high on the second and low on the first, so pushing adoption is not the answer; raising the effect itself or lowering its cost is. That the same method prescribes opposite treatments for the two platforms is the practical value of the result.

Four robustness checks

An estimate from observational data rests on assumptions, so how well the result holds when the assumptions are shaken has to be checked separately. The original lists four checks.

  • Vary the estimation window to test sample stability. For example one half alone, both combined, the period shifted — check that the result points the same way.
  • Screen the counterfactual group for technical applicability. Among non-adopting launches, those that could not have used the platform at all are not comparators. Keep only the launches that could have used it and did not.
  • Adjust for cross-platform overlap with matching. When a launch adopts several platforms at once, compare, for example, similar combinations to keep other platforms' effects from mixing in.
  • Triangulate with alternative measurement approaches. Re-measure the same question by a different route and check the answers do not contradict each other.

Of the four, the second is the one most often skipped in practice. Putting every non-adopting launch into the comparison group lets launches that technically could not adopt — a model type the platform does not support, say — contaminate the comparison. Screening for applicability first is closer to domain knowledge than to methodology, which is why an analytics team cannot do it alone.

Preconditions for carrying the method elsewhere

The original lists four preconditions for the method to hold. An organization lacking them will not get the same kind of answer by following the same procedure.

PreconditionMeaningWhat happens without it
Existence of a counterfactual groupEnough launches that did not adopt the platformWith adoption near 100% there is nothing to compare against and no estimate
Launch-level adoption trackingA record of which launch used which platformTeam-level records are too coarse to filter out confounding
Revenue attribution granularityRevenue divisible to the launch levelIf revenue is only captured per product, no outcome variable can be built
Assembling confounder dataGPU allocation, team composition, timing and model size joined per launchOne missing variable leaves that axis of selection bias untouched

The last three preconditions are data-pipeline problems. Launch-level adoption records, launch-level revenue attribution, and per-launch resource, team and timing information must be joined into one table. In most organizations that join takes longer than the analysis. The first precondition is a matter of organizational structure. In an organization where every team is required to use the same platform, the method has nothing to measure — without an alternative, no effect is defined.

With the preconditions in place, the order to start in is as follows.

  • Run the naive comparison once first. Simply compare the mean revenue of adopting and non-adopting launches. How much that value shrinks after controls is the size of the selection effect — and as with the foundation layer, most of it can vanish.
  • Judge applicability together with the platform team. Which launches could technically have used the platform is something the analytics team cannot know alone. That judgement table is the definition of the comparison group.
  • Validate one half-year's result against the next. The original, too, used two halves. A single-period estimate sways with chance; two periods pointing the same way are grounds for a prescription.

Limits and cautions in reading

  • The confidence intervals are wide. A limit the original states itself. "Roughly twice" is a point estimate, and the width around it is not disclosed. Whether it is wide enough to reverse the ranking cannot be known from the original.
  • Unobserved confounding remains. The four variables are observable ones. If factors such as team priorities or leadership attention acted on both selection and outcome, they are mixed into the estimate.
  • Per-launch and total effects differ. A platform with low adoption can have a large per-launch effect and a small total. Budget allocation must look at both.
  • It is one company at one point in time. The result comes from two halves, seven platforms and Meta's model launches. There is no guarantee the same direction appears in another industry.
  • How this article read the original is also a limit. The original is a company blog, not a reviewed paper. The method is standard, but the implementation details are not published.

What could not be verified

  • Absolute revenue amounts and the width of the confidence intervals. Not disclosed.
  • The names and individual results of the other five platforms.
  • The specific estimator — regression, matching, weighting — used as the main model. The original mentions only the selection-on-observables assumption and matching for overlap adjustment.
  • Whether "roughly twice" is statistically significant, and by what criterion.
  • The criteria used to judge technical applicability, and who made that judgement.
  • Whether the result was reflected in actual budget allocation.
