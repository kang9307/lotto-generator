The trigger - not every 45 minutes, but at minutes 0 and 45

The article on cron.withuse.io that GeekNews introduced states its conclusion in the title: cron's */45 is not a 45-minute interval. According to the GeekNews summary, the expression runs with alternating 45-minute and 15-minute gaps and never settles into a steady 45-minute cycle. The FAQ on the same site has a passage that states the principle in one sentence: a step applies to the range in front of the slash, not to the clock.

The trap survives so long because the expression looks exactly opposite to a reader's intuition. Since */5 means every five minutes and */15 means every fifteen, one infers that */45 means every forty-five. The first two cases are right only because 5 and 15 happen to divide 60 evenly, not because cron promises every N minutes. This article strips away that coincidence and writes down the rule itself. Once the rule is known, not just */45 but other traps with the same structure, such as */7, 0 */5 and 0 0 */10, can be picked out from the expression alone. The rule is short, but it is stated in the documentation rather than in the syntax, which is why it is missed.

A syntax tutorial is not the purpose here. Three things are covered: the definition of what a step actually does, the result of executing that definition as a check, and the methods available when a 45-minute interval is genuinely required. Whether behaviour is the same across implementations is dealt with at the end by separating the scope confirmed from documentation from the scope that was not.

What a step does - it picks multiples of the start value inside the field

The cronie documentation (crontab(5)) defines a step this way. Following a range with a slash and a number specifies skips of that number through the range. The example is 0-23/2 in the hour field, meaning every other hour. Immediately after, comes the sentence that is the subject of this article: steps are evaluated just within the field they are applied to. The example in the documentation is */23 in the hour field, which means the job runs at hour 0 and hour 23 within a calendar day. The same documentation gives */35 in the minute field as a case that runs at minute 0 and minute 35 of each hour rather than every 35 minutes.

Unpacked, the definition reads as follows.

  • The range comes first. The asterisk means the whole range of that field (0 to 59 for minutes, 0 to 23 for hours), and a step skips through that range from the start value.
  • The calculation ends inside the field. A step in the minute field does not continue across the hour boundary; it starts over at minute 0 of every hour.
  • The result is multiples of the start value. With a start value of 0, only those of 0, N, 2N and so on that fall within the range remain. It is not every N minutes but at times that are multiples of N.
  • What is left breaks apart at the boundary. If the length of the range is not a multiple of N, the gap from the last value to the start value of the next range is shorter than N.

The fourth item is the identity of */45. Skipping by 45 through the minute range 0 to 59 leaves 0 and 45, and the next would be 90, which is outside the range. When the clock crosses into the next hour and minute 0 arrives, the calculation begins again from the start. So the gap from minute 0 to minute 45 is 45 minutes and the gap from minute 45 to minute 0 of the next hour is 15 minutes, and the two alternate. The systemd.time documentation has a definition of the same structure. When a value is suffixed with a slash and a repetition value, the value itself and the value plus all multiples of the repetition value are matched. When attached to a range, all times starting with the start value and continuing with multiples of the repetition value, ending at the end value at the latest, are matched. The notation differs, but the definition of multiples relative to a start value inside a range is the same. In short, the step never sees the clock, only the field.

Verification by execution - the range expanded in Python

Whether the definition holds can be checked by hand, but with several expressions mistakes creep in. The script below expands each field range with range and computes the gaps between neighbouring run times, without touching any crontab. It was run on Python 3.13 and carries no load. Because the script only does integer arithmetic on ranges, its result does not depend on the machine it runs on. The last two parts check a full day of */45 and the four-line arrangement (section 7) that produces an exact 45-minute cadence.

import calendar
                    
                    # step expansion inside one field range (no crontab access)
                    def expand(lo, hi, step):
                        return list(range(lo, hi + 1, step))
                    
                    # gaps between neighbours, including the wrap to the next period
                    def gaps(vals, period):
                        g = [b - a for a, b in zip(vals, vals[1:])]
                        g.append(vals[0] + period - vals[-1])
                        return g
                    
                    cases = [("*/15 * * * *", 0, 59, 60, 15), ("*/20 * * * *", 0, 59, 60, 20),
                             ("*/45 * * * *", 0, 59, 60, 45), ("*/40 * * * *", 0, 59, 60, 40),
                             ("*/25 * * * *", 0, 59, 60, 25), ("*/7 * * * *", 0, 59, 60, 7),
                             ("0 */8 * * *", 0, 23, 24, 8), ("0 */5 * * *", 0, 23, 24, 5),
                             ("0 */7 * * *", 0, 23, 24, 7)]
                    for expr, lo, hi, period, step in cases:
                        v = expand(lo, hi, step)
                        print(expr, v, sorted(set(gaps(v, period))))
                    
                    # one day of */45: clock times, gaps, runs per day
                    mins = [h * 60 + m for h in range(24) for m in expand(0, 59, 45)]
                    print([f"{t // 60:02d}:{t % 60:02d}" for t in mins[:6]],
                          sorted(set(b - a for a, b in zip(mins, mins[1:]))), len(mins), 1440 // 45)
                    
                    # day-of-month field: 0 0 */10 in April, May, February 2026
                    days = expand(1, 31, 10)
                    for m in (4, 5, 2):
                        n = calendar.monthrange(2026, m)[1]
                        d = [x for x in days if x <= n]
                        print(m, d, d[0] + n - d[-1])
                    
                    # exact 45-minute cadence from four lines (3-hour cycle, midnight wrap included)
                    lines = [(0, (0, 21, 3)), (45, (0, 21, 3)), (30, (1, 22, 3)), (15, (2, 23, 3))]
                    t = sorted(h * 60 + m for m, (a, b, s) in lines for h in expand(a, b, s))
                    print(sorted(set(y - x for x, y in zip(t, t[1:] + [t[0] + 1440]))), len(t))

What can be read from the result is as follows.

  • The third line is */45. The minute values are only 0 and 45 and the set of gaps is {15, 45}. That means 45 minutes is not a single kind of gap.
  • The tenth line is a full day. The schedule runs 00:00, 00:45, 01:00, 01:45 and so on, and the number of runs per day is 48. A true 45-minute cycle would be 1,440 minutes divided by 45, which is 32, so in reality the job runs half again as often.
  • Expanding the hour-field */23 that the cronie documentation cites with the same function gives [0, 23]. The description in the documentation and the rule in the script do not disagree.
  • The last line is the exact 45-minute arrangement. The set of gaps is the single value {45} and the number of runs is 32 (section 7).

The general rule - when a step divides the field and when it does not

Generalizing the results above reduces to a single test. If the number of values in the field (60 for minutes, 24 for hours) is divisible by the step, the gap holds across the boundary; if it is not, one short gap intrudes at the boundary. The table organizes the expansion results of the same script by expression. Where a gap takes two or more values, they are listed in order.

Three rules stand out in the table.

  • The safe steps in the minute field are the divisors of 60: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30. These values give the same gap at every hour.
  • The safe steps in the hour field are the divisors of 24: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8 and 12. Values such as 5 or 7 look like a whole-hour unit, but a short gap intrudes at the end of the day.
  • A value that is not a divisor has to be read not as every so many minutes but as at times that are multiples of N within the day or within the hour. Making that reading a habit lets a trap be recognized from the expression alone.

The larger the step relative to the length of the range, the more pronounced the drift. Because 45 minutes is close to 60, the leftover piece is as much as 15 minutes, and the result is not an occasional short gap but a rise in daily runs from 32 to 48, a factor of 1.5. That difference translates directly into cost and quota for jobs that carry load, call external APIs, or are billed per run. A team that budgets by counting runs per day will notice the difference first in the quota, not in the schedule.

In the hour and day fields the drift shows more

Drift in the minute field ends within one hour, so it is hard to notice. The hour and day fields are different. 0 */5 * * * runs at hours 0, 5, 10, 15 and 20, and because the next run after hour 20 is hour 0 of the next day, only the last gap is 4 hours. An operator who believed it runs every 5 hours meets an unexpected run at midnight, 4 hours after the run at hour 20. Because the problem arises near midnight, checking in daytime misses it. The same pattern appears in every field whose length is not a multiple of the step.

The day field has one more feature. The range 1 to 31 in the day field has a different real length in each month. 0 0 */10 * * picks days 1, 11, 21 and 31, but in a month with no 31st, the run after the 21st is the 1st of the next month. The values the script above calculated for three months of 2026 are as follows.

Month of 2026Days on which it runsFrom the last run to the first run of the next month
April (30 days)Days 1, 11, 2110 days

In May, one day after the run on the 31st it runs again on June 1. A job expected to run every ten days can therefore run two days in a row at month end. The month field */5 likewise leaves only January, June and November in the range 1 to 12, and because the run after November is January of the next year, a gap of two months intrudes.

Such drift is no one's fault; it follows from the definition that a step attaches to a field and not to a clock. So the alternative lies not in rewriting the expression further but in using some other basis than multiples calculated inside a field (section 7).

Is it the same everywhere - the scope confirmed from documentation

Several implementations exist under the name cron, and when the scheduler differs the meaning of an expression can differ. The scope in which this article confirmed sentences directly from official documentation is shown in the table below. Implementations that could not be confirmed are left as a dash, and the reason is given later under what could not be confirmed.

Implementation or featureWhat the documentation says about stepsLevel of confirmation here

Vixie cron, Quartz, Kubernetes CronJob——

Three points in the table deserve mention. First, that the same characters */45 can mean different things depending on the implementation is a general remark and not a fact this article confirmed. The two documents actually confirmed, cronie and systemd.time, use different syntax and the same multiples rule. Second, the fact of using the same rule does not mean all implementations are the same. The empty last row marks that boundary. Third, schedulers in cloud and container environments, such as Kubernetes CronJob, need their own documentation checked, and it is safer not to assume the same rule merely because an expression looks like cron. A safe habit is to write down, for each scheduler in use, which document the expression semantics were read from.

When an exact 45-minute cadence is needed

The methods available when a true 45-minute cycle is needed are divided into three by the kind of evidence behind them.

1. The relative timer of a systemd timer (confirmed from documentation). In the systemd.timer documentation, OnUnitActiveSec= is a timer relative to when the unit the timer activates was last activated. Written as OnUnitActiveSec=45min, the next run comes 45 minutes after the last run regardless of the clock time. The documentation also defines OnUnitInactiveSec=, which is based on when the unit was last deactivated. Whether the time a run takes counts toward the interval is the difference between the two options, and which to use depends on whether the duration of the job varies. Combinations with the other options that decide the first run were not verified here.

2. Four lines with cron alone (verified by calculation). The least common multiple of 45 minutes and 24 hours is 180 minutes, that is, 3 hours. A three-hour cycle contains four run times (0:00, 0:45, 1:30, 2:15), so writing them as four lines is enough.

  • 0 0-21/3 * * * — minute 0 of hours 0, 3, ..., 21
  • 45 0-21/3 * * * — minute 45 of hours 0, 3, ..., 21
  • 30 1-22/3 * * * — minute 30 of hours 1, 4, ..., 22
  • 15 2-23/3 * * * — minute 15 of hours 2, 5, ..., 23

The last block of the script above expanded the times these four lines produce and calculated the neighbouring gaps including the stretch that crosses midnight (23:15 to 00:00). The result is the single gap value {45} and 32 runs a day. This method is a wall-clock schedule tied to the time boundaries, so time zones with daylight saving time and environments where clock time jumps were not verified. Because there are four lines, the management burden also grows.

3. Checking the time inside the job (a general design with no documentation behind it). The script is woken at a short interval (for example every minute), records the last success time in a file, and exits at once if 45 minutes have not passed. The documents quoted in this article do not describe this approach. Its features are that it works under any scheduler and that responsibility for guaranteeing the interval moves from the expression to the script. The script has to bear the locking and the failure to update the record file. A wrapper of this kind is also the natural place to log why a run was skipped.

The criterion for choosing among the three is simple. In a systemd environment the first is the most direct. In an environment where only expressions are available, switching to a divisor of 60 (30 or 60 minutes) rather than insisting on 45 is the safest, and if the meaning of a 45-minute interval is truly needed, the second or third is used.

A read-only check for traps in an existing crontab

A crontab on a server already in operation may still hold the same trap. Every check below is read-only and carries no load.

  • Use crontab -l only to print what is registered for the current user. The edit option (-e) is not used.
  • Gather the expressions of the form */N and see whether N is a divisor of 60 in the minute field and a divisor of 24 in the hour field.
  • For those that are not divisors, expand the actual run times in the manner of the table above and compare with the intent of the job (an exact interval, or simply a frequency).
  • Mark the */N in the day or month field separately, since its gap wavers at month end and year end whether or not N is a divisor.
  • If the expansion differs from the intent, first decide whether the job requires an exact interval before rewriting the expression. If it does not, changing to a divisor of 60 is enough.

Keep the result of the check as a record. When it is written down which expression produces which real times and why it was left as it is, the next person in charge does not repeat the same reasoning from the beginning. A short note beside each flagged expression is enough.

What could not be confirmed

  • The full body of the original article. What opened on cron.withuse.io was part of the FAQ, and the conclusion of alternating 45 and 15 minutes was taken from the GeekNews summary.
  • Step handling in Vixie cron, Quartz and Kubernetes CronJob. Only the cronie and systemd.time documents were opened, and the official documentation of the rest was not.
  • Execution on a real daemon. The calculation expands the definition and was not confirmed from logs by starting cron or systemd.
  • The real result of systemd OnCalendar when written as *:0/45 in the minute field. It is an inference from the definition and was not confirmed with a tool such as systemd-analyze calendar.
  • Combinations of OnUnitActiveSec with the options that decide the first run, and the behaviour of the four-line arrangement in time zones with daylight saving time.
