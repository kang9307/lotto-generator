Introduction: Your Deposit May Be Everything You Own

For many renters, a lease deposit is not just "rent money" but effectively their entire net worth. We hand over a sum ranging from tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of dollars and then live in someone else's property. So when the landlord changes, the home goes to auction, or the lease ends and the deposit is not returned, the shock is not a mere inconvenience but a life-shaking crisis. Fortunately, the law protects this vulnerable party through a special statute: Korea's Housing Lease Protection Act.

The problem is that too many people have "heard of it but don't really understand it." Why resident registration and a fixed date matter, how opposing power differs from priority repayment, when and how to use the renewal request right, and what role a lease registration order and a return guarantee play when the deposit goes unpaid — this article gathers, in one place, the essentials a tenant needs to protect a deposit. It centers on Korea's Housing Lease Protection Act, while repeatedly noting that all figures and thresholds can change by time and region.

What Is the Housing Lease Protection Act?

The Housing Lease Protection Act is a special law created to protect tenants who would be inadequately covered by the general lease provisions of the Civil Act. It sets exceptions more favorable to tenants than ordinary principles, aiming to support housing stability and deposit recovery for those who rent. Its core has two branches. The first is opposing power, which protects you from being evicted even when the home you live in passes into other hands. The second is the priority repayment right, which lets you recover your deposit ahead of others when the home is sold at auction or public sale.

Crucially, this protection is not granted "automatically." The tenant must personally meet the prescribed requirements (move-in, resident registration, fixed date) before the law's shield activates. That is why two people paying the same deposit can end up so differently — one keeps it all, the other recovers nothing. The concepts in the next sections make that difference.

Opposing Power - The Force to Stay Even If the Home Is Sold

Opposing power is the legal force allowing a tenant to assert the lease against a new owner (transferee) or other interested party of the leased home. Put simply, even if the home you live in is sold or auctioned, you can stand up to the new owner and say, "I am a lawful tenant. I may live here for the lease term, and my deposit must be returned."

2.1 Requirements - Move-in Plus Resident Registration

Opposing power arises once two requirements are met: first, delivery of the home (actual move-in); second, resident registration (the move-in report). Once both are done, opposing power takes effect from 00:00 the next day. That "next day" matters. Because effect begins the day after the report rather than on the same day, a single day between your registration and the timing of another right (such as a mortgage) can decide your fate.

One line to remember: opposing power begins the day after "move-in + resident registration." So once you move, register without delay.

2.2 When the Landlord Changes - The Duty to Return Is Inherited

When the home of a tenant with opposing power changes owner through a sale, the new owner is deemed to succeed to the former landlord's position. That means the duty to return the deposit also passes to the new owner. So a sale does not by itself cost you your deposit; the tenant can assert occupancy for the lease term and return of the deposit at maturity against the new owner. That said, you may struggle to prevail against senior rights (such as a mortgage) created before you acquired opposing power, which is why checking the register before move-in is so important.

Priority Repayment Right - Your Place in Line at Auction

The priority repayment right is the right to recover your deposit ahead of junior right-holders and general creditors from the sale proceeds when the leased home is disposed of by auction or public sale. If opposing power is "the force to stay," the priority repayment right sets "your place in line to be paid."

3.1 Requirements - The Move-in Requirements Plus a Fixed Date

The priority repayment right is acquired by meeting (1) the move-in requirements (delivery of the home and resident registration) plus (2) a fixed date on the lease contract. A fixed date is a system that officially stamps a date onto the contract, proving the contract existed at that point in time — a safeguard preventing the landlord and tenant from later faking the date to manipulate the repayment order.

3.2 Where to Get a Fixed Date

A fixed date can be obtained at the eup/myeon office, dong community center, city/county/district branch office, district court and its branches, registry office, or notary in the area where the home is located. These days you can also obtain one online through the Internet Registry Office and similar channels. The cleanest approach is to bring your contract when you go to file your resident registration and get the fixed date at the same time. The institutions that grant fixed dates are listed in Article 3-6(1) of the Housing Lease Protection Act.

If you file a rental report, you do not need a separate fixed date. A housing lease with a deposit over KRW 60 million or monthly rent over KRW 300,000 (excluding county areas outside Gyeonggi Province) must be reported jointly by landlord and tenant to the local reporting office within 30 days of signing (Article 6-2 of the Act on Report on Real Estate Transactions). For contracts signed on or after June 1, 2025, failing to report within 30 days or reporting falsely leads to a fine, and the fine for late reporting ranges from KRW 20,000 to KRW 300,000. If you submit the contract with the report, a fixed date is deemed granted and its number is printed on the lease report certificate, so no separate application is needed (Korea Policy Briefing, April 28, 2025).

Opposing power and the priority repayment right are separate. If you do resident registration but skip the fixed date, you may have opposing power to assert occupancy against a new owner yet lack the priority repayment right to be paid first at auction. Treat resident registration and the fixed date as a set.

Small-Deposit Priority Repayment - A Safety Net for the Most Vulnerable

The priority repayment right pays out "in the order of the fixed date," but a low-income tenant with a small deposit can be pushed down the line and recover nothing. The small-deposit priority repayment system exists to prevent this. A small-deposit tenant whose deposit is below a certain threshold can recover a fixed amount ahead of even other secured creditors, regardless of the fixed date or repayment order.

4.1 Requirements and Cap

To receive priority repayment, the deposit must be at or below the region-specific threshold, and the tenant must have met the move-in requirements (move-in plus resident registration) before the auction commencement is registered. There is also a cap: if the total amount protected by priority repayment exceeds half the property's value (the winning bid), only up to that half is recognized (Enforcement Decree, Article 10(2)). The move-in requirements met before the auction registration must also be maintained until the deadline for demanding distribution.

4.2 Regional Thresholds Are Set by the Enforcement Decree

The deposit threshold to qualify as a small-deposit tenant and the priority repayment amount are set by Articles 10 and 11 of the Enforcement Decree of the Housing Lease Protection Act, which divides the country into four bands. These thresholds are revised periodically to track property prices. The current text checked on the National Law Information Center on September 29, 2026 still carries the amounts set by the February 21, 2023 amendment, shown below.

RegionSmall-deposit threshold (at or below)Priority amount (up to)

So if the register shows an older mortgage, the calculation may follow the previous thresholds. The safe course is to check the creation date of any security interest on the register and then ask an expert which band applies to you.

Key point: priority repayment is a "small-deposit" safety net. If your deposit exceeds that threshold, you won't get this protection — so pair it with other tools like the priority repayment right (fixed date) and a deposit guarantee.

Lease Term and Renewal - The Renewal Request Right

The Housing Lease Protection Act guarantees a minimum lease term. Even where no term was set or a term shorter than two years was set, a tenant may assert two years. Added on top of this by the 2020 amendment is the renewal request right.

5.1 One Renewal, Two Years Guaranteed

A tenant may request a renewal of the contract from the landlord between six and two months before the lease ends, and this renewal request right may be exercised once. The renewed lease term is treated as two years. So on top of the base two years, you can add two renewal years, supporting the often-cited "2+2 years" of housing stability.

5.2 Grounds for the Landlord to Refuse

The landlord, however, is not obliged to grant renewal unconditionally. Renewal may be refused for statutory grounds, such as where the landlord (or their lineal ascendants/descendants) intends to actually move in. If a landlord refuses renewal citing self-occupancy but then leases to a third party without just cause, liability for damages to the tenant can arise. Because this area sees frequent disputes, specific situations call for professional advice.

5.3 Implied Renewal

If the landlord fails to give notice of refusal or of changed conditions between six and two months before the term ends, an implied renewal on the same terms as before may apply. Under an implied renewal, the tenant can terminate the contract relatively freely, among other distinct rules.

The Cap on Rent Increases - The Rent Cap System

If a landlord could raise the deposit or rent at will on renewal, the renewal right would lose meaning. Hence the rent cap system was introduced alongside it. When renewing by exercising the renewal request right, even if the landlord demands an increase in rent or deposit, it generally cannot exceed 5% (one-twentieth) of the existing amount (Housing Lease Protection Act, Article 7(2)).

That said, this 5% cap applies to renewals made by exercising the renewal request right; it is understood not to apply to a brand-new contract first signed with a new tenant. An increase cannot be demanded within one year of the contract or of the previous increase (Article 7(1)), and a special city, metropolitan city, special self-governing city, province, or special self-governing province may set a different cap by ordinance within that 5% range (proviso to Article 7(2)). Detailed calculations, such as the conversion rate between deposit and monthly rent, follow separate rules, so if an increase seems improper, verify it.

The 5% cap applies to "renewals by exercising the renewal right." Its application may differ for a new contract signed by agreement or where the tenant changes, so first clarify whether you have a renewal or a new contract.

When Your Deposit Isn't Returned (1) - The Lease Registration Order

The lease ends, you must move out, but the landlord won't return the deposit. The most dangerous move here is to "just move out and drop your resident registration." The moment you lose the move-in requirements (residence plus registration), you also lose opposing power and the priority repayment right.

7.1 A Device to Keep Your Rights Even After Moving

The system that resolves this dilemma is the lease registration order. If a tenant who has not received the deposit after the lease ended applies to the court and completes the lease registration, the opposing power and priority repayment right already acquired survive even after moving out and transferring registration. In other words, it is a safety device that lets you move to a new home without fear of losing the deposit.

7.2 Overview of the Procedure

The lease registration order is filed with the district court, its branch court, or the city or county court having jurisdiction over the location of the leased home (Housing Lease Protection Act, Article 3-3(1)). You must show the lease contract, the fact that the lease ended, and the fact of non-return of the deposit. Once the registration is complete, the lease is recorded on the property register and becomes assertable against later right-holders. Since the amendment of April 18, 2023, Article 292(3) of the Civil Execution Act (which allows execution even before the decision is served on the debtor) applies to this procedure, so the registration can be carried out even before the order is served on the landlord. The costs of the application and registration can be claimed from the landlord (Article 3-3(8)). If you must move out without the deposit, it is generally recommended to file for a lease registration order before moving.

When Your Deposit Isn't Returned (2) - Demands and Lawsuits

Once you have preserved your rights through lease registration, the next step is actually collecting the money. Depending on the situation, several paths exist.

  • Send a certified letter (content-certified mail): formally demand return of the deposit and leave evidence for any future dispute.
  • Payment order (summary procedure): a relatively simple way to obtain a debt title where there is little dispute.
  • Deposit-return lawsuit: if the landlord does not comply, obtain a judgment through a formal lawsuit.
  • Compulsory execution / auction filing: based on a title such as a judgment, put the leased home to auction and receive a distribution.

For a small claim, you can use the small-claims adjudication procedure to cut cost and time. If you are in financial hardship, look into the free legal counseling and litigation aid of the Korea Legal Aid Corporation. Because requirements and effects differ for each procedure, it is safest to decide which path fits your situation with an expert.

When Your Deposit Isn't Returned (3) - The Deposit Return Guarantee

Lawsuits take time and money. So the surest preventive step is to take out, in advance, an insurance under which "the guarantor returns the deposit even if the landlord can't." This is the deposit return guarantee.

9.1 Guarantor Institutions - HUG, HF, SGI

The representative guarantor institutions are the Korea Housing and Urban Guarantee Corporation (HUG), the Korea Housing Finance Corporation (HF), and Seoul Guarantee Insurance (SGI). When the tenant pays a premium and signs up, the guarantor pays the deposit first if the landlord cannot return it at maturity, then seeks reimbursement from the landlord. Enrollment conditions, guarantee limits, and premium rates differ by institution, so compare and choose a product matched to your property and deposit.

9.2 Cautions on Enrollment and Claims

The return guarantee can be taken out only when enrollment requirements (housing type, ratio of senior debt to deposit, etc.) are met, and at maturity you must file a claim with the prescribed procedures and documents. Missing a prerequisite such as lease registration can lead to a claim being rejected, so it pays to learn the procedure from enrollment through the maturity claim in advance. Some local governments subsidize premiums for groups such as young people and newlyweds, which is worth checking.

Enrollment criteria have also changed. In February 2023 the government announced that the jeonse-to-price ratio for HUG's deposit return guarantee would be lowered from 100% to 90% and that house prices would be assessed with priority on actual transaction prices and official prices rather than appraisals; the 90% standard applied to new jeonse contracts from May 1, 2023 (Korea Policy Briefing, February 2, 2023). Later institution-specific details (price assessment ratios, guarantee limits, premium rates) could not be rechecked in official sources for this update, so confirm the current criteria with each guarantor before enrolling.

Jeonse Fraud Prevention Checkpoints

The best response is to avoid a risky contract in the first place. Dividing this into before, during, and after the contract, here are the essentials.

10.1 Before the Contract - Check Rights and Market Value

  • Check the property register: look for senior mortgages, provisional seizures, and trust registrations, and confirm the true owner (landlord) matches.
  • Underwater-lease check: if the deposit plus senior debt is excessive relative to market value, you may recover nothing at auction.
  • Check the building ledger: illegal uses such as neighborhood-living facilities can create problems for resident registration and opposing power.
  • Beware of agent deals: carefully verify authority via power of attorney, seal certificates, and the like.

10.2 During and After - Finish With Registration and a Guarantee

  • Resident registration + fixed date on move-in day: secure opposing power and the priority repayment right as fast as possible.
  • Enroll in a deposit return guarantee: where possible, choose an eligible property and finish with insurance.
  • Spell out special terms: add safeguards such as "no senior rights to be created until the balance date" as special clauses in the contract.
Most jeonse fraud starts where "a check was skipped." Simply covering all four — register, market value, registration filing, and guarantee — can cut a substantial share of the risk.

If you have already been harmed: the validity period of the Special Act on Support for Victims of Jeonse Fraud and Housing Stability has been extended by two years to May 31, 2027, so you can apply for a victim determination under the act until then. However, tenants who first signed a lease on or after June 1, 2025 are excluded (Korea Policy Briefing, May 2, 2025). Whether you qualify as a victim is decided under the requirements set by the act, so do not assume the answer yourself; check with the competent authority or an expert.

By Situation - Who Should Watch for What

Even with the same system, priorities differ by circumstance. Here are common situations. These are general guidance only; your actual decisions should be made with an expert based on your own case.

11.1 First-Time Renters Living Alone

Deposits are often small, so you may fall under the protection of small-deposit priority repayment. Even so, resident registration and a fixed date on move-in day are basics among basics. If your deposit exceeds the threshold, it is wise to actively consider a return guarantee.

11.2 High-Value Jeonse Tenants

With a large deposit, small-deposit protection is hard to obtain, and a single incident causes large losses. Securing the priority repayment right through a fixed date, checking senior debt, and enrolling in a deposit return guarantee for double and triple safeguards are especially important.

11.3 Tenants Nearing the End of the Term

If you want to renew, exercise the renewal request right in the six-to-two-month window before maturity, and negotiate increases within the 5% cap. If you must leave but the return of your deposit is uncertain, it is safest to first consider filing for a lease registration order before moving.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q. What happens if I forget to do resident registration?

Since resident registration is the starting point of opposing power and the priority repayment right, a late registration delays your rights by the same amount. If a mortgage is created in the meantime, you can be pushed to a junior position and put at a disadvantage. Once you move, registering the same day is safest.

Q. Do I have to rewrite the contract if the landlord changes?

If you are a tenant with opposing power, the new owner succeeds to the landlord's position, so the existing contract terms generally remain in force. There is in principle no duty to rewrite it, but it is wise to confirm changes such as the landlord's information.

Q. I was asked for an increase above the 5% cap.

For a renewal made by exercising the renewal request right, an increase above 5% is in principle restricted. However, application differs depending on whether it is a new contract or a renewal, so if there is a dispute, you can contest it through the Housing Lease Dispute Mediation Committee or with professional advice.

Conclusion - Rights Work Only If You Secure Them "In Advance, Yourself"

The Housing Lease Protection Act is a sturdy shield for tenants. But that shield is not handed to someone who sits still. Securing opposing power through move-in and resident registration, the priority repayment right through a fixed date, and adding one more layer with a return guarantee if the deposit is large — building these basics early in the contract is the surest way to protect your deposit.

Finally, three reminders. First, opposing power and the priority repayment right are completed by the actions of move-in, resident registration, and a fixed date. Second, when the deposit is at risk, there is a staged response: the lease registration order, the return guarantee, and litigation. Third, all figures, such as small-deposit thresholds and renewal conditions, change by time and region. Once more: this article is informational and not legal advice. For specific matters, be sure to consult a licensed professional such as a lawyer, judicial scrivener, or the Korea Legal Aid Corporation (132).
