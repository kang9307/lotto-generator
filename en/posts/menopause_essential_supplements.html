Menopause (gaengnyeongi in Korean) often begins in the late 40s. It is a natural transition many women go through, yet sudden hot flashes, sleepless nights and mood changes can weigh on daily life, which is why many people look to supplements.

This article introduces seven supplements often discussed for menopause and lays out what level of evidence was found for each. The word "essential" in the title does not mean all seven must be taken. Supplements do not replace hormone therapy, and if symptoms interfere with daily life, treatment options should be discussed with a clinician.

Seven Supplements and the Evidence Found

1. Isoflavone

Plant compounds in soy that resemble estrogen. They do not simply top up female hormones and do not replace hormone therapy.

Evidence checked this time

  • The 2023 nonhormone therapy position statement of The North American Menopause Society (NAMS) does not recommend soy foods, soy extracts or the metabolite equol for hot flashes and night sweats, because the evidence of benefit is mixed.
  • On Korea's Food Safety Korea page for menopause functional ingredients, the notified ingredients listed are red ginseng and Sophora japonica fruit extract. Whether soy isoflavone has an approved menopause claim was not confirmed this time.

Talk to a clinician first if

If you have a history of, or are being treated for, an estrogen-sensitive condition such as breast cancer, talk to a clinician before taking it.

2. Calcium

The nutrient most often cited for bone density, which declines after menopause. Bone health is where the evidence is clearest.

Evidence checked this time

  • According to the US NIH Office of Dietary Supplements (ODS), the recommended amount for women aged 51 to 70 is 1,200 mg a day, with an upper limit of 2,000 mg (total from food and supplements, US standard).
  • In Korea, mineral values including calcium were adjusted for some age groups in the 2025 Korean Dietary Reference Intakes (Ministry of Health and Welfare press release). So the figures above are the US standard, not the Korean one; see the original Korean tables for the adjusted values.

Talk to a clinician first if

Whether a supplement is needed depends on dietary intake and bone density results. If you have kidney disease or take other medication, ask a clinician or pharmacist first.

3. Vitamin D

A nutrient handled alongside calcium for bone health. The earlier claims of immune support and mood relief were removed for lack of supporting evidence.

Evidence checked this time

  • Treating it with calcium as a bone-health nutrient is common to national and international intake standards, The specific recommended amounts are not given in this article.
  • The earlier "1,000 to 2,000 IU a day" was deleted because no primary source backs it.

Talk to a clinician first if

The amount should follow your blood 25-hydroxyvitamin D test and a clinician's judgment.

4. Omega-3 Fatty Acids

Fatty acids including EPA and DHA. No primary source supporting relief of menopause symptoms is cited here.

Evidence checked this time

  • The NAMS 2023 statement does not recommend over-the-counter supplements and herbal therapies as a group for hot flashes and night sweats, citing a lack of rigorous research.
  • The omega-3 claim approved by Korea's food regulator and its daily intake are listed under "What Could Not Be Confirmed" below. The cardiovascular, inflammation and cognition claims and the 1 to 2 g figure were removed for lack of evidence.

Talk to a clinician first if

If you take an anticoagulant or antiplatelet drug, talk to a clinician before taking it.

5. Magnesium

A mineral involved in nerve and muscle function. The earlier sleep and anxiety claims were removed for lack of supporting evidence.

Evidence checked this time

  • Magnesium is also among the minerals whose values were adjusted in the 2025 Korean Dietary Reference Intakes (Ministry of Health and Welfare press release). See the original Korean tables for the adjusted values.
  • The earlier "320 mg a day" was deleted because no source could be attached, and the claim that bedtime dosing aids sleep was removed too.

Talk to a clinician first if

If you have kidney disease, be sure to talk to a clinician before taking it.

6. Vitamin E

An antioxidant nutrient. The earlier claim that it eases hot flashes is not supported by confirmed evidence.

Evidence checked this time

  • Among the supplements reviewed in the NAMS 2023 statement, none is recommended for treating hot flashes, and vitamin E is included in that.
  • The earlier "400 to 800 IU a day" was deleted. For the upper intake level and bleeding risk, see "What Could Not Be Confirmed" below.

Talk to a clinician first if

If you take an anticoagulant or plan to take a high dose for a long time, talk to a clinician first.

7. B-Complex

A group of water-soluble vitamins involved in energy metabolism. The fatigue, mood and cognition claims were removed for lack of supporting evidence.

Evidence checked this time

  • No primary source supporting an effect on menopause symptoms is cited here.
  • The statement that "being water-soluble, excess is excreted" was also deleted for lack of evidence. Check the amount of each ingredient on the product label.

Talk to a clinician first if

Ask a pharmacist whether ingredients overlap with your medication or other supplements.

Important Precautions

  • Talk to a clinician: if you take medication or have a chronic condition, ask a doctor or pharmacist before starting.
  • Not a replacement for hormone therapy: if hot flashes or other symptoms disrupt daily life, discuss treatment options, including hormone therapy, with a clinician.
  • Start gradually: do not start everything at once; add one at a time and watch how your body responds.
  • Check the label: check ingredients and amounts on each product and make sure products do not overlap.

Additional Tips for a Smoother Menopause

Lifestyle Guide

  • Regular exercise: keep at it within your ability, and check your health status with a clinician before starting.
  • Balanced diet: before supplements, get calcium, protein and other nutrients evenly from meals.
  • Sleep habits: if trouble falling asleep lasts, talk to a clinician.
  • Stress management: find what suits you, such as meditation, yoga or hobbies.
  • No smoking, moderate drinking: for bone and overall health, discuss how to do this with a clinician.

Final Thoughts: You Are Not Alone

Menopause can be a hard stretch, but with enough information and a clinician's help, you can find a way of managing it that suits you. Supplements are only one part, and which ones you need depends on your diet, test results and medication.

If symptoms continue, or before you start or stop anything, do not decide alone; talk to a doctor or pharmacist first.

You are not alone.

When you need it, get help from a professional for a healthier menopause.

## 2. Before-you-buy checklist

- [ ] Do your symptoms interfere with daily life? If so, discuss treatment options, including hormone therapy, with a clinician first.
- [ ] Have you told a doctor or pharmacist about your medication and conditions?
- [ ] Do you have a history of an estrogen-sensitive condition such as breast cancer? If so, talk to a clinician before isoflavones.
- [ ] Do you have kidney disease? If so, talk to a clinician before calcium or magnesium.
- [ ] Do you take an anticoagulant or antiplatelet drug? If so, talk to a clinician before omega-3 or vitamin E.
