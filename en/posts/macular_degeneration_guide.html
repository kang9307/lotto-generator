The Complete Guide to Macular Degeneration - Symptoms, Types, Tests, Treatment and Prevention

Don't ignore wavy lines or a blurry spot - dry and wet AMD, the Amsler grid self-test, anti-VEGF treatment, and prevention

Jul 5, 2026 Health & Wellness

Introduction: When straight lines start looking wavy

Have you ever noticed that the slats of your mini blinds look slightly bent, or that the grout lines on your bathroom tiles seem to ripple? Have you found that the middle of a word or sentence looks oddly blurry while you're reading? It's easy to write these changes off as eye strain or "just getting older," but especially past age 50, they can be early signs of macular degeneration.

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) gradually, or in some cases fairly quickly, damages your central vision, the part of your sight you rely on most for reading, recognizing faces, and driving. Unlike total blindness, where the entire visual field goes dark, AMD often leaves peripheral vision largely intact for a long time, which is exactly why it can feel like you're "seeing fine" even as central vision quietly deteriorates, making early detection harder.

This guide walks through what macular degeneration is, the difference between the dry and wet types, risk factors like aging, smoking, genetics, and UV exposure, the Amsler grid self-test you can do at home, the dilated eye exam and OCT scan used in clinics, anti-VEGF injection treatment for wet AMD, nutritional and lifestyle management for dry AMD, and prevention steps including smoking cessation, based on publicly available sources as of July 2026. It isn't meant to replace a diagnosis or prescription, but to give you the background for a more informed conversation with an eye doctor.

1. What is macular degeneration

Toward the center of the retina, at the back of the eye, sits a small region called the macula. Although it covers only a fraction of the retina's total area, it holds the highest concentration of light-sensing cells and is responsible for sharp central vision, the kind needed to read, recognize a friend's face, or read a street sign while driving.

Macular degeneration is a condition in which this macular tissue is damaged over time, reducing central vision. Peripheral vision, handled by the outer retina, is often preserved much longer, so walking around a room or seeing the outline of a large object may not feel especially difficult. But daily tasks that require sharp central vision, such as reading, recognizing faces, or driving, can become significantly harder.

2. Types - dry AMD and wet AMD

2.1 Dry (atrophic) AMD

Most people with AMD have the dry type. It develops as the photoreceptors (light-sensing cells) in the macula gradually thin and break down, typically progressing through early, intermediate, and late stages. In late-stage dry AMD, the areas where photoreceptors have been lost can expand into a map-like pattern, a condition called geographic atrophy. Dry AMD usually progresses over several years, and one of its earliest hallmark signs on a dilated eye exam is the presence of yellow deposits under the retina called drusen.

2.2 Wet (neovascular) AMD

Wet AMD accounts for a smaller share of AMD cases but tends to progress much faster and can cause sudden, significant vision loss. It begins when a protein called vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) drives the growth of abnormal new blood vessels from the choroid underneath the macula. These new vessels are fragile and prone to leaking blood and fluid, which can swell the macula or leave scar tissue, damaging central vision in a relatively short time. The key difference from dry AMD is that wet AMD has a comparatively well-established and effective treatment: anti-VEGF injections, covered later in this guide.

3. Why it happens - causes and risk factors

3.1 Aging

As the name suggests, age is the single biggest risk factor. Studies report that risk rises sharply with age, with some analyses finding the risk several times higher in one's 70s compared with the late 50s, and dozens of times higher past age 80. The exact multiples vary between studies, but the direction is consistent: risk climbs steeply with age.

3.2 Smoking

Smoking is considered the most significant modifiable risk factor for AMD. One analysis estimated that if smoking were eliminated entirely, the prevalence of severe AMD could fall by roughly a third. The oxidative stress from cigarette smoke is believed to be a key mechanism, accumulating damage in retinal blood vessels and tissue over time.

3.3 Genetic factors

AMD is understood to result from a combination of genetic and environmental factors. Multiple studies have linked genetic variants such as CFH, ARMS2, and C3 to AMD risk, and having a parent or sibling with the condition can raise your own risk. If you have a family history, it's worth considering earlier and more frequent eye exams.

3.4 UV exposure and other factors

UV exposure is also cited as a factor that may increase risk. Cardiovascular risk factors such as high blood pressure and high cholesterol, obesity, and a diet high in saturated fat are frequently mentioned as well, underscoring how closely eye health is tied to overall vascular health.

4. How common is it

Based on published estimates, roughly 8% of people worldwide show some sign of AMD, and the condition is estimated to affect around 11 million people in the United States and roughly 170 million people worldwide. As populations age, these numbers are expected to keep rising, making AMD one of the leading causes of vision loss among older adults in developed countries.

5. Symptoms and early warning signs - metamorphopsia and scotoma

Early AMD symptoms usually appear in one eye first and are painless, which makes them easy to miss on your own. Watch for the following changes.

  • Metamorphopsia: straight lines that should look straight appear wavy or broken. This is often first noticed while looking at things that are naturally straight, such as window blinds, door frames, tile grout lines, or telephone poles.
  • Central scotoma: a blurry smudge or a gray or dark spot in the middle of the visual field. It may start small and faint, then grow larger and darker as the condition progresses.
  • Reduced color and contrast perception: difficulty distinguishing similar colors, or colors that seem duller than they used to.
  • Slower adaptation to changing light: taking noticeably longer than before to adjust when moving from a dark room to a bright one, or vice versa.
  • Difficulty reading or recognizing faces: needing brighter light to read text, or struggling to make out someone's features clearly even at close range.

Each of these signs can seem minor on its own, but they share a common thread as early indicators of a condition affecting central vision. If one or more recur, it's worth getting a proper eye exam rather than waiting it out.

6. Self-check - how to use the Amsler grid

If you have AMD or are in a higher-risk group, the Amsler grid is a simple tool you can use at home to monitor changes between clinic visits. It's a small square grid with a dot in the center, and it's a standard self-monitoring method recommended by the American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) and other eye health organizations.

  1. If you normally wear reading glasses, put them on. In good lighting, hold the grid about 12 to 15 inches (30 to 38 cm) from your face.
  2. Cover one eye with your hand, and with the other eye, look directly at the dot in the center of the grid.
  3. While keeping your gaze on the center dot, check whether the surrounding grid lines all look straight, or whether any area looks blurry, wavy, dark, or blank.
  4. Switch eyes and repeat the same steps.

If any lines look wavy or an area of the grid looks dark or missing, contact an ophthalmologist without delay. If you've already been diagnosed with AMD in one eye, checking the other eye daily with the Amsler grid is commonly recommended. That said, this self-check is a supplement to, not a replacement for, regular eye exams.

7. Testing and diagnosis - dilated eye exam and OCT

7.1 Dilated fundus exam

After dilating the pupils with eye drops, an eye doctor examines the inside of the retina directly using an ophthalmoscope or slit lamp. This exam looks for drusen (yellow deposits), changes in the retinal pigment epithelium, atrophy, and signs of bleeding, helping determine whether AMD is dry or wet and what stage it's at. Fundus photographs are often taken as a baseline to compare against future visits.

7.2 Optical coherence tomography (OCT)

OCT uses light waves to capture detailed cross-sectional images of the retina, almost like thin slices. The scan is painless, takes just a few minutes, and precisely shows the thickness of each retinal layer along with any fluid buildup. It's especially useful for detecting fluid or bleeding from leaking vessels in wet AMD, or measuring the extent of geographic atrophy in late-stage dry AMD, and has become a central tool for both diagnosing AMD and monitoring it over time. Fluorescein angiography, which involves injecting a dye into a vein to image blood vessels, may be added when needed.

The American Academy of Ophthalmology recommends a baseline eye exam starting at age 40, even without symptoms, followed by whatever schedule your eye doctor recommends afterward. Exams are typically scheduled more frequently for those with risk factors or at older ages.

8. Why early detection matters

Early detection is emphasized in AMD partly because the two types call for different treatment strategies. For dry AMD, there's growing evidence that starting appropriate nutritional management at the intermediate stage can slow progression to the late stage, so catching it at the right time matters. Wet AMD is even more time-sensitive. The longer treatment is delayed after abnormal vessels begin damaging the macula, the more likely scar tissue is to form and cause vision loss that's hard to reverse, whereas starting anti-VEGF treatment early in the course of symptoms has been associated with better outcomes, sometimes preserving or even partially improving vision. If you've already been diagnosed with AMD in one eye, the other eye carries an elevated risk of converting to wet AMD, so combining Amsler grid self-checks with regular eye exams is a key strategy for catching it early.

9. Treatment and management (1): wet AMD - anti-VEGF injections

The cornerstone of wet AMD treatment is anti-VEGF intravitreal injection. Drugs such as ranibizumab, aflibercept, and brolucizumab work by blocking the VEGF protein that drives abnormal vessel growth, reducing leakage and further growth. Treatment typically starts with an initial "loading phase" of one injection a month for about three months, after which the interval is often extended to every 4 to 8 weeks based on how the eye responds, monitored with OCT scans, an approach commonly called treat-and-extend. The injection itself is a brief in-office procedure done under local anesthesia, but it's important to understand that this is usually not a one-time treatment; it's a long-term condition that requires ongoing injections and monitoring.

10. Treatment and management (2): dry AMD - lifestyle and nutrition

There is currently no treatment that restores photoreceptors already damaged by dry AMD. Management instead focuses on slowing progression. The best-established approach is the AREDS2 nutrient formula, which consists of vitamin C (500 mg), vitamin E (400 IU), zinc (80 mg), copper (2 mg), lutein (10 mg), and zeaxanthin (2 mg). Large clinical studies found that this combination can lower the risk of progressing to late-stage AMD in people with intermediate AMD, or advanced AMD in one eye. It's important to note that this formula isn't a preventive supplement for people with no AMD or only early-stage disease; it's specifically intended to slow progression in those already at intermediate stage or beyond, so it should only be taken after discussing it with an eye doctor.

For geographic atrophy, the advanced form of dry AMD, newer injectable drugs aimed at slowing the growth of atrophic lesions have been approved in some countries in recent years. These treatments are designed to slow further spread of atrophy rather than restore vision already lost, and approval and availability vary by country, so they require a conversation with your ophthalmologist. Separately, low-vision aids and rehabilitation, including magnifiers, special glasses, and electronic devices, along with the guidance of a low-vision specialist, are an important part of managing dry AMD and making the most of remaining vision.

11. Prevention - quitting smoking, nutrition, UV protection, and regular exams

There's no guaranteed way to prevent AMD entirely, but several habits are known to help lower the risk.

  • Quit smoking: since it's considered the most influential modifiable risk factor, quitting smoking is generally regarded as the top priority for AMD prevention.
  • A diet rich in lutein and zeaxanthin: dark leafy greens such as spinach and kale, along with omega-3 fatty acids from fatty fish, are believed to support macular health.
  • UV protection: wearing sunglasses that block UV rays is a basic, widely recommended part of long-term eye care.
  • Cardiovascular health: keeping blood pressure, cholesterol, and weight within a healthy range is also connected to eye health.
  • Regular eye exams: especially with a family history or past age 50, regular checkups to catch changes early are the most reliable form of preventive care available.

12. When to see an eye doctor

Don't rely on self-observation alone in the following situations; see an ophthalmologist as soon as possible.

  • Lines look wavy on the Amsler grid, or part of the grid looks dark or missing
  • Central vision in one eye suddenly becomes blurry or drops over the course of a few days
  • A new spot or smudge appears in the center of your visual field
  • You've already been diagnosed with AMD in one eye and notice similar changes starting in the other eye
  • You have a family history of AMD or are past age 50 and due for a routine eye exam
⚠️ Medical disclaimer. This article is for education and information only and is not a substitute for medical diagnosis or treatment. If you suspect a vision problem or notice any of the signs above, consult an ophthalmologist. The type, stage, and appropriate treatment of AMD (choice of anti-VEGF drug, whether to take a nutritional supplement, candidacy for geographic atrophy treatments, and so on) vary greatly by individual eye and overall health, and the figures and thresholds in this article are general reference points from public sources, not a recommendation for any specific diagnosis or treatment. Do not decide on supplements, injections, or surgery on your own; always consult a professional.

13. Conclusion - start by not missing the small changes

Macular degeneration can damage central vision gradually, or, in the case of wet AMD, fairly quickly, yet it carries a paradox: because peripheral vision is often preserved, it can be genuinely hard to notice on your own. That's exactly why a small change, like a slightly bent line on a mini blind or a faint smudge picked up on an Amsler grid, can be the critical early signal.

If it's dry AMD, slow progression with nutrition and lifestyle management; if it's wet AMD, respond actively with anti-VEGF injections, and the starting point for either path is early detection. The easiest thing you can do today is check both eyes once with an Amsler grid, and start building habits like not smoking, eating more leafy greens, and wearing UV-blocking sunglasses into your routine. If you have any doubt at all, don't hesitate to get a proper exam from an ophthalmologist.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1. What are the main types of macular degeneration?

Dry (atrophic) and wet (neovascular). Dry AMD, the more common type, involves gradual thinning of the macula's photoreceptors and tends to progress slowly. Wet AMD involves VEGF-driven abnormal vessels that leak blood and fluid, causing rapid vision loss, but it has a relatively effective treatment in anti-VEGF injections.

Q2. What are the main symptoms of macular degeneration?

Metamorphopsia (straight lines looking wavy) and a central scotoma (a blurry or dark spot in the middle of your vision) are the hallmark symptoms. Reduced color perception and slower adaptation to dim light are also common. Symptoms usually start in one eye and are painless.

Q3. What are the risk factors for macular degeneration?

Age is the biggest risk factor, with risk rising sharply as you get older. Smoking is considered the most significant modifiable risk factor, and genetic variants such as CFH and ARMS2, family history, and UV exposure are also commonly cited contributors.

Q4. How do you use the Amsler grid?

Hold the grid about 12 to 15 inches (30 to 38 cm) away, cover one eye, and stare at the center dot while checking whether the surrounding lines look straight. Repeat with the other eye. Contact an ophthalmologist immediately if lines look wavy or part of the grid looks dark or missing; this self-check doesn't replace regular exams.

Q5. Can macular degeneration be treated or prevented?

Wet AMD is managed with anti-VEGF injections that suppress vessel leakage and growth, while dry AMD management centers on slowing progression with the AREDS2 nutrient formula and lifestyle changes. There's no guaranteed prevention, but not smoking, eating more leafy greens, UV protection, and regular eye exams are known to help lower risk.
