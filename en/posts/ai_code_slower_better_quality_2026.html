Introduction: The 2026 Signal - Waking Up From Fast AI Coding

Around May 2026, when this article was first published, a different question kept resurfacing in developer communities. For a while, many conference keynotes promised an X-times productivity multiplier and the dominant question was "how do I get AI to write code faster." Now a growing number of developers are asking something else: "I am using AI heavily, so why hasn't the quality of my code improved the way I expected?" Production incidents, undefended abstractions, and fractured codebase consistency are catching up to the speed gains. There is also public research showing that the feeling of being faster and the measured result can diverge; it is collected in the "Speed vs Quality" section below.

The immediate trigger for this article is a post shared on the Korean tech community GeekNews (news.hada.io) on May 26, 2026. The original is "Using AI to write better code more slowly," written by developer Nolan Lawson on his blog on May 25, 2026, and it was also discussed on Hacker News and Lobsters. Its core argument is that AI coding does not have to mean spewing out barely-passable code and merging it unvetted; LLMs are flexible enough to be used just as effectively to write high-quality code more slowly. The workflow it describes cross-reviews pull requests with several models (Claude sub-agents, Codex, Cursor Bugbot), filters out false positives, fixes only the critical and high severity findings, and asks agents how a change works and how it could fail. What makes the signal worth taking seriously is the source. This is not anti-AI sentiment. It is a deliberate slowdown proposed by people who use AI heavily, and that is why this article takes it seriously.

This article does two things. First, it names five hidden costs of fast AI coding. Second, it lays out seven principles for using the same AI tools more slowly and more deliberately, three personal metrics to check your own practice, an AI-versus-human responsibility matrix, and three actions you can apply tomorrow. The five costs and seven principles are not a summary of the original post; they are this article's rules of thumb and recommendations built on its concern, and their effects have not been measured. This is not an argument against AI coding. The opposite. It is an argument that the right way to extract value from AI is depth, not speed, much as the slow food movement that began in Italy in 1986 started as opposition to fast food and went on to rewrite the definition of good food.

The Five Hidden Costs of Fast AI Coding

The cost of fast AI coding can be organized into five repeating patterns. They share a root, but each one can independently wipe out the headline speed gain, and teams can hit several at once without noticing.

Trap 1) Unverified Fast Merges

The most common and the most expensive trap. AI generated code "looks like it works," the unit tests pass, and it gets merged to main without a serious second pass. Team-level statistics point the same way: DORA's 2025 report found that AI adoption now has a positive relationship with software delivery throughput but continues to have a negative relationship with delivery stability. Fast merges raise the defect rate directly, and those defects produce hotfix PRs and rollback PRs that further inflate the velocity number. The dashboard can look better while the underlying business value stands still.

Trap 2) Adopting Unknown Abstractions

The second trap is accepting an AI-recommended library, design pattern, or abstraction without understanding why it fits. The AI says "the Strategy pattern is appropriate here," the developer accepts it, and six months later when the abstraction needs to change neither the original author nor anyone else on the team can explain why it exists. Codebases accumulate "patterns without reasons," and maintenance cost climbs steeply. This is technical debt of a particularly toxic kind because the debt is invisible until you try to refactor.

Trap 3) AI Patterns Fracture Codebase Consistency

The third trap is that AI gives a reasonable answer to each problem in isolation, but does not give a consistent answer within your codebase. The same kind of function ends up named differently, structured differently, and handling errors differently depending on which file it sits in. Consistency is a codebase's largest asset, and unmanaged AI usage erodes that asset quickly. When every file looks different, new hires need that much longer to learn the codebase.

Trap 4) Learning Gaps (Use Without Understanding)

The fourth trap is the most expensive on a long horizon. The faster AI generates code, the less developers ask "why does this work?" and the more learning quietly stops. Juniors look productive in the short term but never reach the senior capability they would have hit in two to three years. Seniors stop learning skills outside their strong domain because AI can fake those skills in the moment. The real risk of the AI era is not that AI takes jobs; it is that AI causes us to stop learning, which makes the next ten years of capability development hollow.

Trap 5) Rising Token Cost With Flat Value

The fifth trap is operational. Fast AI coding multiplies token consumption: more frequent prompts, longer contexts, context reinjected every session. Doubling token spend does not double code quality. The original author also says he burns a lot of tokens, but he spends them on discovering early that a whole plan is flawed rather than on shipping features faster. Rising token cost is rising activity, not by itself rising value. What makes the difference is where the tokens go.

The Seven Principles of "Slower, Better"

How do you escape the five traps without abandoning the tools? Here are the seven principles this article recommends, all applicable with the same AI tools you already have - only the usage pattern changes.

Principle 1) Always Rewrite AI Output Once

The simplest principle and the highest leverage. Do not merge AI-generated code as-is. Touch it once - rename a variable, split a function, add a comment, anything. This one cycle does two things at once. First, it gives you one more chance to catch defects in the AI output. Second, it forces you into an "understood" state with the code, so that six months later when it needs to change you can actually explain it. Only code you can re-derive belongs in your codebase under your name.

Principle 2) Provide Context Explicitly, Upfront

The second principle is to spend five minutes before the first prompt setting context: "this codebase uses functional style," "errors are handled with a Result type," "external HTTP goes through axios only." Without explicit constraints, AI produces a different pattern every time, and that variance is what creates Trap 3 (consistency fracture). One five-minute context-setting investment buys a long stretch of pattern consistency, which is one of the highest ROI moves available in the AI era.

Principle 3) Verify in Small Units

The third principle is to refuse large AI outputs. Instead of "build the whole API endpoint," ask for the function signature first, verify, ask for the body next, verify, and so on. Each step you understand before moving forward. Defects in large AI outputs hide; defects in small AI outputs are obvious. Verification gets sharply harder as the unit grows, and that is the reasoning behind this principle.

Principle 4) Ask AI "Why," Not Just "What"

The fourth principle is to explicitly ask AI to justify its output. "Why a Map here," "why structure try-catch this way," "why this library and not the alternative." Read the answer, decide whether you agree, and if you do not, push back. Asking "how could this change fail?", as the original post recommends, is the same principle. This single cycle is the direct counter to Trap 4 (learning gaps). Code that you accept without understanding the reasoning is not your code; it is a temporary lease.

Principle 5) Let AI Infer Conventions, Do Not Dictate Them

The fifth principle is slightly counterintuitive. In this article's judgment, telling AI a convention explicitly ("this codebase uses camelCase") keeps consistency less reliably than showing AI a slice of the codebase and asking it to infer the conventions, then follow them ("study these files, summarize the conventions, then follow them"). Real code shows details that a rule list leaves out. Real code examples are often a clearer signal to AI than a list of rules, and your prompt strategy should use that.

Principle 6) Demand Justification Before New Patterns

The sixth principle is the direct counter to Trap 2 (unknown abstractions). When AI proposes a new pattern, library, or abstraction, do not accept it immediately. Ask "why is the existing pattern in this codebase insufficient." If AI cannot give a convincing answer, do not adopt. Of the rules this article recommends, this is the most direct way to slow the accumulation of technical debt. "If you cannot justify it, you do not introduce it" is the bedrock discipline of senior engineering, and it applies to AI suggestions exactly as it does to junior engineer suggestions.

Principle 7) Review Your Own AI Usage Weekly

The seventh principle is meta. Every Friday, fifteen minutes, look at how you actually used AI that week. How many PRs did I merge unchanged? How many times did I ask "why"? What percent of AI output did I rewrite? Without this check, the previous six principles collapse easily. Saying you practice slow AI coding and actually practicing it diverge invisibly, and measurement is what keeps them aligned.

This article reads two things from the table. First, perception is not a reliable measuring instrument: METR participants felt faster while they were slower. So the conclusion is not "AI makes you slower" but "measure delivery performance over longer windows, not one-day perception." That is why this article recommends a quarter or longer as the default unit for measuring AI impact.

Measuring ROI Under Slow AI Coding - 1 Week, 1 Month, 1 Quarter

If you are running slow AI coding, what do you measure at each time horizon? These are the metrics this article recommends.

One Week: Learning Metrics

At one week, ignore output and look at learning. (a) Number of new concepts, libraries, or patterns you actually understood. (b) Number of times you asked AI "why." (c) Percentage of AI outputs you rewrote at least once. Stable values on these three are the signal that slow AI coding is actually running, not just being talked about.

One Month: Value PR Ratio

At one month, look at the split between value PRs (new features, user-visible change) and hotfix PRs (fixes to defects you just shipped). There is no correct ratio for every team. Compare against your team's own history and watch whether the split is drifting toward hotfixes. Same merge count, very different business value.

One Quarter: Defect Rate and Business KPIs

At one quarter, look at production defect trend and correlation with core business KPIs (conversion, retention, NPS). If slow AI coding is working, what you hope to see is a flat or declining defect trend while KPIs keep improving. Defects climbing quarter over quarter while KPIs stay flat is the sign that you have drifted into fast AI coding.

The AI vs Human Responsibility Matrix

Slow AI coding is not "use AI less." It is "draw the line clearly between AI-owned and human-owned work." The four-quadrant matrix this article recommends makes this concrete.

High value / Low frequency (design, core logic) Low value / High frequency (boilerplate, tests)
Human-ledHuman decides; AI suggests alternatives and verifies(do not spend time here)
AI-led(never delegate)Full AI delegation; human verifies output only

The decisive quadrants are top-left (human-led, high value) and bottom-right (AI-led, low value). Bottom-left is wasted time. Top-right is the most dangerous quadrant in the AI era. Slow AI coding is the discipline of keeping humans out of the AI quadrant and AI out of the human quadrant, and blurring that line is how many AI-related problems start.

The "Slow Code" Movement - 1980 vs 2026

This article calls the trend the "slow code" movement because of a structural similarity to the slow food movement that began in Italy in 1986. Slow Food grew out of Arcigola, a forerunner organization Carlo Petrini founded to resist the opening of a McDonald's near the Spanish Steps in Rome. But it did not stop at opposition. It rewrote the definition of good food around local food and traditional cooking, and in 1989 delegates from 15 countries signed its founding manifesto in Paris, making it an international movement.

Slow code in 2026 follows a similar path. It starts as a reflection on fast AI coding, but it does not reject AI coding; it rewrites the definition of good code. Good code is not code that was merged fast; it is code you can still explain six months from now, code a new hire can understand, code that does not generate production incidents. AI is not a threat to that definition. It can be the most powerful tool available for achieving it - if used slowly, deliberately, and with learning baked in.

Seniors vs Juniors - Same Principles, Different Emphasis

All seven principles apply universally, but the emphasis differs by experience level.

For Seniors

Seniors lean on Principles 2 (explicit context), 5 (inferred conventions), and 6 (justification before new patterns). Seniors know the codebase best, so they extract the most value from giving AI explicit context. They also own the codebase conventions, so they get the most leverage from prompts that ask AI to infer and follow those conventions. Justifying new patterns is fundamentally a senior responsibility, with or without AI.

For Juniors

Juniors lean on Principles 1 (rewrite once) and 4 (ask why). The largest risk for juniors is learning gaps, and these two principles attack that risk directly. Rewriting AI output once forces engagement with the code, and asking why turns the AI tool into a learning tool. The original author also points out that telling whether a race condition flagged by AI review is a real problem or a theoretical one is hard for less experienced developers, so juniors are better off triaging AI findings together with a senior rather than alone. In this article's judgment, a junior who uses AI as a learning tool can learn faster than when working without AI.

Applying This in High-Speed Markets

In environments where billing structures or investor cycles push for short-term speed metrics, slow AI coding feels harder. Three pressures and concrete counters.

First, activity-priced engineering work. Where revenue is tied directly to lines of code or person-months, fast AI coding inflates short-term billing. Counter: internally pair every line-count metric with at least one non-activity metric (defect rate, review time, incident count). One paired column is enough to make the cost of fast AI coding visible.

Second, short investor cycles. Startups cannot wait for quarter-long trends before the next board update. Counter: report learning metrics weekly, value PR ratio monthly, and defect trend once per quarter. Each board only needs the appropriate slice at the appropriate cadence, not every metric at every meeting.

Third, hierarchical decision cultures. When executives request the AI productivity report, teams build the report the executive wants to read. Counter: put this article's "Speed vs Quality" table into the report. Once public data such as "participants felt 20% faster but actually took 19% longer" (METR 2025) sits next to the team's one-day numbers, decision-makers are less swayed by perceived speed.

The Traps - Five Ways "Slower" Becomes Lazy

The biggest risk of slow AI coding is that "slower" turns into a justification for inaction. Five anti-patterns to flag in advance.

  1. "I'm careful, so I'm slow," with no learning log. Without Principle 4 (ask why) and Principle 7 (weekly review), "careful" is unverifiable.
  2. "I'm verifying," with no record of what verification found. Principle 1 (rewrite once) must produce a written diff, not a feeling.
  3. "Quality first," with no business KPI in view. The point of quality is business value, not code as an end in itself. Slow code without KPI tracking becomes slow business.
  4. "I don't rely on AI," and the developer writes boilerplate by hand. Doing the bottom-right quadrant manually is wasted hours.
  5. "I'm learning," with no shipped output. Learning happens inside output, not instead of it. Zero merged PRs in a week is stall, not study.

Personal Measurement - Three Friday Metrics

The simplest way to keep yourself honest is three one-line entries every Friday afternoon. There are no published benchmark values, so watch your own trend rather than absolute numbers.

Metric 1) Code Quality (24-Hour Hotfix Rate)

Percentage of your merged PRs this week that triggered a hotfix within twenty-four hours. If this rate rises over a four-week trend, you are drifting toward fast AI coding; if it stays low, your verification cycle is working.

Metric 2) Learning Load (New Concepts Count)

Number of new concepts, libraries, or patterns you genuinely understood this week. One line per item is enough ("understood the difference between TanStack Query's staleTime and cacheTime"). When slow AI coding is working, this accumulates steadily; several weeks of zero is the sign that learning has stopped.

Metric 3) Self-Explanation Score

On Friday, rate one to ten how confidently you could rewrite this week's merged code without AI. Ten means full ownership, and the lower the score, the more you are leaning on AI. A declining trend over several months is your earliest warning that AI is hollowing your skill base, not extending it.

Conclusion and Three Things You Can Do Tomorrow

The one-sentence summary: the right question in the AI era is not "how much faster did I get" but "where did I reinvest the time AI gave me." Reinvest it in verification and learning, and you compound into better code and a better developer in six months. Reinvest it in more merges, and you compound into more defects and a shallower developer in six months. Same tool, opposite outcome.

Three concrete actions this article recommends you apply tomorrow.

  1. Apply Principle 1 (rewrite once) to every PR you merge this week. Do not ship AI-generated code untouched. Rename a variable, split a function, or add a comment. Of this article's recommendations, it is the smallest and the easiest to start today.
  2. Spend fifteen minutes every Friday logging the three personal metrics above. 24-hour hotfix rate, new concept count, self-explanation score. Four weeks of data is enough to see your real pattern.
  3. In your next AI productivity report, put one-day and one-quarter numbers on the same slide. Stop comparing single points. Trends across time horizons make decision-makers stop chasing short-term numbers on their own.

This article is contrarian, but not oppositional. The argument is that the real value of AI coding only becomes visible once you change the measurement unit, and once you change the measurement unit your usage pattern naturally changes too. At publication (May 2026), this article's outlook was that the developers and teams who win the second half of 2026 would not be the ones who used AI the fastest, but the ones who used AI the deepest. Slow AI coding is, in the end, a paradox worth keeping: one extra cycle through the same tool is the fastest path to both quality and learning.

# Slow AI Coding - Weekly Checklist and Decision Tables

As of: 2026-09-29

The principles and metrics below are this article's recommendations, not measured effects. Public research figures are collected in section 4 with sources.

## 1. Pre-merge checklist (the seven principles)

- [ ] I rewrote the AI-generated code once by hand (at least one of: variable names, function split, comments)
- [ ] The first prompt stated the codebase constraints (style, error handling, allowed libraries)
- [ ] I asked for small units (signature, then body) instead of one big chunk and verified each step
- [ ] I asked AI "why this approach?" and "how could this change fail?"
- [ ] I showed existing code and let AI infer conventions instead of dictating rules
- [ ] New patterns, libraries, or abstractions went in only after an answer to "why is the existing pattern insufficient?"
- [ ] I could explain this code again without AI
