How to Read a Colonoscopy Report - Cecal Intubation, Bowel Prep Score, and Polyp Type Set Your Next Interval

They removed a polyp is not one statement. The name on the pathology report, and a single line about bowel preparation, move your next exam by years

August 18, 2026 Health & Wellness
Before anything else: this article is for information only and does not substitute for medical diagnosis or treatment. Have your results interpreted, and your next interval set, by the facility that performed the exam. The intervals quoted here are the ranges recorded in published guidelines, not values assigned to any individual. Completeness of resection, preparation quality, family history, age, and comorbidities all enter the calculation, and that judgment belongs to the clinicians who did the procedure. This article recommends no medication and no bowel preparation product.

Introduction: two different things are printed on the same page

You come out of the recovery area holding one sheet of paper. Most eyes land in the same place: how many polyps, and whether tissue was sent to pathology. The other boxes look like internal record keeping.

Those other boxes are the scale you read your own result against. A colonoscopy report mixes two things with different natures. One is what was found in your colon. The other is how trustworthy that particular exam was. Read only the first and you never see that the second is holding it up.

Concretely, this is what happens. The same phrase no abnormality means one thing after an exam that reached the cecum through a clean colon, and something weaker after an exam that barely got past the left side. The same phrase two polyps removed can put your next appointment ten years out or one year out, depending entirely on what the pathologist writes.

This article is not about treating or staging colorectal cancer. Screening programs as a system are covered in Korea's National Health Screening - Who Is Eligible, When, and What Gets Tested, and blood panel values in How to Read Your Blood Test Results. What is on the table here is one thing: how to read a single colonoscopy report.

1. What the exam is looking for - screening and diagnosis start from different places

Same equipment, same position, but a colonoscopy splits three ways depending on why you are having it. That split is the starting point for every interval discussed later.

  • Screening — looking for lesions that have not announced themselves in a person without symptoms. Eligibility is set by age alone.
  • Diagnostic — going in to explain something that already exists: rectal bleeding, unexplained anemia, a change in bowel habit, or a positive fecal occult blood test.
  • Surveillance — re-examining someone whose polyps were removed before, at a set interval. The premise is a colon that has already grown lesions.

The distinction is practical because even the operator's scorecard changes with it. The 2024 colonoscopy quality indicators jointly updated by the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy and the American College of Gastroenterology set an adenoma detection rate target of at least 35 percent across screening, surveillance, and diagnostic exams combined, but carve out colonoscopy following a positive fecal test at 50 percent or more. When the signal has already fired, finding more is the expected outcome, so the same yardstick would be the wrong one.

Korea's program currently runs on a two-stage structure. National cancer screening offers a fecal immunochemical test annually from age 50, and an abnormal result routes you to colonoscopy or double-contrast barium enema. In other words, most colonoscopies reached through that route are diagnostic. In July 2025 a reform direction was announced that would install colonoscopy itself as the primary test, recommended every ten years for ages 45 to 74, but the stated target is 2028 and it is a proposal, not a scheduled change.

One place this routinely goes sideways: the sheet handed to you on the day is only half the document. Pathology reporting takes roughly three to seven days according to Seoul National University Hospital's patient information, and until that name is fixed, the next date cannot be. A blank follow-up box, or one reading after pathology, is not an omission. It means the sequence is proceeding correctly.

3. Did it reach the cecum - extent, withdrawal time, and detection rate

A colonoscope enters through the anus, travels the length of the colon to the cecum, and then observes on the way back out. Because of that order, two things govern the quality of the result: whether it got all the way up, and how slowly it came back down.

Cecal intubation is documented, not assumed

Cecal intubation is defined as passing the tip beyond the ileocecal valve so that the entire cecal caput is in view. Guidelines ask that the landmarks identified be named in the report, with photographic backup: an image of the appendiceal orifice, and an image of the cecum taken from beyond the ileocecal valve.

  • The 2024 joint quality indicators set a cecal intubation rate of 95 percent or higher as the target.
  • Seoul National University Hospital's patient information notes that even experienced operators do not reach 100 percent cecal intubation, and that polyp detection rate and cecal intubation rate in a general population are used to judge exam quality.
  • When it was not reached, the report names the point that was. Splenic flexure or descending colon means everything proximal to that was not assessed on this exam.

Withdrawal time is observation time

The most visible change in the 2024 update is the withdrawal time target. The new figure is a mean withdrawal time of at least 8 minutes in normal colonoscopies without biopsy or polypectomy, in people aged 45 and over. That is higher than the prior benchmark. Time alone is not a measure of skill, but it tracks with adenoma and serrated lesion detection closely enough to be kept as an indicator.

Adenoma detection rate - absent from your report, present in your outcome

Adenoma detection rate is the share of screening colonoscopies in which the operator finds at least one adenoma. It does not appear on your sheet. It is still worth knowing, because it connects to hard outcomes.

  • A 2014 New England Journal of Medicine analysis followed 314,872 colonoscopies performed by 136 gastroenterologists. Detection rates ranged from 7.4 percent to 52.5 percent across those operators.
  • Sorted into quintiles by detection rate, interval cancer fell from 9.8 to 4.8 cases per 10,000 person-years from lowest to highest. Roughly a twofold gap.
  • The 2024 targets are an adenoma detection rate of 35 percent or more (40 percent in men, 30 percent in women) and a newly added sessile serrated lesion detection rate of 6 percent or more.

Whether you can ask a facility for that number is a separate question. What you can check is whether withdrawal time and a bowel preparation score are printed on your own report at all, which is itself information about how that institution documents its own work.

4. Bowel preparation - one line that sets the reliability of everything else

Find this box first. If preparation is recorded as inadequate, then no abnormality or one polyp printed beneath it becomes a correspondingly less certain statement. This box is not a finding. It is a note on the confidence of the findings.

The most widely used instrument is the Boston Bowel Preparation Scale. The colon is divided into right, transverse, and left segments, each scored 0 to 3, totaling 0 to 9. A total of 6 or more, with no segment below 2 is the standard definition of adequate preparation. A total of 2 or below is treated as inadequate, generally warranting a repeat within a year.

Why one line carries that much weight shows up in the numbers.

  • A Korean multicenter study gathered cases rescoped within two years after a suboptimally prepared exam. Adenomas that had not been caught the first time appeared in 42.4 percent of patients, and 35.8 percent per adenoma. Advanced adenomas came in at 5.4 percent per patient and 18.9 percent per lesion.
  • The 2020 consensus intervals from the US Multi-Society Task Force are calculated on the premise of an exam completed to the cecum with preparation adequate to detect lesions larger than 5mm. Break the premise and the table's numbers no longer apply as written.
  • That same document names poor bowel preparation and incomplete colonoscopy as independent risk factors for incident colorectal cancer, and says shortened intervals warrant consideration in affected patients.
  • The 2024 quality indicators promoted adequate bowel preparation in 90 percent or more of outpatients to priority status. That is an institutional figure, not a personal one.

What the tables do not settle is how much earlier. Which segments were obscured, what the exam already turned up, and your own risk profile all enter it. If your report says fair or poor, or shows a 1 in any segment, walking into the consultation already knowing that is better than walking out having only checked the year.

On preparation agents themselves this article recommends nothing. There are several, and the choice varies with kidney function and current medications, so the prescription and the dosing schedule belong to your clinician. One practical note: if you felt the preparation went wrong, say so before the exam. That information ends up in the preparation box.

5. They removed a polyp does not mean one thing

This is the core of the article. Polyp is a shape word, not a diagnosis. It covers anything protruding inward from the colonic mucosa, and what it actually was is settled by putting the removed piece under a microscope.

Operators do estimate the family from morphology and surface pattern during the procedure. But an estimate is an estimate, and two polyps on the report and two tubular adenomas on the pathology are statements of different weight.

The adenoma family

Adenomas are lesions with disordered cell architecture and are the classic precursor to colorectal cancer. By tissue pattern they divide into tubular, tubulovillous, and villous; by degree of cellular abnormality into low-grade and high-grade dysplasia. Once villous components or high-grade dysplasia appear, the lesion moves to the shorter interval regardless of count.

The serrated family

The World Health Organization sorts serrated lesions into hyperplastic polyps, sessile serrated lesions, traditional serrated adenomas, and unclassified. Everything with a serrated outline used to be filed as a hyperplastic polyp with no malignant potential. That is no longer how it is handled.

  • Under current definitions, more than 70 percent of serrated lesions are hyperplastic polyps and 4 to 25 percent are sessile serrated lesions.
  • Evidence has accumulated that the serrated pathway may account for 15 to 35 percent of colorectal cancers.
  • Sessile serrated lesions are flat and pale, which makes them less conspicuous. That the tandem meta-analysis put serrated lesion miss rates at 27 percent, above adenomas, is not unrelated to that property.

Compressed into one line: I had two polyps removed tells you nothing about whether the next one is in ten years, five, or three. Two lesions taken from the same area at the same size can behave differently. If one is a rectal hyperplastic polyp and the other a 12mm sessile serrated lesion, the second is what the calculation runs on. That is the practical reason to keep the pathology report.

The conditions the 2022 Korean revision groups as high-risk adenoma are listed below. Meeting any one of them moves you toward three years; meeting none classifies you as low risk, in the 5 to 10 year band.

  • Adenoma 10mm or larger
  • Three or more adenomas
  • Tubulovillous or villous histology
  • High-grade dysplasia
  • Traditional serrated adenoma
  • Sessile serrated lesion with dysplasia
  • Serrated polyp 10mm or larger
  • Five or more sessile serrated lesions

One axis does not fit in the table. In the US consensus, piecemeal resection of a lesion 20mm or larger runs a schedule of its own: six months, then one year after that, then three years after that. It is a removal technique where complete excision is hard to confirm visually. The Korean revision likewise puts six months on piecemeal resection of polyps 20mm or larger.

Note also that a few cells disagree, and the disagreement was left in. One or two tubular adenomas under 10mm read 7 to 10 years in the US and 5 to 10 years in Korea. That the guidelines still carry that spread is itself information. The final interval is set by the clinicians who performed the exam, not by the table.

7. A fecal test is not a miniature colonoscopy

For most people screened through a national program, the test they actually received was a fecal one. The two are commonly understood as a quick version and a thorough version. More precisely, they look at different objects.

  • A fecal occult blood test asks whether human blood is present in stool. The immunochemical method now in use detects the globin portion of human hemoglobin with antibodies, so it needs no dietary restriction and picks up lower gastrointestinal bleeding selectively.
  • A colonoscopy asks what is inside the colon, directly. Lesions that are not bleeding are still visible, and what is visible can be removed on the spot.
  • So a negative result does not mean a clean colon. Korea's national health information portal, run by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency, notes that bleeding which is intermittent or small in volume can read negative.
  • Conversely, a positive result does not mean cancer. Ulcer bleeding in the digestive tract and bleeding near the anus from hemorrhoids produce the same signal, which is why a positive result routes to colonoscopy or barium enema to locate the source.

The program sits on that structure. Korea's national cancer screening offers an annual fecal immunochemical test from age 50, with an abnormal result leading to colonoscopy or double-contrast barium enema and, where needed, biopsy. The National Cancer Information Center also states that for the general population it recommends colonoscopy every 5 to 10 years from age 50, and that higher-risk individuals should have their method and interval decided individually after specialist consultation.

The reform direction announced in July 2025 would invert the order, placing colonoscopy as the primary test at ten-year intervals for ages 45 to 74. As noted above, the stated target is 2028 and the work is in progress. The screening you receive today still runs in two stages.

8. When normal is not reassurance

Clean is a good result. How many years of weight that word carries, though, is decided by the other boxes on the same page. Where the following apply, the arithmetic of normal, therefore ten years does not hold as written.

  • Preparation was poor — the 42.4 percent figure above attaches here.
  • The cecum was not reached — the segment never reached was not assessed this time.
  • Withdrawal was fast — observation time moves with detection rate.
  • The lesion type is one that hides — a meta-analysis pooling more than 15,000 tandem colonoscopies reported miss rates of 26 percent for adenomas, 9 percent for advanced adenomas, 27 percent for serrated lesions, and 34 percent for flat lesions. Proximal advanced adenomas came in at 14 percent.
  • Cancers do appear after a colonoscopy — 58 percent of post-colonoscopy colorectal cancers have been attributed to missed lesions.

There is also a premise inside the interval itself. Ten years, or five, assumes you remain symptom free. If any of the following appears while you are waiting, being seen is the right move regardless of the date on the page.

  • Rectal bleeding or black stool
  • Anemia with no explanation
  • A persistent change in bowel habit
  • Unexplained weight loss
  • Persistent abdominal pain

Family history pulls the starting line forward. Korean patient guidance recommends beginning ten years before the age at which the relative was diagnosed, or by age 40 at the latest. A family history suggestive of Lynch syndrome, or hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer, is handled on a separate track starting considerably earlier, since it tends to present at a younger age and favors the right side of the colon compared with sporadic disease.

9. The days after - complications and the signs that mean go in

A colonoscopy inflates the colon with gas so it can be seen. Some abdominal distension or urge to defecate right afterwards is ordinary, and minor bleeding or mild discomfort can occur. What matters is anything beyond that range.

On frequency: a 2023 systematic review looking out to 30 days after screening colonoscopy estimated perforation at 7.6 to 8.5 per 10,000 and bleeding at 16.4 to 36.2 per 10,000. Rare, but not zero, which is exactly why the warning signs are worth knowing.

The combination that means the emergency department: Seoul National University Hospital's patient information advises going to the emergency room for persistent abdominal pain or fever, heavy bleeding such as large-volume black stool or hematochezia, or severe pain, accompanied by dizziness, a racing pulse, or cold sweats. This can appear days later rather than on the day itself, so keep that window in mind after a resection.
  • Fasting after resection — after a simple polypectomy, a fast of roughly three to four hours is described as a precaution against delayed microperforation. An X-ray may be taken to check for perforation.
  • Eating that day — a large or heavily seasoned meal on the day of the exam can provoke abdominal pain.
  • Pathology results — reporting takes about three to seven days. Your next interval is set once that arrives.
  • If you had sedation — follow the facility's instructions on driving and on anything requiring judgment for the rest of the day.
If you take antiplatelet or anticoagulant medication: do not decide on your own when to stop or restart. Post-resection bleeding risk and the original reason you are on the drug are entangled, so follow the instructions of the prescribing clinician. This article gives no guidance on stopping or resuming any medication.

Frequently asked questions

A polyp was removed but the report does not give a next date

Nothing is final before pathology, and that is normal. The three axes that move the interval are what it was called, how many there were, and whether the largest reached 10mm. One or two tubular adenomas under 10mm fall in the 5 to 10 year band, three or four at 3 to 5 years, five to ten at 3 years, more than ten at 1 year. Villous components or high-grade dysplasia push it to three years regardless of count.

Preparation was poor but nothing was found. Do I need a repeat?

The preparation box records the confidence of the exam, not a finding, so a poor score weakens the conclusion of nothing found. In a Korean multicenter study, patients rescoped within two years after suboptimal preparation showed previously uncaught adenomas 42.4 percent of the time. How much earlier to repeat depends on which segments were obscured and on your own risk.

How is a hyperplastic polyp different from an adenoma?

Different families. More than 70 percent of the WHO serrated category are hyperplastic polyps, and those under 10mm in the rectum or sigmoid are handled at ten years in the US guideline, same as a normal exam. The complication is that a sessile serrated lesion in the same family can look similar on screen, so the deciding word is on the pathology report, not the impression during the procedure.

If it was normal, am I safe for ten years?

Normal carries ten years of weight when the exam performed. The tandem colonoscopy meta-analysis reported miss rates of 26 percent for adenomas, 27 percent for serrated lesions, and 34 percent for flat lesions. Ten years also assumes you stay symptom free, so rectal bleeding or unexplained anemia in the interim is a reason to be seen regardless.

If the fecal test was negative, can I skip colonoscopy?

They look at different objects. The fecal test asks whether blood is present; colonoscopy asks what is there. Intermittent or small-volume bleeding can read negative, and a positive can come from an ulcer or hemorrhoids rather than a tumor. Which test fits you depends on age, family history, and symptoms, and that is decided at a medical facility.

Conclusion

Next time you open the report, reading it in this order changes what you see.

  1. Did it reach the cecum? If not, everything beyond that point was not examined this time.
  2. What is the bowel preparation score? A poor number makes every row below it less certain.
  3. What was removed, and how much? Count, size of the largest, method of resection.
  4. What name did pathology give it? This is the main determinant of the next interval.

The order is useful because the third and fourth items rest on the first and second. If extent and preparation wobble, every judgment above them wobbles with them. When those two are solid, no abnormality genuinely becomes a statement worth years.

And they removed a polyp is one sentence in conversation but several branches on paper. Hyperplastic, ten years. Two tubular adenomas under 10mm, five to ten. A single 12mm sessile serrated lesion, three. A 20mm lesion taken in pieces, six months. The same sentence, and a twentyfold spread in when you come back. Keeping the pathology report and confirming that name at your next visit is the practical version of preparedness.

Finally, the numbers in the tables are ranges recorded by guidelines, not values issued to an individual. Completeness of resection, preparation, family history, age, and comorbidities all enter the calculation. The final judgment belongs to the clinicians who performed the exam.
