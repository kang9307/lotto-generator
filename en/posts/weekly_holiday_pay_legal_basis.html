Weekly Holiday Pay in Korea - The Legal Basis

What the statute actually says, and where your work pattern lands - as of August 2026

2026-08-07 Life & Law

Read this first - scope, date and limits

This article describes Korean law as of August 2026 and is not legal advice. It applies only to workplaces covered by the Korean Labor Standards Act, so nothing here transfers to employment outside Korea. The provisions and interpretations set out below are general explanation. Whether weekly holiday pay actually arises at a given workplace, how much it is, and whether non-payment occurred depends on the terms of the employment contract and the facts of that workplace. Those questions are decided by the competent regional employment and labor office and the courts, not by this page. For an individual case, contact the Ministry of Employment and Labor counselling centre (1350) or a licensed labor attorney. Statutes and administrative interpretations change, so read the current text at the Korean Law Information Center (law.go.kr).

It is not a bonus - it is a paid rest day

Search the Labor Standards Act for the phrase weekly holiday pay and you will not find it. What the Act contains is a single provision titled Holidays. Article 55 paragraph 1 reads:

An employer shall guarantee to each employee an average of at least one paid holiday per week.

Break the sentence apart and three things fall out. The duty sits on the employer. The frequency is an average of at least once per week. And the nature of the day is paid. That third element is the whole point. It is a day off on which wages still run, and those wages are what people call weekly holiday pay in practice. It is not a discretionary bonus. It is the wage attaching to a rest day the statute has fixed as paid.

The distinction matters. Treat it as a bonus and it feels like something an employer may or may not hand out. The reverse is true. Where the conditions are met a statutory paid rest day arises, and failing to pay for it is handled as unpaid wages. A clause in an employment contract saying weekly holiday pay will not be paid falls below the statutory floor, and clauses below the floor are unlikely to be given effect.

Article 55 also has a second paragraph. It requires the employer to guarantee as paid the holidays prescribed by Presidential Decree, and Article 30 paragraph 2 of the Enforcement Decree identifies those as the public holidays under Article 2 of the Regulations on Public Holidays of Government Offices (excluding subparagraph 1) and the substitute holidays under Article 3 of the same Decree. That is where the paid public-holiday rule comes from. This article is about paragraph 1. Paragraph 2 has a different scope and is dealt with separately below.

Three gates that settle it quickly

The conditions look scattered across the statute book, but they reduce to three blocks. Walk them in order and the answer comes fast.

  1. Status - are you an employee under the Labor Standards Act? A freelance-style contract does not settle it if the substance is employment
  2. Hours - do contractual working hours average at least 15 per week over four weeks?
  3. Attendance - were the scheduled working days of that week worked in full?

Each gate has its authority in a different place. That is precisely why a single search never returns the whole picture.

Clearing all three gates means the conditions may be satisfied. It does not fix an amount or confirm that payment is owed. That turns on the specific contract terms and the facts of the workplace.

The 15 hours are not the hours you worked

This is where most people go wrong. The test is not actual hours worked but contractual working hours. Under the Act, contractual working hours means the hours agreed between employee and employer within the statutory working-hour limits - in plain terms, the hours written into the employment contract.

The exclusion sits in Article 18 paragraph 3:

Articles 55 and 60 shall not apply to an employee whose contractual working hours per week, averaged over four weeks (or over the actual period where employment is shorter than four weeks), are less than 15 hours.

Three things need reading out of that text.

  • The unit is a four-week average, not a single week. One light week does not knock you out on its own
  • If employment ran for less than four weeks, the average is taken over that period. A two-week stint is not outside the test
  • Two provisions are excluded, not one. Article 55 and Article 60 - so annual paid leave goes with it

This is the provision that defines the so-called very short-hours worker. It also explains why contracts pitched at exactly 14 hours a week are so common. Several situations turn on it in practice.

  • Contract says 14 hours, reality was 18 every week - the wording alone may not settle it. What the contractual hours truly were is assessed against the contract, rosters, and payroll records together
  • Hours that swing week to week - the four-week average decides whether the 15-hour line is crossed
  • Hours inflated by overtime - overtime is not contractual hours. Whether the agreed baseline itself moved is a different question from extra work being requested ad hoc
  • No written contract at all - putting working conditions in writing is the employer duty, and the absence of a contract does not automatically resolve against the employee. It does make proof harder if a dispute starts
Never having received a written contract is its own issue. Failure to state working conditions in writing is handled separately from weekly holiday pay, so it is worth raising both together if you file.

What actually breaks perfect attendance

The second gate is Article 30 paragraph 1 of the Enforcement Decree:

The paid holiday under Article 55 paragraph 1 of the Act shall be granted to a person who has worked all scheduled working days during one week.

The contested word is perfect attendance. The statute leaves it undefined, which is how workplaces end up applying it arbitrarily. The Ministry of Employment and Labor position is reasonably clear: perfect attendance means not being absent on a scheduled working day, and absence means a day on which the employee voluntarily did not provide work. On that basis, lateness, early departure, rest days, leave and shutdowns falling on scheduled working days cannot be treated as absence.

Applying that test to the situations that keep recurring:

The murkiest row is sick leave. Because it is not a statutory institution there is no uniform rule. Whether the workplace treats it as paid and present, or as unpaid absence, decides the outcome. Check your own rules of employment before assuming either way.

There is also a pattern employers get wrong in the other direction: an internal rule that three latenesses equal one absence, used to strip out weekly holiday pay. Tracking lateness for pay deduction or performance review sits on one level. Using that tally to erase a statutory paid rest day sits on another, and the second looks like setting conditions below the statutory floor.

The row that produces the most disputes is daily-hire work. Even where the paperwork says daily, someone who came in on the same days at the same times week after week looks in substance like an employment relationship with fixed scheduled days. Where work is instead assigned call by call with nothing scheduled for the following week, the analysis changes. The line is drawn by how the arrangement actually ran, not by the label.

Weekend work attracts its own myth - that two working days means no weekly rest day. But attendance is measured against that person's scheduled working days, not the opening days of the business. If Saturday and Sunday are the scheduled days, showing up for both is the test.

Where the small-workplace argument runs out

Article 11 of the Labor Standards Act sets the scope of application and leaves it to Presidential Decree to list which provisions reach workplaces with four or fewer regular employees. That list is Enforcement Decree Article 7 and Table 1, and what it includes or omits determines working conditions at small workplaces.

On the weekly rest day the answer is comparatively firm. Article 55 paragraph 1 is described as applying to workplaces with four or fewer regular employees. Ministry of Employment and Labor counselling responses say the same thing: even below five employees, where contractual hours average at least 15 per week over four weeks and the scheduled working days were worked in full, weekly holiday pay is due.

The confusion comes from the items that genuinely do drop out below five.

  • Annual paid leave (Article 60) - described as not applying to workplaces with four or fewer employees
  • Premium pay for overtime, night and holiday work (Article 56) - the premium element is described as not attaching below five
  • Statutory working-hour ceilings (Article 50) - the 40-hour week and 8-hour day limits do not apply in the same way
  • Unfair dismissal remedy (Article 28) - the Labor Relations Commission route is for workplaces with five or more
  • The paid public holidays in Article 55 paragraph 2 - different scope from paragraph 1, so check it separately

That list is long enough to have created the impression that the Labor Standards Act barely applies below five employees. In reality a set of rules binds regardless of size: the wage payment principle (Article 43), settlement of accounts on departure (Article 36), the weekly rest day (Article 55 paragraph 1), retirement benefits, and the minimum wage. Weekly holiday pay is one of them.

The employee headcount itself is a variable - whether the owner counts, and how part-timers are counted, changes the result. If a workplace hovers around five, start by checking the counting method.

The first week and the last week - what changed in 2021

The most disputed spot is the final week. On 4 August 2021 the Ministry of Employment and Labor changed its administrative interpretation here. The guidance is numbered Wage and Working Hours Division-1736, and earlier interpretations inconsistent with it were withdrawn from that date.

What changed:

  • Before - weekly holiday pay arose only if the scheduled working days were completed and work was scheduled in the following week, the so-called eighth-day requirement
  • After - where the employment relationship subsisted for one week and the scheduled working days in that period were worked in full, weekly holiday pay arises even with no work scheduled beyond that week
  • Effect - a person who works Monday to Friday and resigns the following Monday now falls inside the entitlement, where the old reading kept them outside it

The phrase to read precisely is subsisted for one week. The requirement moved from work scheduled next week to was a full week completed. It did not become open-ended. So the cases split like this:

  • Leaving mid-week - where the scheduled days were not completed and no full week subsisted, the analysis is that nothing arises for that week
  • Completing a week then resigning - the revised reading opens the entitlement. Payroll still run on the old rule becomes a live dispute
  • The first week of employment - starting mid-week usually means the week's scheduled days were not worked in full. In practice the entitlement commonly starts from the first complete week
  • Which day is the rest day - it need not be Sunday. The workplace sets it, and in shift or weekend operations it will often be another day

Because the outcome can turn on a few days, the final week should be checked with the leaving date and the designated rest day side by side. The conclusion in any individual case, however, is reached by the competent labor office after examining the contract and the actual work history.

Why an above-minimum hourly rate can still be a violation

An hourly rate written above the minimum wage can still produce a minimum wage violation. The mechanism is the conversion rule in Article 5 of the Enforcement Decree of the Minimum Wage Act.

The minimum wage is published as an hourly figure, so weekly or monthly pay has to be converted before comparison - and the divisor includes the paid weekly rest hours. The Decree directs that weekly-set wages be divided by the number of hours forming the weekly basis for minimum wage application, defined as the contractual working hours for the week plus the hours treated as paid under Article 55 paragraph 1 of the Labor Standards Act.

That definition produces the numbers everyone has seen.

  • For a 40-hour week the weekly basis becomes 40 contractual hours + 8 paid rest hours = 48 hours
  • Converted to a month, 48 hours × (365 days ÷ 7 days) ÷ 12 months ≈ 208.57 hours, which practice rounds to 209 hours
  • If base pay on the payslip divided by 209 falls below the minimum wage, a violation finding is possible whatever hourly figure the contract shows

So weekly holiday pay is not money added on top. It is time already sitting in the denominator when the minimum wage is converted to an hourly basis. Miss that structure and you divide a monthly salary by actual hours worked only, and conclude everything is fine.

The minimum wage figures themselves and worked monthly conversions are set out in the 2026 minimum wage impact analysis. This article is about why the paid rest hours belong in the denominator, not about the amount.

When it is already included in the salary is a true statement

Employers often say weekly holiday pay is already included in your salary. Sometimes that holds and sometimes it does not. The dividing lines are roughly these.

  • Are the pay components separated? - the contract and the payslip should let you see how base pay, weekly holiday pay and other allowances are split
  • What hour base was used? - a salary built on 209 hours has the paid rest hours in it. A salary computed on actual hours worked alone may not
  • Does it say comprehensive pay? - saying so does not automatically validate the arithmetic. If the result falls below the statutory floor, the shortfall is the issue
  • Hourly pay with no weekly holiday line? - if hourly work exceeds 15 hours a week and the payslip shows nothing related to the rest day, that is a signal worth checking

The checking order is simple. Put the employment contract, then the payslip, then the actual work records side by side and see whether all three agree. Issuing a payslip is an employer duty, and it must set out the pay components and how they were calculated. If you are not receiving one, asking for it first strengthens everything that follows.

Records worth gathering while you still can:

  • A copy of the employment contract - the original showing contractual hours, scheduled days and pay structure
  • Payslips - every month. A point where a line item name changed is itself a clue
  • Attendance records - time-clock screenshots, shift rosters, store schedules
  • Bank records of pay received - the deposit and the payslip do not always match
  • Work instructions - messages or notices that establish which days and hours you were told to work

If it was not paid - the complaint route and the three-year clock

Weekly holiday pay is a wage, so non-payment is handled as wage arrears. The relevant provisions first:

  • Labor Standards Act Article 43 - wages shall be paid in currency, directly to the employee, in full
  • Labor Standards Act Article 36 - where an employee leaves, the employer shall pay wages and other money within 14 days of the date the obligation arises. The period may be extended by agreement between the parties
  • Labor Standards Act Article 49 - a wage claim under the Act is extinguished after three years if not exercised
  • Labor Standards Act Article 109 - non-payment of wages carries up to three years imprisonment or a fine of up to 30 million won, and prosecution cannot proceed against the express wishes of the victim

There are two routes. A complaint aims at getting the arrears paid. A criminal accusation aims at punishing the employer. In practice most people start with the complaint.

  1. Identify jurisdiction - it follows the address of the workplace, not your home address
  2. File - in person at the office, or online through the Ministry of Employment and Labor portal (labor.moel.go.kr)
  3. Investigation - a labor inspector summons the parties and reviews statements and documents. The processing period is 25 days excluding Saturdays and public holidays, and may be extended
  4. Payment direction - once arrears are confirmed the inspector directs payment, and the case closes when payment is made
  5. Non-compliance - failure to comply moves the matter into criminal handling and referral to the prosecution

The limitation period governs everything. The three years on a wage claim runs separately from each payday, so at an old workplace the earliest amounts expire first, in order. If you left some time ago, check this before anything else.

A few procedural questions that come up constantly:

  • Can you file while still employed? - procedurally yes. The Act prohibits detrimental treatment because a violation was reported
  • Is there a minimum amount? - there is no floor. Whether it is worth the time is a separate judgment
  • Does it cost anything? - filing a complaint is not structured as a fee-bearing step. Moving on to civil proceedings is a different matter
  • What if you are pushed to settle? - settlement during the investigation is your own call. If you do not accept the terms you can let the process continue
  • Want advice before filing? - the Ministry of Employment and Labor counselling centre on 1350 handles preliminary questions
This describes the shape of the procedure. Which route serves a particular case depends on the facts. Where the workplace or the amount is large, consider taking advice from a licensed labor attorney alongside it.

Frequently asked questions

Q. Do part-time and casual workers qualify?

The Labor Standards Act has no separate casual status. If you are an employee under the Act, contractual hours average at least 15 per week over four weeks, and the week's scheduled days were worked in full, the paid rest day under Article 55 paragraph 1 may apply. The label on the contract is not the test - the agreed contractual hours and your attendance are. The final call is made on the facts of the workplace.

Q. Is the 15 hours actual hours or contracted hours?

It is contractual working hours, meaning contracted hours. Article 18 paragraph 3 disapplies Articles 55 and 60 where those hours average under 15 per week across four weeks. That said, if the written hours and the actual operation diverged persistently, what the contractual hours really were becomes the disputed question, examined against rosters, payslips and attendance records.

Q. Does being late three times cancel it?

The Ministry of Employment and Labor position is that lateness and early departure on scheduled working days cannot be recorded as absence. Converting accumulated lateness into absence to erase the paid rest day looks like setting conditions below the statutory floor. Deducting pay for the minutes actually missed is a separate matter, and the shape of any dispute will depend on workplace rules and how they were applied.

Q. Do workplaces with fewer than five employees have to pay?

For the weekly rest day, it is described as applying regardless of size. Confusion arises because annual paid leave and premium pay for overtime, night and holiday work do require five or more. Article 55 paragraph 1 is explained as reaching workplaces with four or fewer employees. The paid public holidays in Article 55 paragraph 2 have a different scope and need separate checking.

Q. Does the final week of employment count?

The interpretation changed on 4 August 2021. Where the employment relationship subsisted for one week and the scheduled working days in that period were worked in full, the entitlement arises even with no work scheduled afterwards. Conversely, leaving mid-week without completing a full week is analysed as producing nothing for that week. Because the leaving date and the designated rest day drive the outcome, the calculation has to be done on the actual work history.

Summary - five things to check, in order

Weekly holiday pay is not settled by one provision. It runs Article 55 → Enforcement Decree Article 30 → Article 18 paragraph 3 → Table 1, which is why it never resolves at a glance. To check your own position, take it in this order.

  1. Contractual hours in the contract - do they average 15 or more per week over four weeks? If contract and reality diverge, which was the real agreement?
  2. Scheduled days and attendance that week - was there an absence? Is lateness, early departure or annual leave being recorded as absence?
  3. The hour base on the payslip - built on something like 209 hours with the rest hours in it, or on actual hours worked alone?
  4. Weeks containing a start or a departure - did a full week subsist? Does the leaving date fall before or after the rest day?
  5. Limitation - have three years passed since each payday?

The fifth is last on the list but often the first thing to check, because correct arithmetic is worthless once the period has run. And these five steps can get you as far as this looks wrong. They cannot get you to non-payment is established. That conclusion requires a factual investigation.
