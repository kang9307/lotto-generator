Conjunctivitis Symptoms and Causes - Why Viral, Bacterial and Allergic Cannot Be Told Apart by Looking

The response differs completely by cause, and the eye does not tell you which one you have

Aug 18, 2026 Health & Wellness
Before anything else. This article is for information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. If symptoms persist, or if you have pain or any change in vision, see an eye doctor. Nothing below should be used to decide your own cause or to delay being examined.

You wake up and one eye is stuck shut. The white is red, it feels gritty, and it keeps watering. Most people go one of two ways from here. They leave it, assuming it will pass in a few days, or they reach into a drawer for eye drops left over from something else.

Both moves have a hazard in them. The single name conjunctivitis covers causes whose correct responses are opposites, and the eye itself does not separate them well. The branch where antibiotics do nothing is the most common one, and leftover drops have a route by which they make things worse. On top of that, some red eyes are not conjunctivitis at all.

So the axis of this article is not which type do I have. It is what actually differs, and where self assessment stops working.

Signs that mean see an eye doctor now

This goes first on purpose. People get absorbed in sorting out which type of conjunctivitis they have and miss the situation that was never conjunctivitis.

Do not wait — if any of the following applies, the problem may not be confined to the conjunctiva. These are the items that the clinical reference from the US National Library of Medicine, the American Academy of Ophthalmology and the UK National Health Service all place in the prompt evaluation group.
  • Moderate to severe eye pain — actual pain, distinct from grittiness
  • Vision has dropped or gone blurry
  • Light hurts or haloes, and you struggle to keep the eye open (photophobia)
  • A cloudy spot on the cornea, or a deep ring of redness right around the iris
  • Symptoms began while wearing contact lenses
  • A newborn under 30 days old with a red, sticky eye
The same applies when things are getting worse rather than better, or when the same episode keeps returning. Lens wearers should remove the lenses and be examined even if none of the above applies.

Note what kind of list this is. These are signs that it may not be conjunctivitis, not signs that the conjunctivitis is bad. Inflammation limited to the conjunctiva is usually described as burning or gritty. When the words become painful, blurred, or I cannot face the light, that points toward the cornea or something deeper. Keratitis, uveitis and acute angle closure glaucoma all begin as a red eye.

What to take from the table is not the diagnosis in each row. The same symptom arises from several causes, and telling them apart requires a slit lamp view of the cornea and anterior chamber. The value of the table is in when not to wait.

What the conjunctiva does

The conjunctiva is a thin, transparent membrane that begins inside the eyelid, covers the white of the eye, and ends at the edge of the cornea. It is the boundary that keeps dust and microbes from reaching deeper into the ocular surface, it is part of the tear film because its goblet cells supply the mucin layer that lets tears spread evenly over the cornea, and it is surveillance tissue where resident immune cells detect antigens. Those three roles map directly onto the three branches of conjunctivitis. It is exposed first, so infection lands there. When the surveillance response runs hot, that is allergy. And when it is damaged for long enough the tear film destabilises, which is why dry eye sometimes lingers after conjunctivitis.

Fine vessels run through the conjunctiva like a net, so when inflammation arrives they dilate and a redness you normally do not see becomes visible. Grittiness, tearing and discharge sit on top of that. This is also where the difference from a red eye that is not conjunctivitis shows up. Dilated conjunctival vessels tend to spread redness evenly across the white, whereas a deep ring of redness concentrated around the iris is what gets described with corneal or intraocular inflammation. Even that is a supporting observation rather than a rule. Symptoms that stay dry and stinging over a long stretch may be a tear film problem rather than conjunctivitis, and that axis is covered separately in dry eye and digital eye strain.

Three branches, and why the response differs completely

Conjunctivitis splits into infectious and non infectious, and the infectious side splits again into viral and bacterial. Three branches matter in practice, and their responses do not overlap.

Viral - the most common, and antibiotics do nothing

The clinical reference from the US National Library of Medicine states that about 80% of acute conjunctivitis is viral, and that adenovirus accounts for 65% to 90% of the viral cases. Most of what people call pink eye sits in this branch.

  • Discharge tends to be reported as watery
  • Follicles are seen on the conjunctiva, often with a tender, swollen preauricular lymph node
  • A preceding upper respiratory infection frequently appears in the history, and the course typically runs 2 to 3 weeks
  • There is no specific antiviral. Korean public health material likewise states that no particular antiviral exists for epidemic keratoconjunctivitis and that management centres on symptom relief

The first practical consequence follows. The most common cause gets nothing from antibiotics. That is not how they are actually used. In a study of roughly 340,000 enrollees in a large US managed care network, 58% of people newly diagnosed with acute conjunctivitis filled a prescription for antibiotic eye drops. Fill rates were markedly higher when the first diagnosis came from urgent care, internal medicine, paediatrics or family practice than from an ophthalmologist.

Bacterial - less common, and usually shorter

  • Purulent or mucopurulent discharge is described as characteristic
  • Eyelids matted shut in the morning is frequently mentioned
  • Preauricular lymph node swelling is uncommon, and the course is described as around 7 to 10 days
  • Its share rises between December and April relative to viral cases

Allergic - not an infection, and it does not spread

The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency defines allergic conjunctivitis as inflammation arising when the immune response overreacts to external irritants such as pollen, dust, mites or lenses. The clinical reference states that allergic conjunctivitis is the most common non infectious form and affects 15% to 40% of the population.

  • The hallmark symptoms are itching in both eyes, redness, tearing, stringy discharge and grittiness, and it does not pass between people
  • Seasonal and year round perennial allergic conjunctivitis are the most common forms
  • Vernal keratoconjunctivitis, which typically starts around age 10 and is more frequent in boys, is a severe form that can leave corneal and conjunctival damage if untreated
  • Atopic keratoconjunctivitis in people with atopic dermatitis or eczema commonly leaves sequelae, and giant papillary conjunctivitis is triggered by mechanical irritation from contact lenses, ocular prostheses or surgical sutures

Why appearance does not separate them

Set those three lists side by side and they look separable. They are not. This section is the most important part of the article.

Start with the most widespread rule of thumb: itchy means allergic, thick yellow discharge means bacterial. The clinical reference carries the evidence that this rule does not hold. 58% of patients with culture-confirmed bacterial conjunctivitis also reported itchy eyes. Use itching as a marker for allergy and you read nearly six in ten of them backwards. Discharge behaves the same way. Viral cases thicken over time, and bacterial cases are not always yellow. Examination findings such as papillae and follicles are likewise described as nonspecific, seen across several causes.

So why include the table. To be read across. The last two rows are the point. Contagious and non contagious branches sit side by side, only one of the three gets anything from antibiotics, and even there the benefit is small. That is why deciding your own cause and then choosing a response is a sequence that does not work.

Epidemic keratoconjunctivitis - course, contagiousness and containment

This is the adenoviral form that causes the most trouble. The kerato in the name is there for a reason. It does not stop at the conjunctiva.

Course

The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency defines epidemic keratoconjunctivitis as adenoviral inflammation of the cornea and conjunctiva, mainly caused by types 8 and 19, with types 3, 4, 7 and 37 also able to trigger it.

  • Incubation — 7 days on average, ranging from 4 to 10
  • Symptoms — conjunctival injection and swelling, tearing, grittiness, photophobia, reduced vision
  • Keratitis — arises about 4 days after infection, and punctate keratitis may appear
  • Course — usually improves spontaneously around 14 days after symptom onset, running 3 to 4 weeks or longer in severe cases, and transmissibility is strong for 2 weeks after symptoms begin
  • Complications — subepithelial corneal infiltrates, secondary infection, dry eye, conjunctival scarring, symblepharon, and reduced vision from corneal opacity

The clinical reference places epidemic keratoconjunctivitis as the most severe form of viral conjunctivitis and states that keratitis develops in 80% of patients with serovars 8, 19 and 37. That is why this form gets its own section. However ordinary the rest of the course looks, once the cornea is involved sight is on the table.

How it spreads

Transmission is driven by contact, not the respiratory route. Korean public health material lists direct and indirect contact through infected ocular secretions, hands, towels, washing items and bedding. The most common path is that a hand which touched an infected eye lands on a door handle or a keyboard, and the next hand to touch that surface goes to its own eye. Within a household, sharing a single towel is enough to break containment, and an eye drop bottle whose tip has touched the eye, along with eye makeup, becomes the same kind of conduit. That is why swimming pools and public baths get named during outbreaks.

Containment is not a technique. It is the same behaviours, repeated. The prevention list is short.

  • Wash your hands often, and always after touching your eyes
  • Do not rub your eyes. For itching, a cold compress is advised instead of rubbing
  • Separate towels and washing items. During an outbreak, one set per person
  • Avoid shared facilities such as swimming pools and public baths
  • If one eye is affected, keep wiping materials and hands separate so it does not cross to the other eye

Outbreak seasons and clusters

The clinical reference describes a bimodal distribution in emergency department data. One peak sits in children under 7, highest at ages 0 to 4, with a second peak among adults. Incidence peaks in March for young children and May for other age groups, while bacterial rates are highest from December to April. Korean public health messaging repeatedly notes that eye infection outbreaks are pronounced in summer among children and adolescents in group settings.

Exclusion policy differs between countries, and the difference is worth carrying over as it stands. The UK National Health Service says you do not need to stay away from work or school because of conjunctivitis unless you or your child feel very unwell. In Korea, Article 22 of the Enforcement Decree of the School Health Act allows a school principal to order a student with an infectious disease, a suspected case, or a carrier to stay away, and to shorten or extend that period according to the severity and the state of the outbreak.

In other words, how many days you stay out is not a number an individual sets. It follows the clinician who made the diagnosis and the policy of the institution, and in dense group settings containment sits ahead of personal inconvenience. One more thing worth holding on to: feeling somewhat better does not mean the contagious window has closed.

The separate risk that attaches to contact lens wearers

A red eye in someone who wears lenses has to be treated as a distinct problem. Not because conjunctivitis is worse in them, but because a different disease starts with the same face.

Lens wear is a known risk factor for ulcerative, that is microbial, keratitis. In the Californian epidemiological study cited in the clinical reference, the rate of ulcerative keratitis among lens wearers was 27.6 cases per 100,000 person-years. The organisms involved behave differently too. Pseudomonas causes extensive collagen breakdown in the cornea, progresses rapidly and can reach perforation if treatment is late, while Acanthamoeba is a protozoal infection with a strong reported association with lens wear. Antibiotic choice therefore changes for lens wearers, and the clinical reference describes covering Pseudomonas empirically in this group. That means the approach is not the same as ordinary conjunctivitis, and the decision belongs to whoever prescribes.

What separates keratitis from conjunctivitis is the degree of pain, photophobia, and whether infiltrate is visible in the cornea. Early on, that separation is not clear cut. Which pushes the practical rule toward the simple side.

  • If the eye turns red, take the lenses out. The American Academy of Ophthalmology says not to wear lenses while the eye is infected, and the UK National Health Service advises avoiding them until recovery
  • Do not remove them and then watch to see whether it improves. Move it to an examination
  • After recovery, replace or thoroughly clean lenses as instructed. Never substitute water for storage case solution
  • If there is also a problem around the eyelid, it may be a reaction to the lens itself. Giant papillary conjunctivitis, triggered by contact lenses, overlaps here

Do not use eye drops casually - steroids and antibiotics

Do not use leftover eye drops. Do not put drops from an old prescription, a family member, or a different complaint into the eye you have now. A topical corticosteroid may be among them, and depending on the cause it can make things worse. This article does not tell you which drops to use or how to use them. Choosing and using an eye drop is for the clinician who examined you.

It is worth saying why this warning is set so hard. The clinical reference states that topical steroids can slow the healing rate, increase the risk of corneal melting, and increase the risk of elevated intraocular pressure. Korean public health material likewise states that long term steroid drop use can bring complications such as glaucoma and cataract, which is why regular eye examinations are required. It is a drug with a place, but a drug that requires someone to judge that place and monitor the course. Using it alone without knowing the cause removes that monitoring entirely, and the practical basis for the warning is that diseases which steroids can worsen badly also begin as a red eye.

Antibiotic drops are a different kind of question. Here the issue is not whether there is benefit but how much. The 2023 update of the Cochrane systematic review comparing antibiotics with placebo in bacterial conjunctivitis pooled 21 randomised controlled trials and 8,805 participants.

  • In the placebo arm, 55% (408 of 735) resolved spontaneously between days 4 and 9
  • In the antibiotic arm the figure was 68.2% (504 of 739)
  • Absolute risk difference 15%, number needed to treat 7, certainty of evidence moderate
  • No serious adverse outcomes were reported in the trials

Read it this way. Antibiotics do move recovery forward if the cause really is bacterial, but the size of that shift is one extra person improving for every seven treated. And about 80% of acute conjunctivitis was never bacterial to begin with. That is where the reason not to reach into the drawer becomes visible. Which drug is needed, or whether none is, is a question that only has an answer once the cause is settled.

Relief that has evidence behind it, and relief that does not

Here it is honest to separate strengths of evidence. Making symptoms less unpleasant and changing the course of the illness are different questions, and in conjunctivitis they sit especially far apart.

Children and newborns

Children - the spread is faster

One peak of incidence sits at ages 0 to 4, with the seasonal peak in March for young children. Nurseries and schools are environments where hands and objects circulate constantly, so an infection that starts with one child travels quickly. The measures are the same as for adults but held more firmly.

  • Separate towels, pillows and washing items per child
  • Wash hands often and keep nails short so the eyes are rubbed less
  • Whether the child attends follows the diagnosing clinician and the institution policy. Do not decide it yourself
  • The UK National Health Service notes that treatment requires a prescription for children under 2 years old

Newborns - a different thing entirely

A newborn under 30 days old with a red eye or sticky discharge needs to be seen without delay. The UK National Health Service treats this as an urgent presentation. Do not file it in the same category as ordinary conjunctivitis.

Neonatal conjunctivitis shares only a name with the adult illness. The clinical reference states that it can be observed in up to 10% of neonates and is transferred from the mother during delivery. Chlamydia, Neisseria gonorrhoeae and herpes simplex virus type 2 are named as causes, and the presentation is described as varying with the organism. Staphylococcal cases produce sticky eyes, HSV-2 produces watery discharge with vesicular lesions, and chlamydia produces mucopurulent discharge and pseudomembranes.

Why self assessment does not apply here is plain. Different organisms need different management, and some of these do not stay confined to the eye. A red eye or discharge in a newborn is itself the reason to be seen.

What could not be confirmed

Gathered here so that unconfirmed things are not written as though they were confirmed.

  • The statutory notifiable disease class of epidemic keratoconjunctivitis and acute haemorrhagic conjunctivitis in Korea — the specific provision could not be located in the primary legal text, so no class number appears above
  • A standard exclusion period — no official table giving a number of days could be confirmed. Only the discretionary provision in Article 22 of the Enforcement Decree of the School Health Act was verified, so no duration is stated
  • Cause distribution in Korean patients — no current domestic statistic for the share of each branch could be pinned down. The 80% and similar figures above come from an overseas clinical reference
  • Effect size for cold compresses and artificial tears — their listing as relief measures in official material was confirmed, but randomised controlled trial evidence quantifying the effect was not
  • Domestic keratitis rates in lens wearers, and the detailed course of acute haemorrhagic conjunctivitis — the 27.6 per 100,000 person-years figure is from an overseas study and was not updated with a domestic value, and the standalone national health portal entry for acute haemorrhagic conjunctivitis could not be opened, so it is not covered

Not confirmed does not mean untrue, and it does not mean unimportant. It means the source was not settled enough to put in this article.

Frequently asked questions

Does conjunctivitis clear up on its own?

In many cases it settles with time. The UK National Health Service says conjunctivitis usually gets better within a couple of weeks without treatment, and the clinical reference describes viral cases running 2 to 3 weeks and bacterial cases 7 to 10 days. But clearing up on its own and being safe to watch are two different statements. Some of the conditions that turn an eye red are not conjunctivitis at all, and a few of them threaten sight. Severe pain, reduced vision or haloes around lights are not situations for waiting it out.

If my eyes itch, does that mean it is allergic?

Itching is characteristic of allergic conjunctivitis, but the statement does not reverse. The clinical reference records that 58% of patients with culture-confirmed bacterial conjunctivitis also reported itchy eyes. Examination findings such as papillae and follicles are likewise nonspecific and appear across several causes. What does raise the odds of an allergic cause is a pattern rather than a single symptom: both eyes itching together, a seasonal rhythm, and a history of atopy or asthma.

How long is epidemic keratoconjunctivitis contagious, and what about school or work?

The Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency describes an average incubation of 7 days and states that transmissibility is strong for 2 weeks after symptoms begin. The illness usually improves around 14 days and can run 3 to 4 weeks or longer. Exclusion policy differs by country. The UK National Health Service says you do not need to stay away unless you feel very unwell, whereas in Korea Article 22 of the Enforcement Decree of the School Health Act lets a school principal order a student to stay away and adjust that period. This is not an individual judgement call. Follow the diagnosing clinician and your institution.

I wear contact lenses and my eye has turned red. Can I keep wearing them?

Take the lenses out and get the eye examined. The American Academy of Ophthalmology says not to wear lenses while your eyes are infected, and the UK National Health Service gives the same advice until recovery. The reason this advice is firmer here is not conjunctivitis itself. Lens wear is a known risk factor for ulcerative keratitis, and corneal infection with Pseudomonas or Acanthamoeba can progress quickly and scar the cornea. Keratitis is marked by pain and photophobia, but early on that distinction is not clear cut.

Can I use eye drops left over from a previous prescription?

No. What conjunctivitis needs depends entirely on the cause, the cause is not separable by looking at the eye, and leftover drops may include a topical corticosteroid. The clinical reference states that steroids can slow healing and raise the risk of corneal melting and elevated intraocular pressure, and Korean public health material notes that long term use can bring complications such as glaucoma and cataract, which is why regular eye examinations are required. A bottle whose tip has touched an infected eye also becomes a route to the other eye or to another person, which is why the American Academy of Ophthalmology advises discarding it once you recover.

Conclusion

What to remember about conjunctivitis is not the label of the cause but the fact that you cannot attach that label yourself. About 80% of acute cases are viral, so antibiotics do nothing there. Allergic conjunctivitis does not spread at all. And in bacterial cases antibiotics buy one extra recovery for every seven treated. Yet the three do not separate by appearance. The single figure that 58% of culture-confirmed bacterial patients reported itchy eyes summarises the whole situation.

Three things survive in practice. Break the hand and towel routes and stop rubbing your eyes — transmission runs through contact, and epidemic keratoconjunctivitis stays strongly contagious for 2 weeks after symptoms begin. Do not reach for leftover drops — topical corticosteroids can worsen things depending on the cause, and judging that and monitoring it belongs to whoever examined you. Move pain, reduced vision, haloes, anything that began during lens wear, and a newborn eye straight to examination — those are signs that it may not be conjunctivitis, not signs that the conjunctivitis is severe.

Stating it once more. This article is for information only and does not replace medical diagnosis or treatment. If symptoms persist, or if you have pain or any change in vision, see an eye doctor. Which cause your symptoms belong to, and which examinations and treatment are needed, are matters for the clinician treating you. Nothing here should be used to justify using eye drops on your own or delaying care.
