A Subscription and an API Do Not Sell the Same Thing - The Clauses Waiting at the Boundary

A price set on one person using a service and a price set on a single call, and the place where a tool bridging the two runs into the text

2026-09-05 Tech & IT

The same company sells the same model two ways. One is a monthly subscription, the other is an API billed per call. Put the two price sheets side by side and there are ranges where the second looks expensive, and then the question arrives on its own. If the subscription is already paid for, why pay the API price again?

The question is reasonable. The answer, however, is not on the price sheet. The two products price different things, so the kind of limit attached to each is different, and most decisively, they are governed by two entirely separate contracts. Once that structure is visible, it becomes clear both why the price gap sits where it does and which clauses a tool that tries to cross the boundary will run into.

The clauses quoted below come from the published terms of each provider, with the effective date given alongside. Terms get revised, and when the effective date moves the wording can move with it. This is an informational summary; the assessment of any specific situation follows the current terms of the provider concerned and the interpretation of the competent authority or court. Installation and configuration steps for any particular tool fall outside the scope of this piece.

The trigger - a tool that exposes subscription accounts as an API

In early September 2026 a repository circulated again in developer communities, introduced as a proxy that exposes AI subscription accounts as a compatible API. The name is CLIProxyAPI, and it does what the name says. It takes sessions already signed in to several providers subscription products, publishes them at a local endpoint, and lets clients that expect a standard API format attach directly.

Tools of this shape are neither the first nor the last of their kind. Which is why looking at the position the tool occupies stays useful longer than scoring the tool itself. That position compresses into one sentence: a subscription and an API sell the same model, but they are not the same product.

From there the subject forks. One branch asks why the two products were designed differently. The other asks which clauses come into play when the boundary is crossed. The first is a question about economic structure and the second is a question about contract text. Mixing them blurs both answers, so they come in order here.

The unit of pricing is different

The price gap between a subscription and an API is a consequence of the billing unit, not of marketing. A subscription prices one person using the service for a period of time. An API prices the number of tokens processed. Everything else follows from that single line.

Because a subscription is a flat fee, heavy use does not raise that month invoice. Which means that on the provider side there is no longer any brake on excessive use sitting in the price. So limits get attached where the meter would have been. Usage caps per hour or per period, response speed, and how many sessions may run at once. An API works the other way: since consumption is billed, restrictions of that kind sit looser, and in exchange there is no ceiling on the invoice.

AxisSubscriptionAPI
What is pricedOne person for a period of useThe number of tokens processed
How the fee movesFlat. More usage does not change the invoice for the monthRises in proportion to consumption
Brake on excessive useLimits rather than price. Usage, speed, concurrencyEffectively none. The spending ceiling is set by the buyer
Assumed userOne person in a conversation in front of a screenA program running on a server
The predictable sideSpendingThroughput
Sanctioned way to lower costChanging tierBatch processing, caching, model selection
Governing contractConsumer terms of useA separate business and developer agreement

The fifth row is the shortest summary of what separates the two. A subscription is a product with predictable spending and uncertain throughput; an API is a product with predictable throughput and uncertain spending. Neither is superior; each absorbs the uncertainty the other refuses. A subscription looks cheap not because the provider is taking a loss, but because the fee is the price of usage with limits attached. The limits are not a defect in the offer; they are the mechanism that makes a flat fee possible at all. Remove them and the product stops being a subscription, because nothing is left holding the average cost per subscriber inside the band the price was set against.

So trying to obtain, at a subscription price, throughput that exceeds the subscription limits is less a negotiation over price than an attempt to redefine the product. The last row of the table confirms exactly that in the language of contracts.

There were two contracts from the start

That last row is not a metaphor. Major providers attach different contract documents to their consumer service and their developer service, and the two documents explicitly exclude each other from their scope.

The OpenAI business and developer agreement cuts its own scope in the first sentence. The edition effective 1 January 2026 opens by stating that the agreement 「only applies to use of OpenAI's APIs, ChatGPT Enterprise, ChatGPT Business, ChatGPT for Clinicians, and other services for customers who are businesses and developers, and does not apply to OpenAI services used by consumers or individuals unless specified above」. The consumer terms of use, effective the same day, take precisely the opposite side, defining themselves as applying to 「ChatGPT, DALL·E, and OpenAI's other services for individuals」.

The Anthropic consumer terms write that boundary even more plainly. The edition effective 8 October 2025 notes at the top that the commercial terms of service govern use of an Anthropic API key and the Console, and then states that those commercial terms do not cover use of Claude.ai or Claude Pro by an individual or an entity. One company sells the same model under two agreements running in parallel, and carves the boundary into the documents so they do not overlap.

ProviderConsumer agreementBusiness and developer agreement
OpenAITerms of Use, effective 1 January 2026OpenAI Services Agreement, effective 1 January 2026, updated 1 December 2025
AnthropicConsumer Terms of Service, effective 8 October 2025Commercial Terms of Service, named by the consumer terms as covering the API key and Console
GoogleGoogle Terms of Service, effective 30 July 2026Product-specific additional terms exist separately
SpaceXAITerms of Service - Consumer, effective 1 September 2026Exists as a document separate from the consumer terms

What this table means is simple. The moment access obtained through a subscription account is used the way an API is used, the document that applies to that use is still the consumer agreement. The conditions of the developer contract do not follow automatically. Data handling, warranties, limitation of liability and dispute resolution all remain those of the consumer document. The price moves across; the contract does not.

Automated access - the wording splits by provider

From here the clauses go on the table as written. How consumer agreements treat automated or scripted access varies by provider in both strength and placement. That it does not generalise into a single sentence is the point of the table.

ProviderWording on automated or scripted accessEffective
OpenAIThe prohibited conduct list includes 「Automatically or programmatically extract data or Output」, followed by 「Interfere with or disrupt our Services, including circumvent any rate limits or restrictions or bypass any protective measures or safety mitigations we put on our Services」2026-01-01
AnthropicLists as prohibited conduct 「Except when you are accessing our Services via an Anthropic API Key or where we otherwise explicitly permit it, to access the Services through automated or non-human means, whether through a bot, script, or otherwise」2025-10-08
GoogleThe service abuse section prohibits bypassing systems and protective measures, and gives automated access that violates the machine-readable instructions on its web pages as an example. Separately it prohibits copying, distributing, selling or leasing any part of the services or software2026-07-30
SpaceXAIThe consumer terms carry no sentence directly governing automated access, and hand the determination of permitted and prohibited conduct to an Acceptable Use Policy document2026-09-01

What has to be read across those four rows is the divergence, not the overlap. The one-line summary that AI subscriptions all ban automation does not survive this table. One agreement lists automation itself as prohibited conduct; another aims at the outcome, at data extraction and limit circumvention; a third moves the determination into a different document. Which means changing providers changes the answer.

The place worth noting in the Anthropic wording is the exception clause. What is prohibited is not automation as such but automation that does not go through an API key, and a second exception follows it: 「or where we otherwise explicitly permit it」. That sentence structure gets used again further down.

Account credentials - here the wording converges

The automation clauses diverged; the account clauses very nearly converge. Three providers put a sentence of the same effect in the registration section of their consumer agreement.

The OpenAI terms of use say 「You may not share your account credentials or make your account available to anyone else and are responsible for all activities that occur under your account」. The Anthropic consumer terms split the same content across two sentences: 「You may not share your Account login information, Anthropic API key, or Account credentials with anyone else. You also may not make your Account available to anyone else」. The SpaceXAI consumer terms carry a functionally identical sentence in their registration clause. The Google terms of service place their wording on redistribution instead, with 「You may not copy, modify, distribute, sell, or lease any part of our services or software」.

What matters in those sentences is the tail rather than the head. A clause stating that the account holder is responsible for all activity occurring under the account is attached in common. Wherever the credentials were placed, and whatever used them, the outcome attaches to the holder.

This is also where a setup that pools several accounts behind one endpoint meets the clause. In such a setup the party actually carrying the exposure is not whoever stood up the endpoint but the holder of each account. If those accounts belong to members of an organisation, the exposure moves inside the organisation.

The one sentence that separates permitted clients

A familiar objection arrives here. Coding agents that run on subscription authentication already exist in numbers. Command-line tools and editor extensions built and shipped by the providers themselves are exactly that. Those are programs too, and nobody types each call by hand. Yet they are not a problem.

The answer sits in the exception clause flagged above. The 「or where we otherwise explicitly permit it」 in the Anthropic clause is the sentence that leaves that space open. In other words, the line the terms draw is not automation versus no automation. The line is drawn somewhere else.

  • Authentication path — does it attach with personal subscription credentials or with a developer key? As shown above, those two sit under different contracts
  • Origin of the client — was it built and shipped by the provider, or by a third party? When an agreement speaks of permission, the subject is generally the former
  • Explicitness of the permission — has the provider permitted that mode of use in writing? Tolerance and common practice belong to a different layer than the text
  • Treatment of limits — does the setup honour the usage and rate limits the provider has set? Several agreements make circumventing limits a separate prohibited item

The answers to those four differ by provider, by product, and by each revision of the terms. Which is why the judgement is not made by looking at the tool but by looking at which provider and which authentication the tool uses.

Suspension and termination - where notice attaches and where it does not

Reading clauses, people usually stop at the prohibition list. In practice what comes next matters more. Once a breach is determined, what happens and in what order. Here too the wording splits.

ProviderGrounds for suspension or terminationAdvance notice
OpenAIBreach of the terms or usage policies, necessity to comply with law, and use that could cause risk or harm to the company, its users or anyone elseAdvance notice is stated for terminating an account inactive for over a year without a paid plan. The same sentence is not attached to suspension grounded in a breach. An appeal path for a mistaken suspension is spelled out
AnthropicA determination of breach, or necessity to comply with lawStates that access may be suspended or terminated at any time without notice
GoogleGrounds are enumerated in a dedicated sectionStates that advance notice is provided before taking such action where reasonably possible, with exceptions such as preventing harm and complying with law
SpaceXAIBreach of the terms, acceptable use policy or guidelines, compliance with law, risk or harm, and inactivity over a yearStates that the company may terminate or suspend at its sole discretion without notice. An appeal path is set out separately

What this table shows is not the probability of suspension. Nobody publishes that. What it shows is how the procedure is designed. Wording that carries no advance notice means whatever was running can stop without warning, and the loss at that moment is determined not by the suspension itself but by what was hanging off that account. An appeal path, where one exists, also tells you something about timing rather than outcome: an appeal runs after access has already stopped, so whatever depended on that access is down for the length of the review. Reading the notice column and the appeal column together is closer to a recovery-time estimate than to a compliance question.

What hangs off a single account

The blast radius of a suspension scales with how many things the account was the key to. One clause common to consumer agreements enlarges that radius considerably: the clause about accounts created with a company domain address.

The OpenAI terms of use contain a clause to the effect that an account created with an email address owned by an organisation may be added to that organisation business account, after which the organisation administrator can access content and restrict or remove access to the account. The Anthropic consumer terms carry a business domain clause of the same effect. An account that began as a personal tool becomes an asset of the organisation at some point.

  • Login federation — other services signed into with that account get blocked alongside it
  • Organisational absorption — an account created on a company domain can pass under administrator control
  • Work product — conversation history, projects and uploaded files live inside the account
  • Billing — the suspension clause and the refund clause sit in the same document

What each clause type decides is broken into six strands in When one sentence in the terms can stop your account, so it is not repeated here. What this piece needs is one line: a route exists by which a choice made to save money returns, through suspension risk, as a far larger cost.

Four routes to lower cost inside the terms

The cost problem itself is real, and there are several routes for handling it inside the contract. Each gains and gives up something different, so the sensible move is to lay them out and choose.

Routing design at the request and task level, and why caching is an architectural decision rather than a discount, are in Controlling AI coding costs at the organization level; designing budgets, limits and attribution at the org level is in Governing AI token spend at the org level. The value and the cost of a brokerage layer that binds several providers into one contract belong to multi-model routing.

The fourth route, self-hosting, is of a different character. It steps out of the pricing structure and takes on the whole operational burden instead, so the break-even point varies sharply between organisations. The point of the table is not the individual techniques. It is that all six rows move cost without changing the contract. What is given up is stated, and giving it up is a design choice rather than a breach.

Why tools like this keep appearing

That the same kind of tool appears repeatedly means the demand is real. Seeing what that demand actually consists of stays useful longer than arguing over whether one tool is right or wrong.

  • The gap in pricing structure — agent work that handles long contexts repeatedly burns a lot of tokens. A flat subscription absorbs that variance while metered pricing passes it straight to the invoice. There genuinely are ranges where the felt price of the same task diverges widely between the two products
  • Convenience of consolidation — the wish to gather differing API formats behind a single endpoint. That demand is legitimate on its own, which is why gateway and router products doing the same job with developer keys form a market of their own
  • Operational load — moving between several providers and several accounts makes authentication and limit management a job each. The pressure to consolidate comes from here
  • Ecosystem inertia — clients frequently default to one particular API format, so publishing in that format suddenly makes a great many things attachable

Of those four, the last three hold just as well with a developer key rather than a subscription. That market exists separately and has several products in it. Which means most of the demand can be met without crossing between contracts, and the part that can only be met by crossing narrows to the first item alone: the gap in pricing structure.

That narrowing is worth holding on to, because it changes what the conversation is about. A demand for consolidation, for one authentication surface, for one format that many clients already speak, is a demand for engineering that any provider can serve and several already do. A demand to get metered throughput at a flat price is a demand about the commercial terms themselves, and no piece of software can settle that on the buyer behalf. The two get bundled together because a single tool can appear to answer both at once, but they separate cleanly the moment the question is asked in the order used here: what is priced, what limit is attached, which contract applies.

Reading a cause is not the same as recommending an answer

Explaining why a demand arises and recommending one particular way to satisfy it are different acts. The first looks at structure; the second makes someone else decision for them. As long as the wording diverges between providers and shifts with each revision, writing the second down in generalised form is likely to conflict with the document the reader will actually face.

What remains instead is an order of reading. A subscription and an API price different things, so the kind of limit attached differs, so the contracts are two. When a tool proposes to cross that boundary, what to check is not the polish of the tool but four items in that provider consumer agreement: the wording on automated access, the account credentials clause, the scope of clients explicitly permitted, and whether notice attaches when a breach is determined. Those four come out differently by provider, exactly as the tables above showed.

And there is still room inside the contract to move cost. Batching, caching and model selection are areas where the figure of half the standard rate is written directly into provider documentation, and what has to be given up is written into the same documentation beside it. A choice whose cost is stated can be calculated. That is what separates it from a choice that absorbs an unstated risk.

What could not be confirmed

The items below failed confirmation and were kept out of the body. Nothing was filled in by estimate.

  • Per-token API rates by provider — the OpenAI API pricing page redirected to business plan information on two attempts, so the token rate table could not be opened. The passage comparing subscription and API prices numerically was therefore left out, and only the difference in billing unit is covered
  • The SpaceXAI acceptable use policy text — the consumer terms pass the determination on automated access to that document, which could not be opened. That is why the SpaceXAI row is shorter than the other three
  • Product-specific additional terms — only the general Google terms of service are covered. Additional terms attached to individual AI products may carry separate clauses on automation or access method, and those were not examined
  • Regional availability differences — the claim that subscription and API availability differ by region appears often in explanations of demand for these tools. Provider-by-provider regional lists could not be established from primary sources, so the item was dropped from the demand section
  • Enforcement statistics — no public material was found on how often, and through what procedure, suspensions grounded in a breach actually occur. That is why the body covers procedure rather than probability
  • Runtime behaviour of the repository — the code was neither read nor run. The table is limited to published metadata and README descriptions, and whether actual behaviour matches those descriptions is a separate question
