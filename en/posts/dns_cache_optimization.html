What DNS Caching Teaches - Where to Keep What, and for How Long

TTL as a contract, four layers of cache, the problem of deciding what to throw away, and the time caching quietly locks into your deploys and rollbacks

Aug 29, 2026 Dev & Infra

Introduction: 953 bytes down to 420, and the question that follows

You finish a deploy and some people see the new screen while others still see the old one. Same code, same servers. The only difference is how far their machine had to travel to turn a name into an address. Half of everything about caching lives inside that one sentence.

There is a reason to bring this up now. Cloudflare published an engineering post describing how it shrank the memory each cache entry occupies in its DNS platform. By the company's own account, that platform sits behind several DNS services including 1.1.1.1 and holds more than 250 billion cache entries at any given time. Five optimizations later, per-entry memory dropped from 953 bytes to 420 bytes, a 56% reduction in their own benchmarks.

The 100 TB figure making the rounds deserves a careful reading. The original phrasing is that across their fleet the changes freed up roughly 100 terabytes of memory, equivalent to the RAM in 130 of their Gen 13 servers. It is not the saving on one machine, not a figure for one data center, and not an amount accumulated over some period. It is an aggregate across many machines. Whether that number is a peak or an average, and how many machines the fleet contains, the post does not say. The 953-to-420 figure is likewise a benchmark result rather than a production measurement; the production number quoted separately is p99 instance resident memory falling from 9.3 GB to 5.3 GB, and the post itself notes that resident memory includes the cache alongside other process data. All of it consists of values the operator published about itself, not figures verified by a third party.

That is as far as the product story goes. This article is not going to tell you which DNS service to use. The interesting part is the shape of the problem they ran into. Caching is ultimately a question of where you keep something, what you keep, and how long you keep it, and DNS is where those three decisions show up most plainly. What follows walks through each of them. For who actually holds a name, the layers of nameserver delegation and trust sits alongside this; for the same principle applied to images, see what really decides image delivery.

1. What a DNS cache actually stores

If you picture a DNS cache as a table mapping one name to one address, you are about halfway off. The unit of caching is not an address but the entire response to a query.

The key is a combination of three things: the name, the record type, and the class. For the same name, an A query asking for an IPv4 address and an AAAA query asking for IPv6 are separate entries. Having one cached and not the other is a perfectly normal state.

  • A and AAAA — the addresses a name resolves to. The most common, and the axis that changes most often.
  • CNAME — an alias pointing at another name. Following it produces one more lookup.
  • NS — who is responsible for this zone. The delegation path itself gets cached.
  • MX — where mail should go. Rarely changed, but when wrong, delivery stops.
  • TXT — ownership proofs, mail policies and other strings. Verification workflows often hang off these.
  • SOA — the zone's reference data. The lifetime of a negative answer comes from here.
  • CAA — which certificate authorities may issue for this name.

The value side is not a single line either. All three response sections — answer, authority, additional — go in whole, with bookkeeping attached. The example Cloudflare gave while describing its own structure has the same shape: alongside the response body, the value carries a creation time, a hit counter, and the remaining TTL. Why a hit counter matters comes back in section 5.

And there is one more item people routinely forget. Absence is stored too. The answer that a name does not exist and the answer that the name exists but has no record of that type are different situations, and both are cacheable. This is called negative caching, and section 8 explains why it is a question of stability rather than performance.

2. TTL as a contract - authoritative sets it, resolvers keep it

How long something stays cached is not decided by whoever holds the cache. The party publishing the record — the domain owner — decides. The TTL rides along with the response, and the resolver promises not to reuse the answer past that window. That ownership and retention are held by different parties determines most of the character of DNS caching.

The contract is only strict in one direction. RFC 2181 states that a TTL specifies a maximum time to live, not a mandatory one, and that implementations may impose upper bounds on received values. A resolver clipping a one-day TTL down to an hour is within spec. Extending it is not. The same document sets other rules that keep surfacing in practice.

  • Every record in an RRSet must carry the same TTL. Mixed values produce a half-cached state where only part of the set survives.
  • A TTL is unsigned with a maximum of 2,147,483,647. Only the lower 31 bits of the 32-bit field are used.
  • A TTL arriving with the most significant bit set must be treated as zero. This is the guard against a bogus huge value living forever.
  • A TTL of zero means do not cache. It is the shortest possible lifetime and sends every query to the origin.

Choosing a TTL is therefore not picking a number but picking a trade. What you gain and what you give up splits like this.

AxisWith a short TTLWith a long TTL
Speed of change propagationFast. Failovers and rollbacks land on timeSlow. The old answer lives on for that long
Load on authoritative serversQueries rise, in proportion to trafficMost queries end at the cache and never arrive
User-perceived latencyFrequent misses put lookup round trips in front of the pageLookup cost effectively disappears
During an authoritative outageThe cache drains fast, so impact arrives quicklyIt holds for a while — but recovery shows up late too
When you publish a wrong valueRecall is quickThe wrong answer circulates for the whole window
Resolver memoryHigh turnover, entries do not lingerEntries sit for a long time and occupy space

The two columns are traded against each other. There is no setting that gives you one side for free. Where this goes wrong in practice is usually treating a short TTL purely as insurance without pricing the load side, or the reverse: setting a long TTL while planning to fail over via DNS during an incident. The second one hurts more. From the moment you decide to switch, you wait for every cached copy already out there to expire before traffic actually moves.

3. There is more than one cache

Ask for the same name twice and the answer may come from different places. Which layer it hits changes the perceived speed, the way you flush it, and who is affected.

LayerWho controls itBlast radiusHow it clears
Application or browser cacheThat programA single processRestart the program, or its own setting
Operating system stub resolverThe device's administratorThat whole deviceAn OS-provided facility, or a reboot
Recursive resolver (internal or public)The resolver operatorEveryone using that resolverOnly the operator. Users wait for expiry
Authoritative serverThe domain ownerThe origin. Not a cacheNothing to clear. You steer it with TTL

The third row explains most of the trouble. A recursive resolver's cache is a shared resource. Whatever you clear on your own machine, that layer is untouched. People seeing different screens right after a deploy, or regions picking up a change at different times, usually trace back here, because every resolver expires on its own clock.

The layering forces one planning decision. If you shorten a TTL, plan from the moment propagation completes, not from the moment you make the change. Lowering a TTL to five minutes is itself a record change that reaches each resolver only as the old TTL expires. If the old value was a day, it can take up to a day for everyone to learn the new one. Shortening TTLs a day before a migration is a convention that falls straight out of this property, not superstition.

One more thing is worth adding. Which recursive resolver you use is not only a question of response time. There is a separate axis: where queries are recorded and under what policy they are handled. A resolver sits in a position to see which names a user looked up and when, and no latency number substitutes for that. Concluding it is fast, so use it is a conclusion drawn from one of two axes. Published policies and retention periods differ between operators, so anyone choosing should read the relevant official documentation and decide for themselves.

4. At scale, memory becomes the wall first

On a personal machine, DNS cache size is not a topic. A few thousand entries take a few megabytes. But for anyone running recursive resolvers at scale, that entry count reaches billions to hundreds of billions. As noted, Cloudflare stated its platform holds more than 250 billion entries at any given time.

At that scale memory grows as the product of three terms.

  • Entry count — the number of distinct name-and-type combinations. One name produces several entries.
  • Size per entry — response body, key strings, bookkeeping, and the overhead of the data structure itself.
  • Replication — the same cache exists on many instances in many locations. Bytes saved on one machine multiply by the count.

The third term is what the 100 TB figure actually is. Roughly 500 bytes saved per entry, multiplied across the fleet, produces an aggregate — it does not mean any single machine now uses 100 TB less.

An uncomfortable property of cache design surfaces here. Popular names are few, and most names are used once and never again. A handful of large services get queried countless times per second, and behind them stretches a long tail of names requested once and never returned to: autogenerated hostnames, tracking subdomains, one-off verification strings, and typos. A substantial share of cache capacity is occupied by things that will not be used again.

That is waste, but it also follows directly from what a cache is. A cache must decide without knowing what will arrive next. If you could identify the entries that will never return, you would not have admitted them in the first place. So the remaining problem narrows to one thing: when space runs out, what do you throw away?

5. What to throw away - eviction policy and its traps

Entries leave a DNS cache by two routes. One is the lifetime running out, the other is running out of room. The first is a contract; the second is a policy. And there is no correct answer to the second.

The sentence Cloudflare used to describe its own behavior maps onto this exactly: as queries arrive, the cache fills until it hits its maximum entry count, at which point older or less popular items are evicted to make room. Note that two criteria appear together in that description.

  • How recently was it used (LRU) — discard what has gone untouched longest. Simple to implement and tracks recent shifts well.
  • How often was it used (LFU) — discard the lowest hit counts. Protects sustained popularity.

Both fail, in their own ways. LRU's weakness is a scanning workload. When names queried exactly once flood in, each becomes the most recently used entry in turn and pushes out entries that have held their place for a long time. Things that just arrived and will never be needed again evict things that will certainly be needed next. This actually happens when automation or a scanner queries nonexistent names in bulk.

LFU's weakness sits at the opposite end. An entry that was once popular keeps its seat on the strength of its counter. New arrivals have low counts and get pushed out before they can establish themselves, so the cache freezes into the past. Real implementations therefore rarely use either in pure form: they blend the two, give new entries a probation window, or decay counters over time. None of those is free; it is a matter of matching the policy to the workload. Keeping eviction minimal and choosing simplicity is still a valid design, and that line of thinking is covered separately in the case for a deliberately simple cache server.

DNS adds one more condition on top. Remaining lifetime belongs in the eviction decision. An entry about to expire cannot hold its place long, while an entry with a long remaining life is worth keeping if it is still being used. Recency, frequency and remaining lifetime all feed one judgment.

6. Shrinking the representation - never store the same value twice

Everything so far was about what to discard. There is an opposite approach: discard nothing, and store the same thing in fewer bytes. If you can cut size without giving up an ounce of correctness, this direction always comes first.

The five items Cloudflare published all belong to this family. The implementation details are tied to their language and their data structures, but the ideas travel anywhere caching happens. Grouped by principle, the items the company described read like this.

  • Do not carry spare capacity — a container that might grow later always reserves more room than it holds. Once an entry is in the cache its contents never change, so those growable containers become fixed-size representations. The post states outright that once a response is stored, it is never modified again, and that immutability is the premise the rest rests on.
  • Do not split the list — keeping the three response sections as separate lists means paying bookkeeping per list. Merge them into one and mark the boundaries, and that cost is paid once.
  • Drop repeated names — records within a response commonly share the same owner name. Store the string once and omit the rest instead of repeating it.
  • Do not size everything for the largest case — a type that holds several shapes is generally sized to the largest shape. Move the rare large variant out of line and the common cases shrink.
  • Do not rebuild a representation you already have — records arrive in wire format. Rather than unpacking them into structures, keep the original bytes and parse on demand.

One principle runs through all five: never hold the same information twice, and never pre-purchase room you will not use. There is a cost. A packed representation like the last item pays a parsing cost on read. That is the classic space-for-time trade, except in this case the company reported that lookup latency in its own benchmarks fell 19%, from 828 to 670 nanoseconds. The reason is guessable: smaller data fits more readily into processor caches, and that gain can outweigh the parsing cost. But this came out of their workload and their benchmark, and nothing guarantees the same direction for a different data structure.

7. What caching costs you - stale answers and tied hands

A cache is not free speed. It is a trade of some correctness for time, and the bill arrives precisely when you are least able to pay it.

  • The window where stale answers circulate — from the moment you change a value until every cache sees the new one, there is a gap of up to one TTL. During it, both answers are live.
  • Rollback is slower than deploy — undoing a wrong value suffers the same delay. You may catch the problem in five seconds, but recall still takes a TTL.
  • Asymmetry between outage and recovery — a long TTL masks an authoritative outage for a while. That sounds good, until you notice it also means the news that you recovered arrives late.
  • Partial rollout — resolvers expire on different clocks, so at the same instant different users go to different destinations. Debugging gets hard when sessions straddle two generations.
  • Verification workflows stall — anything that confirms a record exists, like ownership proof or certificate issuance, runs into the cached old state. Particularly when an absence is cached, a record you just added stays invisible for a while.

There is a view that refusing to use expired answers is not the right answer either. RFC 8767 describes using stale data in the exceptional circumstance that a recursive resolver is unable to refresh the information — the judgment being that an old answer beats no answer when the origin is down. The document says to attempt a refresh first and use stale data only after a genuine failure, and puts forward recommended values: a retention window after expiry of 1 to 3 days, and a TTL of 30 seconds on stale responses returned to clients.

The risks the same document lists deserve equal reading. Signatures may be returned outside their validity period, the window for attacks against domain-validated certificates may widen, and holding expired data at length increases memory pressure. In short, this feature sells freshness to buy availability. It is not a question of on or off, but of which failure is more expensive for you.

8. Absence and typos - what negative caching does

Queries for names that do not exist are more common than you would guess: typos, names left behind in retired configs, badly built automation, and traffic that throws random labels at you. Sending all of them to the authoritative server means using the most expensive path to fetch the least useful answer.

RFC 2308 addresses this head on. The document splits negative answers into two kinds. NXDOMAIN means the queried name itself does not exist. NODATA means the response carried no error but contains no records of the requested type. They are different situations and belong handled differently.

Where does the lifetime come from? A negative answer ships with the zone's SOA record, and the document specifies the entry's TTL is taken from the minimum of the SOA MINIMUM field and the TTL of the SOA itself. It also records a recommended range: values of one to three hours have been found to work well, and values exceeding one day have been found to be problematic. On the effect, it argues that a large proportion of DNS traffic on the Internet could be eliminated if all resolvers implemented negative caching.

For anyone operating a zone, this section means two things.

  • This is why the record you just created is not visible. If anything queried the name before you created it, that negative answer is sitting in a cache. The wait is governed by the remaining lifetime of the old negative response, not by the new record's TTL.
  • The SOA MINIMUM value is an easily forgotten dial. It gets set once at zone creation and never revisited, yet it decides how long absences circulate.

The way negative caching pollutes a cache is worth spelling out too. Nonexistent names do not overlap with each other. When near-random names arrive in bulk, entry count rises accordingly, and as section 5 showed, each becomes the most recently used and claims a seat. None of them will ever be queried again. Negative caching protects the origin, and it pays for that protection with a different resource: cache space.

9. What operators actually decide - how to pick a TTL

This section is not going to say use N seconds. That number differs per name, and borrowing someone else's value usually does not fit. What goes here instead is the questions that must be answered before a number exists.

Two axes carry most of it: how often does this name change, and how long can you tolerate a wrong value being live. Once those are settled, the rest follows.

Answer this firstSignals pointing shorterSignals pointing longer
How often the destination changesScaling and failover genuinely happen oftenIt has pointed to the same place for years
Tolerable time with a wrong value livePayments or login, where failure costs immediatelyStatic assets, where an old value is survivable
What performs the failoverDNS is the only mechanism you haveAnycast or a load balancer absorbs it below
Headroom on authoritative serversYou can absorb more queriesAlready tight, or billed per query
Near-term change plansA migration window is scheduledNothing planned for a while
Procedures tied to this recordCertificate issuance or ownership proof depends on itIt is closer to a human-readable convenience

The way to use the table is simple. Do not apply one TTL to a whole domain; split it by record character. There is no reason the A record of a service you move often should carry the same value as an MX record that has been fixed for years. And if a migration is coming, put the property from section 3 into the schedule: the shortening itself takes the old TTL to propagate.

One last point. A short TTL is not a substitute for availability design. A plan to fail over via DNS during an incident rests on the assumption that every cache cooperates, and the spec does not guarantee that assumption. Resolvers may clip a TTL shorter but are not obliged to honor it exactly, and where serving stale data is enabled, answers survive longer in exactly the exceptional situations you were planning for. DNS is a way of announcing a switch, not a way of forcing one instantly.

10. Measurement - what hit rate alone hides

Hit rate sits at the top of every cache dashboard, and that single number says remarkably little. When a small popular set dominates total queries, the ratio stays high even if everything else misses. Whether 90% is excellent or dreadful cannot be judged on its own.

What you need is the character of the misses. There are at least three kinds, and each calls for a different remedy.

  • First-ever request — never requested before, so it could not have been cached. Unavoidable; addressing it means talking about prefetching, not caching.
  • Expired entry — it was there and its lifetime ran out. If this is rising, TTL settings are the place to look.
  • Evicted entry — pushed out for space before expiry, then requested again. If this kind is growing, the problem is capacity or eviction policy. It is also the only direct signal that the cache is genuinely being wasted.

A few companions are worth watching alongside it.

  • Latency distribution on misses — look at the tail, not the mean. Few misses that are each very expensive still feel bad.
  • Entry count against memory — only by watching both together do you notice per-entry size creeping upward.
  • Share of negative responses — a rising share of absences suggests the pollution described in section 8.
  • Eviction rate — always count departures by expiry separately from departures by eviction.
  • Share of entries never reused — admitted, then gone without a single further hit. The most honest indicator of whether the cache is doing work.

One principle of measurement to add: comparison only holds once conditions match. Mixing warm-cache and cold-cache runs makes neither number trustworthy. When you change a policy, change one thing at a time and record both what you changed and what you measured.

What could not be confirmed

  • Whether the 100 TB figure is a peak or an average, and how many machines the fleet contains, is not stated in the original. What is confirmable is that it is a fleet-wide aggregate and that it converts to the RAM in 130 Gen 13 servers.
  • Per-entry 953 to 420 bytes, insert throughput of 625,000 to 893,000 per second, and lookup latency of 828 to 670 nanoseconds are all the operator's own benchmark values. Hardware and workload composition could not be confirmed at the time of writing.
  • The production figure of p99 instance resident memory falling from 9.3 to 5.3 GB carries the post's own caveat that it includes process data beyond the cache. It cannot be read as a cache-only saving.
  • Every figure is published by the operator about itself; no third-party verification was found.
  • What share of those 250 billion entries is actually reused has not been published. The long-tail discussion in section 4 rests on general cache behavior, not on that platform's measurements.
  • The original post does not cover negative caching or NXDOMAIN. Section 8 comes entirely from RFC 2308.

Wrapping up

Cache design compresses into three decisions: where to keep it, what to keep, and how long to keep it. DNS makes a good textbook because those three sit in different hands. Where it lives is decided per layer by different people, what gets discarded is decided by the resolver operator, and how long it survives is decided by the domain owner. When the three fall out of step, different screens for different people emerges from the gap.

Three things to take away. First, a TTL is a contract, not a performance knob. What you gain and what you surrender split across the two columns of that table, and deciding from one side alone guarantees a bill from the other. Second, when memory becomes the constraint, there are two directions — decide what to discard, or store the same thing smaller. The one that costs no correctness always comes first. Third, hit rate is where the question starts, not where it ends. What you missed and why determines the remedy.

Next time someone says a deploy is landing differently for different people, answer one question before hunting for causes: which layer is holding the old answer right now? Naming that layer alone separates what you can do from what you cannot.
