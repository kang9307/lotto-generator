Individuals Got Faster and the Organization Did Not - The Gains Leak at Boundaries, Not Inside the Work

The time spent waiting inside approvals and handoffs, and the metric that was defined not to see it

2026-09-07 Trends & Culture

Two speeds now run on separate clocks inside the same company. On one clock, work that took half a day until yesterday finishes in an hour. On the other clock, that same output still takes two weeks to reach the next stage. People feel the first change in their own hands. Nobody feels the second one, because on the second clock nothing has changed at all.

The usual prescription for this mismatch is change the culture. The trouble with that prescription is that culture is not observed, and anything that is not observed cannot be checked for whether it went down. The axis here is different. Instead of naming a mood, it locates the specific points where a gain produced by an individual fails to carry into organizational output and leaks away. Those points are not inside the work. They sit between one piece of work and the next, at every place where ownership changes hands. Call them boundaries.

Two kinds of numbers appear below. One comes from a consultancy running its own survey; the other comes from a research programme that publishes the definitions of its metrics. For the first kind, the claim stops at the survey name and the sample size, because parts of the methodology are not public. For the second kind, the definition is quoted as written. The argument from the essay that prompted this piece is marked as a claim, not as an established fact.

The trigger - what was named the elephant

The starting point is a single personal essay with the word brownfield in its title. In engineering usage a brownfield is ground where systems are already running, an environment that does not begin from a blank page. Map that onto a company and you get an organization that already has departments, an approval chain and named owners for everything. The essay places an elephant in the middle of that brownfield, in the sense of the thing everybody in the room can see and nobody names.

What it names as the elephant is neither tooling nor budget. According to the Korean summary that carried the essay, the elephant is identity that has become fused with a role and with departmental authority. The same summary reports three strands of argument. Founders move with high autonomy across boundaries that stay fluid, while an established organization partitions work by ownership and demands coordination at every layer, shaving autonomy off at each crossing. Because of that, an individual automating their own tasks inside an existing role cannot substitute for organizational transformation. What is actually required is a structural redesign of authority, decision rights and responsibility.

All of that is a claim. It comes from an essay written by one person, not from a controlled study. The claim is nevertheless convertible into something testable. Saying autonomy gets shaved off at each crossing is the same as saying time is added every time a boundary is crossed, and time can be measured. Performing that conversion is what the rest of this piece does.

Individual speed and organizational speed measure different intervals

The two speeds share a word and measure different things. Individual speed shrinks hands-on time: the stretch during which somebody is drafting, coding, or assembling material. Organizational speed measures from the moment a request is received to the moment a result is delivered. That second interval holds hands-on time plus several other things: time spent waiting for a turn, time spent waiting for an approval, time spent queuing against another department schedule, and time spent redoing whatever came back.

Once the structure looks like that, the outcome is settled by arithmetic. The smaller the share of total elapsed time that hands-on work occupies, the less total elapsed time falls no matter how far that share is compressed. Halving hands-on time and halving total elapsed time are entirely different achievements. One person can accomplish the first. No individual can accomplish the second, because the second is a property of the wiring rather than of any seat in it.

One more effect stacks on top. When the upstream step accelerates, work arrives in front of the downstream step sooner. If the downstream step still has the same throughput, everything that arrives earlier simply joins a longer queue. That mechanism was worked through in What Actually Speeds Up When You Speed Up a Non-Bottleneck in terms of work in progress, batch size and utilization, so only the conclusion is borrowed here: upstream acceleration lengthens the queue before it shortens the total. The wider version of the gap between perceived speed and delivered output is in The Productivity Mirage.

The leak is not inside the work, it is at the boundary

A boundary is a point where ownership changes. It sits between departments, between teams, between job families, between systems, and between legal entities. Often the lines drawn on the org chart are the boundaries, but plenty of boundaries never appear on that chart. If data ownership is split by department, a boundary exists inside a single division even when the chart shows none.

An individual gain is created inside a boundary. It is then settled at the boundary. The settlement metaphor is exact, because crossing carries a price. The item has to be reshaped into a form the receiving side can accept. It has to take its place in someone else schedule. And it has to leave a record behind in case it comes back later. None of those three gets cheaper because the work that preceded them got faster.

One pattern runs down the table. Everything in the right-hand column is somebody else calendar. Your work getting faster affects the left column, and the right column is what sets total elapsed time. The reason individual gains fail to carry into organizational output fits into that single sheet.

Four things a single boundary actually charges you

Lumping boundary cost together as coordination overhead makes it impossible to attack, because a lump has no line items. Split into items, each one can be measured and priced separately.

Translation - the price of changing the format

This is the work of rewriting the same content into the form the other side reads. An engineering design note becomes a product brief, the brief becomes a funding request, the request becomes contract language. Nothing in the substance changes and the clock still runs. This price does not fall when the preceding work speeds up. If anything it rises, because faster upstream work produces more artifacts and every artifact needs translating.

Consent - the price of getting approval

This is what gets added when the decision right does not sit with the person doing the work. Its character differs from the other three. Translation, compliance and rework are all working time; consent is mostly waiting time. That is why adding people does not shrink it. When there is one approver, that one calendar is the ceiling, and no amount of upstream capacity moves the ceiling.

Compliance - the price of matching policy and procedure

Security review, privacy impact assessment, accounting treatment, audit trail. This price rises with organizational size, rises further in regulated industries, and rises again after any incident. It is not a price that can be removed. It is a price that can be settled in advance. When criteria exist in writing beforehand, review becomes verification. When they do not, every review becomes a fresh act of judgment with a fresh and unpredictable duration.

Rework - the price of redoing what came back

The most expensive line item by a wide margin. When something bounces back, what disappears is not only the time spent redoing it. Two waiting intervals are added: the wait before it returned, and the wait after it is sent again. The real cost of one bounce is therefore several times the rework itself, and the excess lands on no report anywhere.

Where approval is the default configuration, not the exception

Even at the frontier of automation, the human gate does not disappear. In the survey Bain published inside its own report, the share running agents fully autonomously in production is 7 percent. The share that requires human approval for agent decisions is 38 percent, and the share operating with guardrails where a human handles exceptions is 32 percent.

Those three values are not added together into one ratio here. The question wording and whether the options were mutually exclusive are not published, so there is no basis for deciding whether summing is even valid. Something reads clearly without summing, though. Each of the two human-in-the-loop answers is roughly five times larger than the fully autonomous answer.

The conclusion is not that automation failed. It is that automation did not remove the boundary. The work moved to a machine while the gate stayed in the same human seat. When that happens, total elapsed time is set by the part that stayed, not by the part that moved. The separate question of what a single person newly needs when they start delegating was handled in When You Stop Writing Code and Start Delegating It. That one is a problem inside one seat; this one is a problem in the wiring between seats.

The diagnosis that the technology worked and the value did not arrive

The same report compresses its diagnosis into one line: the technology worked, and the value did not arrive. The source printed against the charts is the Bain Automation and AI Pathfinder Survey 2026, and the sample notation is 951 global companies.

The figures describe the gap between what was targeted and what landed. Thirty-seven percent of respondents aimed at cost reductions in the 11 to 20 percent band, while roughly 40 percent of those actually measuring outcomes came in at 0 to 10 percent. Despite that, 90 percent are increasing budgets. And 44 percent answered that they intend to fund the next wave of investment out of savings from the previous round of automation, which means the next line of spending is already booked against savings that have not shown up.

What Bain names as the number one obstacle is data access and integration. Forty-one percent picked it as the single biggest barrier, ahead of compliance, budget and skills gaps. Look at the nature of that item again and it stops reading as a purely technical problem. It is rare for a company to be unable to reach its own data because the storage technology is missing. The usual reason is that data ownership is split by department, moving it requires an approval, and the formats do not match. Which makes it a boundary.

One qualifier belongs next to these numbers. A survey a consultancy runs on its own is not independent of what that consultancy sells. A diagnosis that operating models need redesigning also comes from the party that would be engaged to redesign them. That is not a reason to discard the figures, but whether an independent study reproduces the same values remains a separate and open question.

Where identity gets caught

Here is the essay central claim. When a role definition has become a self definition, moving authority stops being an adjustment of duties and becomes a change to how a person is defined. That is why, inside the same organization, rolling out a tool passes without friction while reassigning decision rights takes months. A tool does not touch the role. Authority is the role.

Supporting that claim with a psychological theory would strengthen it, and that link is not made here. What is done instead is a conversion into observable form. Who is resisting is a question that never yields a usable answer, because resistance is voiced in the language of procedure rather than the language of people. These questions work better.

  • Has any item been removed from the approval list in the past year? A list that only grows in one direction is not a risk control instrument, it is a marker of authority
  • Among rejection reasons, what is the ratio between objections to content and objections to process? If the second dominates, what the gate performs is not quality control
  • Is there a recent case where the same decision was moved down to a lower seat? If there is none, autonomy is not yet an item under discussion, whatever is said about it
  • Do the role descriptions list deliverables or outcomes? If only deliverables appear, results beyond the boundary belong to nobody by definition

None of the four evaluates a person. All four are answered from documents and logs. The reason identity is hard to work with is often not that it is an emotion. It is that no observation point was ever chosen for it.

The metric cannot see the boundary - the clock starts at the commit

This is the most immediately usable section. The most widely used speed metrics in software organizations are the four keys from DORA, and the published definition of change lead time reads as follows: the amount of time it takes for a change to go from committed to version control to deployed in production.

Read literally, the clock starts at the commit. Everything before it is outside the clock. The request arriving, reaching a prioritization meeting, attracting budget, getting an owner assigned, having a design agreed, receiving a go-ahead: none of that enters this metric. And the boundaries described in every section above sit precisely in that stretch.

This is not an argument that the DORA metric is wrong. It was defined to measure the health of the delivery path and it does that job well. The problem appears when it is treated as the representative figure for organizational speed, because the interval a tool improves overlaps the interval the metric measures. Less time writing code shortens commit to deploy and the metric improves. The metric improves even if none of that improvement carries into intake to delivery.

The 2025 DORA study points the same way in its own diagnosis. It characterizes the primary role of AI as an amplifier that magnifies the strengths and the weaknesses an organization already has. Amplifier is the precise word because it specifies no direction. Where boundary cost is low, an individual gain flows straight through. Where boundary cost is high, the identical gain piles up in front of the boundary and stays there.

Four metrics that cross boundaries

Nothing existing has to be thrown away. It is enough to stand a second metric beside each one, with the clock starting somewhere else.

  • Total lead time from intake to delivery — the same measurement with the start moved from commit to request received. If a ticketing system is already in use, no new instrumentation is needed; the difference between created and closed produces it. Put it side by side with commit-based lead time and the gap between them is the size of the boundary stretch
  • Waiting time ratio — the share of total lead time during which nobody has hands on the item. Derive it by summing the stretches in the status change history where no owner was assigned or the state was pending review. If this value exceeds 80 percent, then every attempt to speed up the work itself has a ceiling of 20 percent
  • Handoff count — how many times one request changes owner, approximated by counting changes to the assignee field. This value differs in kind from the others. It measures structure rather than time, which is why it does not fall when people simply try harder
  • Bounce rate — the proportion of items that cross a boundary and come back. Derive it from the share of histories where a state moved backwards. It resembles the DORA change failure rate but measures a different place: change failure rate looks after deployment, and bounces happen before it

All four can be built from logs that already exist, with no new tooling. And all four do not attribute to individuals. That property matters more than it looks. The moment a metric attributes to a person it becomes an evaluation instrument, and the moment it becomes an evaluation instrument people begin managing the number rather than the thing the number was standing in for.

Two different prescriptions - fewer boundaries or cheaper crossings

Two things that get mixed together need separating here. Reducing the number of boundaries and reducing the price of crossing one are different prescriptions, with different effect sizes and different levels of resistance.

Fewer boundaries - merge the ownership

This direction builds a unit that owns one request all the way to the end. Reorganizing functional groups into mission groups, moving decision rights down to the working seat, letting one team own everything from design to operation: all of it belongs here. The effect is large. Removing one boundary removes the four cost items attached to it in a single stroke.

The resistance is equally large. Merging ownership means rewriting somebody role definition, which runs straight into the identity problem from the previous section. This prescription therefore usually has to start from above, and it takes a long time even after it starts. The wider discussion about redrawing organizational shape itself sits separately in What Is an AI-Native Organization.

Cheaper crossings - make the toll smaller

This direction leaves the boundaries in place and lowers the toll. Fix intake conditions in writing, settle approval criteria in advance so that matching items only need verifying, standardize the handoff format, and route only exceptions to a person. The effect is smaller than removing boundaries, but it touches no role and can start today. For internal documents to play that part they have to be governed as documents, and what happens when that condition collapses is set out in Your Internal Knowledge Base Rots Quietly.

The bottom row is the decision rule. When waiting piles up at a single approval, lowering the toll recovers most of it. When one request changes owner six or seven times, no reduction in the toll survives being multiplied by six, and the count itself has to come down. Which case applies cannot be known before measuring. That is why measurement comes before redesign, not after it.

Trap one - individual metrics multiply boundaries

Lines of code, tickets closed, cases handled: all easy to count per person, and used constantly for exactly that reason. They share one property. They are stamped at the moment an individual finishes. The item is recorded as complete before it has crossed the boundary at all.

Where the metric is stamped is where optimization happens. If finishing quickly inside your own stretch is what counts, then time spent polishing what you hand over is a loss. So handoff quality falls, bounces rise, and boundary cost goes up. That is the route by which strengthening individual metrics makes organizational lead time worse.

There is a second effect. Measuring performance per person creates pressure to slice work smaller, because each contribution has to be separable to be counted. Slicing work adds owners, adding owners adds boundaries, and adding boundaries multiplies the costs above by the new count. The general problem of easy-to-count things becoming the metric was covered in The Productivity Mirage, and the tendency for completion signals to arrive before outcome signals is in Why Effort Drifts Toward the Controllable.

The test fits on one line. If a metric is stamped at an individual completion, that metric cannot see boundaries. To see boundaries, the stamp has to happen at delivery.

Trap two - what the conclusion that the org is broken leaves behind

Everything so far converges on one conclusion: the bottleneck is the wiring, not individual capability. The conclusion may well be correct, and it carries a dangerous property. For anyone without decision rights it leaves no available action. The statement that structure is the problem tends to land as the statement that there is nothing I can do.

So one last separation is worth making. Removing a boundary requires authority. Reducing the waiting that piles up in front of one is available today to practitioners on either side of it.

  • Hand over in a form that needs no follow-up question — preventing one bounce removes two waiting intervals. Collecting the last five questions the receiving side asked is usually enough to show what should have been filled in beforehand
  • Pre-fill the fields the approver actually uses — an approval chain generally looks at a fixed handful of things. Empty fields add a round trip; filled fields leave only the judgment
  • Split into approvable sizes — one large item waits for a large block in the approver calendar. Several small ones get handled in the gaps. The same total volume waits very differently
  • Do not push more in just because upstream got faster — if throughput past the boundary is unchanged, everything pushed in only lengthens the queue. Your completion count rises and the organization delivery date slips
  • Log the waiting time in your own stretch — this alone shortens nothing. It produces the numbers you will need when arguing that the structure should change. In that argument the weakest card is impression and the strongest is a record

None of the five substitutes for organizational redesign, and treating them as a substitute would be the local optimization described above. Their usefulness lies elsewhere. They make the interval before a redesign begins survivable, and they manufacture the material with which a redesign can be demanded. The mirror-image problem, where people progressively lose the ability to verify as automation advances, is handled separately in AI Anesthetizes the Entire Organization.

What could not be confirmed

  • The original essay text — the source page refused access, so its body could not be compared sentence by sentence. The essay argument is therefore carried only through the Korean summary, and no direct quotation appears above. The definition of the elephant, the autonomy gap and the identity fusion are all second-hand through that summary
  • The Bain survey methodology — what the public report page prints is the survey name and the sample size of 951. The fielding period, respondent eligibility, industry mix, country distribution and question wording are not carried there. Press coverage adds a fielding month, a revenue threshold and a sector count, but those details were not reproduced in the same form in the report itself
  • Whether the three agent-deployment values can be summed — it is not published whether the options behind 7 percent, 38 percent and 32 percent were mutually exclusive or allowed multiple selection. No summed ratio was therefore used in the body
  • An industry benchmark for flow efficiency — no primary source was available giving a typical percentage for actual working time as a share of total lead time. Waiting time ratio is therefore presented only as a value each organization must measure, with no standard figure offered
  • Research on identity fused with role — related concepts exist in psychology, but the original papers could not be checked, so not even the concept names were borrowed. That section keeps to the essay claim and to questions answerable from documents
  • Boundary cost expressed in money — no public data was found converting the cost of one crossing into a monetary figure. Every item in the point-by-point table is expressed in time and counts only
