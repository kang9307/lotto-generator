Can Company Data Go Into an LLM - Judgment Criteria and Building an Internal Policy

Classification and routing, not prohibition - how to design an AI data policy that actually runs

2026-07-27 Dev & Infra
About this article — A practitioner's reference, not legal advice; discuss specific situations with a lawyer or your data protection officer. The legal discussion below is based on South Korean law; elsewhere the underlying questions are the same but the statutes are not, so map them onto your own jurisdiction. The vendor policies quoted here reflect documentation reviewed as of July 2026, and terms in this field change often. Read the primary sources yourself when you make an adoption decision.

Introduction: Start by Admitting Why Prohibition Rules Never Hold

Almost every company produces the same first document when generative AI arrives. Do not enter company information into external AI services. One line, one prohibition. The problem is that it is broken almost without exception, and the reason is not employee ethics. It is a failure of policy design.

The numbers make the case. In the TrustedTech workplace shadow AI survey published in May 2026, 65 percent of decision makers said they use unapproved AI tools, against 31 percent of staff below them. The people writing the rules break them more often than the people they are written for. In the same survey, roughly 44 percent said their organization offers no training on safe AI use, and about a third said they would keep using these tools even under a ban carrying disciplinary consequences. A separate 2026 PagerDuty survey of 1,250 office workers at companies with revenue above 500 million dollars found 66 percent had used AI for work while believing internal policy did not allow it.

The real effect of a prohibition rule is not to eliminate usage but to push it outside the company's field of view. Work moves from the corporate account to a personal one, from the company laptop to a personal phone. Once it crosses that line a different set of terms applies, and the organization loses even the ability to know what data left, when, and to whom. It is the classic pattern of losing control by trying to assert it.

This article is therefore not about how to phrase a prohibition, but how an organization builds its own judgment criteria and turns them into a policy document. The structure is simple: classify data into tiers, assign each tier an approved route, and provide tooling that makes those routes usable. A policy missing any one of the three exists only on paper.

1. The Starting Point - The Same Model Behaves Differently Depending on the Door

One fact has to be settled before writing a single clause. What employees casually call putting it into the AI actually refers to several completely different routes. Even for the same model from the same vendor, entering through a consumer chat window, a paid API, or a managed enterprise workspace changes whether the data trains the model, how long it is retained, and what contractual position you hold.

1.1 The Fork Between Consumer Chat and API or Enterprise Agreements

Broadly, free and personal consumer plans lean toward training use and longer retention, while paid APIs and enterprise agreements lean toward no training use and shorter retention. The reason is structural. An individual user has no lever beyond accepting the terms, whereas a corporate customer can demand a data processing agreement, and that demand became a feature worth selling.

What makes this dangerous in practice is that the two routes look identical on screen. A chat window in a browser gives no visual clue whether it is signed in with a personal or a corporate account. If the policy does not spell out the difference, employees have no realistic way to tell them apart.

This contrast gives your policy language its authority. The sentence do not put company data into consumer-plan chat is not vague caution. It is a conclusion drawn from differences the vendors themselves publish.

1.3 What Zero Data Retention Actually Covers

Zero data retention is presented in sales material as though it were absolute, but the documentation reveals a tightly bounded scope. Anthropic's data retention documentation lists a long set of exclusions: the console and prompt experimentation interface, managed agents that persist session state, consumer plans, batch processing and the files API, and code execution containers. Certain models are documented as requiring 30-day retention, so zero data retention cannot apply to them at all, and content flagged by trust and safety systems is described as retained for up to two years regardless of contract form.

OpenAI's documentation carries a comparable caveat: even where zero data retention or relaxed abuse monitoring applies, image and file inputs may be retained to detect child sexual abuse material. Zero data retention is a substantial risk reduction, not a guarantee that nothing persists, and your policy should carry that nuance verbatim. If people come away believing we have a contract, so anything goes, you are worse off than before you signed.

2. What Sits Above the Contract - Legal Preservation Obligations

However carefully you read the documentation, one variable stays outside your control. Litigation. A contractual deletion promise can be overridden by a court preservation order.

There is a concrete example. In the copyright litigation brought by the New York Times against OpenAI, it was reported that in May 2025 OpenAI was ordered to preserve chat logs. On October 9, 2025 the presiding judge was reported to have lifted the preservation obligation for output logs generated going forward, while historical data from specified periods and logs tied to accounts identified by the plaintiffs remained subject to retention. In November 2025, reporting indicated that an order requiring production of a large volume of de-identified conversation logs in discovery was maintained.

Adjudicating that dispute is not the purpose of this article. The operational lessons that survive it are these.

  • A deletion policy is a peacetime promise. Once a preservation obligation attaches, the ordering of commitments can change.
  • Nothing is retained today does not mean nothing tomorrow. Policies shift with litigation, regulation, and product changes.
  • The last line of defense is not the contract but not sending the data. This is the fundamental reason tier classification exists.
  • Your policy must state a review cadence explicitly. A document that surveys the landscape once and coasts for three years is a liability here.

3. What the Law Actually Requires

Your policy does not rest on vendor terms alone. Statutory requirements bind the company regardless of what a vendor promises. The statutes in this section are South Korean law: the Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA), Article 28-8 on cross-border transfers and Article 28-2 on pseudonymized data, and the Unfair Competition Prevention and Trade Secret Protection Act, Article 2(2) on trade secrets. Elsewhere you face a different but structurally analogous regime - GDPR Chapter V governs personal data leaving the EEA, and every major jurisdiction has a trade secret statute with its own reasonable-measures test. Read what follows for the questions it forces you to answer, then map those onto your local rules rather than copying the citations.

3.1 Cross-Border Transfer of Personal Data - PIPA Article 28-8

Sending personal data to an overseas provider's AI service raises the question of cross-border transfer. Article 28-8(1) prohibits, as a general rule, providing personal data abroad, outsourcing its processing abroad, or storing it abroad, then sets out the following exceptions. It expressly states that provision includes cases where the data is accessed, so API calls processed on overseas servers can fall within scope.

  • Where the data subject has given separate consent to the transfer
  • Where a statute, treaty, or international agreement contains a special provision on cross-border transfer
  • Where it is outsourced processing or storage necessary to conclude and perform a contract with the data subject, disclosed in the privacy policy or notified by email or similar means
  • Where the recipient holds a certification published by the Personal Information Protection Commission (having implemented safeguards and rights-protection measures)
  • Where the Commission has recognized the destination country's regime as providing an equivalent level of protection

If you rely on consent, paragraph 2 requires that you notify in advance the items transferred, the destination country with the timing and method of transfer, the recipient, the purpose and retention period, and the method for refusing. Paragraph 3 requires renewed notice and consent when any notified item changes, and paragraph 5 prohibits concluding a transfer contract on terms that violate the Act. In practice the point that most often trips organizations up is mundane: whether the privacy policy actually says that AI services are used and that data crosses borders.

3.2 Pseudonymization as a Detour and Its Limits - Article 28-2

Article 28-2(1) permits processing pseudonymized data without the data subject's consent for purposes such as statistics, scientific research, and archiving in the public interest. It adds that when such data goes to a third party it must not include information that could identify a specific individual.

Some read this as meaning pseudonymized data can be fed to an AI freely, but it is a special exception operating within the enumerated purposes. It is hard to argue that ordinary chatbot use for workplace efficiency falls automatically within statistics, scientific research, or public-interest archiving. On August 6, 2025 the Personal Information Protection Commission published its guidance on personal data processing for developing and using generative AI, splitting the lifecycle into four stages - purpose setting, strategy, training and development, and deployment and management - with considerations for each. In Korea, treat that guidance as the drafting baseline; elsewhere, look for the equivalent regulator guidance.

3.3 Secrecy Management for Trade Secrets - Unfair Competition Prevention Act Article 2(2)

For data that is not personal data - source code, design documents, price and cost sheets - a different body of law applies. Article 2(2) defines a trade secret as information on production or sales methods, or other technical or managerial information useful to business, that is not publicly known, has independent economic value, and has been managed as a secret. Practitioners break this into three elements.

  • Non-publicity — not disclosed through publications or similar channels and not ordinarily obtainable except through the holder
  • Economic usefulness — it confers a competitive advantage, or its acquisition or development required substantial cost, time, and effort
  • Secrecy management — confidentiality markings or notices, restrictions on who may access it and how, and imposed confidentiality obligations, such that the fact of secrecy management is objectively recognizable

The third element is wired directly into your AI policy. If a company left exports to external AI services uncontrolled, an opposing party in a later dispute has room to argue about whether the information was in fact managed as a secret. Conversely, documented tier definitions, an approved tool list, access controls, training records, and approval logs become evidence of management effort. Building an AI policy is therefore not only compliance but the work of protecting your own assets. Courts decide each case on the full factual record, so where the asset genuinely matters, design the framework alongside counsel.

4. Data Tier Classification - The Backbone of the Policy

This is the article's central claim. At the center of your policy there should be a tier table, not a prohibition clause. What it does is narrow and precise: it lets an employee look at the data in front of them and decide, this is tier N, therefore it can go here, in about five seconds. Anything slower gets skipped under deadline pressure.

TierDefinition and examplesConsumer-plan chatCorporate API or enterprise workspaceInternal or on-premises model
L1 Public Already public. Product descriptions, public docs, job postings, code in public repositories Allowed Allowed Allowed
L2 Internal Not public, but limited harm if leaked. Routine work email, draft meeting notes, ordinary wiki pages Prohibited as a rule (exception by individual approval once identifiers are removed) Allowed Allowed
L3 Confidential Competitive harm if leaked. Source code, architecture designs, price and cost sheets, unreleased roadmaps, contracts, M&A materials Prohibited Conditionally allowed (data processing agreement, retention policy verified, prior approval, logging) Allowed
L4 Regulated Personal data, sensitive data, unique identifiers, credit information, medical records, third-party data under a customer confidentiality obligation Prohibited Prohibited as a rule. Only with a legal basis, cross-border requirements met, and individual review passed Conditionally allowed (internal access control, logging, minimization assumed)

Several mistakes recur when organizations design a tier table.

  • Do not build all four tiers on day one. The maintenance cost is real. Starting with two - things you may send and things you may not - and splitting them as you operate produces far higher adoption.
  • Tiers attach to data, not documents. One wiki page can hold an L2 paragraph and an L4 paragraph side by side. The judgment applies to the chunk you are about to copy.
  • Aggregation raises the tier. Individually L2 information can become effectively L3 once many records are pasted together, because the combination enables re-identification or reveals a commercial pattern.
  • Outputs carry tiers too. A result produced from L3 input is itself L3. Without this rule people treat summaries as sanitized and paste them anywhere.
  • Third-party data is separate. For customer materials, the restrictions in the contract take precedence over your internal tier.

5. The Decision Flow - May This Data Go In

Even with a tier table the judgment needs an order of operations. The flow below is designed so one employee can walk it at their own desk in about thirty seconds. Put it verbatim in an appendix and link it from the top of your intranet portal, and the rate at which it is actually used changes dramatically.

  1. Is personal data mixed in? If names, contact details, national ID numbers, account numbers, employee IDs, customer identifiers, or facial photographs are present, start at L4.
  2. Can the purpose still be met with that data removed or masked? Where possible, strip or substitute it and reclassify. Most requests are answerable without identifiers at all.
  3. Is it covered by a confidentiality obligation to a third party? For customer or partner materials, read the contract first. Absent explicit permission, stop and ask legal.
  4. What harm would a leak cause? If you can state in one sentence what happens when a competitor reads it, the tier assigns itself.
  5. Does an approved route for that tier actually exist? If not, the right action is not to accept a violation but to file a tooling request. Without that path, people quietly use a personal account.
  6. Has that route's policy been verified recently? Confirm training use, retention, subprocessors, processing region, and certifications, as of your adoption date.
  7. Is a record kept? If you cannot determine afterward who sent what to which tool and when, you cannot even scope the exposure in an incident.
  8. If it is still ambiguous, do not send it. Data that produces split judgments is usually L3 or above. The ambiguity is the signal.

6. Twelve Things Every Internal Policy Needs

Here is a table of contents to work from when drafting the document. Length is not a virtue; a policy that fits in three to five pages and is searchable beats a comprehensive one nobody opens. Push detailed procedures into appendices.

  1. Scope and definitions — who it covers (employees, contractors, agency staff, partners), which devices (company assets, personal devices), and which services
  2. Data tier criteria — definitions with worked examples. A tier table without examples does not get used
  3. Approved-route matrix by tier — put the tier-by-route table in the body of the policy, not an annex
  4. Approved tool list and refresh cadence — an allowlist. A policy with no list offloads the judgment onto individuals
  5. Approval procedure and turnaround time — write down who approves and within how many days an answer arrives, as a number. Approval with no deadline is functionally a ban
  6. Exception approvals and expiry — every exception carries an expiration date. Permanent exceptions hollow out the policy
  7. Logging and monitoring scope, and notice to workers — state in advance what is recorded and who may read it. Monitoring is itself personal data processing, so minimization and purpose limitation apply
  8. Vendor due diligence checklist — training use, retention, whether zero data retention is available and what it excludes, subprocessors, processing region, certifications, audit and notification duties
  9. Cross-border basis and privacy policy alignment — on what basis data is transferred, whether it is disclosed, and if consent is used, whether the notice items are complete
  10. Incident reporting procedure — include how to report after you have already pasted something. Unless prompt self-reporting reduces the penalty, incidents stay hidden
  11. Consequences of violation — graduated and proportionate. Putting a first minor lapse and deliberate exfiltration in one box guarantees nobody reports
  12. Training and review cadence — training at least annually, vendor policies re-verified quarterly, the policy reviewed for amendment twice a year

The two most often omitted are number 5 (turnaround time) and number 10 (incident reporting). If approval takes two weeks, an employee facing a deadline routes around the policy. And if the reporting channel is the disciplinary channel, nobody reports, and the company learns about the incident six months later.

7. Practical Alternatives - Masking, Summarizing, Contracts, Local

A high tier does not mean AI is off the table. There are several ways to reach the objective without sending the data as-is. Each has clear limits, and using one without understanding them produces the worst state of all: carrying the risk while believing you are safe.

TechniqueWhat it doesWhere it fitsLimits and cautions
Masking and tokenization Swaps identifiers for temporary tokens before sending, then restores them in the response Structured identifiers such as names, contact details, and account numbers Individuals can still be re-identified from context in free-form text. Useless where the content itself is the secret, as with source code
Summarize or abstract first Sends a human-written summary or pseudocode instead of the original Design review, ideation, directional consultation No use for debugging or contract review needing exact wording. Summaries can still carry secrets, so the result needs reclassification
Retrieval separation (RAG) Keeps documents in-house and passes only the fragments a query needs Internal document question answering, knowledge search The fragments carry secrets too. Without permission-aware filtering, unauthorized staff read restricted material through search
Enterprise agreement with zero data retention Data processing agreement, minimized retention, region pinning, audit rights Organization-wide adoption, industries handling regulated data Costs money and procurement time. Zero data retention has feature-by-feature scope limits and yields to legal preservation orders
On-premises or local models Runs open-weight models on your own infrastructure Assets that legally cannot leave, air-gapped environments Quality gap, GPU cost, operations headcount. Keeping it inside does not create access control by itself

Sequencing matters as much as selection. For most organizations the best return comes in this order: tier classification, approved tool designation, enterprise contracts, masking automation, and only then local models. A surprising number start from the opposite end. Without tier classification, deploying a local model changes nothing: people still use a consumer chatbot on a personal account.

8. When On-Premises Is the Answer and When It Is Not

When a security discussion runs long, the conclusion often drifts toward let us just run it locally. Sometimes that is the right call. Sometimes it merely relocates the problem.

8.1 When On-Premises Genuinely Is the Answer

  • When statute or contract makes sending the data outside impossible. There is only one option, and performance is a secondary conversation
  • Air-gapped or segmented environments. Places where an external API call is physically impossible
  • High-volume repetitive inference on a consistent pattern. Above a sufficient call volume there is a crossover where unit economics favor self-hosting. But this is to calculate, not to believe
  • When the data is the company's only competitive advantage. The expected loss from exposure exceeds the cost of reduced model quality

8.2 When On-Premises Is Not the Answer

  • The illusion that local means safe. Access control, authentication, audit logging, prompt injection, and output exfiltration all remain. Wiring every internal document into an internal model without permission filters means you have built a new internal leak path
  • Work needing current frontier quality. Open-weight performance has climbed, but task-by-task gaps persist. If quality falls short the tool goes unused and people return to external services
  • Organizations with no operations staff. GPU procurement, model refreshes, availability, and security patching become standing work. This does not survive as one person's side project
  • Strong audit demands with no logging design. External services at least leave billing records and an admin console; a hastily assembled internal deployment often has neither

In short, on-premises is one route among several, not a substitute for a policy. If your tier table sends L4 to the internal model, that model needs the same access control and logging requirements attached to it.

9. A Policy Without Tools Will Fail

This is the point the article most wants to press. The policy publication date and the tooling availability date must be the same day. Get the order wrong and the policy is dead from the moment it ships.

The surveys cited earlier explain why. Many employees already work with AI, and many know it is not permitted and do it anyway. Layer a tool-free prohibition on that and there is one outcome: usage relocates to places the company cannot see. What happens on a personal account leaves the company with no logs, no contract, and no standing to request deletion.

There is a well-known case where this surfaced at scale. In April 2023 it emerged that employees in Samsung Electronics' semiconductor division had entered internal source code and meeting content into ChatGPT, and in May 2023 the company was reported by Bloomberg, Forbes, and others to have restricted generative AI use on company devices and internal networks. Even within what was publicly reported the implication is clear: the incident arose not from malice but from the normal motive of finishing work faster, and was reported to have recurred within a short window after use was permitted. That points less to individual misconduct than to a structural absence of any safe route.

So when you publish the policy, at minimum the following should ship alongside it.

  • At least one approved tool on a company account — without this, every other clause is decorative
  • A one-line answer to where do I ask — the channel, the owner, and the response deadline
  • Worked examples for five common tasks — meeting summaries, code review, translation, document drafts, data cleanup, each showing which route to use
  • Three things not to do — write more and it goes unread. Compress to no personal accounts, no customer data, no unreviewed use of model output
  • A self-reporting channel — where someone who has already pasted something can report it quietly

10. The Limits of This Approach - What Policy Cannot Stop

For balance, here is what this methodology does not solve. Tier classification is not a universal remedy.

  • Personal devices and accounts are hard to block technically. Network blocking and data loss prevention only operate on company assets. Photographing a screen is addressable only through policy and culture
  • Tier classification carries maintenance cost. As the organization grows, judgments diverge and exceptions accumulate. Without periodic cleanup the table drifts out of alignment with reality
  • Vendor policies keep changing. Consumer terms in particular have been revised repeatedly in recent years. Do not assume what you verified today holds in six months
  • Contracts are not absolute either. As shown above, legal preservation obligations and safety-system flagging records can persist independently of any deletion promise
  • A policy will not drive incidents to zero. The goal is to lower the rate and, when one occurs, cap the loss at the tier boundary. Incidents at L1 and L2 should be ones you can absorb
  • Output rights and accuracy are a separate problem. This article covered input control only. Copyright in outputs, factual errors, and bias review each need their own policy

Conclusion: What You Can Do in 90 Days

I regularly see organizations spend six months trying to produce a perfect policy in one pass. Data keeps leaving the building throughout those six months. A realistic sequence looks like this.

  1. Weeks 1 to 2: map the current state. Ask without penalty. Collect what people use and where via an anonymous survey. These answers become the worked examples in your tier table
  2. Weeks 3 to 4: publish an interim two-tier standard. A single page separating what may be sent from what may not. At the same time, open at least one company-account tool
  3. Weeks 5 to 8: vendor due diligence and contract cleanup. Get training use, retention, exclusion scope, and region in writing, and if you handle personal data, check your cross-border basis and whether the privacy policy reflects it
  4. Weeks 9 to 12: finalize the policy. Expand the tier table to four levels, put a turnaround deadline into approvals, notify workers of the logging scope, and open the self-reporting channel
  5. Ongoing thereafter: re-verify vendor policies quarterly, review the policy for amendment semiannually, and train annually.

Reduced to one sentence: the goal of an LLM data policy is not to stop employees from using AI, but to make it unnecessary for them to judge. When the tier of the data in front of someone is written down, when the door that tier goes through is written down, and when that door is actually open, most incidents never begin. A policy is not prohibition but outsourcing the judgment.
