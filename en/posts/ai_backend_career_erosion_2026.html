How AI Erodes Expertise - Where Does a Decade of Backend Experience Go

What Commoditizes, What Becomes Scarce, and Where Seniors Stand

Jun 9, 2026 Tech & IT

Introduction: When a Decade Suddenly Feels Shaky

A software engineer posted in a developer community with a familiar lament: "The expertise I built over ten years working on backend systems feels like it is losing value in front of AI tools." The deep knowledge that was once a weapon in interviews, the know-how colleagues sought when they got stuck - now anyone can approximate it with a single prompt. This anxiety is not one person's overreaction. In 2026, a great many senior developers feel the same tremor.

This article faces that anxiety head-on, but it refuses to slide into pure pessimism. Two questions sit at the center. First, what really gets commoditized? Second, what becomes more valuable instead? Once we honestly separate the two, the vague fear that "a decade of experience is becoming worthless" turns into the far more manageable reality that "the center of gravity of a decade of experience is shifting." The short version: AI does not destroy expertise. It redistributes it. It drives the value of some knowledge toward zero while making other kinds of judgment more expensive than ever.

1. What Actually Gets Commoditized

Let us begin by honestly conceding the part that hurts. AI does weaken certain expertise, and the targets are not vague but quite specific.

  • Repetitive implementation: CRUD handlers, REST endpoints, boilerplate, config files, data-mapping code. Writing these "fast and clean" used to prove skill, but AI pours out hundreds of such lines per minute.
  • Memorized knowledge: API signatures of a particular framework, library usage, frequently used regular expressions, the subtle differences in SQL dialects. The volume you held in your head used to be a competitive edge; now it is summoned instantly, without even a search.
  • Implementing standard algorithms and data structures: Sorting, tree traversal, graph search - textbook implementations. Independent of understanding the concept, the market value of the ability to "type it out correctly by hand" is dropping fast.
  • Well-documented domain knowledge: This is the most painful point. Even domain knowledge that once separated seniors from juniors - PCI compliance, payment protocols - can be synthesized by an LLM trained on that documentation, as long as the domain is well documented.

Recent analyses sum up the trend in one line: "Domain knowledge, debugging, and architectural judgment are being commoditized faster than most engineers expected." The last item in particular - documented domain knowledge - was the moat seniors believed was safest, which is why its erosion lands so hard.

The gap in routine knowledge that once separated seniors from juniors closes the moment AI can synthesize that knowledge from its training data.

2. But Some Things Do Not Commoditize

Here is where balance matters. Look closely at the list above and one thing stands out: every item is an area where the answer exists in documentation, the patterns repeat, and verification is relatively easy. Put differently, the opposite side - where the answer is not spelled out, where it changes with context, and where verification itself demands expertise - sits outside the range of commoditization.

What lives on that other side? The research and industry reporting of 2026 consistently point to a cluster of skills.

  • System design and architectural judgment: A deep understanding of existing and alternative architectures, weighing constraints, and carefully comparing the costs and benefits of multiple design solutions. The more deeply AI coding tools integrate, the more high-level system design matters relative to low-level code writing.
  • Debugging and verification: The ability to detect when AI-generated code is wrong and to understand why it fails. Developers must curate and select among the outputs AI provides, and build instincts for when it succeeds and when it does not.
  • Critical judgment and genuine expertise: Genuine expertise becomes more valuable as LLMs spread, because someone has to verify the output - and that requires knowing the domain. Even the most reliable systems cannot fully replace the human judgment needed to handle uncertainty and make complex decisions.

The crux is an asymmetry. AI is strong at generation but needs a human for verification. And verification is a different muscle from generation. As the ability to write fast becomes common, the relative value of the ability to doubt, verify, and take responsibility rises. This dovetails exactly with how the "economics of code" moves from writing to understanding (related analysis).

3. The Test That Separates the Two Kinds of Expertise

So how do you tell which of your skills are being eroded and which are being strengthened? Here is a practical diagnostic.

Dimension Expertise that commoditizes Expertise that grows scarcer
Form of knowledge Spelled out in docs (APIs, syntax, standards) Buried in context (why, when, at what cost)
Existence of an answer Converges to one right answer Trade-offs; depends on the situation
Difficulty of verification Easy (just run it) Hard (verification itself needs expertise)
Representative examples Writing CRUD, memorizing syntax, standard impl Debugging incidents, architecture decisions, domain intuition

The practical implication is clear. The larger the share of your day spent on work that is "documented, single-answer, easy to verify," the faster that work moves to AI. Conversely, the larger the share of work that is "buried in context, trade-off-driven, and requires expertise even to verify," the safer - and more valuable - your seat becomes. The first step of any career strategy is to deliberately shift your time toward the latter.

4. What the Hiring Market Is Signaling

This shift is not just an abstract worry; it is stamped clearly into hiring data. Cross-referencing the 2026 indicators reveals a consistent pattern.

  • 62% of developers use AI tools and see roughly 35% higher productivity, while junior hiring at Big Tech fell about 25%.
  • New grads make up just 7% of Big Tech hires in 2026, down from 32% in 2019. The focus has shifted toward senior developers who validate AI output.
  • Entry-level postings are down about 28% from their 2022 peak, and the market shows a structural shift toward "fewer but higher-skilled" roles.
  • This is not the elimination of job titles but a quiet raising of the experience requirements for roles once open to new graduates.

A paradox emerges here. For seniors, AI is less an eliminator and more an amplifier. Seniors now hand to AI the tasks they would once have delegated to an intern or a new grad. Juniors, by contrast, often find AI tools more stressful, because they lack the experience to tell the AI exactly what to do or to quickly confirm whether its output is correct. Paradoxically, the people who use AI best are the ones who could do the work without it.

5. Repositioning the Senior - Not a Manager but a Verifier and Designer

So how should a ten-year senior reposition? A common mistake is to treat "escaping into management" as the answer. But where the 2026 field points is a little different.

According to industry surveys, 65% of developers expect their role to be redefined in 2026 - moving from routine coding toward architecture, integration, and AI-enabled decision-making. And two groups are in clearest demand: senior engineers with strong architecture sense, product sense, and domain expertise, and so-called "agentic engineers" who treat AI as a team of juniors they manage, review, and direct.

That second phrase cuts to the heart of it. The very capability a senior used to apply to a team of interns - deciding what to assign, reviewing the result, steering direction - now points at AI agents. The senior's value repositions from "the hands that write" to "the head that knows what to have written, and how to verify and integrate the result." This connects to the argument that domain expertise is the real moat (related analysis).

6. Scar Tissue Cannot Be Downloaded

On what a senior's real asset is, one engineering leader offered a striking line: "The scar tissue you have accumulated, the instinct for where the edge cases hide, the ability to debug systems you have never seen before - these take years to develop and cannot be downloaded."

This matters because it pinpoints the boundary between what AI can learn and what it cannot. AI synthesizes documented knowledge. But the instinct earned while chasing a 3 a.m. outage, the pattern realized after days hunting a bug that reproduces only under one specific combination, the gut sense that "this design will absolutely break in six months" - none of these are documented anywhere. So they are not in the training data, and they cannot be synthesized.

A paradox follows. The more a senior invests in "AI-first" skills, the fewer chances they get to accumulate new scar tissue. When AI does the hard work, the intuition that comes only from hard work disappears with it. So the core of repositioning is not merely "using AI well" but "deliberately preserving chances to earn scar tissue even while using AI."

7. Practical Advice for Career Anxiety

Now the most practical part. How do you convert vague anxiety into concrete action? Synthesizing advice from many senior engineers and the industry, three practices recur.

  1. Seek scar tissue deliberately. Volunteer for on-call rotations. Take the messy migration project nobody else wants. When something breaks, be the one who stays to understand why. Only this kind of experience builds the undownloadable intuition.
  2. Do not outsource your understanding. AI tools are incredible accelerators, but they can also become a crutch. When the model gives you an answer, take the time to understand why it works. The goal is not to reject AI; it is to use it without losing the ability to think for yourself.
  3. Mentor actively. Find a junior and invest in their growth, even if nobody asks you to. This is more fun than it used to be: you explore AI tooling together, learning alongside each other rather than just handing down wisdom.

The common principle behind all three: deliberately avoid the easy path AI offers, and consciously accumulate the assets that difficulty leaves behind. Follow only the comfortable path and short-term productivity rises, but the assets that make you irreplaceable do not.

8. The Shift in Learning Strategy - Fundamentals Matter More

Behind career anxiety sits learning anxiety: "What do I learn now? Do I switch frameworks again?" The 2026 advice is surprisingly classical: before chasing new tools, strengthen the fundamentals.

AI tools help you code, but using them well still requires strong fundamentals: data structures and algorithms for problem-solving; operating systems, databases, and networking for system-level understanding; and a deep grasp of core languages. Paradoxically, those who thrive in the AI era are the ones who blend strong fundamentals with system-level understanding, cross-disciplinary intuition, and ethical foresight.

Why do fundamentals matter more? Because verifying AI-generated code requires understanding what it does from first principles. AI fills in the surface syntax of a framework, but "why is this query slow," "why does this concurrency bug appear," "where does this memory leak come from" can be pinned down only by someone whose fundamentals are solid. Surface knowledge commoditizes; first principles remain the foundation of verification.

9. The Junior's Dilemma and the Learning Curve

The hardest position in this shift belongs to juniors. Verification and judgment come from experience, yet the "bottom rung" where that experience accrues is shrinking. Traditionally, juniors grew by writing code, getting stuck, and debugging their way out. When AI does that struggle for them, the very fuel of growth diminishes.

Indeed, one study reported that learners with AI assistance finished short tasks faster but scored lower on later comprehension and debugging. They "finished fast but learned less." This is not an individual failing but a hint of a structural risk that the learning curve itself flattens. If organizations use AI only as "a tool for juniors to skip the struggle," they will not grow the seniors who can verify five years from now.

So advice for juniors runs in the same direction as for seniors but at higher intensity. Do not stop at copying the code AI gives you; take it apart and understand why it works. Use AI as a tutor, not an answer key. And build deliberate practice into your routine by seeking out hard problems - ambiguous design questions AI cannot answer instantly, bugs that are tricky to reproduce. This connects to the learning path toward senior (related analysis).

10. A Balanced Outlook - Redistribution, Not Erosion

Step back and the picture clarifies. The claim that "AI erodes expertise" is only half right. More precisely, AI redistributes expertise. It drives the value of one side - repetitive implementation, memorized knowledge, documented domains - toward zero, while raising the value of the other - design, judgment, debugging, verification.

Winners and losers diverge in this redistribution. Those whose expertise was bound mostly to the former - who built their identity on "writing fast and clean" - feel the value drop. Those whose center of gravity sat in the latter - who treasured "making hard decisions, fixing broken things, knowing the domain deeply" - find their scarcity rising.

The real value of a decade depends on where you spent that decade. Ten years of repeating similar CRUD is vulnerable to commoditization. But ten years of chasing varied outages, living through the costs of multiple architectures, and absorbing the traps of a domain is an asset that cannot be downloaded. The same "ten years" splits in fate according to the density inside it.

11. What You Can Change Tomorrow

Start not with a grand career change but with small habit corrections.

  • Reallocate your work mix: Consciously move your time from "documented, single-answer work" to "judgment-and-verification work." Delegate the former to AI and reinvest the saved time in the latter.
  • Strengthen the verification routine: Before merging AI-generated code, ask "Can I explain this to a colleague?" If not, read until you understand or make it simpler.
  • Capitalize on scar tissue: After an incident, leave one line in the postmortem about the intuition you newly gained. It is a habit of consciously accumulating undownloadable assets.
  • Dig deep into a domain: Invest in deep mastery of one domain over broad, shallow framework knowledge. The depth you can verify is your irreplaceability.

12. Conclusion + 3 Things to Remember

It is true that AI shakes a decade of experience. But see the nature of that shaking clearly and fear turns into strategy. AI does not destroy expertise; it redistributes it, and who benefits from that redistribution depends largely on individual choice.

To close, three things to remember.

  1. Separate what commoditizes from what grows scarce. Documented, single-answer knowledge loses value; context, judgment, and verification gain it. Moving your time toward the latter is the heart of strategy.
  2. Scar tissue cannot be downloaded. On-call, messy migrations, outages chased to the end - only the intuition that difficulty leaves behind is an asset AI cannot synthesize. Deliberately avoid the easy path.
  3. Do not outsource your understanding. Use AI but keep the ability to think for yourself. Fundamentals matter more now, and verification is the real moat of the next era.
