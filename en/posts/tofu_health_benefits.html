Tofu, known as dubu in Korean, is a food made from soybeans that has served as a major protein source in Asia for thousands of years. This high-protein, low-fat food plays an important role in modern healthy diets and is a versatile food nutritionally. In this article, we explore the key health benefits of tofu and how to incorporate it into your daily diet.

What Is Tofu (Dubu)?

Tofu is made by coagulating soy milk and pressing the resulting curds into solid blocks. The process is similar to cheesemaking, and traditionally uses coagulants such as gansu (nigari / magnesium chloride) or gypsum (calcium sulfate). It comes in a range of textures from silky soft to extra firm, and its mild flavor makes it incredibly versatile in cooking.

History and Cultural Background

Tofu is believed to have been first created in China about 2,000 years ago and subsequently spread throughout East Asia, including Korea and Japan. It became an essential protein source in Buddhist cultures that discouraged eating meat, and in modern times it has become a popular food worldwide. In recent years, tofu has gained particular popularity among vegans and vegetarians as an indispensable plant-based protein.

Nutritional Profile of Tofu

Tofu contains a variety of essential nutrients:

Nutrient Amount (per 100g) Function
Protein Approx. 8-10g Muscle formation, cell regeneration
Fat Approx. 4-5g (mostly unsaturated) Energy supply, hormone production
Calcium Approx. 64mg (nigari-set tofu sold in Korea; calcium-sulfate-set tofu is far higher) Bone and teeth strengthening
Magnesium Approx. 80mg Muscle function, nerve signaling
Calories Approx. 97 kcal Energy supply

These figures are based on 100g of tofu sold in Korea as listed in the Rural Development Administration's National Standard Food Composition Table (via the Food Safety Korea nutrient database), which gives 97 kcal, 9.62 g protein, 4.63 g fat, 64 mg calcium and 80 mg magnesium. Calcium in particular varies enormously with the coagulant used, so it is worth checking the label on the product you buy. The iron and isoflavone rows in the earlier version were removed because their source could not be confirmed. Tofu is a complete protein containing all essential amino acids, and it is cholesterol-free with very low saturated fat content.

8 Key Health Benefits of Tofu

Nothing below means that eating tofu cures or prevents disease. The items describe tofu's nutritional profile and what studies of soy foods and isoflavones have found. Most of that research is observational or used supplements, so the size of any effect cannot be stated with confidence.

1. Excellent Source of Plant-Based Protein

Tofu is a plant-based protein generally described as complete, meaning it contains all the essential amino acids. With approximately 8-10g of protein per 100g, it supplies the amino acids needed for muscle maintenance, cell regeneration, and enzyme and hormone production.

For vegetarians and vegans who limit meat, tofu is a practical way to fill protein needs. Unlike animal foods it contains no cholesterol and is low in saturated fat, which makes it useful for anyone rearranging the protein in their diet.

2. Cardiovascular Health and Cholesterol

NCCIH, part of the US National Institutes of Health, states that "consuming soy protein in place of other proteins may lower cholesterol levels to a small extent" (checked 2026-09-29). What you can expect from replacing other proteins with tofu is therefore a small change, not proof that tofu prevents or treats heart disease. Isoflavone supplements should not be expected to do the same.

The widely quoted "25g a day" is not an FDA intake recommendation. It comes from 21 CFR 101.82, the labeling threshold a food must meet to carry the health claim that soy protein may reduce the risk of heart disease. In October 2017 the FDA also proposed a rule to revoke this claim, and at the time of the earlier check the process was still pending (not re-verified this time). For reference, 150g of tofu provides about 14g of protein.

3. Bone Health and Calcium

The calcium content of tofu varies enormously with the coagulant used. Tofu in Korea is mostly set with nigari (magnesium chloride), so it is not especially calcium-rich: the National Standard Food Composition Table lists about 64mg of calcium per 100g. That falls well short of a glass of milk (about 300mg), so tofu alone will not match milk for calcium.

Tofu set with calcium sulfate (gypsum) is a different story. The USDA FoodData Central database lists 683mg of calcium per 100g for firm tofu prepared with calcium sulfate (earlier check, not re-verified this time). If you are eating tofu as a calcium source, checking the coagulant or the nutrition panel on the package is the reliable approach.

NCCIH says soy isoflavones "probably have a beneficial effect on bone density in postmenopausal women" (checked 2026-09-29), but that does not mean they prevent fractures or osteoporosis. If osteoporosis is a concern, decide on testing and treatment with your clinician.

4. Weight Management and Fullness

At about 97 kcal per 100g tofu is relatively light, and its protein content makes it useful for filling out a meal. Protein also takes more energy to digest. That does not mean eating tofu makes you lose weight, and frying it or using plenty of oil and sauce raises the calories considerably.

5. Menopausal Symptoms

The isoflavones in tofu are plant compounds structurally similar to the hormone estrogen. NCCIH says "soy isoflavone supplements or soy protein may help to reduce the frequency and severity of menopausal hot flashes, but the effect may be small" (checked 2026-09-29). If symptoms interfere with daily life, get medical care rather than relying on food.

6. Isoflavones and Antioxidant Compounds

Tofu contains isoflavones such as genistein and daidzein. Antioxidant properties have been reported in laboratory studies, but this article did not find evidence that eating tofu slows aging.

The trials reporting improved skin elasticity or fewer wrinkles were mostly small studies using concentrated isoflavone supplements or powders rather than foods like tofu, so results from supplements should not be read across to food.

7. Association with Cancer Risk

NCCIH notes that higher soy intake is associated with a lower risk of breast cancer in Asian women, while supplement forms have not shown this benefit (checked 2026-09-29). The American Cancer Society (ACS) likewise says soy intake is associated with either decreased cancer risk or no change in risk. These are observed associations, not proof that tofu prevents cancer.

8. Blood Sugar and Diabetes

NCCIH says people who eat diets high in soy may have a lower risk of type 2 diabetes (checked 2026-09-29). This too is an association with a dietary pattern, not a sign that tofu treats blood sugar. If you take diabetes medication, discuss dietary changes with your prescriber and do not reduce or stop the medication on your own.

Considerations When Eating Tofu

Tofu is an everyday food for most people, but if any of the following applies, it is safer to talk to a professional first:

  1. Soy allergy: People with a soy allergy should avoid tofu. Soybeans are one of the major allergens the US FDA requires to be declared on labels.
  2. Thyroid disease or thyroid medication: In adults with adequate iodine intake there is little evidence that soy foods harm thyroid function, but caution is warranted if iodine intake is low. Soy can also reduce absorption of thyroid hormone medication (levothyroxine), so if you take it, the usual advice is to separate soy foods from your dose (the gap varies by source, and some suggest 3 to 4 hours). The primary source (NIH ODS) could not be opened this time and this was checked through secondary material, so settle the exact interval with your prescriber or pharmacist.
  3. Kidney disease: The National Kidney Foundation (NKF) describes tofu as "a plant-based protein source that is low in phosphorus, potassium, and sodium" and presents it as suitable for people with chronic kidney disease, dialysis or a transplant (checked 2026-09-29). How much you may eat still depends on your stage and blood test results, so decide with a kidney dietitian or doctor. Salty seasoned tofu and processed soy products can be high in sodium and other minerals, so read the nutrition label.
  4. Breast cancer history and isoflavone supplements: The ACS says a large body of evidence supports the safety of soy foods (checked 2026-09-29), but it does not recommend concentrated isoflavone supplements for cancer prevention because they contain far more isoflavones than foods. If you have a history of breast cancer, talk to your doctor about both soy foods and supplements.
  5. MAO-inhibitor antidepressants: NCCIH reports interactions between soy products high in tyramine (such as tofu and soy sauce) and monoamine oxidase inhibitors (checked 2026-09-29). If you take such a drug, talk to your prescriber or pharmacist.

Types of Tofu and How to Choose

There are several types of tofu, each suited for different culinary purposes:

  1. Soft tofu (Sundubu): Has the highest water content and a silky texture. Ideal for smoothies, desserts, and sauces.
  2. Silken tofu: Slightly firmer than soft tofu. Great for salad dressings, dipping sauces, and soups.
  3. Medium-firm tofu (regular dubu): The most common type. Versatile enough for stir-fries, braised dishes, and stews.
  4. Extra-firm tofu (gyeongdubu): Has the lowest water content and holds its shape well. Perfect for grilling, stir-frying, and baking.
  5. Fried tofu (Yubu): Deep-fried tofu that readily absorbs broth. Used in braised dishes and soups.

How to choose good tofu:

  • Fresh tofu should be a clean white color with no off-putting smell.
  • Avoid tofu with puffy packaging or surface discoloration.
  • When possible, opt for organic, non-GMO tofu.

How to Cook with Tofu

Tofu has a mild, neutral flavor on its own, but it becomes delicious with various cooking methods and seasonings:

Basic Tofu Recipes

  1. Pan-fried tofu (Dubu Bokkeum): Cut tofu into cubes, season with salt and pepper, and pan-fry in olive oil until crispy. Adding soy sauce, garlic, and ginger enhances the flavor.
  2. Tofu scramble: Crumble tofu and stir-fry with onions, bell peppers, and tomatoes for an egg scramble alternative. Adding turmeric gives it an egg-like yellow color.
  3. Tofu salad: Dice firm tofu and toss with fresh vegetables and nuts. Sesame dressing or balsamic vinaigrette pair well.
  4. Tofu steak: Slice tofu into thick pieces, marinate, and cook on a pan or grill.
  5. Tofu smoothie: Blend soft tofu with fruit and milk or yogurt for a protein-rich smoothie.

Tofu Dishes from Around the World

  1. Korea: Dubu Kimchi (tofu with stir-fried kimchi), Sundubu Jjigae (soft tofu stew), Dubu Jorim (braised tofu)
  2. China: Mapo Tofu (spicy tofu), Hongshao Doufu (red-braised tofu)
  3. Japan: Agedashi Dofu (deep-fried tofu in dashi broth), Miso Shiru (miso soup with tofu)
  4. Thailand: Tofu Pad Thai, Tofu Curry
  5. Western-style: Tofu burgers, Tofu lasagna, Tofu pizza

How to Store Tofu

Once opened, tofu should be stored in water in the refrigerator, and the water should be changed daily. It is generally best to consume it within 3-5 days. You can also freeze tofu; frozen tofu develops a sponge-like texture after thawing, which allows it to absorb marinades and sauces more effectively.

Conclusion

Tofu is a low-calorie source of protein that can also supply calcium depending on the coagulant. What is established is that eating soy protein in place of other proteins may lower cholesterol to a small extent; links to menopausal symptoms, bones, cancer and diabetes are described in the research as small or as associations. Tofu should not be expected to treat or prevent disease.

Use the types and cooking methods above to make it one easy option among your side dishes and protein choices.
