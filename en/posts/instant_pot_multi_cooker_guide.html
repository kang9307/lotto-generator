Twelve Buttons, Five Different Kinds of Heat

Why pressure is fast, why the number on the display is not the time you wait, and the one thing you must never do with this appliance

August 31, 2026 Life & Practical

What multi-function actually means

There are twelve buttons on the lid and you press two or three of them. That is how it goes in most kitchens. The rest get tried in the first week, produce an ambiguous result, and are never touched again. This is less a failing of the appliance than a consequence of learning only after purchase what multi-function actually means.

It does not mean one pot does twelve things well. It means several different ways of applying heat live inside one pot. Pressure, slow cooking, sauteing, steaming, and low-temperature fermentation move heat into food along completely different paths. Some require a sealed vessel; others require an open one. Some heat the base directly; others carry heat through water. A single appliance has to produce these opposite conditions in turn. That is why some modes rival a dedicated appliance and others are closer to an impression of one.

This article works through the inside of that. Why pressure saves time, what actually determines each mode's performance, why the number on the screen and the time that passes in the kitchen are different, why the way you release pressure changes the food, and finally the one thing you must not do with this appliance. It does not compare specific products or give recipes.

This article explains general principles. Operation, settings, part maintenance, and safety procedures vary by model, and you should always follow the manufacturer manual for the product you own.

Why pressure cooking is fast - the boiling point rises

In an open pot, as long as there is water, the boiling point is the ceiling on temperature. Doubling the flame does not double the temperature. The surplus energy goes into turning water into steam instead of raising the reading. That is why a hard boil and a gentle simmer end up at much the same temperature.

Sealing the pot breaks that balance. Steam cannot escape, so the pressure inside rises, and a higher pressure raises the temperature at which water boils. The water still boils, but it boils hotter. Most of the changes that matter in cooking are strongly temperature-dependent, so a difference of a few tens of degrees shows up as a difference of several times in the clock.

For a sense of scale, the canning specification is useful. University extension material states that low-acid foods must be processed at roughly 116 to 121C, which requires a pressure canner operating at 10 to 15 psi gauge. That is far above the 100C of boiling water. But this is a canning specification, and the pressure and temperature a household electric multi-cooker actually reaches vary by model. Hold on to that distinction; it becomes the core of the safety section later.

There has to be liquid for pressure to exist

Pressure is steam. With no liquid in the pot there is no steam, and with no steam there is no pressure. That is why pressure modes come with a minimum liquid requirement. Extension guidance lists insufficient liquid as the first of the common pressure cooker mistakes, noting that the amount needed depends on the size of the cooker.

Too little liquid produces one of two outcomes. Either the cooker never reaches pressure and keeps heating until it gives up, or the base scorches and a burn sensor trips. A large share of beginner failures live here: liquid was cut back on the assumption that the ingredients would release their own, and the base burned before that happened.

Altitude changes the result

Where the air pressure is lower, water boils at a lower temperature. The same setting therefore lands at a different temperature. This is why results at high elevation differ from results at sea level, and it is also why altitude shows up as a decisive variable in the canning research discussed further down.

All of this defines what pressure is good at. The environment is hot and moist, so the gains are largest with connective-tissue-heavy cuts, dried beans, and whole grains - anything that would otherwise take a long time. By the same token the surface never dries out, so browning and crispness simply do not happen. That is not a shortfall in performance; it is a consequence of the physics.

The modes run on different physics - being good at one says nothing about the rest

Buttons sitting side by side look like peers. In fact the path heat takes differs, and so do the parts that determine performance.

PressureSealed steam, above the boiling point of waterClosed and lockedTough cuts, dried beans, whole grainsNo surface browning

Read the table down one column and something stands out. Only pressure requires a seal; everything else runs with the lid open or loosely covered. One appliance has to alternate between opposite conditions.

Performance therefore does not travel between modes. Pressure performance is set by the seal and the pressure control, saute performance by the wattage and diameter of the base element, and fermentation performance by how accurately a low temperature is held. Different parts are responsible, so being good at one gives no reason to be good at another. A cooker that is excellent under pressure and disappointing at searing is common, and that is a design center of gravity rather than a defect.

The practical buying criterion falls out of this. Pick the one mode you will genuinely use, then look at the part that mode depends on. Daily stews and steamed dishes point at the seal and pressure control; dishes that start with browning point at base diameter and wattage; yogurt or dough proofing points at low-temperature accuracy.

Choosing a capacity - why there are two fill lines

The inner pot usually has lines marked on it, often more than one kind, and each exists for a different reason.

  • Maximum fill line: under pressure there has to be room above the food for steam to gather. Fill past it and that space disappears, pressure does not build properly, and boiling contents get pushed up toward the vent path.
  • Lower line for foaming foods: beans, grains, porridge, and pasta expand and foam as they cook, and many pots mark a separate, lower limit for them. That line is not about taste. It is about a blocked vent.
  • Minimum liquid: exactly as in the previous section. With no water to become steam, no pressure appears.

The trap in the serves-four label

  • The stated capacity is the total volume of the vessel. The practical ceiling is the maximum fill line, and under pressure it is lower still. The gap between label and reality is wider here than with most kitchen appliances.
  • Different dishes occupy different volumes. For the same number of people, a soup takes far more room than rice or a steamed dish, and the label rarely says which dish it assumed.
  • A small pot has a narrow base. During the browning step ingredients overlap, and what you get is released moisture instead of color. Choosing on volume alone leaves you short at exactly that step.
  • A large pot costs time. There is more mass to heat, so both the pressure-up and the pressure-down phases stretch out. On top of that the minimum liquid still applies, so cooking a small amount in a large pot tends to leave the dish soupier than intended.

Working backward is easier. Measure the real volume of the dishes you cook most often and pick a size that holds them comfortably below the maximum fill line. If pressure work dominates, look at volume; if browning is part of most dishes, look at base diameter first. The same approach to capacity math appears in the guides to choosing a rice cooker and choosing an air fryer.

The displayed time is not the real time

The recipe says fifteen minutes, the display says fifteen minutes, and forty minutes have gone by in the kitchen. The appliance is not lying. The number it shows does not cover the whole process.

PhaseWhat happensWhat sets its lengthCounted on the display
Pressure up (preheat)Contents heat and steam fills the pot until the set pressure is reachedAmount and starting temperature of the food, liquid volume, pot sizeNo
HoldThe set pressure is maintained while the food cooksThe value you enteredYes
Pressure downPressure falls until the lid lock releasesRelease method, amount and nature of the contentsNo

So total time equals pressure up plus displayed time plus pressure down, and two of the three sit outside the display. Ingredients straight from the fridge or a nearly full pot stretch the first phase; a lot of liquid stretches the last. The same recipe therefore takes a different amount of time on different days.

One important conclusion follows. Pressure cooking cannot make an already short dish shorter. The two invisible phases behave like a fixed overhead. Run vegetables that cook in minutes under pressure and the total goes up, not down. The gain appears in what was slow to begin with - dried beans, tough cuts, the things that used to take an hour or two in a pot.

Delay-start and keep-warm functions hide this structure. The food is ready and hot, so it feels as though it took no time, when in fact you simply were not standing there. Cooking you can walk away from is the real advantage of this appliance; absolute time savings appear in some dishes and not in others.

There is a clear reason not to nail down the two invisible phases with numbers here. They shift with model, capacity, contents, starting temperature, and altitude. The most accurate way to calibrate your own appliance is to time two or three dishes you cook regularly, and settings and procedures should follow the manufacturer manual.

Safety - how you release pressure changes the result

While pressure remains, the lid does not open; a lock physically prevents it. So every pressure cook ends with a release step, and there are two roads through it.

  • Natural release: heating stops and pressure falls on its own. The inside stays hot and cooking continues throughout.
  • Quick release: the valve is opened and steam is forced out. Pressure and temperature drop together, so cooking effectively stops at that moment.

That is why an identical hold time can produce different food. Extension guidance recommends a quick release for delicate items such as vegetables, eggs, and fish, and a natural release for meats, beans, rice, and stews where the residual heat helps. When a recipe specifies a release method, it is not a matter of preference; the release is part of the cooking time.

Foods that foam are a different story

With foaming foods such as beans, grains, porridge, and pasta, the release method crosses over into safety. Dropping the pressure fast makes the contents surge, and foam and liquid can spray out through the vent or block it. A blocked vent means pressure does not leave on the schedule you expected. This is why a natural release is the default for these foods, and why the lower fill line from the previous section exists as the same protection approached from another direction.

What leaves the vent is steam above boiling

The Korea Consumer Agency has issued a consumer safety alert noting that reported pressure cooker incidents rose year after year. Among reported burn cases the share involving infants and toddlers was overwhelming, and within that group electric models far outnumbered stovetop ones. The reason is a habit rather than a defect: electric units are often placed on the floor or a low table, which puts them at the height of a small child's hands and face. The same alert advises keeping the cooker out of children's reach, not using it on the floor, not opening the lid during cooking, and cleaning the pressure vent and checking the gasket regularly.

The variable you can actually control is where the appliance sits. Keep the direction of the escaping steam clear of walkways, face height, and the cabinet above. Pushing the unit tight against a wall sends steam into the wall covering and the upper cabinet.

The procedure belongs to the manual. Which way the valve turns, whether it may be moved by hand, and whether a tool is required all differ by model, and grabbing the wrong part sends steam toward your hand. This article explains the difference between release methods; it does not describe how to operate them. Always follow the manual for your product when releasing pressure and opening the lid.

What you must not do - no low-acid canning in this appliance

Do not use a household electric multi-cooker to can low-acid foods. The USDA does not recommend the practice, and under-processed low-acid food carries a risk of botulism. This is not a question of taste or efficiency; it is a question of survival.

Low-acid foods are those acidic enough for Clostridium botulinum to grow in. University extension material includes vegetables, beans, meat, poultry, fish, wild game, and soups in the category. Translated to an everyday table, that covers cooked greens, boiled beans, stock, braises, and most soups.

Why it is dangerous

Seal low-acid food that was not sufficiently processed and a toxin with no smell and no taste can form. The defining feature of this hazard is that there is essentially no way to notice the problem before eating. Korea's national health information portal describes botulism as a disease causing nerve paralysis, warns that preserved food not thoroughly cooked before storage can cause it, and states that food in a container that has swelled must not be eaten. The same source notes that the organism can produce toxin even at refrigerator temperatures, so refrigeration alone is not a guarantee of safety.

Why boiling water is not enough

The problem is not the bacteria but the spores. Extension material states that botulinum spores are very hard to destroy at boiling-water temperatures and that the higher the temperature, the more easily they are destroyed. That is why low-acid foods are specified for processing at roughly 116 to 121C, at 10 to 15 psi gauge. The relationship between pressure and boiling point from the earlier section turns into a safety standard here.

Why a multi-cooker cannot do it

  • It has never been tested. University of New Hampshire Extension states plainly that these appliances have not been tested for safety or recipe development by the USDA or by universities for pressure canning low-acid food.
  • Results are not consistent. Utah State University research reports that under the same conditions the food was sometimes adequately processed and sometimes not.
  • It does not stay hot enough for long enough. The same research found that in many cases the food did not reach a high enough temperature for a long enough time to destroy botulism spores.
  • Altitude makes it worse, and there are structural reasons. Under-processing was more frequent at higher elevations, and the researchers concluded that electric programmable pressure cookers of any brand are inconsistent at altitude and should not be used for low-acid canning. Their small size means they heat and cool quickly, and most have no venting function, so pockets of cooler temperature can remain inside.
  • A button label is not a test result. A control marked pressure canning changes none of the above.

The USDA and UNH Extension recommend that only traditional stovetop pressure canners be used for canning low-acid foods. This article does not teach canning. For anyone who intends to do it, the only safe path is a dedicated canner plus a tested recipe from an approved source such as the USDA, the National Center for Home Food Preservation, or a university publication.

To put it plainly, what this appliance does safely is cook the food you are going to eat and make food you will keep refrigerated or frozen. Shelf-stable sealed storage is a different domain and a different appliance's job.

The seal, and what to check when pressure will not build

Pressure is made by the seal, not by the pot. The sealing ring between lid and pot does that work, and it is a consumable part. Silicone stiffens and stretches through repeated heating and cooling. Once it has stretched, cracked, or taken a permanent impression, steam leaks through the gap.

The order to check in

  • Is the release valve in the sealing position? Extension guidance says that when the cooker takes a very long time to reach pressure, the first thing to check is whether the valve is closed or turned to sealing. It is the most common cause and the most anticlimactic one.
  • Is there enough liquid? Without enough water to become steam, pressure does not appear at all.
  • Is the sealing ring seated properly and clean? It often slips out or goes back in twisted after washing, and a single grain of rice caught between ring and groove leaks just as effectively.
  • Has the ring stretched or hardened? If it no longer springs back to its original thickness or the surface has cracked, it is time to replace it.
  • Is the lid fully locked, and are the vent path and float valve underneath it clear? An incomplete lock stops the cooker from heating or halts it partway, and a blocked path is both a pressure problem and a safety problem.

Do not disassemble or modify

The moment a safety device is removed, substituted, or a weight is swapped, the part that defined the pressure ceiling is gone. What makes this especially bad is that nothing changes during normal operation; the entire safety margin simply vanishes in the abnormal case. Use genuine parts for the model, and follow the manual for replacement intervals and for how far the unit may be taken apart.

Judge replacement by condition rather than by months elapsed. It no longer returns to its original thickness, the surface has cracked, an impression stays pressed into it, washing does not remove the smell, pressure now takes noticeably longer to reach. Two or more of those and it is time. Keeping a spare ring also solves the odor problem in the next section.

Odor and cleaning - silicone absorbs smells

Silicone takes on aromas. Cook something strongly flavored and the ring holds the smell, and washing does not fully remove it, so it carries into the next dish. It shows up most clearly when you make something mild, such as yogurt or plain rice. Nothing is broken; it is a property of the material.

  • Sealing ring: remove after use, wash, and dry completely. Keeping separate rings for strongly flavored and mild dishes all but eliminates the transfer.
  • Underside of the lid, float valve, and vent path: food splashes onto these during cooking, and a blockage here turns straight into a safety issue. Make a habit of looking.
  • Condensation cup: standing water left in it is where smell and mold begin. Empty it often.
  • Heating plate and inner pot: the plate sits over electrical parts, so wipe it rather than pouring water in. For the pot, avoid metal tools and abrasive pads on coated versions, and expect staining on stainless ones.
  • Storage: closing the lid before everything is dry traps the smell inside. Leave it open until dry, or take the ring out.

How far the unit may be taken apart and whether parts are dishwasher safe differ by model. This is not a place to generalize; check the manual. Disassembling the wrong way damages the sealing surfaces and lands you back in the previous section.

What it cannot do - stated honestly

What makes this appliance disappointing is not its performance but the direction of the expectation. Knowing in advance what it cannot do sends that expectation somewhere useful.

Does everything means it can do many things, not that it does each as well as a dedicated appliance. Buy with that distinction in mind and there is nothing to be disappointed by; buy without it and you end up using two buttons out of twelve. So who is it for

Conditions instead of a recommendation. Count how many lines below describe your kitchen and the answer appears.

Conditions where it earns its place

  • Slow dishes are the center of your diet - stews, braises, steamed dishes, dried beans, whole grains.
  • Your schedule makes standing at the stove difficult. Cooking you can walk away from is a bigger gain here than raw speed.
  • You cook several meals at once and portion them out.
  • You are short of burners, reluctant to run a gas flame through the summer, or need one appliance to cover several jobs to save counter space.

Conditions where it does not fit

  • Roasting and frying are the center of your diet. That is exactly what this appliance structurally cannot do.
  • You cook single portions on demand. The fixed overhead eats the entire benefit.
  • You already own a pressure cooker, a slow cooker, and a steamer. The overlap is large.
  • Taking parts out to wash and dry every time feels like a burden. Skipping it leads straight back to the odor and sealing problems.

If you have decided to buy, look in this order

  1. Pick one mode you will actually use. Plans to use all twelve do not survive contact with a real week.
  2. Look at the part that mode depends on: the seal for pressure, base diameter and wattage for browning, low-temperature accuracy for fermentation.
  3. Check whether the sealing ring and inner pot can be bought separately. When consumables are unavailable, the life of the appliance ends with the life of the part.
  4. See how specifically the manual documents release procedures and part maintenance. A thin manual means picking up safety information from the internet.
  5. Decide where it will sit first, including the direction of the steam, the height, and the cabinet above it.

If you want one dividing line: cook slow dishes three or more times a week and it earns its keep. Below that, a pot and some time will do, and most of the twelve buttons will sit idle.

What could not be confirmed

  • No public comparative testing of the actual pressure and temperature reached by specific household electric multi-cooker models was found. That is why no pressure figure in this article is presented as an appliance capability.
  • No authoritative standard for sealing ring replacement intervals was found. Guidance from parts sellers varies and is hard to treat as a common standard, so the article uses condition-based criteria instead.
  • No source quantifying odor absorption by silicone was found. The article goes only as far as the property of the material and the experience users consistently report.
  • The Korea Consumer Agency figures come from reports filed between 2011 and 2014, and no updated statistics of the same kind were found.
  • The altitude effect is documented in canning research from US universities. How large the same effect is in ordinary, non-canning cooking was not separately confirmed.

In summary

Three things are worth keeping. First, pressure is fast because the boiling point rises, and the premise for that is water in the pot. Second, the number on the screen counts only the hold phase. Pressure up and pressure down bracket it, so there is no gain on short dishes and a large gain on ones that were slow to begin with. Third, do not can low-acid food in this appliance. That is safety rather than preference, and it is a practice the USDA does not recommend.

The rest is a matter of where your expectations point. Multi-function means several different cooking methods live inside one pot, not that each is done as well as a dedicated appliance would do it. Choose the one mode you will use and look at the part it leans on, and even if you only ever press two of the twelve buttons, those two will do their job.
