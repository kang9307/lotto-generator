Why Sourdough Is Hard - It Is a Problem of Judgement, Not Measurement

What actually happens inside a starter, and what to read instead of the clock

Sep 1, 2026 Life & Practical

Sourdough recipes are usually accurate. So many grams of flour, so many grams of water, so many hours. And yet the first few loaves come out flat, or gummy in the middle, even when the recipe is followed exactly. The measuring is not the problem. A recipe specifies time, while what actually governs the process is temperature and the state of the starter — and neither of those is written in the recipe, because neither can be.

So this piece does not add another table of quantities. It looks at what is happening inside the starter, and at what you read instead of the clock when deciding to move to the next step. One arithmetic rule is enough for the skeleton; everything after that is judgement.

This is general cooking and food information, not medical advice. If you have celiac disease or a wheat allergy, nothing in this article can serve as a basis for deciding what to eat, and you should follow the guidance of the clinicians treating you.

Why following the recipe still does not work

Weighing is the easiest part of baking. With a scale, anyone reproduces it. The trouble starts immediately afterwards. There are living things in the dough, and the speed of living things varies several-fold with conditions. Four hours in a winter kitchen and four hours in a summer kitchen are not the same four hours.

A second thing sits on top of that. Commercial yeast is a manufactured product with its performance standardised; a starter is an individual organism that grew up in your own kitchen. Two starters going by the same name hold different balances and work at different speeds. What you need, then, is not a better recipe but a conversion rule.

A starter is an acid-making ecosystem, not a leavening gadget

Mix flour and water, discard part of it daily and add fresh flour and water, and within days you have a mixture that rises on a predictable rhythm. Nothing was added. Microbes already present on the flour and the equipment simply took up residence. A 2021 survey in eLife analysed 500 starters across four continents. The dominant bacteria were Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis, L. plantarum and L. brevis, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae made up more than half the fungal reads in 77 percent of samples. Acetic acid bacteria appeared in 147 starters, or 29.4 percent.

One result from that survey runs straight into received wisdom. There was little relationship between geography and starter composition, even at continental scale. The story that the air of a particular city makes the bread of that city is widely told, but the fact that starters travel from hand to hand is the more plausible explanation.

What separates this from a commercial yeast loaf is the acid, not the rise. Commercial yeast does one job: it produces carbon dioxide. In sourdough, lactic acid bacteria accumulate lactic and acetic acid, pH drops, and that acidity simultaneously changes flavour, keeping quality and the physical behaviour of the dough. In a 2024 experiment in Foods, half the control loaves showed mould after 14 days refrigerated and all of them by day 21, while bread made with an optimised bacterial blend showed none through 28 days.

  • As acid accumulates the gluten structure weakens. The same dough handles differently once fermentation has run long
  • As acid accumulates the flour's own enzyme activity shifts too. That is why autolyse proceeds further under acidic conditions
  • The word symbiosis deserves care. A 2020 study in Microorganisms could not confirm the cross-feeding account in which yeasts take up glucose released by lactic acid bacteria, and suggested competition may dominate instead. Living together and helping each other are not the same claim

Why it takes so long

Most of the lifting is done by yeast. In that same 2020 work, monocultures raised dough by an average of 0.53 cm for the bacterial strains against 1.87 cm for the yeast strains — more than a threefold gap. And yet the yeasts in a starter were never selected for leavening power. Commercial yeast is the product of picking for that one ability; a starter's yeast is whatever happened to establish itself under the constraints of surviving acidity and coexisting with other organisms.

Composition adds another layer. The eLife survey reported that the higher the proportion of acetic acid bacteria, the slower the dough rose (correlation −0.51), and the same proportion tracked with vinegar-like aromas. Some starters rise slowly with strong aroma, others rise quickly and taste mild. Neither one is broken.

And that slowness is the character of the product. A longer time means a longer time for acids and aroma compounds to accumulate, and that accumulation is what makes sourdough sourdough. Time is not a cost to be endured; it is part of what you are making.

What happens across the two weeks of building a starter

A 2023 study in PeerJ built 40 starters — four each from ten flours — and tracked them daily for 14 days. The pH curve it produced explains most of the confusion beginners run into.

StagepHWhat dominatesWhat you see
Day 15.31Bacteria carried in on the flour (Pantoea, Pseudomonas and others)Almost no change
Day 24.2Diversity peaks. Organisms that will later be displacedSometimes a sudden large rise
Day 33.67Acid-tolerant lactic acid bacteria start taking overThe rise stops and it sinks
Days 6-143.54-3.68Community stabilises, effectively fixed around day 10Rises and falls on a steady cycle

The cells that matter most are days two and three. The day-two rise was very likely not produced by the yeasts that will later raise your bread. It is other organisms briefly active while acidification is incomplete, which is why it stops on day three. Reading that stall as failure and throwing the jar out means quitting at precisely the moment the community changes hands. The unfamiliar smell of the first few days comes from the same place and disappears within days. But this is a description of the normal path, not a safety guarantee. Mould or clear discolouration is a separate event.

The settled composition is worth noting too: on average the climax communities were 48.1 percent Lactobacillus, 22.7 percent Pediococcus and 19.1 percent Gluconobacter. The third of those is an acetic acid bacterium, which connects back to the rise rates above.

Feeding is dilution as much as it is food

If you think of feeding purely as supplying food, it is not obvious why the ratio matters. Feeding is also dilution. The moment you discard part of the starter and add fresh flour and water, the accumulated acid is thinned and the pH climbs back. So the feeding ratio is the handle that sets the length of one cycle. A 1:1:1 feed dilutes little, so it sours quickly and peaks early; 1:5:5 takes far longer to peak and accumulates less acid.

  • For a starter kept at room temperature and used daily, a larger ratio stretches the cycle and is easier to manage — one feed a day lines up with the peak
  • Refrigeration is the other way to stretch the cycle, using temperature instead. But a starter used straight from the fridge is short on power
  • Use a starter near its peak for bread. One that has risen and just begun to sink has already spent much of its food
  • Note the feeding time and the time of maximum volume for a few days and your starter's own cycle emerges. Cutting the discard because it feels wasteful only lets acid keep building

Baker's percentage - flour is always 100

A recipe written only in grams has to be recalculated every time you change the batch size, and two recipes cannot be compared at all. So baking sets the flour weight to 100 and states everything else as a proportion of it. The percentages always add up to more than 100.

baker's percentage = (ingredient weight / total flour weight) x 100

  total flour weight  = 100 %   (the base; several flours together sum to 100)
  water               = hydration
  salt                = typically 1.8 to 2.2 %
  starter (levain)    = typically 10 to 30 %

  ingredient weight = total flour weight x (baker's percentage / 100)

The flour and water inside the starter count too

This is where it most often goes wrong. A starter is already flour and water. Leave it out of the arithmetic and your real hydration differs from the number on the page, and the error grows with the proportion of starter.

total flour 500 g, hydration 75 %, starter 20 %, salt 2 %
(assuming a starter built at equal parts flour and water)

  starter  20 %  = 100 g   -> of which flour 50 g + water 50 g
  salt      2 %  =  10 g
  total water 75 % = 375 g

  flour to add separately = 500 - 50  = 450 g
  water to add separately = 375 - 50  = 325 g

  check : flour 450 + 50 = 500 g,  water 325 + 50 = 375 g
          375 / 500 = 0.75  ->  hydration 75 %
  total dough weight = 500 + 375 + 10 = 885 g

What hydration actually changes

Hydration is not a flavour setting; it is the number that decides handling and crumb. More water and the dough spreads and sticks and resists taking tension, but the crumb opens up and turns irregular. Less water and it handles easily with a fine, even crumb. The 65 to 70 percent range commonly suggested to beginners is not a compromise on taste but a way of removing one variable. Start at 85 percent and there is no way to tell a starter problem from a shaping problem. Change flour and the same hydration behaves differently, so the number has to be found again.

Temperature comes before time

Fermentation is a chemical process, and rate is a function of temperature. A bulk that finished in four hours at 24 degrees for the author will take far longer at 19 and finish far sooner at 28. Keep to the time and the fermentation is wrong; get the fermentation right and the time is wrong. There is no way to have both. And what you measure is not room temperature but dough temperature, which folds in the temperature of the flour, the water and the friction of mixing, and typically sits several degrees away from the room. The industry target of 23 to 25 degrees after autolyse exists to manage exactly that point.

Raise the dough temperatureLower itWhat it means in practice
Everything ferments fasterEverything slowsThe whole recipe timeline shifts
The window for judging narrowsIt widensOn a hot day it over-ferments while you look away
Consistency is harder to holdEasier to reproduceThe cooler side is better for learning
The balance of acids shiftsIt shifts the other wayFermentation temperature changes the character of the aroma

The working rule is simple. Read the recipe's times as a rough lower bound and let the dough decide when it is done. But measure the dough temperature every time and write it down. Two or three records give you your own kitchen's timings, and after that you no longer need the recipe's.

Gluten is not built by kneading alone

Gluten is the network that forms as flour proteins meet water and link up, and that network has to hold gas for bread to rise. Long machine kneading builds it fast, but sourdough usually does not go that way, because time and water do the same job.

Autolyse is the step of mixing flour and water alone and letting it rest for 20 to 60 minutes, credited to the French baker Raymond Calvel in 1974. During that rest water penetrates the starch and protein, protein bonds strengthen in the water-rich environment, and gluten strands increase without mechanical work. Flour enzymes, proteases in particular, are working at the same time, and they are more active under acidic conditions. The dough ends up less sticky, smoother and more extensible.

Stretch and fold sits on top of that. It is not a miniature version of kneading but closer to aligning the network that time has already built and giving it tension.

  • Three or four folds at 30 to 45 minute intervals is usually enough. Stretching autolyse indefinitely does not improve matters — industry references note that dough stability falls as it runs longer
  • Salt and starter normally go in after the autolyse. Salt tightens gluten and slows enzyme action
  • The signal is how the dough changes, not how many folds you have done. If the second and third feel the same, that dough is finished. With a high proportion of wholemeal or rye the network builds less readily, and that is normal

How you know bulk fermentation is done

Bulk is where most failures happen and also the vaguest stage. Percentage rise is convenient but looks different depending on the shape of the container, and time cannot be trusted for the reasons above. So you overlay several signals.

Where to lookUnder-fermentedAbout rightOver-fermented
VolumeBarely changedClearly grown, domed on topGrew, then sank in the centre
Surface and sidesSmooth, no bubblesLarge and small bubbles evenly spreadSparse bubbles, surface looks wet
Fingertip dentSprings straight backReturns about halfway, slowlyStays sunken or collapses
HandlingElastic, resists stretchingInflated but holds its shapeSpreads, will not take tension
After bakingDense crumb, the score bursts wideEven crumb, the score opensFlat, the score does not open

The hardest pair to tell apart is under- and over-fermented. Both end as a flat loaf, so the outside gives nothing away. The crumb is what separates them. Under-fermented bread is dense and the score bursts open dramatically; over-fermented bread is open and irregular and the score barely opens at all. When the call is unclear, stop a little early. Under-fermentation can be made up in the final proof, but gluten weakened by long acid exposure is not recoverable by any later step.

Shaping, final proof and the cold retard

These three are often described as one block, but they do different jobs. Shaping creates tension at the surface. Wrap the outside tightly and that skin directs the gas pushing from inside upwards; without tension the gas escapes sideways and the dough spreads. The final proof refills the gas lost in shaping, so it need not be as long as the bulk. Take it to maximum again and the tension you just built is spent, and there is nothing left to expand in the oven.

The cold retard is a device for separating rates by temperature. At low temperature the balance between yeast and bacteria tilts away from rising and towards acid accumulation, so the sourness sharpens. The practical benefit is just as large: cold dough is firm, easy to score and does not collapse in transfer. Much of the reason beginners succeed more often with a retard is handling, not flavour.

  • Studies of long cold fermentation reported clear changes in the 4 and 10 degree range and noted that 20 and 30 degrees were unsuitable for the purpose
  • But those studies ran for durations incomparable to a night in a domestic fridge. Explaining a home retard with those figures overstates it
  • It is also a scheduling tool. You can split the day you mix from the day you bake, and baking straight from the fridge is usually easier to handle

Baking - most of the oven spring is not the yeast's doing

The sharp expansion in the first minutes in the oven is called oven spring. Plenty of writing explains it as the yeast's last effort, but the actual contribution is smaller than that. Campden BRI, the UK food research body, cites an analysis in which the volume from water turning to steam accounts for more than half of the oven spring, with ethanol and carbon dioxide making up much of the rest. Ethanol vaporisation rises sharply around 70 degrees.

Oven spring is over within the first third of the bake. How far the dough can stretch in that short window sets the final volume, and what stops it is a crust that has already set. Once the surface dries the crust forms early, and a formed crust does not stretch. That is what early steam is for. A wet surface delays crust formation and buys expansion time, while surface starch takes up moisture and gelatinises before setting under dry heat into a thin, glossy shell.

Why a lidded cast iron pot works

Domestic ovens hold steam poorly. It leaks at the door seal and vanishes the moment you open it. A lidded cast iron pot traps the moisture the dough itself gives off in a small volume. The inside becomes humid with no separate equipment, and that state carries the loaf through the oven spring window. The moment you lift the lid is effectively the moment you cut the steam, and that timing sets the thickness and colour of the crust.

For doneness, internal temperature is far more reproducible than colour or the sound of a tap. Water is evaporating throughout the bake, so the crumb cannot exceed 100 degrees, and the baking literature describes a plateau around 98 degrees. Check whether the centre has entered that plateau; colour only tells you about the crust. Cutting a loaf straight out of the oven gives a gummy crumb because moisture is still redistributing, so cool it completely first.

Five reasons it goes wrong

Failures nearly always fall into five categories. But pick a cause from the symptom alone and you will usually pick the wrong one. A flat loaf comes from two opposite causes.

CauseWhat you seeHow to tellWhat to change
Weak starterThe dough barely risesCheck whether the starter itself doubles after a feedFeed twice daily at room temperature for a few days to restore the cycle
Too coldFermentation is far behind the recipe's timingMeasure the dough temperature, not the roomRaise the water temperature or extend the time. Do not keep to the clock
Over-fermentedFlat, open crumb, the score will not openOpen crumb means over, dense crumb means underEnd bulk earlier and switch to the fingertip test
Too much waterThe dough flows and will not take tensionBake one loaf with the same flour and less waterDrop to 65-70 percent, learn the judgement, then raise it again
Not enough shaping tensionFermentation was fine but it spreads in the ovenSee whether it holds its own shape after the final proofRework the motion that wraps the surface tight

Change one thing at a time. Lower the hydration while also shortening the bulk and switching flour, and whatever the result you will not know what caused it. Bread is an experiment that costs hours per run, so the trial count is low and the price of adding variables is high.

When to throw it out - mould and hooch are not the same thing

Keep a starter long enough and liquid pools on the surface. It is commonly called hooch and ranges from grey to brown. Alcoholic fermentation breaks sugar down into carbon dioxide and ethanol together, and the widely used explanation is that alcohol and water separate out on a starter that has run out of food. It is not a signal to discard but a signal that it is hungry. Stir it back in or pour it off and feed.

Mould is an entirely different event. Fuzzy growth, pink, orange or black spots, or a dried skin on the surface all lead to one answer. Discard the whole thing. The same holds for bread. The US Department of Agriculture advises that soft, porous foods such as bread and baked goods be thrown out whole rather than having the mould cut away, because mould has thread-like structures extending past the surface and the visible part is not the whole of it. The exception of cutting at least 2.5 cm below the growth applies only to hard cheese and firm fruit and vegetables.

The idea that baking it off makes it safe fails here too. The toxins some moulds produce are invisible, carry no distinctive taste or smell, and can cause serious illness. Some, aflatoxin among them, are not readily removed by cooking heat. Killing the mould with heat and removing its toxins are two different problems. For symptoms and what to do, see the piece on responding to food poisoning.

  • There is no reason to accept risk out of attachment. A starter can be rebuilt in two weeks — in the study above the community settled around day 10
  • Keep the jar and tools clean and do not leave dried dough on the walls. Dried patches are an easy starting point
  • If the call is unclear, throw it out. Tasting to find out is not an option

How far the health claims actually go

Sourdough attracts an unusual number of health claims. The line between what is established and what is not is fairly clear, so here it is.

A warning about celiac disease. Wheat sourdough is not safe for people with celiac disease. Fermentation does not remove gluten sufficiently, and there is no way to establish whether a home-baked loaf meets a gluten-free labelling threshold. If you have celiac disease or a wheat allergy, follow the guidance of the clinicians treating you.
What you often hearWhat is establishedWhat is not
Gluten is broken down, so people with celiac disease can eat itFermentation did not reduce gluten reactivity to a useful level, and one condition increased itThere is no evidence home fermentation reaches a safe level. This claim is clearly refuted
It raises blood sugar lessA crossover trial in 20 people found no significant difference between wholegrain sourdough and industrial white breadResponses split by individual. The sample and duration are too small for a conclusion
Mineral absorption improvesSingle-meal tests showed higher non-heme iron availability following phytate reductionA systematic review found long-term trials did not improve iron status
It is easier to digestLong fermentation has been reported to cut fermentable sugars substantiallyThose conditions ran about 100 days at low temperature. They do not transfer to an overnight bulk
It moulds laterAn optimised loaf showed no mould through 28 days refrigeratedThat is a preservation effect, not a health benefit, and does not carry to home loaves

The celiac point deserves unpacking. A 2015 study in Nutrients measured how far lactic fermentation reduced the interaction between gluten and the enzyme implicated in triggering celiac disease, and 73 to 102 percent of the unfermented signal remained. Effectively no reduction — and in one condition, 24 hours of fermentation with a particular strain raised available binding sites by 22 percent. The US FDA threshold for a gluten-free claim is below 20 ppm, extended in August 2020 to fermented and hydrolysed foods; because no validated method exists for detecting and quantifying intact gluten in such foods, manufacturers must document compliance before fermentation. No such procedure exists for a home loaf. Background is in celiac disease and the gluten-free diet.

The blood sugar result is interesting in its own way. A 2017 crossover trial in Cell Metabolism fed 20 participants industrial white bread and wholegrain sourdough for a week each, and found no significant difference across more than 20 clinical measures. Individual variation was large, and its direction was predictable from baseline gut microbiome composition. The statement that sourdough lowers blood sugar for everyone is not supported by this data. If that is the goal, managing blood sugar in prediabetes or gut health and probiotics are more directly to the point.

Summary

Sourdough is not hard because the process is complicated. There are four ingredients and only a handful of actions. The difficulty comes from a mismatch: the instructions are written in time, while the actual progress is set by temperature and the state of the microbes. Judgement closes that gap, and judgement comes from knowing the signals.

Five signals are enough. Does the starter reach a peak on a regular schedule after feeding? What is the dough temperature? How does a fingertip dent come back? Does the shaped dough hold its own form? Is there room left to expand in the oven? Answer those and the recipe becomes a reference; fail to answer them and it becomes a charm. And one line is not a matter of judgement at all. If you see mould, throw it out. If you have celiac disease, wheat sourdough is not on the table.
