Putting a Generative Model Into Recommendations - What Changes and What Does Not

From picking items to writing context, and the evaluation and cost problems that stay exactly where they were

2026-08-01 Tech & IT

Opening: the feed keeps showing you what you already saw

You open the app. Six tiles sit on the first screen. Three look like what you watched yesterday, one you already finished last month, and two are the same evergreen hits that have held the same slot for weeks. Nothing new is anywhere on the screen. Scroll three times and an unfamiliar title finally appears, but by then you have already closed the app.

This is not a malfunction. It is what the design produces. Classic recommenders reason from 「people who behaved like you liked this」. So an item with no accumulated behavior has no basis at all. No basis means no score, no score means no slot on screen, and no slot means the behavior never accumulates. That loop is the whole story behind a feed that only shows you what you already saw.

People have been proposing language models as the fix for years. If a model can read the description attached to an item, surely it can place that item without waiting for logs. Large services really are moving that way, and in July 2026 a major streaming provider published an engineering blog post describing how it rebuilt its ranking system around a language model backbone.

Read that post to the end, though, and the impression that stays is a little different. What changed is genuinely large. But the hardest part of recommendation was never the model. The hard parts are two: measuring what counts as a good recommendation, and fitting the computation inside a real-time response budget. Neither one moves when you swap the backbone. This article draws that line.

Sourcing and limits. One engineering blog post published in July 2026 by a large streaming provider was opened directly and used as the primary source. Everything taken from it is marked with 「according to the published post」, and everything else is written as general reasoning. No claim is made about any company's internal implementation. One aggregator site that had reposted the same material was blocked and could not be opened, and nothing in this article comes from it.

How numbers are handled here. Every improvement figure from the post is quoted only together with its conditions. Strip away which metric, against what baseline, and measured when, and the number means nothing at all. Section 5 shows exactly why. Every service example and every figure inside the code blocks below is invented for illustration.

What this article is not. How a language model actually picks the next word is covered in what picking the next word actually means. Personalization as a marketing strategy is covered in hyper-personalized marketing strategies. This one is neither. It is about system design.

1. How classic recommendation worked - a machine that picks

Briefly, because the point is the contrast. Fifteen years of recommendation reduce to one sentence: find people whose behavior overlaps yours, and show what they liked. Collaborative filtering puts users and items on two axes of an enormous matrix, compresses that matrix into a low-dimensional space so each side becomes a vector, and then the whole thing is dot products.

[The classic recommendation pipeline]

  behavior logs (clicks · plays · purchases · searches · bounces)
      |
      v   nightly batch, hours
  user embeddings / item embeddings
      |
      v   approximate nearest neighbor, milliseconds
  candidate generation     millions → hundreds
      |
      v   hundreds to thousands of features, dot product or tree model
  ranking                  hundreds → dozens
      |
      v   dedup · diversity · merchandising rules · availability filter
  final slate              dozens → the 6 tiles you see

  * In this structure, recommendation always means picking from a list
    that already exists.

There are good reasons this structure survived so long.

  • It does not need to know what the item is. Whether the item is a film, a bag of noodles, or a loan product is irrelevant. An id is enough. That is why the same machinery ported into every domain without modification
  • The compute is absurdly cheap. The heavy training happens overnight in batch, and during the day you only do vector arithmetic. A request finishes in under a millisecond
  • It captures taste that people cannot articulate. A user who could never write down what they like still leaves behavior behind. That is why logs beat surveys
  • Operations are simple. When a metric drops you retrain. Failures usually have a narrow, findable cause

And there are places where it hits a wall structurally, meaning no amount of tuning gets past it.

  • With no logs it can do nothing. New items and new users. That is the cold start problem, handled separately in section 3
  • Popularity bias is self-reinforcing. Items that get impressions accumulate more logs and therefore get more impressions. The long tail never starts
  • One new signal shakes the whole pipeline. The single sentence 「let us use recent search queries」 turns into feature definition, training data regeneration, serving schema change, and retraining. Two weeks is normal
  • It cannot say why. The justification is a dot product between 512-dimensional vectors. That is not a human-readable form. 「Members with similar taste watched this」 is a stock phrase, not an explanation
  • Text and images are simply discarded. Descriptions, reviews, subtitles, and cover art exist, but using them requires a separate embedding model, and nothing guarantees that embedding is aligned with the ranking objective

2. What changes when a generative model arrives - and why it is hard

The naive picture is simple. Hand a language model the user history and ask it to write ten things this person might watch next. Not pick, produce. And this picture collapses the moment you actually try it.

The reason is obvious once you remember what a language model is for. It produces statistically natural strings. Your catalog listing has no relationship whatsoever to naturalness. So the model smoothly invents titles that have never existed. The published post confronts this head-on, naming three failure modes of off-the-shelf models: inventing out-of-catalog items, over-recommending popular titles, and ignoring constraints.

2.1 The compromise that was actually adopted

According to the published post the answer was to take generation out of the output path. The structure summarizes like this. A decoder-only Transformer reads the context and produces a single pooled hidden state. On top of it sits a catalog-aware scoring component that combines that representation with each item's learned embedding, through a dot product or a small multilayer perceptron, to produce a score. The result is that the output is confined to the catalog by construction.

Which means the place where 「generation」 happens is not the output but the input. Turning logs, metadata, and context into written natural language becomes a new engineering stage, and the post captures this with the phrase 「the prompt becomes the new feature vector」.

[Input to a language-model ranker - verbalizing context (invented example)]

  raw log (classic features)          natural-language context (within budget)
  --------------------------------    -----------------------------------------
  u_10293  item_88421  dwell 1820     This member watched a long-form
  u_10293  item_77120  dwell 45       documentary to the end on a weeknight
  u_10293  item_51002  skip           and dropped a light variety show within
  u_10293  query 「documentary rec」    a minute. They typed documentary into
  ...  (240 more rows omitted)        search. Last three months in one line:
                                      strong pull toward true-story material.

  compaction principles (the direction named in the post)
    - keep high-signal events in full
    - omit low-signal interactions
    - summarize repetitive behavior
    - elaborate selectively on the items that matter

  * The unit of budget moves from 「number of features」 to 「number of tokens」.

The last line is why this matters operationally. The unit of budget changes. The old question was how many features you could afford. The new question is how many tokens you can afford, and deciding what survives inside that budget becomes an engineering discipline of its own.

2.3 So why is it hard

From the table the right column looks strictly better. Three reasons it does not play out that way.

  • The nonexistent-item risk does not disappear. Constraining scores keeps the ranked list clean, but the risk returns the instant you generate and display a summary or a reason string. 「Based on a true story」 can simply be false
  • Recommendation is an availability problem, not a taste problem. What someone might like and what you can actually show them right now are different sets. Inventory, expiring rights, regional restrictions, age ratings, and payment state all intervene. That rule layer survives the backbone swap intact
  • Popularity bias becomes two layers deep. The classic system's bias came from logs. A language model adds the bias of its pretraining corpus on top, because what the internet discussed a lot is what the model knows well. That is the same phenomenon the post flags in off-the-shelf models

3. Cold start - what improves when the system can read

Cold start is not one problem but two. They have different causes and different remedies, and lumping them under one name is why the discussion so often goes in circles.

  • New item. Interaction count is zero. Collaborative filtering has no material from which to build this item's vector. The conventional responses are to fill with popularity, to have an editor hand-place it, or to deliberately force impressions to harvest logs. The last one pays exploration cost in user satisfaction
  • New user. First session. Unlike an item, this side has no descriptive text either. That is why the taste-picker screen at signup is still alive in 2026

A backbone that understands text genuinely improves the first case. With zero logs it can still read the description, genre, cast, tags, and synopsis and estimate where the item sits relative to everything else. 「With zero logs, an item that has been described in words can still find its place」 is the summary of this section.

Not asserting more than the source supports. The primary source consulted here presents no numeric cold-start result. It mentions only, while describing context construction, that items such as new releases get elaborated selectively. So the claim 「language models solve cold start」 cannot be argued from that publication. What is claimed here goes only as far as there is structural room for it to help.

The second row is the real conclusion of this section. A model that understands text only wins when there is text to understand. That is what leads into section 6.

4. The cost structure changes - what one recommendation costs

In the classic ranker the dominant cost driver was candidate count. Dotting 500 candidates against a 512-dimensional vector is effectively free on a modern CPU. So engineering focus went to shrinking the candidate set and to finishing the batch training before morning.

In a language-model ranker that axis is replaced wholesale. According to the published post, serving cost is roughly proportional to context length. The primary target of optimization stops being the candidate set and becomes the context.

[What one recommendation costs - order of magnitude (all figures invented)]

  classic ranker
    500 candidates × embedding dot product : microseconds
    CPU time per request                   : under 1 millisecond
    axis that drives cost                  : candidate count

  language-model ranker (prefill only)
    read a 2,000-token context once        : tens of milliseconds (accelerator)
    score 500 candidates in that same pass : relatively small addition
    axis that drives cost                  : context length

  the direction of optimization flips
    classic    : shrink the candidate set
    generative : shrink the context

  * According to the published post, context length could be cut to roughly
    one third of the original budget with negligible degradation in ranking
    quality. That number came out of their catalog, their prompt design, and
    their metric. It is not a constant you can carry over.

The post names three cost-control levers, and read in order the logic is clean.

  • Train smaller or distilled models on more data. Cut parameters, compensate with data. This attacks the per-forward-pass cost directly
  • Compact the context aggressively. Since cost tracks context length, this is the most direct handle available. The compaction principles from section 2 are what get applied here
  • Do not emit tokens one at a time. Read the prompt once and score the whole candidate set in a single forward pass. With no autoregressive decoding, the cost term proportional to output length never appears at all

4.1 Where this collides with real-time response

Recommendation has to finish while the screen is painting. If the first-screen render budget is 300 milliseconds, the slice allocated to recommendation is commonly a few tens of milliseconds. A language model forward pass is larger than that. So real deployments almost always land on precomputation and caching.

  • Session-level computation. Compute once when the session starts and reuse it for the whole session. The most common and least dramatic option
  • Nightly batch scoring. Precompute the top few hundred per user and store it, then apply only availability filters and merchandising rules at request time
  • Reranking stage only. Leave candidate generation exactly as it is and rescore only the top few dozen with the heavy model. The smallest cost and the smallest risk
  • Tiered deployment. Open the real-time path only for high-activity users and serve everyone else from cache. This keeps cost from scaling linearly with user count

Caching has a price, though. Freshness. What the user just watched does not show up on the next screen. From the user's side that reads as a feed that does not react, and that is not an improvement but a trade. If you want a feel for how large a context budget actually is, paste a draft context into the token counter and count it.

5. Evaluation gets harder - the gap between offline metrics and real users

This is the most important section here. The previous four were all 「what changes」. From here on it is what does not.

The standard way to evaluate a recommender is to compute offline metrics over historical logs: mean reciprocal rank, normalized discounted cumulative gain, recall at K. The problem is what those metrics are actually measuring. Historical logs were produced by the previous recommender. Items that were never shown have no user response attached, and a response that does not exist cannot be a label.

So the precise meaning of 「offline metrics improved」 is this: it ordered better within the set of things the old system already showed. The ability to surface something new cannot be measured by that metric even in principle. And improving cold start is a goal that sits precisely inside that unmeasurable region.

[Same change, opposite verdict - where offline and online diverge (invented)]

  Change A   lower the popularity weight, raise long-tail exposure
    offline mean reciprocal rank   -3.1 %   long-tail labels barely exist in logs
    online click-through           -0.4 %   fewer familiar titles on screen one
    online 4-week retention        +1.2 %   users run out of things later
    → offline evidence alone would have killed this change

  Change B   summarize the reason text more aggressively
    offline mean reciprocal rank    0.0 %   the ordering never changed
    online click-through           +6.8 %
    online 4-week retention        -2.0 %   more users open it and feel misled
    → short-term evidence alone would have shipped this change

  * All figures are invented for illustration. The point is the direction of
    the signs, not the magnitudes.

5.1 Reading the published numbers together with their conditions

The improvement figures in the post actually illustrate this section's argument rather well. Each one, stated with its conditions.

  • About 1.6 percent improvement on offline mean reciprocal rank. The baseline is a mature production ranker. But the number is meaningless detached from the clause attached to it: it used roughly 40 times fewer phase-two labeled training examples. What the result says is closer to 「matched or beat it with far less labeled data」 than to 「is 1.6 percent more accurate」
  • Phase-one adaptation was worth roughly 10 to 20 percent. That is the offline metric gain from adapting the backbone on proprietary corpora versus using an off-the-shelf model directly. The comparison point being an unadapted model matters
  • Phase-two post-training was worth 35 to 50 percent, growing toward 80 percent depending on when you measure. The first range was measured near the phase-one training cutoff; the larger figure came two weeks later as the backbone went stale. This is the most important number in this article
  • The online test ran on roughly 10 percent of traffic for four weeks. It reports statistically significant improvements on both short-term and long-term metrics while not disclosing the specific values
  • Reinforcement-learning-style methods were deferred. The post notes they showed gains but were set aside because of higher cost. That is a description of a cost-adjusted choice, not of the best available method

Look at the third item again. The measured benefit of the same technique more than doubled purely by changing when it was measured. What does that tell you? The size of an improvement is not an intrinsic property of a technique. It is a function of how stale the thing you compared against was. So an organization that copies 「35 to 50 percent」 into its own expectations has adopted a number that has nothing to do with its own refresh cadence.

5.2 Why A/B is unavoidable, and what it costs

The reason you end up in an online test is clear. How users react to something they were never shown is not in the logs. That data has to be manufactured. And manufacturing it is not cheap.

  • You have to split traffic. Testing at 10 percent means 90 percent keeps receiving the old system. If the new system really is better, you gave up that improvement for the duration
  • The window is long. Short-term and long-term metrics can point in opposite directions, so you need weeks. Change B in the invented example above is exactly that case
  • Statistical power is the binding constraint. If the true effect is around one percent, the sample needed to detect it exceeds what most services generate in a day. With small traffic, months of running still produce no conclusion
  • Experiments contaminate each other. When the same user lands in several tests at once the effects mix. This happens constantly in organizations without an experimentation platform
  • Seasonality and events intrude. One large release or one holiday week can invert the verdict of a four-week test

Which sums to this. You can buy a model, but you cannot buy an evaluation. And a model adopted without one goes into production in a state where nobody knows whether things got better or worse. That is the most expensive failure available.

6. Where a smaller market service stands - thin catalogs and thin metadata

From here on the discussion is purely structural. No claims about any particular company. Only what changes when these conditions hold.

6.1 When the catalog is small

  • With a few thousand items, candidate generation is effectively unnecessary. You can score everything. Which shrinks the relative advantage of techniques that are good at pulling candidates out of millions
  • Instead, the quality of each individual description drives the outcome. The fewer the items, the more each item's representation moves the whole ordering
  • Small catalogs give you very high precomputation cache hit rates. Items are few relative to users, so a precompute strategy fits well. On the cost side this works in your favor
  • On the other hand diversity metrics degrade easily. With little to choose from, every model returns a similar list. No model swap fixes that

6.2 When metadata is thin

This is the condition that most often blocks adoption in practice. Section 3's conclusion, that a text-understanding model needs text to understand, becomes concrete here. The common states look like this.

  • Title present, description empty. If the description field is optional on the item registration screen, it is almost always blank
  • Description is source-language boilerplate or machine translation. Sentences exist, but they say nothing about what the item actually is
  • The category taxonomy no longer matches the catalog. New items get forced into a classification built years ago for a different assortment
  • Tagging conventions differ per person. Five or six spellings of the same concept coexist
  • The main text is user reviews, which are short and mostly interjections. 「Love it」 and 「Fast shipping」 do not distinguish items from each other

The conclusion is cold. Attach a language-model ranker in this state and you pay for expensive compute to read empty sentences. The first job is metadata repair, not model adoption. Interestingly, that repair work is itself largely automatable with a language model. Reversing the order is the entire point.

6.3 Conditions created by the language itself

  • Agglutinative morphology defeats surface matching. The same word appears in many inflected forms, which makes keyword-matching candidate generation brittle. This is exactly where a language model's relative advantage is largest
  • Spacing conventions are unstable. When user input and catalog text space a compound differently, rule-based matching misses entirely
  • Slang and abbreviations turn over fast. Dictionaries and rules cannot keep up, and terms coined after the backbone's training cutoff are unknown to the backbone too
  • Latin-script and native-script spellings coexist. The same item is searched two ways and registered two ways. Without a normalization layer, every model leaks somewhere

6.4 When traffic is small

The statistical power problem from section 5 blocks the adoption decision itself for a mid-sized service. If the effect is around one percent, a few thousand daily users will not produce significance in months. The realistic options are these.

  • Compare the same users across time periods. Alternate the treatment over the whole population in blocks. Vulnerable to seasonality, but it does not split the sample
  • Pick the place where the effect is large. Instead of overall click-through, use a narrow segment with a big swing, such as first-week performance of newly added items
  • Have people read the lists and grade them. Sample recommendation output weekly and have someone evaluate it. No statistical rigor, but better than looking at nothing
  • Use negative signals as the metric. Immediate bounce, scrolling to the end of the shelf, falling back to search. These are easier to detect than satisfaction and show direction on smaller samples

7. Risks - amplified bias, explainability, disclosure duties

A new structure brings new failure modes. An adoption memo missing this section is still half written.

  • Bias stacks in two layers. Pretraining corpus bias lands on top of existing log bias. Items the internet discussed heavily become structurally advantaged, and no amount of log cleaning removes that
  • The feedback loop accelerates. Recommendations create impressions, impressions create logs, logs create the next training set. The stronger the model, the faster this loop converges, and nothing guarantees the fixed point is a good one
  • Readable is not the same as explained. That the context is natural language is a real advantage. But which part of that text drove the score remains opaque. Generating a plausible reason after the fact is not an explanation; it is one more generated artifact
  • User behavior accumulates as readable sentences. A natural-language context means 「this person watches X on weeknights」 is written down somewhere as prose. Log retention, prompt logging, and any outbound transmission path all come under review
  • Sensitive-item accidents are still stopped only by rules. A medical, adult, financial, or political item surfacing because of a single word in the context does not go away with a better backbone. The rule filter layer stays
  • A quiet cost leak appears. Since context length determines cost, one team adding one field to the context raises total serving cost. A context budget without a named owner will inflate

7.1 The legal side requires verification, not assertion

This article does not assert what any law requires. It varies by service type, jurisdiction, and effective date, and this is not the place to adjudicate it. What follows is only what you need to verify. Each item should be settled against the relevant regulator's guidance, your own terms, and legal review.

  • Scope of any AI-content disclosure duty. If a reason string or item summary is model-generated and shown on screen, whether it falls under a labeling obligation needs verification
  • Lawful basis for processing and cross-border transfer. The basis for composing user behavior into a context and feeding it to a model, and the transfer procedure when that model sits abroad, need verification
  • Terms covering item descriptions and reviews. The permitted scope for using third-party-authored text in training or summarization needs verification against contracts and terms of service
  • Rights to an explanation of automated decisions. If recommendation moves beyond convenience and affects pricing or access terms, the bar may change, so this needs verification
  • Age and rating regulation. Requirements for lists shown to minors differ by domain and need verification

8. The adoption call - when it is worth it and when it is overkill

Everything above, compressed into a decision. Two lists first.

Conditions where it is worth it.

  • New items arrive constantly, so logs are permanently insufficient
  • Items already carry text worth reading. Three sentences or more that say something specific
  • Adding one signal already takes two weeks. Surgery cost has passed a threshold
  • You have enough traffic to detect a significant difference in an online test
  • Usage patterns suit precomputation. Long sessions, heavy first-screen weight
  • There is a business requirement to display a reason for the recommendation

Conditions where it is overkill.

  • The catalog holds a few hundred items and rarely changes
  • Item metadata is a title and nothing else. There is nothing to understand
  • Search does not work yet. In a service where search is broken, recommendation is always the second problem
  • The latency budget is single-digit milliseconds. Even precomputation will not fit
  • There is no evaluation system at all. The most common case, and the one where nothing you adopt produces a knowable result
  • One person owns this and also runs operations. Operational load exceeds the model's benefit
[Order of attachment - start where the risk is smallest]

  Step 0   build the evaluation first
           1 offline metric + 2 online metrics (short and long) + sample size
           without this, no step below can be declared a success or a failure

  Step 1   replace only the reranking stage
           leave candidate generation alone, rescore only the top 20 to 50
           if it fails the candidate set is untouched, so the blast radius is small

8.1 Pre-kickoff checklist

  • Checked whether the current recommender's offline and online metrics have ever moved in the same direction
  • Chose one short-term and one long-term metric for the online test in advance
  • Computed the minimum sample and duration needed to detect a significant difference
  • Set a ceiling and an owner for the context budget. Not a state where anyone can add a field
  • Built a rollback path to the existing ranker and actually exercised it
  • Confirmed that availability, rights, and age-rating filters still sit behind the model
  • If generated text is displayed, confirmed disclosure duties and factuality checks
  • Confirmed the lawful basis and retention period for composing behavior into contexts
  • If cold start is the goal, defined a separate metric that measures only that
  • Measured metadata quality numerically. You know the share of items with an empty description

Conclusion: the model is a component, the hard part is unchanged

To summarize. Putting a language model into recommendation changes the shape of the input. Sentences replace ids and numbers, the unit of budget moves from features to tokens, and the cost of adding a signal drops dramatically. Because the system reads text, there is room to work on cold start, and because the context is human-readable, there is room to improve explanation. All of that is real change.

But what does not change matters more. Measuring what counts as a good recommendation is exactly as hard as before. Offline metrics still only rank within what the old system already showed, and the ability to surface something new remains unmeasurable in principle. Learning how users actually react means splitting traffic and waiting weeks, and that cost weighs more the smaller the service. Fitting the computation into a real-time budget is unchanged too. Precomputation and caching are not a solution; they are freshness traded away.

So the conclusion is not 「rebuild recommendation with language models」. The hard part of recommendation lives in evaluation and cost, not in the model. The published figures make this case better than any argument. That the same technique's measured benefit more than doubled purely by moving the measurement date tells you the number is not a property of the technique but a function of the baseline and the conditions.

There is one thing you can do today. Go find out whether your current recommender's offline metrics and real user metrics have ever moved in the same direction. If no such record exists, then whatever you swap the backbone for, you will be operating without any way to know whether it helped. You can buy a model. You cannot buy the basis for that judgment.

| Observed signal | Worth it | Why | Do this first |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hundreds of new items per week | High | Logs always insufficient | Pilot at reranking |
| Descriptions of three sentences or more | High | There is text to understand | Design context serialization |
| Adding a feature takes two weeks | High | Surgery cost already large | Trial a sentence-based signal |
| Reasons must be displayed | Medium | Context is readable | Split out reason generation |
| Few daily active users | Low | Insufficient test power | Evaluation design, manual grading |
| Latency budget under 10 ms | Low | A forward pass will not fit | Review precomputation |
| Metadata is a title only | Low | Nothing to understand | Repair metadata |
| Heavily regulated domain | Low to medium | Review burden | Legal verification first |
| Catalog fixed at a few hundred | Low | Classic approach suffices | Improve diversity rules |

## 3. Evaluation design checklist

- [ ] Narrowed to exactly one offline metric (several means you cherry-pick)
- [ ] Chose one online short-term metric (click-through, first play rate)
- [ ] Chose one online long-term metric (4-week retention, return visits)
- [ ] Wrote down the tie-break rule for when the two move in opposite directions
- [ ] Computed the minimum sample and duration to detect a significant difference
- [ ] Fixed the traffic split ratio and the test window
- [ ] Checked for contamination from other concurrently running experiments
- [ ] Checked whether seasonality, a large release, or a holiday overlaps the window
- [ ] If cold start is the goal, built a separate metric that measures only that
- [ ] Checked whether offline and online metrics have ever moved together historically
- [ ] Wrote the failure criteria and rollback conditions before the test started

## 4. Order of attachment

1. **Step 0 — evaluation first.** Three metrics and a sample size. Without it, do not start
2. **Step 1 — reranking only.** Rescore the top 20 to 50. Smallest blast radius
3. **Step 2 — cold-start path.** Only items below an interaction threshold
4. **Step 3 — reason text.** Ranking untouched, display changed
5. **Step 4 — candidate generation and ranking core.** Only after steps 1 to 3 produced evidence

## 5. Cost checklist

- [ ] Set a ceiling on the context budget and named an owner
- [ ] Wrote down the precompute and caching strategy and the freshness loss you accept
- [ ] Fixed the ratio between the real-time path and the cache path
- [ ] Estimated the growth curve (user count × context length)
- [ ] Built the rollback path to the existing ranker and exercised it

## 6. Risk checklist

- [ ] Availability, rights, and age-rating filters still sit behind the model
- [ ] Rule filters for sensitive items (medical · adult · financial · political) retained
- [ ] If generated text is displayed, checked disclosure duties (verification needed)
- [ ] Checked lawful basis for processing and cross-border transfer (verification needed)
- [ ] Checked terms for third-party-authored text (verification needed)
- [ ] Decided on prompt logging and log retention period
- [ ] Added popularity-bias metrics (coverage, Gini) to monitoring

## 7. Do not do this

- ❌ Swapping the model before building an evaluation — you will not know if it helped
- ❌ Adopting someone else's improvement figure without its conditions — the size is a function of the baseline
- ❌ Attaching a text-understanding model when metadata is a title only
- ❌ Replacing candidate generation wholesale — start with reranking
- ❌ Killing a long-tail change on offline evidence alone
- ❌ Leaving the context budget without an owner — it will inflate
- ❌ Expecting the model to replace your rule filters
